The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior. The live disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, in determining economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions, arguing that they are the key drivers of economic growth. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that their impact is often overstated. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and development strategies aimed at fostering economic growth. Understanding the role of institutions can help policymakers design effective interventions that promote sustainable development. The debate between rival causes and the institutionalist perspective is central to this discussion. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work underscores that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. The English financial revolution, driven by the institutional changes that ensured greater security over time, facilitated the emergence of a long-term capital market. This institutional framework was crucial for economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for investment and market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms reduced transaction costs. This reduction, in turn, supported the growth of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that the success of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. The degree to which the sovereign is bound by these rules is a critical factor in sustaining economic growth. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England illustrate how secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitments, can drive long-term economic development.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, was designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, thereby hindering local economic development. Colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. This dynamic highlights how the initial institutional frameworks set during colonial rule had enduring impacts on economic development. This suggests that while initial institutions were crucial, subsequent economic opportunities and industrialization also significantly influenced long-term economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these institutions were shaped by the interests of powerful elites who sought to maintain their control and privileges. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition. This dynamic illustrates how institutions often emerged to safeguard the interests of the ruling class. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their role in perpetuating inequality and shaping economic outcomes. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that while institutions are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic outcomes; other factors, such as shared beliefs and cultural values, also play a significant role. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping institutions, their impact on economic growth is mediated by a complex interplay of social, political, and cultural forces.

The adverse effects of the slave trade on Africa's economic development underscore the enduring influence of these historical institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust among African societies, which has persisted and impeded economic growth. The slave trade operated for nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule, and its impact on economic development was significant and enduring. This culture of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon describe as a significant factor in reducing trust among different ethnic groups, has had a profound impact on economic development. This mistrust is deeply ingrained in the social fabric. The internal norms of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify as at least twice as large as the external channel, have created a pervasive environment of distrust that continues to hinder economic progress (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust, as Nunn (2008, p.2) notes, was exacerbated by the lack of centralized governments and well-developed markets in many African societies, making it difficult for these societies to recover from the economic devastation caused by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa, highlighting the deep and lasting impact of historical events on contemporary economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share a critique of the "reversal of fortune" thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating a dynamic interplay between local agency and colonial governance (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18) who notes that African economic history has been marked by a variety of institutional responses, some of which were effective in fostering development. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by pointing out that the persistence of poverty in Africa cannot be solely attributed to structural impediments, as the historical record shows periods of economic growth and development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role, their impact on long-run economic growth is mediated by a complex interplay of historical, geographical, and political factors.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective posits that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This is because major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, necessitate complementary adjustments in the institutional and ideological landscape. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is crucial for the diffusion of innovations through growing economies. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing that the growth of economic reasonableness is essential for sustaining economic improvement. Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Mokyr also notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, the institutional and ideological context plays a critical role in sustaining and diffusing these advancements. Kuznets further illustrates this point by noting that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between political and economic structures. Institutions, as defined by North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), shape the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior and outcomes. These institutional frameworks are crucial for managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress. The Bank of England's creation and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shows that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are pivotal for sustained economic development. Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that the institutional evolution and the political delivery of secure private property rights are key to long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The institutions that facilitate market exchanges and provide effective enforcement of property rights are essential for economic growth, as shown by North's discussion on transaction costs and economic development (North 1989, p.6). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19) demonstrates that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and that the creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is crucial for sustained economic development.

The historical evidence from colonialism further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the institutions introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. Similarly, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, showing that the institutions that were adopted in some countries but not in others were shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) demonstrates that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, showing that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence from colonialism and the new economic history of Africa demonstrate that the institutions that were adopted in some countries but not in others were shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, and that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by North (1989, p.9). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the institutions that were adopted in some countries but not in others were shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism.
