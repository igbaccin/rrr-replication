The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. At stake is the very nature of economic progress and the mechanisms that drive it. The debate centers on how institutions matter and which institutions are most consequential. One live disagreement concerns the nature of institutions that drive growth. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions that enforce the rule of law and secure property rights are crucial for fostering investment and economic growth. On the other hand, other scholars posit that distributional conflicts and the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions emerge are more critical in shaping economic outcomes. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy and our understanding of economic development. Institutions are not static but evolve over time, and their impact on economic growth is contingent on the broader historical and political context in which they operate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are critical for long-run economic growth. They establish a mechanism whereby institutions shape economic growth by influencing the security of property rights. North (1989, p.9) highlights that institutional changes, such as the creation of the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs. The downstream implication is that secure property rights encourage investment and economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth depends on whether they are designed to foster growth or to extract resources.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that political factors underpinning economic growth, such as the degree to which the regime was committed to enforcing property rights, were robustly established. This variation implies that the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve play a critical role in determining their effectiveness in promoting economic growth.

Technological advancements and institutional frameworks are complementary in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Both scholars recognize that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; it must be accompanied by institutional developments that facilitate the application and diffusion of new technologies. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to manage the social and political challenges that arise from technological advancements. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They propose that the institutional structures of European economies were shaped by the need to manage the conflicts between different social groups rather than by efficiency considerations. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions was the implementation of government policies that favoured elites, thereby restricting competition and preserving inequality. This pattern is evident in the New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). However, Ogilvie cautions against viewing institutions merely as a fashionable concept; instead, she advocates for their integration into the long-term research programs of economic historians. She suggests that institutions should be treated as indispensable instruments in understanding economic development, rather than as mere accessories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced perspective highlights the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions, shaping their development and influencing long-run economic growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the Black Death led to structural changes that created a wage gap by 1450, which suggests an alternative pathway to economic divergence independent of institutional factors. The Black Death triggered significant demographic shifts, leading to labor scarcity and subsequent wage increases in northwest Europe. This wage differential fostered economic development by incentivizing investment in labor-saving technologies and capital-intensive industries, particularly in regions with high institutional flexibility. For instance, Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that Holland's institutional flexibility allowed for rapid economic adaptation, enabling new towns to flourish and rural manufacturing to thrive under weak monopolies. This institutional environment contrasts with Tuscany, where political authorities strengthened existing monopolies, hindering economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further note that high productivity in the traded goods sector of north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, suggesting that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This economic development was not uniform, as the wages of skilled laborers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those of unskilled laborers, indicating that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly. This uneven distribution of economic gains implies that the structural changes initiated by the Black Death had complex and varied effects on different regions, influencing long-run economic growth in nuanced ways.

Institutional reforms in Africa have been pivotal in driving economic development, particularly in addressing poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the significance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this by noting that neopatrimonial tendencies, where government resources are treated as private property of political patrons, can undermine the delivery of broad-based public goods and services. This suggests that while institutional reforms are crucial, their effectiveness is contingent on the underlying governance structures and the distribution of power. This dynamic interplay between institutional reforms and governance structures underscores the complex nature of economic development in Africa, where the success of reforms is often determined by the ability to overcome entrenched patronage systems.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) identify that the role of women and colonial education in African development highlights specific aspects of colonial rule that affected economic outcomes. They show that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system and redefined customary law, which reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land and economic opportunities. This was particularly evident in the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men and excluded women from key economic activities. Moreover, the implementation of formal education systems under colonial rule altered the perceived benefits of education, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. The downstream implication is that while colonial education systems had significant impacts on African development, the effectiveness of these systems was contingent on the broader institutional frameworks and power dynamics of the time. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with colonial education systems playing a complex role in altering the perceived benefits of education and reinforcing existing power dynamics.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by demonstrating that significant economic growth occurred in the colonial era (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They show that many African economies, particularly Anglophone ones, had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century. This growth was driven by the expansion of foreign trade and export production, which varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner highlight that the timing and scale of this growth were closely tied to the market for exports, suggesting that economic development in colonial Africa was more dynamic than previously thought. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies further supports this view, indicating that poverty was not a persistent feature of African economies before 1950 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This challenges the idea that structural impediments have consistently hindered African economic development. The variability in growth patterns across different regions and periods underscores the complex interplay between local conditions and broader economic forces. This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, their impact was mediated by a range of factors that influenced the effectiveness of these institutions in promoting growth.

Institutions play a foundational role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating the framework within which economic activity operates. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced economic outcomes. They demonstrate that settler colonies, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, experienced different economic trajectories compared to extractive states where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole. This distinction underscores the importance of institutional design in fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) further elaborates on the critical role of institutions in economic development, highlighting how the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs. These institutional changes reflect increased security of property rights, which are essential for long-term economic growth. The significance of institutions in economic growth is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), who argues that institutions are not merely fashion accessories but indispensable instruments in the economic historian's toolkit.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonial Africa. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions are dynamic and responsive to local conditions. This adaptability is crucial for understanding how institutions can promote growth even in challenging environments. The new economic history of Africa, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), provides further insights into the role of institutions in economic development. Hopkins argues that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. This perspective aligns with the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the importance of institutional quality in fostering long-run economic development. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8) is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This extractive nature of institutions is a crucial factor in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. However, the new economic history of Africa, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), provides a different perspective by highlighting the importance of good institutions in providing incentives for investment. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
