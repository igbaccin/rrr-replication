The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. This question is at the heart of a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the causal role of institutions in economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during the colonial period significantly influenced the long-term economic trajectories of colonies. This perspective highlights how extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonizers, hindered economic development in many former colonies. Conversely, other scholars contend that institutions are not the sole determinant of economic growth and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and the opportunity to industrialize also play crucial roles. The debate extends to whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts and the extent to which cultural beliefs influence institutional outcomes. The central question is whether the long-run economic growth of nations is fundamentally determined by the institutions they adopt. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) contend that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This dimension is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that the institutional framework in England not only facilitated economic growth but also ensured that the benefits of economic activity were widely shared, laying the groundwork for sustained economic development. This highlights the importance of the specific institutional changes that occurred in England, which were critical to its economic success.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate. The type of institutions established in colonies was determined by European mortality rates and colonisation strategies, which varied widely. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. Where Europeans could settle more securely, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they established institutions that upheld the rule of law and promoted investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) show that these institutional differences persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth. This trend is reinforced by the fact that equilibrium institutions in colonies were frequently crafted to maximize rents for European colonists, not to stimulate long-term growth. This institutional design, driven by the political and economic interests of the colonising powers, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both examine specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience in older industrial economies have shaped current economic patterns. Specifically, Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, altering institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This highlights how institutional changes can be driven by the interplay between colonial interests and local dynamics. These insights collectively illustrate how the interplay between colonial policies and local economic actions shaped institutional outcomes, providing a more granular view of institutional evolution in pre-industrial Europe. This detailed understanding of institutional dynamics in Europe suggests that similar nuanced analyses could reveal more about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in other regions.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both highlight the importance of education in shaping economic trajectories, suggesting that the legacy of British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa and New World societies had profound effects on their respective economic developments. This educational development was driven by a complex interplay of factors, including the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. These calculations were shaped by the institutional frameworks introduced by the British, which provided new opportunities and incentives for education. The British colonial administration, with its emphasis on bureaucracy and infrastructure, created an environment where formal education was seen as a pathway to social mobility and economic advancement. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders cooperation and reduces the likelihood of mutually beneficial exchanges. The mechanism of mistrust is not solely attributable to the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others, but rather it is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. The persistence of this mistrust can be traced to the slave trade's role in shaping societal norms, which in turn affects economic outcomes. The legacy of the slave trade is evident in the reduced levels of intergroup trust, a factor that is crucial for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates this by noting that the slave trade disrupted existing economic and social institutions, which were essential for supporting trade and economic growth. This disruption impeded the development of robust markets and trading networks, which are critical for sustained economic growth. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are complex and multifaceted, encompassing both internal and external factors that continue to influence African economies today.

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further highlighted by the distributional conflicts that arise from institutional changes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and these beliefs can influence the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts. These institutions were crafted to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, not to maximize long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions are created and how they evolve over time.
