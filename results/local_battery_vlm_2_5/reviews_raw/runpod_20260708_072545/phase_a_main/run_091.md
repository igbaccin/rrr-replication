Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, a relationship that has sparked intense debate among scholars. The core disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, hold more explanatory power. One camp emphasizes the critical role of institutions in fostering economic growth by providing secure property rights and encouraging investment. Another camp argues that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and culture playing significant roles. Additionally, some scholars propose that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by other variables, such as the presence of European settlers or the specific strategies employed by colonial powers. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth in developing economies. The institutional perspective posits that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic growth. This view is supported by evidence showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups often hinder long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of financial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th-century England are notable examples of institutional changes that facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth. These developments reflect a broader trend where political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms led to a decline in transaction costs. Such institutional changes involved economic exchange rules and the political factors governing their enforcement and modification. A critical factor in this process is the regime's commitment to these rules, which underpins the credibility of property rights and encourages investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutional changes is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) notes that while transaction costs per exchange may fall, the aggregate volume of exchanges increases, making the transactions sector a growing proportion of GNP. This implies that the benefits of reduced transaction costs are amplified by the increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn drive long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman's shared argument is that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These scholars jointly establish that the economic institutions in these societies were shaped by the power dynamics between elites and non-elites, with elites using their political power to create institutions that maintained their economic dominance. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, the merchant guilds were powerful institutions that regulated trade and commerce, but they were also instruments of elite control, restricting competition and maintaining high prices. Similarly, in New World societies, institutions like slavery and serfdom were maintained by elites to ensure a steady supply of cheap labor. These distributional conflicts involved the allocation of resources and the power to shape institutions that would perpetuate economic inequality. One downstream implication of this argument is that institutional change is driven by shifts in power dynamics. Ogilvie cautions that this does not mean that institutions are purely a product of power struggles. She acknowledges that efficiency concerns can also play a role in shaping institutions, but argues that they are often secondary to distributional conflicts. This suggests that while institutional efficiency may be a factor in economic growth, it is the resolution of distributional conflicts that ultimately determines the shape and function of economic institutions.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced, leading to either extractive or growth-promoting institutions. This was a result of the strategic choices made by colonizers. The extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run economic growth. These institutions persisted over time and shaped the economic trajectories of the colonies. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics at play, where the colonizers had the upper hand. The influence of extractive institutions on the development experience of former colonies is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among these regions. Acemoglu et al. document this reversal and link it to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This reversal is a crucial implication of the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers, as it altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which influenced cost-benefit calculations at both the macro and micro levels. These institutional changes were not uniform across all colonies; the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The resulting economic growth was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, reflecting the broader impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the importance of long-term economic data in assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade, lasting from 1400 to 1900, had profound effects on African institutions and economic development. The slave trade's impact on African economic development is multifaceted and not limited to the direct loss of human capital. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade had enduring effects on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. The slave trade disrupted local economies and undermined social cohesion and trust, which are crucial for economic development. Nunn notes that the slave trade created a legacy of mistrust that has had lasting effects on economic institutions. This legacy of mistrust has been shown to affect contemporary economic outcomes, including trust and cooperation. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channels, indicating a deep-seated cultural impact. This mistrust has implications for various aspects of economic development, including trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The legacy of mistrust, combined with the disruption of social and economic institutions, has contributed to Africa's underdevelopment. The slave trade's impact on African economic development is complex and multifaceted, with enduring effects on institutions and social norms.

Van Zanden emphasizes that the skill premium predicts long-term economic performance, a claim that Bolt and van Zanden support (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). In particular, van Zanden argues that the skill premium is determined by training costs, institutional barriers to entry, interest rates, and the duration of skilled employment. The role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth is evident in the skill premium, with formal institutions like guilds facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills. The monopolistic tendencies of these institutions could also restrict entry and inflate wages beyond market-clearing levels, potentially offsetting their benefits. Bolt and van Zanden underscore the importance of collaborative research in refining our understanding of economic performance, highlighting the need for accurate measurement. This collaborative approach is essential for improving the precision of long-term economic growth estimates, which in turn can enhance our understanding of the role of institutions in economic development.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver for economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements can independently fuel long-run economic growth without necessarily relying on institutional adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This progress involves the spread of these innovations across substantial parts of the world. This dissemination requires institutional and ideological adjustments to integrate new technologies into existing economic frameworks. Mokyr also emphasizes that technological advancements are the mainspring of economic growth. He highlights that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is crucial for sustaining economic improvements. Without such institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements could be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This implies that while technological advancements are essential, they must be complemented by institutional adjustments to ensure sustainable economic growth. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that while institutions are important, their role is more complex and less predictable. This complexity indicates that technological advancements can drive economic growth independently, but their full potential is realized when supported by effective institutional frameworks.

Reevaluating historical economic theories offers nuanced perspectives on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the role of colonial administrations in shaping African economies (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This is significant because colonial administrations were not monolithic entities; they often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, altering the institutional landscape to suit local interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, a finding that complicates the notion of a singular colonial impact on African economies. The flexibility of colonial institutions, as shown by Austin, aligns with Hopkins' (2009) assertion that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating that effective institutions can emerge even in diverse societies. This implies that the legacy of colonial institutions in Africa is more complex than often portrayed, with potential for both positive and negative impacts on economic development. The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they operated, and their effects are not always predictable or uniform.
