The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the field of economic development. At stake is the ability to explain the divergent economic trajectories of nations over centuries. The debate centres around the extent to which institutions, rather than other factors such as geography or culture, drive economic outcomes. One position argues that institutions are crucial in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment and exchange. This perspective often highlights the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Another position posits that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their influence is often overstated, with other variables such as geography or cultural beliefs playing equally significant roles. A third position suggests that institutions themselves are shaped by underlying economic and political forces, and thus their role in economic growth is a consequence of these deeper structures. The interplay between these positions reveals a complex landscape where the causal mechanisms linking institutions to economic growth are not straightforward. For example, the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, can significantly impact economic outcomes. The debate extends to whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, with implications for policy and historical analysis. Understanding this relationship is crucial for developing effective strategies for economic growth and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This development reflected an increase in the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North (1989, p.6) clarifies the impact of these institutions by noting that while transaction costs per transaction may fall, the aggregate volume of exchanges increases due to specialization and division of labor. This implies that the overall transaction costs, in terms of the aggregate economy, may not decrease as dramatically as initially suggested. Nevertheless, the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights remains a pivotal factor in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. This dynamic highlights the intricate relationship between institutional development and economic performance, where secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are essential for sustained growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutions established by European colonizers played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions varied significantly based on European policies, with settler colonies like the United States and Australia developing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of these institutional legacies is evident in the current income per capita of these regions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further substantiate this by showing that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, yield consistent results in explaining economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial. This interaction suggests that while institutions are foundational, their impact on economic growth is mediated by historical context and economic opportunities.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) challenge the view that institutions are the sole determinant of long-run economic growth, arguing instead that historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes are mediated by more than institutional factors. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is shared by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) who emphasize that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy is one mechanism through which historical factors shape contemporary economic outcomes. The legacy of mistrust is a specific historical factor that can affect economic outcomes by undermining trust, which is crucial for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates this by showing that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on the historical and political context. This highlights the importance of considering the complex interplay between institutions and historical legacies in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). For instance, societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This underscores the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes, independent of ethnic diversity.

This perspective challenges the notion that African economic development is inherently constrained by structural factors, instead pointing to the potential for growth if the right institutions are in place. This implies that institutions, which can be shaped by historical experiences, are pivotal in determining long-run economic growth. This suggests that while historical legacies matter, the quality of institutions can mitigate or exacerbate their effects on economic outcomes.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This technological progress requires institutional adjustments to ensure that innovations are efficiently used. For Kuznets, the institutional framework must evolve to manage growth-induced conflicts, which is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Mokyr suggests that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements might be limited and ultimately reversed by rent-seeking behavior. This implies that while technological progress is the primary driver, the quality of institutions plays a critical role in sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. Kuznets highlights that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community, plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, ensuring long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is complex, but it is clear that both are necessary for sustained economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not institutions themselves, are the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They contend that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by the conflicts over resource distribution. This perspective challenges the efficiency-based explanations, suggesting instead that these institutions were designed to manage and mitigate conflicts between different social groups. For instance, the craft guilds, often seen as mechanisms for ensuring quality and regulating competition, can also be viewed as tools for elites to maintain control over labor and resources. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) elaborate on this by examining how the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that advantaged elites. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that perpetuate inequality and restrict competition. This pattern underscores that the role of institutions in economic growth involves power dynamics and resource allocation. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further emphasizes the need for economic historians to incorporate institutions into their long-term research programs, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these distributional conflicts can provide insights into the economic development of societies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent paths of development among societies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-term growth, experienced significantly lower income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

Institutional change involves power dynamics and resource allocation. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development because they specify property rights and provide enforcement, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Austin, who notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions are shaped by the interplay of power and interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6) shows that institutions evolve in response to political and economic pressures, reflecting the underlying power dynamics of societies.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further emphasized by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.18) shows that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires a nuanced examination of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. The historical evidence of colonial rule and the empirical comparisons of former colonies demonstrate that extractive institutions persist and significantly affect current economic outcomes. This evidence settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
