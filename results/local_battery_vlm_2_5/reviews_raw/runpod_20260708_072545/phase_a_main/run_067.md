The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure interactions, can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes and those who argue for other factors, such as geography or culture, as the primary drivers. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the key causal variable or merely one among many. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced current income per capita, while Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) suggests that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The stakes are high: if institutions are the primary determinant, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could be crucial for economic development. If other factors are more influential, different policy approaches might be necessary. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth has real-world implications for development strategies.

Building on this foundation, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were crucial in this process. These institutions facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and enhancing economic growth. This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, during which certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This trade-off highlights the political underpinnings of economic growth, where the regime's commitment to enforcing rules and property rights is critical. This is evident in the emergence of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by profiling transaction costs in a growing economy, where increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional factors in economic development.

Extending this analysis to a global scale, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The authors argue that European colonizers established different institutions depending on their mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic development and persisted even after independence. Colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy continues to affect the economic trajectories of former colonies today.

Shifting focus to pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) echo this perspective, noting that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to benefit elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics that allowed elites to maintain their control over resources and labor. This power dynamic, in turn, shaped the distribution of wealth and human capital, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality. The implications of this institutional design are profound, as they affected the economic trajectories of these societies and influenced the long-term development of their political and social structures. The legacy of these institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical distributional conflicts on modern economic growth.

Expanding on this historical perspective, Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) re-evaluate the colonial impacts on Africa by focusing on how colonial governments influenced economic activities and rents. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This highlights how colonial administrations responded to local economic and political actions, adjusting their policies to maintain fiscal security and encourage material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) builds on this by emphasizing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. He argues that the prominence of institutions in development policy allows for a deeper understanding of how incentives for investment are crucial for development. This perspective is supported by the idea that 'good' institutions provide the necessary framework for economic growth, aligning with the broader theme of how colonial policies influenced long-term economic trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further qualify this by suggesting that African poverty is not solely a result of persistent structural impediments. They argue for a more nuanced understanding of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth. This implies that the historical and geographical conditions of colonial rule have had varying effects on long-term African welfare development, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The slave trade in Africa has been argued to have had a profound adverse effect on long-run economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day. This mistrust, in turn, has a significant impact on economic development, as it affects both internal norms and external environments, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The slave trade's impact on trust is deeply ingrained in the cultural norms of the affected populations. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates that the slave trade disrupted local economic and social institutions, making it difficult for African societies to develop the necessary infrastructure for sustained trade and economic growth. This disruption was particularly pronounced in regions where centralized governments and well-developed markets were absent, as these areas were less able to support trade with Europeans. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are not limited to the immediate period of the trade itself. The legacy of the slave trade continues to influence economic development in Africa today, as the mistrust and institutional disruptions caused by the trade persist.

In this context, technology and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) explains that technological advancements often necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently integrated into the economy. This dynamic interplay between technological progress and institutional changes is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where societies had to adapt their social structures and political systems to accommodate new technologies. Institutional progress complements technological advancements, ensuring that the benefits of new technologies are sustained over the long term. Mokyr (2005, p.49) adds that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological progress is a driving force, it requires supportive institutions to fully realize its potential. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intertwined with the ability of societies to adapt to and integrate technological advancements effectively.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering an environment conducive to investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. The presence of extractive institutions, which prioritized resource extraction for the benefit of the colonial power, hindered long-term economic growth. Institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment led to more sustainable economic development. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) reinforces this idea by highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated financial transactions and reduced costs, illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic progress.

Moreover, the interaction between institutions and technological advancements is crucial for long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors. These institutional changes determined the ability of societies to adapt to and integrate technological advancements effectively. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. This evolution is influenced by both exogenous factors, such as colonial policies, and endogenous factors, such as indigenous economic and political action. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intertwined with the ability of societies to adapt to and integrate technological advancements effectively.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are shaped by efficiency or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This view is supported by the historical evidence of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, which were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) emphasizes the role of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in promoting economic growth. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated financial transactions and reduced costs. This institutional change, driven by the need to secure property rights and reduce transaction costs, illustrates how efficient institutions can promote economic growth.
