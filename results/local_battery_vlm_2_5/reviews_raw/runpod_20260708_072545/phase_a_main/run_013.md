The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and economic historians. The debate centers on whether institutions, such as property rights, legal systems, and political structures, are the primary drivers of economic development or if they merely complement other factors like geography or culture. A key disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions can be causally linked to economic outcomes, with some scholars arguing that institutions are crucial for fostering growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity, while others contend that institutions are secondary to other, more fundamental determinants such as climate, geography, and culture. This debate has significant implications for understanding the historical paths of economic development and for designing policies aimed at promoting growth in developing economies.

The debate over institutions is closely tied to the broader question of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic growth. This transition is often marked by significant institutional changes, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.9), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they provide the framework for market exchanges and the enforcement of property rights. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Institutions are thus seen as essential for creating the conditions necessary for economic growth, as they provide the stability and predictability that investors and entrepreneurs require. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by creating a favorable environment for investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. They posit that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in areas with lower mortality, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered long-term growth by failing to foster private property rights and stable economic environments. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these extractive institutions has been a significant barrier to economic development in these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) also note that the impact of these institutions is not uniform and can be influenced by various factors. They show that the results hold even when using alternative measures of institutions, indicating the robustness of their findings. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-term economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), illustrating the intricate relationship between historical institutional changes and economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) examine how institutions shaped by inequality and the slave trade influenced economic development in specific regions. The institutions that emerged in societies with high levels of inequality often favored elites, creating barriers to economic growth. This dynamic is evident in New World societies, where initial inequalities in wealth and human capital led to the establishment of institutions that perpetuated these disparities. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this perspective by highlighting the enduring effects of the slave trade on social trust, a critical component of economic development. This mistrust, deeply ingrained through historical experiences, continues to affect economic interactions and institutional effectiveness. By understanding these historical influences, it becomes clear that the legacy of inequality and the slave trade has had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of affected regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that colonial rule in Africa had specific economic impacts, including the role of women in economic development and the differential effects of British rule on education. Women's contributions to economic growth during colonial periods were significant, yet often overlooked. This gendered division of labor had profound implications for economic development. This pattern of education development varied across regions, with British rule being more conducive to formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate that the growth and shrinkage of economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the complexity of economic development under colonial rule, where different regions experienced diverse outcomes based on institutional frameworks and resource endowments. The differential impacts of colonial rule on education and economic development highlight the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, focusing instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). He studied wage data from various European cities and regions, concluding that there was no straightforward relationship between economic growth and improvements in living standards. This finding is significant because it challenges the idea of an "industrious revolution" in Western Europe during this era. This period saw a shift in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England. In the 17th century, economic development in these regions led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries by 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift highlights the regional disparities in economic development within Europe, influenced by factors such as institutional flexibility. Pamuk (2007, p.23) notes that Holland's rapid response to new economic opportunities was facilitated by its institutional flexibility, allowing new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to thrive under weak monopolies. This institutional environment contrasts with Tuscany, where political authorities strengthened existing monopolies, including their own (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, such as the wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, underscore the role of historical events in shaping regional economic trajectories. The urbanisation rates in northwestern and southern Europe also diverged significantly, with northwestern Europe experiencing higher urbanisation and productivity levels. This divergence suggests that institutional factors played a crucial role in determining the economic development paths of different regions. The institutional environment in Holland, characterised by flexibility and weak monopolies, enabled it to rapidly adapt to new economic opportunities, whereas Tuscany's rigid institutional structure hindered its economic growth. This institutional flexibility in Holland likely contributed to its transformation into one of the most advanced, urbanised, and commercialised regions of the continent.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that long-run economic growth is primarily driven by technological advancements and the efficient use of innovations, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets highlights that technological progress and economic growth are interdependent, with technological changes necessitating corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments. This interplay is crucial for sustaining growth over long periods. Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge, which is the mainspring of economic growth, requires institutional progress to complement technological progress. Without these institutional adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited, as Mokyr notes that without institutional progress, economic improvements might be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets elaborates on this mechanism, explaining that technological progress introduces conflicts that must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible. These conflicts are social and political, as well as technical, requiring the sovereign state to play a crucial role in resolving them. The state's authority, based on loyalty and a community of feeling, is essential for peacefully managing these growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that they do not weaken the political fabric of society. Kuznets's analysis underscores that the sovereign state's role in resolving these conflicts is pivotal, as it ensures that the institutional adjustments needed for technological progress are made without excessive costs. Kuznets also notes that the pace and nature of technological changes and the corresponding institutional adjustments vary over centuries and among regions. This variation suggests that the specific institutional and ideological adjustments required for efficient use of innovations can differ significantly across different contexts.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, a critique shared by Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique is particularly salient in the context of colonial Africa, where the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often adapted in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, Austin shows that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labour, and that fiscal security motivated colonial administrations to adjust their institutional frameworks (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie, meanwhile, argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency does. She contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by power struggles and the interests of dominant groups, rather than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth was a complex interplay of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie’s emphasis on distributional conflicts highlights the need to consider the political and social context in which institutions emerge and evolve. This perspective qualifies the notion that institutions are adopted based on their efficiency, suggesting that historical and political factors play a significant role in shaping economic institutions.
