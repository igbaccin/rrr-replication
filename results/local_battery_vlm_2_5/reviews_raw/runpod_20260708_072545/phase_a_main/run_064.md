The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are necessary for sustained economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key determinants of economic growth, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors that influence economic development. The stakes are high: if institutions are indeed the primary drivers of economic growth, then policies aimed at institutional reform could have profound implications for economic development in regions where institutions are weak. If other factors are more important, focusing on institutional reform may be misguided. The debate is further complicated by the fact that institutions are not static entities; they evolve over time and are shaped by a variety of factors, including historical events, cultural norms, and political dynamics. Understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a nuanced analysis of these complex interactions. A key point of contention is the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and how they interact with other factors such as geography and culture. While some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, others argue that institutions are themselves shaped by economic and political forces. This debate has significant implications for policy, as it determines the most effective strategies for promoting economic development. This institutional change was critical for both a long-term capital market and for economic growth itself. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that these institutional changes were significant, but they were not revolutionary in isolation. This relationship is further complicated by the diverse paths of institutional development in different colonies, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). These institutions were fundamentally shaped by European mortality rates, which in turn determined the type of colonization strategy employed. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This strategy was evident in colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was resource extraction, not long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions were more conducive to economic growth and development. The persistence of these institutional frameworks has had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The efficiency of these institutions is reflected in various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all of which correlate with economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This thesis, however, fails to account for the complex interplay of factors influencing economic development over time, as noted by Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). This perspective highlights the need to consider the specific institutional trajectories of countries, not relying on a single explanatory framework. Austin (2008, p.2) identifies that colonial institutions were not uniform and their impact varied based on local conditions. For instance, colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, indicating a more nuanced dynamic than a simple extractive institutional framework (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuance is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who show that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and influences economic outcomes. The implications of this historical mistrust are profound, as it affects various aspects of economic development, including trust in political institutions and firm management practices. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement the findings of Austin (2008, p.2) by illustrating that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This highlights the deep-seated nature of institutional legacies and their enduring influence on economic outcomes, even in the face of changing external conditions.

This perspective challenges the view that institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations alone. Instead, these scholars suggest that institutions often arise from the struggle between different social groups over the distribution of resources. For instance, Ogilvie highlights that the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe can be attributed to the landowning elite's efforts to maintain their control over the labor force (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions evolved to protect the interests of elites, who used their political power to enact policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie cautions that distributional conflicts are crucial, but they do not operate in isolation.
