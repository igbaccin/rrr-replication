The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with far-reaching implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: while some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, others contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, with other factors such as geography or culture playing significant roles. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The stakes are high, as understanding the true role of institutions can inform effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. This debate is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and other factors, such as the impact of colonial policies on institutional development. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they operate in conjunction with other factors, remains a contentious issue. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) share the position that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs. This development fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Their evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this, showing that the institutional changes described significantly enhanced economic performance. This is evident in the remarkable success of England's political institutions, which were committed to enforcing rules that underpinned economic growth and the development of markets. The degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules is a critical political factor. This commitment is crucial because it ensures that the rules governing economic exchange are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon procedures. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) qualify that this institutional commitment is not the only factor driving economic growth; the rules governing economic exchange also play a significant role. The institutional changes in England during the 17th century reduced transaction costs and created a stable environment for economic activities. This stability is essential for long-run economic growth, as it encourages investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The European colonizers' mortality rates were a significant determinant of the institutions they established in their colonies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they were less likely to settle permanently and instead established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, which enforced few property rights and provided limited economic incentives, persisted long after the colonial period ended. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that this pattern of institutional development resulted in lower income per capita in these former colonies. These institutions were designed to extract resources for the benefit of the colonizers. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even after independence, has had lasting negative effects on economic development. This is because extractive institutions do not encourage investment or innovation, which are crucial for long-run economic growth, and the political power of elites who benefit from these institutions can hinder institutional change. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that even when there is potential for economic gains, the lack of direct benefits to the ruling elite can make them reluctant to initiate institutional changes.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This aligns with Frankema (2012, p.15), who argues that British colonial rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa by altering the perceived benefits of education through new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is underscored by the fact that they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The impact of colonial institutions on African economic development is nuanced. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified customary law, which often reinforced the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification of customary law had downstream implications for land access and economic opportunities, particularly for women. The interplay between colonial institutions and economic development in Africa is complex, with institutions playing a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing investment incentives and public goods provision.

The literature on African economic performance and historical development does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, instead focusing on other factors such as the skill premium and economic data availability (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the role of institutions in guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts, particularly the guilds in western Europe, which facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills. These institutions were also criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, which could counterbalance their beneficial effects. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) underscore the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The interplay between these factors and institutions suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects are mediated by a complex web of economic and social conditions.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by differences in productivity and urbanization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gupta and Pamuk share a focus on the role of urbanization and productivity in traded goods sectors. Pamuk suggests that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap, in turn, fostered institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, enabling rapid economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta further elaborate on the importance of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, highlighting that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This underscores the critical role of institutions in facilitating economic growth by enhancing productivity and urbanization. Pamuk's findings on the early divergence in urbanization rates between northwestern and southern Europe further support this view, indicating that institutional factors played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) cautions that economic development did not necessarily lead to improvements in the standard of living. This qualification suggests that while institutions may drive economic growth, their impact on living standards is more complex and mediated by various factors. The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, with productivity and urbanization serving as key mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) focus on specific regional dynamics and socio-economic issues in Africa, emphasizing that post-independence economic trends were shaped by particular expenditure patterns and land management systems. These expenditures reflect a neopatrimonial logic in which government resources are treated as the private property of political patrons, used to buy loyalty and deliver broad-based public goods. This dynamic is evident in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. Military spending also grew significantly due to regional instability and domestic dissent, shifting the economic landscape during the independence period (Simson 2019, p.11). Meanwhile, Peters (2004, p.19) discusses how pervasive competition and conflict over land in sub-Saharan Africa reveal processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions (Peters 2004, p.19). This competition is driven by a perception in official circles that customary landholding practices are static and inhibiting individual investment, a view that was prominent during the push for agricultural development in the final decade of colonial rule. The dissatisfaction expressed by agricultural officers highlights the tensions between traditional practices and modernized farming initiatives (Peters 2004, p.5). The downstream implication of these expenditure and land management patterns is a complex interplay between political patronage and economic development, often leading to consumption-focused policy-making and investment in public goods. This dynamic underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, where political and socio-economic factors intertwine to influence economic outcomes.

The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This dynamic is evident in the policies implemented by governments, which often served to entrench the power of the elite class. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this view, asserting that Africa's slave trades had a profound adverse effect on economic development, a factor that often overshadows the impact of colonialism or more recent events. The persistence of these inequalities can be seen in the enduring mistrust within African societies, a legacy of the slave trade that continues to influence contemporary economic and social dynamics (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is not solely a product of the external environment, such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, but is deeply ingrained in the internal norms of mistrust within these societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalization of mistrust, rooted in historical inequalities, continues to shape economic and social interactions, underscoring the enduring impact of slavery on institutional development.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets share a view that economic growth is fundamentally tied to technological advancements. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr argues, is essential for sustained economic improvement. This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who highlights that technological progress requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations. For instance, the development of new technologies often necessitates changes in the social and political fabric of society to accommodate these advancements. Kuznets notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, requiring significant institutional and ideological adjustments to integrate these new technologies into the economy. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for economic growth. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is essential, it is not sufficient on its own. The growth of economic reasonableness, which Mokyr defines as the ability to make rational economic decisions, is also necessary for sustainable economic improvement. Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and might ultimately be eroded by rent-seeking behavior.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between historical paths and institutional evolution. This challenge is compounded by the fact that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, and not adhering to a uniform model of institutional development (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) extends this critique by arguing that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for economic historians, and not mere fashion accessories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by a desire for economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The implication is that the historical development of institutions is more nuanced and context-dependent than institutional theories often acknowledge.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and distribution of resources. Acemoglu et al. contend that the nature of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North underscores that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, was crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This highlights the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic development. These institutions ensured the security of property rights and fostered an environment conducive to long-term investment and economic activity. The establishment of such institutions reflects a broader historical trend where political and economic stability are intertwined, with secure property rights being a cornerstone of economic prosperity.

The influence of institutions on economic growth is further evidenced by the historical development of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of this period, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups often did not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and colonial periods demonstrates that institutions are tools for economic efficiency and products of political and social dynamics. These dynamics shape the institutions that, in turn, influence long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between colonial policies and local economic conditions. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior shows that institutions evolve in response to changing circumstances. Hopkins emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that understanding the historical context of institutional development is crucial for designing effective policies. The historical evidence from Africa and other regions shows that institutions matter for economic growth, but their effectiveness depends on their adaptability and responsiveness to local conditions. The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The measurement of income per capita in former colonies shows that extractive institutions significantly affected current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The case of the Bank of England illustrates how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
