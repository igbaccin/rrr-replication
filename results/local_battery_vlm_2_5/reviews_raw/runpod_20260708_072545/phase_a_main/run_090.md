The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectory of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lie competing views on the causal mechanisms driving economic growth. One prominent perspective posits that institutions, by defining and enforcing property rights and reducing transaction costs, are fundamental to fostering economic growth. This view emphasizes the political and legal frameworks that underpin economic activity. Another perspective posits that the efficiency of institutions takes a backseat to distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs, which mold the institutional landscape to favor the interests of powerful groups over broad-based economic development. The tension between these views underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their impact on economic outcomes. Understanding this interplay is crucial for policy-makers aiming to design effective strategies for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This decline reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change underpins the credibility of commitments to property rights, which in turn facilitates economic growth. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP. This pattern of decreasing transaction costs and increasing market exchanges is a direct outcome of the institutional changes that secure property rights. This institutional framework governs economic exchange and ensures that these rules are enforced and can be changed in a predictable manner, thus fostering economic growth.

Distributional conflicts molded the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, with Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) agreeing that these institutions were primarily influenced by struggles over wealth and power, not efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions should be treated as essential tools in the toolkit of economic historians. This perspective highlights the need to understand how distributional conflicts influenced the development of institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. These institutions, in turn, governed economic exchange and shaped long-run economic growth by determining who had access to resources and opportunities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) elaborate on this by showing that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is crucial because it illustrates how the initial distribution of power and resources can lock in institutional structures that hinder economic growth.

Colonial rule in Africa influenced institutional change through negotiation and imposition dynamics, which in turn affected long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The legacy of colonial rule, including the imposition of formal education systems, further influenced institutional development. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This development of formal education systems had a significant impact on the institutional environment, altering the benefits of formal education and shaping long-run economic growth. The persistence of mistrust and the legacy of colonial institutions indicate that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development is complex and multifaceted. The negotiation and imposition dynamics of colonial rule left enduring marks on African institutions, which continue to shape economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective challenges views that marginalize the role of African women and the economic performance of the region. This outcome was due to both colonial imposition and patriarchal alliances that shaped customary law. This data reveals that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further note that these patterns of growing and shrinking were in most countries tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports, suggesting that the economic performance of African economies was closely linked to global trade dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) observe that the codification of customary law under colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced the subordinate status of women, which had implications for economic development. This codification process was a key avenue for the marginalization of women's economic contributions. The implications of these findings are significant, as they suggest that the economic contributions of African women and the performance of African economies were shaped by a complex interplay of local, colonial, and global factors.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is pivotal for economic growth. This echoes Kuznets (1973, p.1) who argues that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that the integration of technological advancements into the economic fabric requires a supportive institutional environment. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural modes of life and extended family patterns, indicating that the adoption of new technologies requires significant social and cultural adjustments. This highlights that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, they also require a conducive institutional and social environment to be fully realized.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that colonial institutions are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism through which these institutions operate is by shaping the economic incentives and political power dynamics within the colonies. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that the impact of colonial institutions was not uniform across all colonies (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects were mediated by local conditions and responses. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by North's work on the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.9). North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets and economic growth. Ogilvie extends this discussion by emphasizing the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutional structures that emerged from these conflicts, such as serfdom and guilds, had profound implications for economic development. These institutions, shaped by the distributional struggles of the time, influenced the economic trajectories of these regions.
