The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures, rules, and norms influence sustained economic prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and the specific historical contexts of colonization are more determinative. Still others propose that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies shape economic institutions in ways that prioritize efficiency. The interplay between these positions highlights the complexity of economic growth and the multifaceted nature of institutional influence. It is the interplay between these positions that underlines the importance of understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This was achieved through the establishment of a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the sovereign, thereby ensuring that property rights were protected from arbitrary seizure. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments further solidified this security, dramatically reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by explaining that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to a reduction in transaction costs. This, in turn, facilitated market exchanges and increased specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges and thus the size of the transactions sector. The significance of secure property rights is further underscored by the fact that these institutional changes were both economic and political, reflecting a broader commitment to the rule of law and the protection of individual rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) qualify the impact of these institutional changes by noting that while they were crucial, the degree to which the regime was committed to these rules was also a critical factor. This commitment ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were established, enforced, and could be changed in a predictable manner. The implications of this commitment are profound, as it underpins the development of markets and economic growth. The evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England thus provides a clear example of how secure property rights, enforced through credible commitments, can drive long-term economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize rent extraction. This institutional divergence was driven by the settler's incentives, which were shaped by the mortality environment and the resulting economic and political conditions. In colonies with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutions has had profound effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional differences are robust to various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the initial institutional framework established during colonialism continues to influence economic performance today. The implications of these findings are significant, as they highlight the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) re-evaluate historical economic performance and colonial impacts by examining specific cases of commodity production and educational development under different colonial administrations. They find that colonial administrations could encourage commodity production when settlers were not competing for local labor. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments often sought fiscal security and would adjust their institutional policies to respond to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions. This suggests that colonial administrations were not monolithic entities and could adapt to local conditions, which could foster economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Frankema (2012, p.15) extends this point by showing that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, highlighting the role of colonial education policies in shaping long-term economic growth. This implies that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform and could vary based on the specific policies and adaptations made by different colonial administrations.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, with Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasizing that these conflicts were more influential than efficiency considerations. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be understood as a means for landowners to maintain control over the labor force, ensuring their economic dominance. Similarly, craft and merchant guilds, while ostensibly regulating quality and training, also served to restrict competition and maintain the economic privileges of their members. This perspective highlights that the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe was deeply influenced by the struggles between various social classes, with each group seeking to secure its economic interests through institutional arrangements. The specific mechanisms by which these distributional conflicts translated into institutional forms varied across different regions and historical contexts. For example, in some areas, the balance of power might have favored the creation of institutions that restricted economic activity, while in others, it might have led to more inclusive institutions that fostered economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time also depended on the ability of the dominant groups to maintain their power and influence, which could be challenged by internal and external factors such as political upheavals or technological changes. For instance, the decline of guilds in some regions can be attributed to the rise of new economic activities that were not easily controlled by traditional guild structures, or to political changes that weakened the power of guilds. This underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to changing economic and political conditions.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn both argue that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies, which has persisted and continues to hinder economic growth. This mistrust has been shown to affect intergroup relations and economic interactions, creating a hostile environment for trade and investment. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust is deeply ingrained within the social fabric of African societies. This internal norm of mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate, is at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this view, emphasizing that technological advancements and the diffusion of knowledge are central to economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. This process is not linear; technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors if not complemented by institutional progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) underscores that coordination failures and the need for policies to rectify them are recognized as essential for sustaining economic growth. This interplay between technological and institutional progress is evident in the historical development of formal institutions aimed at reducing access costs to knowledge. The growth of economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is vital for the long-term sustainability of economic improvement. The development of scholarly societies and periodicals during the 17th and 18th centuries in Europe illustrates how institutional frameworks can support the dissemination of useful knowledge, thereby fostering economic growth. The role of these institutions in facilitating technological progress and economic development is a testament to the interconnected nature of technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic performance.

Institutions, as the primary drivers of economic growth, can be understood through their role in shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. North and Weingast show that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England, facilitated by institutions governing public choice, fostered economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. reinforce the importance of institutions by arguing that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores the interconnected nature of institutions and economic outcomes, where the type of institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, determines long-term economic performance. These insights are further supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development because they specify and enforce property rights, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the historical context of colonialism and its impact on institutional development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is consistent with North's argument that institutions shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.6). Austin challenges the simplistic view of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows that European settlers' presence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production, and colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Austin is the role of European settlers in shaping institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. show that the presence of European settlers led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional frameworks in former European colonies and the impact of European settlers on these frameworks. This evidence shows that the type of institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, determines long-term economic performance and that the presence of European settlers played a crucial role in shaping these institutions.
