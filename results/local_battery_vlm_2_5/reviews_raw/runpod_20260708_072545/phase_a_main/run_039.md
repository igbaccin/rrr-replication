The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the efficacy of contemporary policy interventions. At stake is the identification of causal mechanisms that drive economic performance over extended periods. The debate centres around the primacy of institutions in fostering growth, with some scholars arguing that institutions are pivotal in creating the necessary conditions for sustained economic development, while others contend that institutions are merely one of several factors, with other causes such as geography or culture playing equally significant roles. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely complementary factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) share the view that institutions are crucial for economic growth, with North (1989, p.9) arguing that institutions determine the efficiency of property rights and their enforcement, thereby reducing transaction costs. These institutions are key in shaping the long-run economic growth of nations, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) pointing out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications of these findings suggest that the type of institutions established in a country can have a lasting impact on its economic development, with extractive institutions potentially hindering growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the institutional evolution in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. The institutional changes in England, particularly the establishment of credible commitments and the enforcement of property rights, were crucial for economic development. This process was not merely about the rules governing economic exchange but also about the institutions that enforced and changed these rules. The political factors underpinning economic growth, such as the regime's commitment to these rules, were essential for the development of markets. This perspective highlights the importance of political institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic growth. The historical paths and patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience offer valuable insights into the conditions that foster economic development. This institutional framework was not limited to Europe but also extended to colonial administrations. For instance, in colonial contexts where European settlers were not present, indigenous commodity production was often encouraged by colonial governments seeking fiscal security. This dynamic influenced the institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth is further supported by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the institutional changes in England were significant for economic development. However, Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the institutionalist approach has limitations, particularly in explaining why institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth, suggesting that while secure property rights and credible commitments are crucial, the broader institutional context and political factors also play a significant role.

The colonial experience in Africa underscores the enduring impact of external governance on local institutions, illustrating the intricate relationship between institutional frameworks and economic trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the nature of colonial rule significantly affected the institutional landscape in Africa. A key mechanism through which this influence was exerted was the codification of customary law, which often reinforced existing gender inequalities. Women were systematically marginalized from access to land and other productive resources, a pattern that was exacerbated under colonial rule. This codification process, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), further entrenched patriarchal structures, limiting women's economic contributions and undermining potential economic growth. The implications of these institutional changes were profound, as they not only affected economic outcomes but also shaped social dynamics in ways that had lasting effects on development trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the idea that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He posits that this growth requires institutional progress to complement technological progress. This institutional progress is vital for reducing access costs and mitigating coordination failures. Such failures can arise from the lack of mechanisms to harness and disseminate new knowledge efficiently. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that technological progress demands institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from technological changes. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts. The need for institutional progress to complement technological progress is essential to sustain economic improvement. This is because the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited without such institutional progress. This economic reasonableness helps in rectifying coordination failures and suggests policies to complement technological progress. The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, is not just about technological advancements but also about the institutional frameworks that support and sustain these advancements.

The slave trades in Africa had long-term adverse effects on economic development, challenging the notion that Africa's underdevelopment is solely attributable to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound impact on Africa's economic trajectory. One specific mechanism through which the slave trades affected economic development was the erosion of trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust hinders economic development by impeding cooperation and reducing the efficacy of institutions. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as the external channels, suggest that the legacy of the slave trade is deeply ingrained in social norms. This internal mistrust is particularly detrimental because it undermines the very foundations of economic interaction and institutional functioning. The absence of these institutions, as Vansina notes, impeded the ability of local societies to facilitate foreign trade effectively. The long-term effects of the slave trade on Africa's economic development are thus multifaceted, encompassing both the erosion of trust and the disruption of institutional frameworks.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, a shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of inequality in New World societies, for instance, is linked to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that economic institutions are primarily designed for efficiency. Instead, it highlights how power dynamics and distributional struggles can lead to the creation of institutions that serve the interests of dominant groups (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that favor elites, even when they may not be the most efficient for overall economic growth. This approach underscores the importance of considering the role of power and distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and power within societies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with high mortality rates were more likely to develop extractive institutions, which focused on transferring resources to the metropole rather than fostering local economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that favor elites, even when they may not be the most efficient for overall economic growth. Ogilvie explains that institutional frameworks are deeply intertwined with the social and political structures of societies, which means that understanding economic growth requires a nuanced view of how these institutions evolve and function (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This approach underscores the importance of considering the role of power and distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

The evolution of institutions is closely tied to the historical context and the specific challenges faced by societies. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were critical in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutions are not static but adapt in response to economic and political actions, which can either facilitate or hinder long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between political power and economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is supported by Hopkins, who shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The presence of poor institutions is crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This highlights the need for institutions that can adapt to changing economic conditions and provide the necessary incentives for growth.

Institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth because they determine the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. who demonstrate that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth are a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This indicates that the historical and political context in which institutions evolve is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Austin on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the measurement of property rights and contract enforcement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15).
