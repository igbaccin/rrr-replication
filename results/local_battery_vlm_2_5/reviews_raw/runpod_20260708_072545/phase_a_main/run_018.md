The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory. At stake is the degree to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, drive economic development. One position argues that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, while another position suggests that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that their impact is often overstated. The disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutions are endogenous to economic growth, or whether they are exogenous variables that can be manipulated to achieve desired economic outcomes. Institutions are often defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal constraints (laws, constitutions) and informal constraints (norms, conventions). The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or whether they are merely a byproduct of other factors such as geography or culture. Institutions can be endogenous to economic growth, meaning that they are shaped by economic factors and in turn shape economic outcomes. Institutions can also be exogenous, shaped by factors outside the economic sphere and manipulated to achieve desired economic outcomes. This debate has important implications for policy, as it suggests that institutions can be designed to promote economic growth, or that other factors such as geography or culture may be more important. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is closely tied to the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional transformation was marked by critical developments such as the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the introduction of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. These changes reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, highlighting the political underpinnings of these institutional changes. This political context facilitated the development of economic institutions that enhanced market exchanges, further reducing transaction costs and fostering a growing economy (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Institutional changes have implications that go beyond economic growth. North observes that as economies grow, the transaction costs per exchange decrease, but the overall volume of exchanges increases, making the transactions sector a larger proportion of GNP. This pattern reflects the increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The development of institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is thus crucial for sustained economic growth, as it supports the complex web of market interactions that drive modern economies.

These institutional changes, driven by colonialism, had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, differed from settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted and had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) contend that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over economic development. This dynamic highlights how the initial institutional frameworks established during colonialism could lock former colonies into paths of underdevelopment. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, underscores their profound impact on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism not only affected the initial economic conditions but also influenced the long-term economic prospects of former colonies by shaping their industrialization paths.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a significant adverse effect on economic development. Their analysis reveals that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today. They show that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not explained by the effect of the slave trade on the trustworthiness of others. Instead, it operates through internal norms of mistrust. Nunn (2008, p.2) also highlights that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, far exceeds the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This temporal dimension underscores the profound and enduring impact of the slave trades on Africa's economic trajectory. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions are indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian, suggesting that while the slave trades had a significant impact, institutions also play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions, as emphasized by Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), is essential for understanding sustained economic growth. Kuznets highlights that technological progress is not only about advancements in production but also requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments. This is because modern technology is incompatible with traditional modes of life and family structures, necessitating profound changes in societal norms and institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He emphasizes that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress is essential to complement technological advancements and ensure their sustainability. Without institutional adjustments, economic improvements risk being eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. Kuznets (1973, p.6) underscores the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully managing such conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements may be steady, the institutional changes required to support them can be more complex and subject to reversals. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is therefore dynamic and multifaceted, requiring careful management to sustain long-term economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by highlighting periods of growth tied to foreign trade and export markets (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The economic performance of African economies during the early twentieth century was significantly influenced by their integration into global trade networks. Export production varied widely across countries due to factors such as local resource endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability in export production patterns underscores the dynamic nature of African economies during this period, which responded to global economic forces. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies reveals that the economic development in these regions was determined by historical and geographical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while foreign trade and export markets drove growth, the long-term welfare development in Africa was shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including the distribution of income and the specific conditions of each colony. This interplay highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa during the colonial era, where both external influences and internal conditions played crucial roles.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with van Zanden, build on this by examining the early modern period, offering a deeper understanding of the economic divergence between Europe and Asia before the Industrial Revolution. These authors argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia had begun well before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). One specific mechanism by which this divergence occurred was the role of the service sector in economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta point out that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance as much as by production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This indicates that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. This uneven distribution is further supported by van Zanden's analysis, which shows that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming the most expensive country after 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Van Zanden also notes that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This qualification suggests that while economic growth was occurring, it did not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all.

Colonial governments' interests in indigenous commodity production significantly shaped institutional evolution, which is adjacent to the topic of institutions causing long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For instance, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production to secure fiscal stability and maintain political control, as Austin (2008, p.2) observes, "where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments" (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who note that the institutional frameworks in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The downstream implications of these institutional frameworks were profound, as they often led to the entrenchment of unequal power structures and limited economic opportunities for the broader population. This variability in institutional responses underscores the complex interplay between colonial interests and local economic dynamics, which ultimately influenced long-run economic growth in diverse ways.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, which prioritized secure property rights and efficient enforcement, was crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions provided a stable environment for economic transactions, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.9).

The specific outcomes of these institutional arrangements varied significantly across different contexts. Austin argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability highlights the dynamic nature of institutional responses to local conditions, which ultimately influenced long-run economic growth in diverse ways. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of power dynamics and political interests in shaping institutions.
