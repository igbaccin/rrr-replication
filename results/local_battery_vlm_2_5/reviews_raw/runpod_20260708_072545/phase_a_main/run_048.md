The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high because understanding this role can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. There are two main positions in the debate: one emphasises the causal role of institutions, while the other emphasises rival causes that do not operate through institutions. Some scholars argue that institutions, such as property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary incentives for investment and innovation. Others propose that factors such as geography, culture, or colonial policies play a more significant role in determining economic outcomes. These positions conflict over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether other factors take precedence. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the key to unlocking long-run economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography and culture, are more determinant. This question is central to understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) identify the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England as pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by profiling transaction costs in a growing economy, emphasizing that the emergence of political institutions specifying and enforcing efficient property rights should be evident in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. Consequently, costs per transaction decrease, while the size of the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP. The institutional changes in England, particularly the commitment to secure property rights, fostered a stable environment for economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) identify a critical political factor in this process as the regime's commitment to these rules, emphasizing the importance of credible commitment in sustaining economic development.

The influence of these institutions extended far beyond Europe, shaping the economic trajectories of colonies around the world. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that European colonists implemented diverse institutional frameworks, ranging from inclusive to extractive, based on factors such as mortality rates and settler motivations. Where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where mortality rates were lower, Europeans settled and developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these institutional differences persisted and significantly influenced current income per capita. Extractive institutions, which maximized rents for European colonists, had lasting negative effects on economic development. This institutional legacy is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where those with extractive institutions lagged behind those with more inclusive ones. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex, as different measures of institutions yield similar results. This complexity suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effects are mediated by other factors, such as the efficiency of the judiciary and the rule of law.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. To explore this, they reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This historical understanding is crucial for identifying the institutional legacies that continue to shape economic outcomes in Africa today.

The slave trade had a direct adverse effect on Africa's economic development, a perspective shared by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2), offering an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment solely to institutions or more recent events. This adverse impact resulted from both the immediate disruption caused by the trade and the enduring cultural and social effects it left behind. This mistrust, in turn, impedes the development of social capital, a critical factor in economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, and the approximately 75 years of colonial rule. This prolonged period of exploitation and disruption would have had profound and lasting effects on Africa's economic and social institutions, creating a legacy of underdevelopment that is not easily overcome. The mechanisms through which the slave trade affected economic development are complex and multifaceted, involving both the immediate economic costs and the long-term social and cultural impacts. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates this complexity by describing how the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans, suggesting that the presence of strong institutions could mitigate some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. This indicates that while the slave trade had a significant negative impact on Africa's economic development, the presence of strong institutions could have helped to mitigate some of these effects.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that technological progress, particularly the epochal innovations that constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, requires institutional progress to complement it. This is because, without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the diffusion of knowledge and technological advancements, thereby fostering economic growth. He notes that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights the importance of institutions in managing the social and political impacts of technological change, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable. The role of institutions in facilitating the diffusion of knowledge and technological advancements is further emphasized by the historical evidence of the "epochal innovations" that have driven long-term economic growth. The institutional framework that supports the growth of useful knowledge is not static; it evolves in response to technological progress and the changing needs of society. This evolution is essential for sustaining economic growth over the long run.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) offer nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena in pre-industrial Europe by examining specific institutional arrangements. Their shared focus on institutional evolution and its impact on economic outcomes highlights the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This suggests that the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including fiscal security and indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the role of power dynamics and conflict in shaping institutional outcomes, which can have significant implications for economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing the need for a careful and nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and impact economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of power and resources within societies. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, rather than fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North argues that the emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by power dynamics and conflicts, which had significant implications for economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Austin shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to changing economic and political conditions. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the role of power dynamics and conflict in shaping institutional outcomes.

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous factors versus endogenous processes. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in facilitating economic growth through secure property rights and falling transaction costs (North 1989, p.9) settles this disagreement by demonstrating the endogenous processes through which institutions can shape economic growth.
