Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, yet the precise mechanisms and the extent of their influence remain subjects of intense debate. The central question is how institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern behavior, interact with economic outcomes over extended periods. This debate pits those who emphasize the causal role of institutions against those who argue that other factors, such as culture or geography, are more determinative. One stream of scholarship posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while another stream contends that cultural factors, shaped by historical experiences such as the slave trade, are more fundamental. A third stream suggests that the efficiency of institutions is less important than their role in managing distributional conflicts. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the roots of economic development and for designing policies aimed at fostering growth. A deeper understanding of the role of institutions in economic growth requires a nuanced examination of these competing perspectives. The importance of institutions in economic growth is highlighted by their ability to shape property rights and enforce contracts, which are crucial for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. They show that the rise of secure property rights, facilitated by the development of political institutions, significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered economic growth. This institutional evolution was both an economic and a political phenomenon, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate through their analysis of seventeenth-century England. They emphasize that the success of these institutions hinged on the regime's commitment to enforce and uphold the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework, by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement, created a stable environment conducive to long-term investment and economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is also contingent on broader economic and political contexts. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that as economic specialization and division of labor increase, the volume of market exchanges grows, making the efficiency of property rights and their enforcement even more critical for sustaining economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This highlights the interconnected nature of institutional development and economic growth, where institutions both facilitate and drive economic progress.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. Colonial institutions were shaped by European mortality rates, which in turn determined the type of institutions established. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, characterized by poor property rights and weak rule of law, hindered long-run economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound effects on the economic development of former colonies. In regions where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, economic growth was more robust. This pattern underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, as these institutions shaped the economic trajectory of former colonies long after the colonial period ended. This persistence highlights the challenge of institutional change in former colonies, where the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These authors contend that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over the distribution of resources and power. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions emerged primarily to enhance economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to the New World, showing how the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in colonial societies led to institutions that advantaged elites. This dynamic is evident in the policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality, which in turn influenced the long-run economic trajectory of these societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) qualifies this by noting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be viewed as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians. This implies that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific mechanisms by which they shape institutions can vary widely, depending on the historical and social context. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa generated a pervasive culture of mistrust, which has had enduring effects on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). In particular, the slave trade led to a reduction in intergroup trust, which is a key determinant of economic development. This mistrust, in turn, has had a significant impact on the development of institutions in Africa, as it has made it difficult for individuals to cooperate and form stable, effective institutions. This mistrust has also made it difficult for individuals to engage in economic activities that require trust, such as trade and investment. This has led to a vicious cycle, where mistrust prevents the development of effective institutions, which in turn prevents economic growth, which further reinforces mistrust. The slave trade has also had a direct impact on economic development, as it led to a significant loss of human capital, which is a key determinant of economic growth. The slave trade also led to a significant loss of cultural and social capital, which is a key determinant of economic development. This loss of cultural and social capital has made it difficult for individuals to cooperate and form stable, effective institutions, which has further hindered economic development. For example, the Kongo kingdom was able to support trade with the Europeans because it had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade led to a significant loss of human capital, which made it difficult for individuals to cooperate and form stable, effective institutions. This has further hindered economic development. This suggests that the slave trade has had a significant impact on economic development, both through its direct effects on human capital and through its indirect effects on the development of institutions.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight that technological innovation is a key determinant of long-term economic growth, with Mokyr emphasizing the role of useful knowledge and Kuznets focusing on the institutional adjustments required for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets elaborates on how technological progress necessitates adjustments in institutional and ideological frameworks to facilitate economic growth. He points out that major technological breakthroughs, or "epochal innovations, " have historically driven sustained economic growth over long periods. This progress requires societal changes that can be disruptive, such as shifts from rural to urban lifestyles and alterations in family structures. These disruptions can create conflicts that, if not resolved peacefully, can impede economic growth. Kuznets underscores the crucial role of the modern national state in managing these conflicts and ensuring that economic growth remains sustainable. However, Mokyr notes that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness, the institutional and ideological adjustments that support technological progress, is not as straightforward. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, the institutional framework that supports and sustains these advancements is equally important. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is critical for long-term economic growth.

(2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the types of institutions established by colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. In particular, colonies with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment experienced higher levels of economic development compared to those with extractive institutions designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. Institutions that reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights facilitate market exchanges and support long-term economic development. This is evident in the historical context of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and supported economic growth. The opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, highlighting the importance of institutions in economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their effectiveness. The commitment to secure property rights and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange were critical factors in England's economic development.
