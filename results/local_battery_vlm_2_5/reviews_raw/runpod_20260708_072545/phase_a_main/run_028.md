The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. Institutions, encompassing rules and norms governing economic interactions, can either foster or hinder growth. The live disagreement centers around the efficacy of institutions in driving economic development. There are those who argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Some scholars suggest that institutions merely mirror deeper underlying factors, such as cultural beliefs or technological advancements. Institutions' role is also debated in relation to other factors, such as population density and geographic conditions. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. Understanding how institutions influence long-run economic growth can guide the design of effective policies and institutional reforms. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for development strategies in both developed and developing countries. The question of what institutions matter most, and how they interact with other factors, is a key area of inquiry that can shed light on the complex dynamics of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They demonstrate that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. This financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This shift in political institutions to specify and enforce property rights led to the development of economic institutions that facilitated market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this dynamic, noting that as the economy grew, the emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs. The transactions sector would grow as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) qualify this pattern by highlighting that the development of capital markets in England provided a striking indication of this institutional evolution. They argue that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary. The success of these institutions in England, as evidenced by capital markets, underscores the critical role of political factors in underpinning economic growth and the development of markets.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. They argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly across colonies, shaped by factors such as European mortality rates. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, they tended to establish extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, fostering long-term economic growth. These institutions often involved minimal investment in local infrastructure and institutions that enforced the rule of law. This pattern is evident in colonies where Europeans settled in large numbers and created institutions that encouraged investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) underscore that these institutional differences persist and continue to influence economic outcomes today. In particular, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is a critical factor in understanding the economic disparities observed among former colonies.

Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions and thus in determining long-run economic growth, particularly in pre-industrial societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both emphasise that institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but are deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts among different social groups. Ogilvie highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to the persistence of seemingly inefficient institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, which serve to protect the interests of elites rather than maximise overall economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to promote growth, illustrating that they often reflect the power struggles and distributional conflicts within society. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this idea by showing how societies with greater initial inequality developed institutions that further entrenched the power of elites, thereby restricting competition and economic opportunities for the broader population. This mechanism of institutional entrenchment of elite interests is particularly evident in New World societies, where colonial institutions were designed to maximise the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth. The downstream implication is that the legacy of these institutions persists, continuing to influence economic outcomes today. Ogilvie cautions that the role of institutions in economic growth is not straightforward, and that their impact can vary depending on the specific historical and cultural context.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that the impact of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. This institutional support for education was tied to broader economic developments, particularly the growth of export production, which varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The long-term economic benefits of British colonial education policies were not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa, and the legacy of these institutions continues to influence economic outcomes today.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) underscore that the economic contributions and institutional reforms in Africa have been significantly influenced by various groups, notably women, and the legacy of colonial rule on governance, even if the evidence does not always directly address the fundamental causal mechanisms at play. The new economic history of Africa, which Hopkins (2009, p.18) advocates, brings attention to the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, but rather by the presence of progressive institutions. The de-privileging of women in access to land for cash crops was a key mechanism through which colonial institutions limited economic opportunities. The downstream implication of these findings is that understanding the historical contributions of different groups and the impact of colonial institutions is crucial for designing effective economic policies in Africa today.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade has shaped African development by inducing a pervasive culture of mistrust, which remains a significant barrier to economic growth. The slave trade's role involves the transfer of people and the transfer of norms and cultural practices that have persisted over generations. These norms, particularly the mistrust between ethnic groups, have been deeply ingrained in African societies. The mistrust generated by the slave trade is a fundamental factor in Africa's underdevelopment. The slave trade's legacy has created a deep-seated mistrust within African societies, which has hindered economic development by affecting intra-group relations and the ability to cooperate. This mistrust has been a significant barrier to economic growth, as it affects the willingness of individuals to engage in economic activities that require trust, such as trade and investment. Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, suggesting a pathway to long-run economic growth that emphasizes technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, necessitating significant institutional and ideological changes to integrate technological progress into economic growth. This argument is supported by Mokyr, who highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr further posits that institutional progress is essential to complement technological advancements, as without it, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is critical for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring long-term economic sustainability. Kuznets notes that the resolution of such conflicts must occur without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This underscores the importance of a strong national state in managing these transitions, as it plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, is instrumental in facilitating the institutional adjustments necessary for modern economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complex historical paths of African economic development, which were shaped by colonial interventions and local economic and political actions. These scholars challenge the conventional view that African poverty has been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This suggests that local economic and political actions could influence colonial institutional prescriptions, which in turn shaped African economic development. This mechanism is qualified by the role of fiscal security in colonial administrations, which encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further illustrate that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. They reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to explore the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This approach underscores the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. This nuanced understanding of African economic history, which considers local actions and colonial interventions, is crucial for comprehending the complex paths of African economic development and the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) share a focus on measuring and understanding economic performance through various metrics such as the skill premium and collaborative research efforts. The skill premium, for example, is a critical metric for assessing long-term economic performance. van Zanden (2009, p.19; 2009, p.3) shows that the skill premium is determined by several factors, including the costs of training, institutional barriers to entry, interest rates, and the duration of skilled work. These factors collectively influence the supply and demand dynamics of skilled labor, ultimately shaping economic performance. The skill premium's role in economic development is further complicated by the presence of institutions like guilds, which, while facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, may also restrict entry into trades and form local cartels. This dual nature of institutions underscores the complexity of their impact on economic growth. The collaborative research efforts highlighted by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) further emphasize the importance of accurate measurement and the need for ongoing scholarly engagement to refine our understanding of economic performance. This collaborative approach not only improves the precision of economic measurements but also encourages continuous scholarly debate, which is essential for advancing knowledge in this field.

Broadberry and Gupta's work, shared by van Zanden (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1), highlights that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta argue that this growth was uneven, benefiting skilled labourers more than unskilled ones. This suggests that early modern economic growth varied significantly across different regions and sectors. Van Zanden's analysis supports this perspective by demonstrating that economic development did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living across Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This uneven growth pattern is further complicated by van Zanden's observation that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, particularly in regions like England after 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This price increase indicates that economic growth did not necessarily lead to widespread prosperity but rather to specific economic advantages for certain segments of the population. This pattern of uneven growth and its implications for different social classes underscores the complexity of early modern economic development and its long-term effects on societal structures.

Pamuk (2007, p.23; 2007, p.16) argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence is attributed to the varying institutional responses to the economic opportunities that emerged post-plague. The northwest, particularly regions like Holland, exhibited a high degree of institutional flexibility. This flexibility allowed for the rapid development of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies, fostering economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Regions like Tuscany, which strengthened existing monopolies, experienced stagnation and a decline in urban ranks (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This pattern of institutional adaptability versus rigidity had profound implications for economic development. Urbanisation rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, further highlight these divergent trajectories. Kuznets (Pamuk 2007, p.16) emphasised that economic growth in the early modern and modern eras is characterised by structural change, particularly the shift from agrarian to industrial economies. This structural change was more pronounced in regions with flexible institutions, which facilitated the transition to urbanised and commercialised economies. The institutional flexibility that drove economic growth in the northwest also led to significant social and economic disparities. The wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 underscores the long-term effects of institutional responses to the Black Death, setting the stage for centuries of economic divergence.

The effects of competition, conflict, and environmental factors on socio-economic processes are significant, even when institutions directly influencing long-run economic growth are not the focus. Peters (2004, p.19) and Stern (2004, p.1) jointly argue that these factors shape economic outcomes in profound ways, often through mechanisms that are not directly institutional. Peters (2004, p.19) highlights how competition and conflict over land in sub-Saharan Africa have led to processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions. This dynamic is evident in the shift from state institutions to private commercial transactions, which Peters (2004, p.19) describes as a significant departure from traditional systems (Peters 2004, p.19). This shift underscores how economic activities can evolve in response to environmental and competitive pressures, even in the absence of direct institutional intervention. Stern (2004, p.1) complements this by critiquing the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis, which posits that economic growth will eventually lead to environmental improvement. This critique implies that the environmental impacts of economic growth are more complex than simple models suggest, and that institutional factors may not be the sole determinants of these impacts. As a result, understanding the long-term effects of economic growth requires a nuanced approach that considers both institutional and non-institutional factors.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the economic environment and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly impacted the economic development of colonies, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding is supported by North, who argues that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure transactions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century exemplifies how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin adds that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations often responded to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie emphasizes that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights collectively underscore that institutions matter profoundly for long-run economic growth, as they determine the economic environment and the incentives for investment.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex than simple models suggest (Austin 2008, p.2). Specifically, Austin argues that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic view of institutions is further supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in lowering transaction costs and fostering economic growth settles the disagreement by showing that institutional changes can have a direct and measurable impact on economic development (North 1989, p.9). This evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they determine the economic environment and the incentives for investment.
