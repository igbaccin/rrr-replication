The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies prosper while others stagnate, and whether the roots of these disparities lie in the political and economic frameworks that govern societies. One live disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus geographic or cultural factors. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the role of property rights, secure contracts, and political stability. Others contend that geographic factors, such as climate and natural resources, or cultural beliefs and practices, are more influential. The debate hinges on whether institutions can be seen as endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they are shaped by historical contingencies or by deeper structural factors.

The institutions-first perspective is evident in the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They posit that a credible commitment to property rights and the rule of law enabled England to achieve sustained economic growth, while other European countries lagged behind due to institutional deficiencies. This view is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The underlying premise is that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions, and these strategies had long-lasting effects on economic development. The institutions-first perspective posits that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. In the context of the development experience of the former colonies, this implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694 led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that the success of these institutions was underpinned by political factors, including the regime's commitment to enforce these rules. This political stability was essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights. North (1989, p.6) highlights that as political institutions specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, transaction costs per exchange fell, and the volume of market exchanges increased. This dynamic facilitated economic growth by enabling greater specialization and division of labor. This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, the specifics of bureaucratic structure mattered less than the overall commitment to secure property rights and political stability. The evolution of these institutions in England demonstrates how secure property rights and political stability can drive long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped the long-run economic growth of former colonies by introducing extractive institutions in high-mortality regions, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Extractive states, such as those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence was driven by European mortality rates, with high-mortality regions more likely to develop extractive institutions. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies highlights how initial institutional frameworks can lock in economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term gains over long-term development. This dynamic underscores the importance of initial institutional choices in determining economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al., further illustrates how institutional changes introduced by colonialism have shaped long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the development of these colonies. These findings emphasize that the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to influence economic growth in former colonies today.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions emerge solely to maximize economic efficiency. Ogilvie contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped more by the struggles over resource distribution than by a drive for efficiency. This distributional conflict approach reveals how economic institutions can be deeply rooted in power struggles and social dynamics. Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies supports this view. They show that institutions in societies with greater inequality, like those in Latin America, were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that institutions designed to maintain elite power can hinder economic growth by limiting competition and innovation. Ogilvie cautions that this perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional development. Institutions are shaped by the underlying social and political dynamics of a society. This nuanced understanding of institutions highlights the importance of considering both distributional conflicts and efficiency in analyzing long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and others (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) contend that the impact of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa is distinct from the broader discussion on institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This development was driven by a complex interplay of factors, including the demand for missionary education. This demand was shaped by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families at both macro and micro levels. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This data demonstrates that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding suggests that the economic impact of colonial institutions was not uniformly negative, as previously characterized. Austin (2008, p.2) cautions that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This caution underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions influenced long-run economic growth in Africa, particularly in relation to education and economic performance.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had a profound adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. The slave trade's impact on economic development is explained through the development of a culture of mistrust. This mistrust, which persists to this day, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The internal channel, or the mistrustful norms internalized by individuals, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the slave trades in Africa lasted for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, whereas colonial rule lasted only about 75 years. This temporal disparity highlights the potential long-term impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are further evidenced by the lack of sustained trade in regions with small, undifferentiated societies. Regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, were able to support trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the slave trade's impact on economic development was not uniform across Africa, and that precolonial institutions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This technological progress requires complementary institutional advancements to ensure sustainability. Without such institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements may be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting long-term economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional framework plays a pivotal role in harnessing these advancements for sustained economic growth. Kuznets also points out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is essential for modern economic growth, underscoring the importance of a stable political framework (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This stability is crucial for the effective implementation of technological innovations and the realization of their economic benefits. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is vital for long-term economic development.

The institutional framework plays a pivotal role in economic growth by shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development, highlighting the critical role of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes. North (1989, p.6) similarly argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for economic development. The historical evidence from the Bank of England's establishment in 1694 and the subsequent financial revolution in England further underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The development of new financial instruments and the increased security of property rights led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is a critical factor in economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its long-term effects on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is crucial for understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long run. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups. This highlights the complex interplay between political power and institutional design, where institutions are often shaped by the interests of those in power. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further complicates this picture by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by the distribution of power and the resolution of conflicts within society.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in long-run development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth, arguing that the degree to which the regime is committed to or bound by rules governing economic exchange is critical for economic development. This evidence highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes and underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with economic opportunities to drive growth.
