The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. The disagreement centres on whether institutions directly cause economic growth or if they operate through other factors such as geographic endowments or cultural traits. One position argues that institutions fundamentally shape economic outcomes by determining the security of property rights and the enforcement of contracts, thereby fostering investment and innovation. Another position contends that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that geographic factors, such as climate and disease environment, or cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, play a more significant role in determining economic growth. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and for designing policies aimed at promoting economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

These institutional frameworks, which reduced transaction costs and ensured secure property rights, were instrumental in fostering long-run economic growth in England, as demonstrated by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The Bank of England's creation in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The political model outlined by North (1989, p.9) highlights that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change was part of a broader evolution of political institutions governing public choice, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue. Their success is evident in the capital markets, where the institutional changes in England fostered a more secure environment for economic activity. The extent of these institutional changes' revolutionary nature is debated. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that institutions per se were not the sole determinant of economic growth. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impact, where the effectiveness of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights is contingent on broader political and economic contexts. North (1989, p.6) discussion on transaction costs in a growing economy further supports this, emphasizing that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling costs per transaction. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how institutional changes in seventeenth-century England facilitated long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The nature of these institutions was primarily determined by the mortality rates Europeans faced in these colonies. In regions where Europeans experienced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development. This approach contrasts with colonies where Europeans settled in large numbers and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutions has had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita in the long run. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate this dynamic by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This finding is supported by the observation that European colonists designed institutions to maximize their own rents, not to foster long-run growth in the colonies. The implications of this institutional legacy are far-reaching, as it suggests that the economic disparities observed today are deeply rooted in the colonial past. These institutions, shaped by the initial conditions and strategic interests of European powers, continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

Mokyr argues that the key driver of economic growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge, a perspective that Kuznets supports (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is rooted in the idea that technological advancements and the dissemination of knowledge are the primary engines of economic progress. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting that technological progress is crucial for sustained economic improvement. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is essential for modern economic growth. Kuznets illustrates this point by noting that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, are termed epochal innovations. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, driving economic growth. Kuznets also recognizes that conflicts may arise between traditional societal structures and new technological paradigms. These conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to ensure that the political fabric of society remains strong. Kuznets notes that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts. The role of the state in facilitating this resolution is highlighted, as it ensures that the benefits of technological progress are sustained over the long term.

This perspective is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), who argue that the slave trade in Africa had profound and lasting effects on economic development, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This culture of mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative ventures and economic growth. The slave trade disrupted traditional social structures and economic institutions, leading to a legacy of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress. Nunn notes that the slave trades in Africa lasted for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, significantly outlasting the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to 1960. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) share a posture that historical and educational developments are critical to understanding economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa. Both authors identify specific historical factors that shaped African economies, such as the introduction of formal education systems. This formal education was not universally negative; instead, it was shaped by complex cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The British colonial administration's impact on education was particularly significant because it introduced new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The economic development of these regions was not uniform. Factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played crucial roles in determining the timing and scale of economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in economic growth patterns across different African countries underscores the importance of local conditions and institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The variability in export production and its connection to foreign trade highlight the intricate relationship between economic development and external factors. This relationship is further complicated by the role of political institutions, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. The interplay between local conditions, transport costs, and political institutions suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is multifaceted and context-dependent.

This nuanced view is further supported by Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who share the view that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis has oversimplified the historical and economic contexts of African poverty. The thesis, which posits that Africa's current poverty is a result of historical disadvantages, neglects the varied paths of economic development and institutional evolution across different regions and periods. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that in the absence of European settlers, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production, suggesting that local economic and political actions could influence institutional changes. This dynamic highlights the agency of local actors in shaping economic outcomes, even within the constraints of colonial rule. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies to address this gap, demonstrating that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon. Their findings suggest that structural impediments may not be as deterministic as previously thought. This nuanced understanding of African economic history underscores the importance of contextual factors and local agency in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Institutional frameworks play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society (North 1989, p.9), encompass formal rules like constitutions and laws, as well as informal constraints such as norms and conventions (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established during the colonial period significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. In particular, colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment experienced higher levels of economic growth compared to those where extractive institutions were imposed (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding highlights the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The development of economic institutions that support market exchanges, in turn, leads to a growing proportion of the economy being dedicated to transactions, which is a key driver of economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the importance of local agency in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The legacy of the slave trade further complicates the picture. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in facilitating economic exchanges and supporting political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate. There is a shared finding that institutions matter for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The extent to which institutions are shaped by local agency versus external factors such as colonial policies remains a point of contention. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's effect on trust works through both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This suggests that both internal and external factors play a role in shaping institutions and, by extension, economic growth. The specific historical evidence of colonial policies and their impact on institutional development, as documented by Acemoglu et al., provides a clear measurement of how institutions shaped long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
