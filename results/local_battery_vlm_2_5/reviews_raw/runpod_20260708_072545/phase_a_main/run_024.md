The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and whether the path to sustained growth is determined by underlying institutions. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive economic growth. One position argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Another position contends that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that other variables, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks, play equally significant roles. A third position suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on historical and geographical contexts, and that institutional arrangements are shaped by a complex interplay of factors. The debate highlights the need to understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and the conditions under which they are most effective. The causal role of institutions in economic growth is a topic of intense debate, with scholars arguing over the primacy of institutions versus other factors such as culture and geography. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. This is because institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives and constraints that guide individual behavior. For example, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 led to a significant decrease in transaction costs, which facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights. The transaction costs' decline reflects the development of economic institutions that support market exchanges, which in turn drives economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. This effect is visible in the historical context of the English financial revolution, where the establishment of new financial instruments and the Bank of England lowered transaction costs and promoted economic development (North 1989, p.9). The same mechanism is evident in the colonization strategies adopted by Europeans in different colonies. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which had a lasting impact on economic growth. This pattern highlights how institutions that support secure property rights and low transaction costs are crucial for long-term economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that institutions are not always designed to maximize long-run growth; instead, they may be shaped by politically powerful groups to maximize their own rents. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes, as institutions can sometimes hinder growth if they are not aligned with long-term economic development goals.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They contend that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a pivotal moment when constitutional changes established constraints on the monarchy, thereby enhancing the credibility of commitments to property rights. This institutional shift fostered a stable environment conducive to long-term economic investment. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) builds on this by emphasizing the importance of institutions in economic history, advocating for their integration into long-term research programs. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this perspective by noting that shared beliefs also form part of the institutional structure of a society. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the efficiency of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, can be better understood through distributional conflicts. This qualification suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader social and political context in which they operate. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes, highlighting that institutions can both facilitate and hinder growth depending on their alignment with long-term economic development goals.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality, offering a rival explanation to how institutions fundamentally cause economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is shared by Simson, who argues that the persistence of inequality can be understood through the lens of institutional policies that favor elites (Simson 2019, p.2). The specific mechanism through which this occurs is the creation of institutions that protect elite interests, such as those that restrict competition and limit access to resources for the broader population. This institutional design, rooted in the political and economic power of elites, perpetuates inequality by ensuring that policies and resources are directed towards maintaining the status quo. This phenomenon is evident in post-independence East Africa, where government policies were influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, and military expenditure, which often prioritized elite interests over broader economic development (Simson 2019, p.11). The persistence of inequality is also a product of the broader social and political context in which these institutions operate. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes, highlighting that institutions can both facilitate and hinder growth depending on their alignment with long-term economic development goals.

The slave trade has significantly shaped African economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) jointly argue. This mistrust, stemming from the historical trauma of the slave trade, has created a pervasive environment of uncertainty and diminished trust, which is a critical factor in economic development. This internal mistrust has been shown to be at least twice as influential as external factors in shaping trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade's legacy has therefore left a lasting imprint on African societies, affecting their economic growth by undermining the very foundations of trust necessary for sustainable development. This prolonged period of exploitation has had enduring effects on African societies, shaping their economic institutions and development trajectories in profound ways.

Akyeampong and Fofack and Frankema together show that colonial rule in Africa had multifaceted impacts on economic development, shaping both women's contributions and educational institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts. This codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land. The colonial rule also altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions. This was part of a broader shift in which the implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The extent to which these impacts were positive or negative varied across different colonial powers and regions. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that despite the challenges posed by colonial rule, there were also opportunities for economic growth and development in some regions.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its implications for African development highlight the complexities of historical paths and institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This is evident in colonial contexts where European settlers were absent; the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were responsive to indigenous economic and political actions. The colonial governments adapted their policies in response to local incentives, a pattern that complicates the notion of a uniform colonial impact on African development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) elaborates on this institutional responsiveness by noting that by the 1990s, the scope of development policy had widened to incorporate governance, allowing institutions and organizations to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This institutional responsiveness suggests that the impact of colonial rule on African development varied based on local conditions and adaptive policies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to create and enforce property rights and provide incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectory of their colonies. They show that colonies with low mortality rates, where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, experienced higher economic growth. Colonies with high mortality rates, where extractive institutions were established, faced persistent economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly emphasizes the critical role of institutions in economic growth, noting that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings highlight that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, directly impacts long-term economic performance.

The adaptive nature of institutions in response to local conditions further underscores their importance. Austin argues that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions evolve based on local incentives and actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is crucial because it allows institutions to better align with the needs of the population, fostering economic growth. Moreover, Ogilvie emphasizes the need to transform institutions from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, suggesting that a deeper understanding of institutional dynamics can provide valuable insights into economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This adaptive responsiveness of institutions shows that their impact on economic growth varies based on how they respond to local conditions and adaptive policies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the empirical evidence of institutional change and its effects on economic outcomes. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. and North (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. For example, Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions, while Austin highlights the adaptive nature of institutions in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional quality and its correlation with economic outcomes, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This empirical approach shows that inclusive institutions, which enforce property rights and provide incentives for investment, are crucial for long-term economic growth.
