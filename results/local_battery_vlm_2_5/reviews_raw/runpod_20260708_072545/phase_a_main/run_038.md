The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic progress. The disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints that affect individual behavior and investment. Others posit that institutions act as intermediaries, channeling the effects of underlying factors such as geography or culture. Still others suggest that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper historical and political forces, making their direct impact on growth more indirect. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they are influenced by other factors, thereby playing a secondary role. This question is crucial for policymakers seeking to understand the foundations of economic development and for theorists aiming to construct comprehensive models of growth. The debate highlights the need to disentangle the complex interplay between institutions and other determinants of economic performance. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves understanding the deeper structural factors that enable or hinder economic development, in addition to identifying the right policies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, a crucial factor for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This security was achieved through the political trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, a move driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast further elaborate on this, noting that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets. They argue that political factors, such as the commitment of the regime to enforce rules, are critical for economic growth and market development. The extent of these institutional changes being revolutionary is debated. Recent research has suggested that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was comparable to England's, implying that institutional differences alone may not fully explain economic growth. North (1989, p.9) adds that as economies grow, the transaction costs per transaction fall, but the overall size of the transactions sector increases as specialization and division of labor multiply the volume of exchanges. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth. The implications of these institutional changes extend beyond the immediate economic benefits, influencing the long-term stability and development of capital markets.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that these institutions were primarily shaped by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were not necessarily efficient but rather reflected the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that in New World societies, institutions often advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The specific focus on how different environments in which Europeans established their colonies led to societies with very different degrees of inequality, and how these differences might have persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on the institutions that evolved (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how institutions are shaped by the underlying power structures and distributional conflicts within societies.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the nature of European settlement in colonies determined the type of institutions established, which in turn shaped economic outcomes. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, aimed at rapid resource extraction, contrasts with the institutions set up in colonies where Europeans settled and enforced the rule of law. This dynamic is evident in the differing economic trajectories of colonies like the United States and the Congo, where the former saw institutions that encouraged investment and development, while the latter experienced extractive states that hindered growth. The impact of these institutions is not merely historical; they have persisted and continue to affect current income per capita, illustrating the enduring legacy of colonial policies. This institutional legacy highlights the importance of understanding how historical policies shape contemporary economic outcomes, as the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced growth continue to be relevant in contemporary economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg, argue that African economic performance during the colonial era was more robust than often portrayed, with many economies achieving per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the narrative of persistent poverty in Africa, highlighting that real wage levels and economic data from the colonial period reveal a more nuanced picture of economic development. Broadberry and Gardner note that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This insight underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which these factors interacted to shape economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, suggesting that specific historical or geographical conditions may have had a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This qualification highlights the complexity of African economic history and the need for further research to fully grasp the long-term picture of economic development in the region.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is a fundamental shift in social norms, not just a byproduct of the slave trade's economic disruptions. The internal norms of mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon argue are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law, have had profound implications for African societies. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often altered their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. Colonial policies, which often aimed at securing resources and maintaining control, inadvertently reinforced these norms of mistrust. The enduring legacy of mistrust is a persistent social force that continues to shape contemporary African economic and social development.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this perspective, arguing that the development of knowledge and technology is essential for sustained economic growth. This dynamic interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the institutional adjustments needed for technological progress can be challenging, as they often involve resolving conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies. These conflicts can weaken the political fabric of society if not managed properly, which underscores the importance of a strong, cohesive national state in facilitating economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, it must be supported by complementary institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, with numerous scholars highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies. Specifically, they show that colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment experienced higher long-term economic growth compared to those where extractive states were set up (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. North similarly emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development, noting that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shared emphasis on the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth is a key point of agreement among scholars.

The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations, suggesting instead that they are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. Austin further elaborates on this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with economic and political factors over time. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between the role of efficiency and distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is the case of colonial institutions, which show that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists, rather than those aimed at long-run growth, were the norm (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This empirical evidence from colonial settings demonstrates the primacy of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes.
