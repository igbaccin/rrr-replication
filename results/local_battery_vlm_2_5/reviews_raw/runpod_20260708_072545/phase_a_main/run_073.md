The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions are the rules and norms that structure social interactions, and they can either foster or hinder economic growth. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are essential for economic growth. Others posit that the efficiency of institutions is less important than the distribution of power and resources within a society. The core disagreement revolves around whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors or if they evolve endogenously in response to economic and political pressures. The distinction between these views has profound implications for understanding the historical development of economies and for designing effective policies to promote growth. Institutions can either facilitate or impede economic development, and understanding this dynamic is crucial for policating long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different colonial institutions based on mortality rates, with high-mortality areas receiving extractive institutions that hindered long-term economic growth. This divergence in institutional design had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies. For instance, areas with extractive institutions often struggled with persistent poverty and underdevelopment, while those with more inclusive institutions experienced greater economic prosperity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is tied to these institutional differences, suggesting that the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development. This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, shaping the economic landscape of former colonies for generations. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of initial conditions and the long-term effects of historical policies on economic development.

However, North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the influence of institutions extends beyond immediate economic impacts, focusing on specific developments like the creation of the Bank of England and their role in reducing transaction costs. The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights over time. This financial revolution was a product of political and fiscal crises, illustrating how institutional changes can foster economic growth by lowering the costs associated with market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is crucial for economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and increases the volume of transactions (North 1989, p.6). This dynamic is evident in the evolution of capital markets, where secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms drive economic development. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic development is not straightforward and can be influenced by a variety of factors, including the historical and political context (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implications of these findings are significant, as they highlight the complex interplay between institutions, property rights, and economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions. This perspective is mirrored in Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who highlight that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites. These institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This pattern of institutional design in response to distributional conflicts is also evident in the New World, where factor endowments and colonial policies created societies with vastly different degrees of inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped institutions that reinforced the power of elites. The persistence of these institutions over time is not solely due to initial inequality. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that shared beliefs and values are part of the institutional structure of a society, and these beliefs can influence the trajectory of economic development. For example, shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not straightforward and can be influenced by a variety of factors, including the historical and political context.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that institutional evolution in Africa is more complex than simplistic causal explanations suggest. Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths. This thesis does not sufficiently account for the political and economic incentives that shaped institutional development in colonial Africa. For instance, when European settlers were not present, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the institutional landscape in Africa evolved in response to local economic and political dynamics. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has drawn attention to the role of institutions in development. This focus on institutions has been crucial in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further illustrate this point by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, showing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The complexity of institutional evolution in Africa highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical and political contexts shape economic outcomes.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficiently harnessing innovations, driving long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both underscore the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to accommodate technological changes. This resolution is necessary to ensure that the political fabric of society is not weakened in the long term. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable. This is because technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behavior, which institutional progress helps to mitigate. The role of institutions in this context is to facilitate the coordination of economic activities, ensuring that technological advancements are not just isolated events but are integrated into the broader economic framework. This integration is essential for sustained economic growth, as it allows for the efficient use of innovations across various sectors. Kuznets (1973, p.1) illustrates this point by noting that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts. This highlights the importance of strong, effective institutions in managing the complexities that arise from technological progress. The interplay between technology and institutions is complex and dynamic, requiring continuous adjustments to maintain economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) illustrate this dynamic by demonstrating how the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to affect trust levels and long-run economic growth. The slave trade is thought to have caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day. This mistrust has a profound effect on economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that this mistrust is important for economic development, and that their contribution is in helping to understand and explain its origins. They find that both channels are important, but that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. This internalization of mistrust is a key mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy continues to hinder African economic development. The Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, located just south of the Zaire river. Because the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, it was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that strong, centralized institutions could have mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. However, Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, indicating that the lack of such institutions in other African regions exacerbated the negative impacts of the slave trade.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they define the rules that govern economic interactions and allocate resources. Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6) show that efficient institutions, those that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges, are critical for economic development. These institutions reduce transaction costs, which in turn fosters investment and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita. North (1989, p.6) similarly argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for economic growth. This alignment underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic outcomes. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. But Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that institutional change can be driven by local factors as well as colonial policies. The historical evidence from the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, supports the idea that strong institutions can mitigate adverse economic impacts. This suggests that the specific historical context and the interaction between local and colonial institutions are crucial in determining long-run economic growth.
