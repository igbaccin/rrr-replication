The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake is the very nature of economic progress and the mechanisms by which societies ascend from poverty to prosperity. The debate centers around the influence of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture. One key position argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while a rival position posits that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and political conditions, and that they are only one of several factors in economic growth. Another position argues that the institutions themselves are the product of deeper historical and political forces. The debate is far from settled, with scholars continuing to grapple with the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus both a theoretical and empirical puzzle, with significant implications for policy and development strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Their research underscores that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they implemented extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for long-run economic growth. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered economic development by maximizing rents for European colonists. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further explain that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their control and minimize the risk of losing political power. The legacy of these colonial institutions persists, influencing current economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutional changes on economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. The Bank of England's establishment in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, significantly lowered the cost of transacting, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This financial revolution reflected an increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, highlighting the political underpinnings of these institutional changes. The emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grew in proportion to GNP. This pattern underscores the importance of institutional developments in fostering economic growth. The impact of these institutions varies across different contexts. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. Ogilvie posits that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure of a society, but this view is repudiated by the authors. The authors emphasize that economic failures can be attributed to a system of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, which has blocked the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. They also argue that the superior performance of European economies derived from distinctive European beliefs in changing the world through human intervention, and European values for diligence, thrift, tenacity, honesty, and tolerance, which were not shared by non-European cultures. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's view on distributional conflicts is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism highlights how institutional structures can perpetuate economic disparities. Ogilvie argues that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a crucial driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) jointly establish. This growth is a primary force that propels economic development forward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting how technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is pivotal. Kuznets (1973, p.6) illustrates that modern economic growth is contingent on the resolution of growth-induced conflicts, which necessitates a strong political fabric. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further underscores the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress. Without such institutional support, economic gains could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This dynamic suggests that while technological advancements are essential, they must be accompanied by robust institutional frameworks to ensure sustained economic growth. The role of institutions in facilitating the growth of useful knowledge and managing the societal changes it brings about is indispensable for long-term economic development.

The literature on economic contributions and colonial impacts in Africa highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The role of institutions in facilitating economic growth is complex. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that while institutions can drive economic development, their impact is contingent on broader social and political contexts. The codification of customary law, for instance, reinforced patriarchal alliances and altered the economic landscape in ways that were not always beneficial for all segments of society (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic growth, highlighting the need for nuanced understandings of how institutions operate within specific historical and cultural contexts.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) both examine the economic performance and poverty in Africa through various lenses such as skill premiums and per capita income levels, but do not directly address the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including institutional frameworks, when assessing long-run economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) suggest that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, which means that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be more nuanced than simply being a barrier to development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon both show that historical factors, such as the slave trade and colonial policies, have significantly shaped African economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These factors have created unique challenges for Africa that are distinct from the institutional frameworks emphasized in broader economic growth literature. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often encouraged indigenous commodity production and adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political dynamics. This adaptation was not uniform across Africa; in some regions, colonial policies fostered economic growth by aligning with local interests, while in others, they exacerbated existing inequalities. Austin demonstrates this by noting that Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis, indicating that colonial policies were responsive to local contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon further illustrate the enduring impact of historical factors by showing that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and significantly affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, continues to influence contemporary economic interactions and institutional effectiveness. The legacy of these historical factors means that African economic development cannot be understood solely through the lens of contemporary institutional frameworks; instead, it requires a nuanced appreciation of the deep historical roots that shape current economic realities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue, deeply rooted in historical and political contexts. Institutions are fundamental to economic development, as they shape the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical trajectory of institutional development in various regions has significantly influenced their economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in different colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional frameworks, in turn, had lasting impacts on economic growth. Inclusive institutions, which enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, foster economic development by encouraging investment and innovation. Extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to a central authority, hinder economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. This dynamic is evident in the divergent economic paths of former European colonies, where the type of institutions established during the colonial period continues to influence contemporary economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The legacy of colonial institutions is particularly pronounced in Africa, where historical factors have shaped contemporary economic realities. The slave trade, for example, fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly affects economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, continues to influence contemporary economic interactions and institutional effectiveness. The legacy of these historical factors means that African economic development cannot be understood solely through the lens of contemporary institutional frameworks; instead, it requires a nuanced appreciation of the deep historical roots that shape current economic realities. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the long-term effects of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The new economic history of Africa shows that institutional reforms can inform policies to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This approach highlights the potential for institutional changes to drive economic development, even in regions with a history of extractive institutions.

Shared beliefs and cultural factors also play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective emphasizes the importance of understanding the underlying social and political dynamics that shape institutional development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution. Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in driving economic growth.
