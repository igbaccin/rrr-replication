The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics and economic history. At stake is the understanding of why some nations prosper while others stagnate, and what policies might foster development. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position highlights the causal impact of institutions on economic outcomes, emphasizing that institutions shape incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. This perspective argues that secure property rights and effective governance are crucial for sustained growth. A rival position proposes that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geography, culture, or other variables might be more decisive. This view suggests that institutions are endogenous to deeper structural factors, and that focusing on institutions alone may overlook more fundamental determinants of economic growth. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are exogenous drivers of growth or endogenous responses to other underlying factors. This tension is evident in the varying explanations for the persistence of economic disparities among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) highlight that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in 17th century England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the emergence of new financial instruments were pivotal in this transformation, leading to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, illustrating the political underpinnings of these economic changes. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured the enforcement and adaptability of these rules, underpinning the regime's commitment to these changes.

In contrast, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutional frameworks in societies with high inequality have historically favored elites, shaping economic policies that restrict competition and perpetuate disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This elite bias is evident in policies that limit access to education and economic opportunities, thereby maintaining a stratified social structure. For instance, in New World societies, the concentration of political power among a few elites led to institutions that protected their interests, often at the expense of broader economic development. This dynamic is further illustrated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who show that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust, which has enduring effects on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, rooted in historical injustices, creates barriers to cooperation and economic exchange, perpetuating the cycle of inequality. The legacy of these institutions is profound, as they not only stifle economic growth but also reinforce social divisions that hinder collective progress.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by European policies, which varied based on mortality rates and the extractive potential of colonies. For instance, European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, in colonies with high mortality rates, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions persist and significantly affect current income per capita. This dynamic highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, as these extractive institutions continue to influence economic outcomes long after the colonial period.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) shared finding that the nature of colonial rule significantly influenced educational development in sub-Saharan Africa. This dynamic highlights the importance of local endowments and political institutions in shaping economic outcomes during the colonial period, as these factors influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the impact of British rule on economic development was not uniform. This nuanced view underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic divergence across colonial Africa, as well as the long-term picture of economic development in the region.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The colonial governments' response to indigenous economic and political action shaped the economic trajectory. Colonial administrators adapted their policies to the incentives created by local conditions, which influenced the production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view aligns with Ogilvie's (2007) argument that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that shared beliefs and cultural values significantly influenced institutional structures, challenging the idea that institutions were solely driven by efficiency. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between cultural values and institutional development, which can provide a more comprehensive explanation for the long-term economic trajectories of different regions. The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with the broader social and political contexts in which they operate.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is a central force behind economic growth. This progress requires not only the generation of new ideas but also the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological innovations often necessitate significant social and political changes, which can create conflicts within societies. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that coordination failures can impede economic improvement if not addressed through institutional progress. This interplay between technological and institutional changes is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth. This implies that while knowledge and technology are essential drivers of economic growth, the effectiveness of institutions in adapting to these changes can significantly influence the sustainability of economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the frameworks that govern economic activities and interactions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies. They emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to the metropole, hindered long-term economic development. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) illustrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This underscores the importance of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as opposed to those that merely facilitate resource extraction. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by their impact on transaction costs and property rights. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by reducing transaction costs and specifying efficient property rights. He shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) also emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This indicates that institutions that adapt to local conditions and incentives can promote economic growth.

The precise mechanism by which institutions influence economic growth involves the interaction between technological and institutional changes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. They show that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in adapting to technological changes can significantly influence the sustainability of economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She shows that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that these beliefs can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This suggests that the cultural and political context in which institutions operate is crucial for their effectiveness in promoting economic growth.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing the frameworks that govern economic activities and interactions. All sources agree that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies, while North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) shows that institutions that reduce transaction costs and specify efficient property rights facilitate long-term capital markets and economic growth. The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions can adapt to technological changes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's impact on transaction costs and property rights settles this disagreement, as it demonstrates the adaptive capacity of institutions in promoting economic growth.
