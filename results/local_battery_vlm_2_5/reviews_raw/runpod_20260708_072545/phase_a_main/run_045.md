The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. This question is at the heart of a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the causal role of institutions in economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. This perspective posits that well-functioning institutions, such as secure property rights and effective governance, foster investment and innovation, thereby promoting long-term economic development. Other scholars, however, contend that the impact of institutions on economic growth is less direct, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographical conditions, may play more significant roles. This debate is crucial for understanding historical economic trajectories and for informing contemporary policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. The central question is whether institutions are the primary cause of long-run economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors.

One stream of literature focuses on the direct causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth and development, highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective governance (North 1989, p.9). North's work underscores the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional development reflects the increased security of property rights, which is critical for a long-term capital market. North (1989, p.6) posits that as political institutions evolve to specify and enforce property rights, the costs per transaction decrease. This reduction in transaction costs is a direct result of the growing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) clarify that the success of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement. The degree to which the sovereign is bound by these rules is a critical political factor in sustaining economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African economies experienced periods of growth during the early twentieth century, driven by foreign trade and export markets. The economic performance of these economies, particularly Anglophone African economies, was tied closely to the expansion and contraction of export production. This pattern of growth was influenced by various factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These institutional factors played a crucial role in shaping the economic trajectory of African economies during this period. The impact of these institutions varied across different regions and countries. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight the absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates, which complicates the assessment of long-term economic development. This gap in data underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. Such an understanding is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development. The variability in institutional effectiveness across different contexts suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. 2001 and 2002 argue that European colonial powers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. These frameworks ranged from inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The latter type of institutions, characterized by their extractive nature, persisted and had lasting effects on economic development. This mechanism is supported by Acemoglu et al. 2002, who emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions has had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies, leading to a reversal in relative incomes among them. This institutional legacy has been a critical factor in shaping the economic performance of these regions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The specific pattern of institutional change introduced by colonialism has led to the persistence of extractive institutions, which in turn has hindered economic growth and development in many former colonies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) share the view that institutional reforms are pivotal for economic development in Africa, particularly in alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the new economic history of Africa has shifted focus to the role of institutions, highlighting that "good" institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This emphasis on institutional quality aligns with the broader perspective that effective governance and strong institutions can drive economic growth. Simson (2019, p.2) elaborates on this by discussing how neopatrimonial tendencies in Africa have led to a governance structure where resources are often used to buy loyalty rather than deliver broad-based public goods (Simson 2019, p.2). This pattern of resource allocation can impede economic development by diverting funds away from productive investments. However, Simson (2019, p.2) also points out that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were influenced by various factors, including military spending and debt service payments, which complicates the straightforward narrative of institutional reform driving economic growth (Simson 2019, p.11). These expenditure patterns reveal that the path to economic development in Africa is influenced by a complex interplay of institutional factors, military priorities, and debt management, all of which must be addressed to achieve sustainable growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels. This mistrust is a result of both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust. The slave trade not only disrupted social structures but also instilled a deep-seated lack of trust among African communities. The legacy of the slave trade extends beyond economic impacts, shaping social dynamics that continue to influence contemporary African societies. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates on this by showing how colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that the institutional changes were not uniform and were influenced by local dynamics. This suggests that the impact of the slave trade on trust is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent factor that continues to shape African societies today. The enduring mistrust, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), highlights the need for policies that address both the external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust, ensuring that historical legacies do not hinder future economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that the accumulation of knowledge and its application in technology is crucial for sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are pivotal, they must be supported by institutional frameworks that facilitate their effective implementation and diffusion. This suggests that the institutional adjustments necessary for technological progress to translate into economic growth may face significant challenges and are not guaranteed to be sustained over the long term.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, are better at explaining the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World. These conflicts are driven by the interests of elites to preserve their advantages, which can be seen in institutions that restrict competition and maintain inequality. The persistence of inequality in the New World, where societies began with an abundance of land and natural resources, suggests that the initial factor endowments did not determine the long-term economic trajectory. Instead, the evolution of institutions that favored elites played a crucial role. For instance, in Latin America, institutions that secured property rights and contract enforcement were often blocked by shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. These beliefs, while not directly tied to efficiency, significantly influenced the development of institutions that either facilitated or hindered economic growth. The persistence of inequality and the evolution of institutions that favor elites can lead to stagnation. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary for a comprehensive analysis of economic development.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and effectiveness of resource allocation, investment, and innovation. Institutions that enforce the rule of law and secure property rights encourage investment and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9). Conversely, extractive institutions that prioritize the interests of elites can hinder growth by limiting investment and innovation. This dynamic is evident in the historical development of European colonies, where different colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). In the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to sustained economic growth. In contrast, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, resulting in persistent economic stagnation. The persistence of inequality and the evolution of institutions that favor elites can lead to stagnation. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of inequality and the evolution of institutions that favor elites can lead to stagnation. The evolution of institutions in response to economic and political pressures is a key factor in long-run economic growth. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, and tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political pressures.
