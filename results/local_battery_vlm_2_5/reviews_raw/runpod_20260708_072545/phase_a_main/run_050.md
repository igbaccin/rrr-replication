The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies achieve sustained prosperity. Institutions, encompassing the rules, norms, and organizations that structure social interactions, are seen by some scholars as the primary drivers of economic growth. This perspective posits that institutions that protect property rights, enforce contracts, and provide a stable framework for economic activity are essential for fostering investment and innovation. A lively debate exists regarding the mechanisms by which institutions affect economic growth. One position highlights the direct impact of institutions on economic performance, emphasizing their role in reducing transaction costs and providing incentives for investment. Another position argues that institutions are shaped by broader historical and cultural factors, and that their impact on economic growth is mediated through these underlying forces. A third position suggests that the role of institutions is contingent on specific historical and geographical contexts, and that their impact on economic growth varies accordingly. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they are merely one of many factors that contribute to economic development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue, with different scholars offering competing explanations for how institutions influence economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, a perspective shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework creates a political environment where rights are traded for revenues, leading to a more secure and efficient economic system. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by showing how different colonization strategies in European colonies resulted in varied institutional setups. For example, colonies like the United States and Australia saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that institutions can also be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional effects, where the same institutional framework can have divergent outcomes depending on the political and economic context. The implications of these institutional differences are profound, as they highlight the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on long-run economic development.

This historical context underscores the importance of understanding how institutions evolved to support economic growth, particularly in the case of seventeenth-century England. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the institutional framework that emerged during this period created a stable environment for economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutional changes in England led to a credible commitment to property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This credible commitment was underpinned by the establishment of a political regime that was bound by the rules governing economic exchange. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight the role of political factors in enforcing these rules, noting that the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules is a critical factor in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment was evident in the functioning of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the institutional changes that fostered economic growth. She argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) shows that the new economic history of Africa reveals that colonial rule was both negotiated and imposed, leading to institutional change driven by African initiatives as well as exogenous factors. This perspective underscores that institutional change in Africa was a complex interplay of local and external forces. The colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial governments in areas without European settlers often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which was in their fiscal interest. This adaptation underscores the dynamic nature of institutional change, where local economic and political actions influenced colonial policies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) add that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty. This suggests that while institutional change was influenced by local actions, the broader economic context and data limitations complicate a full understanding of these dynamics. The interplay between local initiatives and colonial policies shaped the institutional landscape of Africa, with long-run implications for economic development.

This dynamic is also evident in the New World, where colonial institutions were shaped by the initial inequality that advantaged elites, as shown by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15). This inequality was driven by the factor endowments of the colonies; the abundance of land and natural resources led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. As a result, the institutions that emerged were tailored to protect elite interests and restrict competition. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) highlight that these institutions were characterized by policies that reinforced the power of the elite, such as those that limited the rights of the majority and restricted access to education and economic opportunities. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this by noting that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This suggests that while colonial institutions were designed to maintain elite dominance, local perceptions and adaptations could modify the impact of these institutions on economic development.

Similarly, the legacy of the slave trade in Africa has left a deep and enduring impact on the continent's economic development, as Nunn and Wantchekon assert. This impact is primarily through a culture of mistrust that persists long after the initial event. The slave trade disrupted African societies, creating a pervasive mistrust that inhibits economic cooperation and development. This mistrust is an internalized norm that affects individual behavior and social interactions. Nunn and Wantchekon estimate that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are amplified by the internalization of mistrust, which creates a barrier to economic growth. This mistrust is not easily mitigated by external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, as the internal norms of mistrust are deeply ingrained. Nunn argues that the duration of the slave trade, spanning nearly 500 years, had a more profound and lasting impact on African economic development than colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is a more significant factor in understanding Africa's economic stagnation than colonial institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) report, “Moreover, if we compare the point estimates to estimates using the same specification and sample, but not including the interethnic slave exports control (which are −0.104, −0.126 and −0.107, respectively), we find that the point estimates are barely affected by the inclusion of the control.” Nunn (2008, p.2) reports, “For a period of nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, the African continent simultaneously experienced four slave trades.”

This accumulation of knowledge is a critical factor in economic growth, as emphasized by both Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, while Kuznets highlights that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Technological progress is about invention and the institutional frameworks that facilitate the diffusion and application of new knowledge. Kuznets notes that technological changes require adjustments in institutions and ideologies, suggesting that the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr reinforces this idea by pointing out that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay is essential because technological innovations often conflict with existing social structures, requiring resolutions that do not weaken the political fabric of society. Kuznets emphasizes that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological innovation is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional environment must adapt to support and sustain this growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for economic development. These institutions create an environment where property rights are secure and transactions are cost-effective, facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.6) further emphasizes that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is essential for reducing transaction costs and increasing the volume of exchanges, which is critical for economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes often resulted in extractive institutions that maximized rents for European colonists. This highlights the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often responsive to indigenous economic and political action. This indicates that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can vary based on local conditions and responses.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that institutions are about economic efficiency and power dynamics and the distribution of resources. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth. North (1989, p.6) shows that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are critical for economic growth. The remaining disagreement is over whether institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts are more important. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional outcomes across different colonial settings, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19). This variation shows that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of efficiency considerations and power dynamics, with the latter often playing a decisive role in determining institutional outcomes.
