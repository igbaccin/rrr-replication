The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake is the explanation of persistent economic disparities among countries, and the identification of effective policies for economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play a more significant role. The disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Moreover, scholars debate whether the impact of institutions on economic growth is universal or contingent on specific historical and cultural contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths. This suggests that institutions may be more malleable and context-dependent than some theories propose. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play a more significant role, remains a live disagreement. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution reflected an increased security of property rights over time, a dimension vital for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. They argue that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, demonstrating how political factors underpin economic growth and market development. North and Weingast note that the regime or sovereign's commitment to these rules is a critical factor. This commitment to rules is essential for ensuring that the institutions governing economic exchange are effectively enforced and can adapt over time. The development of these institutions in England highlights how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can drive long-run economic growth, a pattern that other historical contexts might not necessarily replicate.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This institutional framework was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The European settlers' differing colonization strategies, shaped by mortality rates, resulted in either settler societies with institutions enforcing the rule of law or extractive states focused on resource transfer. These institutional differences had profound effects on economic development, with settler societies experiencing more robust growth due to the presence of property rights and effective governance. The pattern of institutional development in former colonies highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers can have enduring effects on economic trajectories. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as critiqued by Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), challenges the simplistic view of institutional causation. The thesis does not account for the nuances of institutional evolution and the varied responses of colonial administrations to local economic and political actions. Austin underscores that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior challenges the notion that colonial institutions were uniformly imposed and unchanging. Colonial governance was not a monolithic force but rather a dynamic entity that could be influenced by local conditions and actions. This dynamic interaction between colonial powers and local populations highlights the complexity of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further illustrate the enduring effects of historical legacies on contemporary economic outcomes, showing how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The adaptive nature of colonial institutions and the enduring effects of historical legacies suggest that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is shaped by a complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors.

The African economic development literature, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), reveals the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional impacts. Hopkins (2009, p.18) explains that the quality of public goods provision in Africa is determined by the broader institutional framework. This framework includes the rule of law and progressive institutions, which are crucial for economic development. The new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) describes, emphasizes the role of good institutions in providing incentives for investment. They argue that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This understanding is crucial for evaluating the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This historical context suggests that while institutions play a role in shaping economic outcomes, the specific mechanisms by which they do so are complex and multifaceted.

The historical context of technological progress, as argued by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49), suggests that institutions are just one piece of the puzzle. This perspective highlights the pivotal role of technological advancements in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that the accumulation of knowledge leads to innovations that fundamentally alter production processes and economic structures. Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, illustrating how societies must adapt to harness the full potential of new technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this idea, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for understanding long-term economic growth. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) also points out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is essential for modern economic growth, suggesting that the political fabric of society must remain strong to support technological advancements. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver, the institutional environment must be conducive to absorbing and implementing these advancements effectively.

Distributional conflicts, not institutions, fundamentally shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The distribution of power and wealth within societies drove the formation of institutions like serfdom and guilds. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be understood as a response to elite interests seeking to maintain control over labor. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where the landed elite's desire to secure labor and maintain social order led to the establishment of institutions that restricted mobility and enforced labor obligations. In New World societies, similar patterns emerged, where elites used institutional frameworks to preserve their advantages. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that unequal societies in the New World developed institutions that reinforced existing power structures, such as policies that restricted competition and protected elite interests. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, perpetuated inequality and hindered economic development. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, the evolution of institutions is a complex process influenced by multiple factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlights how institutions can hinder economic development. This is further supported by North (1989, p.9), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing the role of secure property rights and effective enforcement in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs.
