The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes. This debate centers around the tension between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One key position is that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, is crucial for sustained economic growth. Another position emphasizes that institutions are shaped by deeper cultural and historical factors, suggesting that institutional change is influenced by underlying social norms and political dynamics. A third perspective emphasizes the role of extractive institutions, which can impede economic development by diverting resources to a small elite and thus preventing broad-based growth. These competing views highlight the complexity of institutional dynamics and their interplay with economic outcomes. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires examining how different types of institutions influence economic performance over the long term.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) present a compelling argument that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis underscores the role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and facilitating market exchanges, which in turn reduces transaction costs and enhances economic efficiency. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges results in falling costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grows. This dynamic is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also advise that the impact of these institutional changes can be overstated. They point out that while England's institutional changes were significant, France's economic performance before the French Revolution was also strong, suggesting that institutional differences alone do not fully explain economic growth. This qualification highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other economic and social factors to drive long-term growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and extractive potential, which persisted long after colonial rule ended. In high-mortality regions, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, whereas in regions with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering growth and institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts fostering it. This mechanism is evident in the stark differences in economic outcomes between former colonies with settler institutions, such as the United States and Australia, and those with extractive institutions, like the Congo and the Gold Coast. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on growth is not straightforward. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic development trajectories in former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic performance.

Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The economic performance of African economies during colonial times shows that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This is due to the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The growth in export production, tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This export-driven growth pattern indicates that the economic development of African colonies was influenced by external market dynamics and institutional frameworks, in addition to structural impediments. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries. This underscores the complex interplay between local factors and global economic conditions in shaping the economic trajectories of African colonies. The availability of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence, highlights the potential for further research into the long-term economic development of African economies.

Ogilvie posits that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, were the key drivers of pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, each seeking to maximize their share of resources. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be understood as a result of landowners' efforts to maintain control over labor, while craft and merchant guilds served to protect the interests of skilled workers and traders. Austin's work provides a complementary perspective, showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes were driven by the need to manage conflicts and maintain social order, in addition to efficiency concerns. Austin's findings also suggest that the specific historical context, such as the presence of European settlers, could influence the direction of institutional change. This nuance underscores the complexity of institutional evolution, where local power dynamics and global economic conditions intersect to shape long-term economic outcomes.

The slave trade significantly hindered Africa's economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists today. Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development was profound and long-lasting, offering an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment solely to institutions or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). One specific mechanism through which the slave trade affected economic development is the erosion of trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has had lasting effects on economic development, as trust is crucial for various economic activities, including trade and investment. Nunn further elaborates that the slave trade's impact extended well into the colonial era and beyond, not just during the period of active slave trading. This long-term effect is evident in the enduring economic underdevelopment of many African regions, which can be directly linked to the historical slave trades. It is important to note that the impact of the slave trade varied across Africa. Nunn's work shows that regions with different historical experiences of slave trading exhibit varied levels of economic development, suggesting that the specific context and intensity of the slave trade influenced its economic impact.

Technological advancements are pivotal to understanding long-run economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both highlight that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. The process of technological innovation requires the generation of new ideas and their efficient diffusion throughout the economy. Kuznets (1973, p.1) describes the role of epochal innovations, which are major breakthroughs in human knowledge that drive sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These innovations require institutional and ideological adjustments to be effectively utilized, in addition to being technical advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that coordination failures can impede the benefits of technological progress, underscoring the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is essential, its impact on economic growth is contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and support these innovations. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the resolution of conflicts arising from technological changes is crucial for sustained economic growth, indicating that the institutional framework must be robust enough to manage these challenges without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is a key determinant of long-run economic growth, as it ensures that innovations are generated and effectively integrated into the economy.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) agree that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. The specific institutional arrangements that foster growth vary across different historical and geographical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to transfer resources back to the metropole. This variation in institutional design directly influenced the long-run economic outcomes of these colonies, highlighting the importance of institutional quality in economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) both demonstrate that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, is not universally applicable and that other factors, such as indigenous economic and political action, also play a significant role. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.3) both argue that the institutional frameworks that emerged in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) and North (1989, p.9) both show that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North (1989, p.6) both highlight that the emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is crucial for economic growth. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that credible commitment underpins growth. The remaining disagreement is whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the variation in institutional design across European colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), which demonstrates that institutions are shaped by both efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts.
