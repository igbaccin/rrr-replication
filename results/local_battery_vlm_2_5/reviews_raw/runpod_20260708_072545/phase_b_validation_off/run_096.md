The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies prosper while others stagnate, and how the frameworks governing social interactions influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions in providing secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges, while others argue that the efficiency of institutions is less critical than distributional conflicts or the cultural beliefs that underpin them. The interplay between these factors and their relative importance in different historical and geographical contexts remains a contentious issue. The extent to which institutions can be shaped by external factors, such as colonial policies or demographic pressures, further complicates the debate. This interplay is crucial for understanding the long-term trajectories of economic development. The debate over institutional efficiency and its role in economic growth is closely linked to the question of how institutions are shaped by political and social forces.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the effectiveness of these institutional changes. This implies that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not a significant hindrance to economic growth. The critical political factor underlying economic growth is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules. North (1989, p.6) profiles transaction costs in a growing economy, highlighting that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. This institutional framework is crucial for understanding the long-term trajectories of economic development in England.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, characterized by the rule of law and property rights, were established in colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. These institutions fostered economic growth by encouraging investment and protecting property rights. In contrast, extractive institutions were imposed in high-mortality colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was rapid resource extraction for the benefit of the colonial power. These extractive institutions hindered economic development by prioritizing the interests of the colonizers over those of the local population. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic performance of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial in determining the long-run development of these colonies. This dynamic highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, shaping the economic landscape of former colonies for centuries to come.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective highlights the role of power dynamics in institutional development, where institutions were designed to serve the interests of powerful groups rather than to maximize overall economic efficiency. For instance, guilds and serfdom, which are often seen as inefficient, can be understood as mechanisms to protect the interests of existing elites. These institutions were not necessarily adopted because they promoted economic growth but because they helped maintain the status quo and distribute resources in a way that favored the ruling class. This dynamic is evident in the survival of guilds, which were maintained despite their economic inefficiencies because they served the interests of the craftsmen who controlled them. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that institutions were flexible and responsive to the demands of powerful groups. This adaptability suggests that institutions evolved in response to changing power dynamics. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that the persistence of institutions like guilds and serfdom can also be attributed to shared beliefs and cultural values, which reinforced the legitimacy of these institutions. This cultural dimension adds another layer to the understanding of how institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts. The interplay between power dynamics, institutional flexibility, and cultural values underscores the complex nature of institutional development in pre-industrial Europe. This complexity means that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not straightforward but involves a intricate interplay of factors that go beyond simple efficiency considerations.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers, with the implementation of new social and political institutions altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This is because the demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This pattern is distinct from other colonial powers, which often had different institutional frameworks that did not prioritize education in the same way. For instance, the impact of slave trades in Africa, which lasted for nearly 500 years, also played a significant role in shaping subsequent economic development. The centrality of the Kongo kingdom in facilitating foreign trade underscores the importance of centralized governance and well-developed markets in supporting trade with Europeans. This historical context is pivotal in understanding how different colonial institutions influenced educational development in sub-Saharan Africa.

Many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2)(Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This finding underscores the importance of historical national accounting in understanding Africa's economic development. This data reveals that economic growth in African countries was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors influenced the economic performance of African countries, shaping their development trajectories. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, particularly regarding the persistence of poverty and the long-term effects of historical conditions on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Understanding these historical patterns is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the impact of specific historical or geographical conditions on long-term African welfare development.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with Pamuk, document how the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, focusing on the structural changes initiated by the Black Death (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). Pamuk highlights the role of institutional flexibility in enabling regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities, such as the ability of new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta complement this by showing how institutional changes in northwestern Europe led to investment in agriculture, which in turn resulted in high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Pamuk also notes that urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, varied significantly between northwestern and southern Europe during this period (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This variation suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, the specific outcomes depended on the local context and the nature of these changes.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr explains, is the mainspring of economic growth. This process is enabled by the development of formal institutions that reduce access costs to knowledge, such as scholarly societies and journals. Kuznets echoes this view, noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is essential, the institutional framework must adapt to harness its benefits fully. Mokyr acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness, which complements technological progress, is less monotonic and irreversible (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while institutions are necessary for sustaining economic growth, their effectiveness can vary over time and may require continuous adaptation.

The institutional framework plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as institutions determine the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) illustrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists can impede economic development. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) adds that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. These findings highlight that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or growth-promoting, directly influences economic outcomes. The effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs, as shown by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by their impact on the distribution of power and resources. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that institutions are not static but evolve in response to changing circumstances. This adaptability is crucial for sustaining economic growth, as institutions must continuously adjust to new challenges and opportunities. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide evidence that the political factors underpinning economic growth, such as credible commitment to property rights, are essential for fostering a stable economic environment. This highlights that the institutional framework must be robust enough to withstand political pressures and ensure long-term economic stability. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates the significance of political commitment to economic growth, as shown by North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2).

The historical evidence shows that the institutional framework must be robust enough to withstand political pressures and ensure long-term economic stability. The Bank of England's establishment and the financial revolution it sparked (North 1989, p.9) demonstrate the transformative power of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. The adaptability of institutions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) further underscores their role in sustaining long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence of the Bank of England's establishment (North 1989, p.9) and the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) settles the disagreement by showing that robust institutions committed to property rights and adaptable to changing circumstances are essential for long-run economic growth.
