The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue with significant implications for understanding economic development trajectories. Institutions are deeply intertwined with the distribution of power and resources, and their evolution can foster or hinder economic growth. The debate centres on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography or culture. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, arguing that institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for sustained growth. Another position, however, suggests that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their impact is often overstated, with geography and culture playing equally significant roles. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. This question is central to understanding the historical and contemporary differences in economic performance among nations. The debate between these positions is crucial for policymakers and scholars alike, as it informs strategies for economic development and the allocation of resources. The evolution of institutions over time and their interaction with other factors such as population density and prosperity are key areas of inquiry. This interplay highlights the complex nature of economic growth and the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping it.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) together argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, allowing for falling costs per transaction while increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets of the time, which highlights the critical role of political factors in underpinning economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further underscore that the regime's commitment to enforcing these rules was a pivotal factor in this process (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent, with other factors such as bureaucratic efficiency also playing a role.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated by the differential impact of European settlement patterns. This indicates that the type of institutions established during colonial rule played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the establishment of extractive institutions was one factor at play. The effectiveness of these institutions in maximizing rents for European colonists was a key determinant of their impact on economic development. Thus, the institutional framework introduced by colonial powers had a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectories of former colonies, with the specific nature of these institutions being a critical factor.

Therefore, the efficient use of innovations through technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) propose that technological advancements are central to economic growth, and that institutions must adapt to facilitate the efficient use of these innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress often requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments, which can vary across regions and time periods. This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth. The institutional framework must evolve to accommodate technological innovations, ensuring that societal conflicts arising from these changes are resolved peacefully. The modern national state plays a pivotal role in this process, as it provides the authority and community cohesion necessary to manage growth-induced conflicts without undermining societal stability (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.54) underscores that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting the sustainability of economic improvements. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional environment must also evolve to support and sustain these advancements. In this way, the institutional framework acts as a facilitator and stabilizer of technological progress, ensuring that economic growth is both robust and enduring.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), along with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), highlight how historical inequalities embedded in institutional frameworks can persistently affect societal outcomes. This is evident in how the legacy of slavery and inequality has shaped economic development in the New World. For instance, colonial institutions in societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, often favored elites through government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework, characterized by unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power, has had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these inequalities can be seen in the enduring mistrust within African societies, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue has significantly impacted economic development. This mistrust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust, shaped by historical inequalities, continues to hinder economic growth by affecting societal trust and cooperation, which are crucial for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) underscores the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development. The prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, has had a more enduring impact than the relatively shorter period of colonial rule, which lasted about 75 years. The legacy of slavery and inequality thus continues to shape economic development, highlighting the importance of addressing historical inequalities to foster long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. These findings underscore the significance of re-evaluating historical economic performance in Africa, as they reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that the economic development in these regions was more robust than previously characterized. Broadberry and Gardner show that the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence, has been underestimated. They note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg also highlight the importance of exploring the determinants of the real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. This deeper understanding of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, will help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development. These findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa may have been more nuanced and complex than previously understood, warranting further investigation into the specific mechanisms by which institutions influenced economic outcomes.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic response indicates that institutions evolved based on local conditions and power struggles. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are uniformly adopted based on their growth-enhancing properties. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions should be seen as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and function within specific contexts.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and security of economic transactions. North highlights that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is further supported by North and Weingast, who emphasize that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing rules, which in turn supported the development of markets and long-term economic growth. Austin complements this view by noting that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience of older industrial economies are essential for understanding current economic patterns (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared emphasis on the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and ensuring secure property rights underscores their fundamental importance in economic development. Austin also argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political dynamics, further highlighting their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that institutions are deeply intertwined with societal values and power dynamics. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding demonstrates that institutions are influenced by the interests of powerful groups within society. Acemoglu et al. further argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and influenced by a variety of factors, including the interests of powerful groups and the historical context in which they operate.
