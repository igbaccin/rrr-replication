The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development studies. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules of the game in a society, are pivotal in determining the incentives that guide economic behavior. The live disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position argues that institutions shape economic growth through the enforcement of property rights and the rule of law. Another position posits that institutions' impact on economic growth is mediated by cultural and historical factors, including the legacy of colonial policies and the persistence of mistrust. A third perspective emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions. The interplay between these factors highlights the complex nature of institutional influence on economic growth. This interplay is evident in the contrasting views on whether institutions' impact is direct or mediated by other socio-economic factors. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable development. The debate around the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) assert that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, with North (1989, p.9) showing that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a significant reduction in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth, as evident in the English financial revolution. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this by highlighting that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional variation had lasting effects on economic development, as seen in the contrasting outcomes between settler colonies like the United States and extractive states like the Congo (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify the relationship between institutions and economic growth by noting that equilibrium institutions may not always promote development. These institutions are often shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic underscores the complexity of institutional impact, where the design and enforcement of institutions are influenced by the interests of those in power. The interplay between institutional design and political dynamics highlights the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, where the benefits of institutional development are not always straightforward or universally beneficial.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (Austin 2008, p.2) jointly argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional evolution was marked by a significant shift in political dynamics, where the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights became a critical factor. North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions can be seen in the development of capital markets, which thrived under the secure property rights regime. Austin (2008, p.15) elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations, which sought fiscal security, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political actions. This adjustment highlights the adaptive nature of institutions, where they evolve in response to the incentives created by economic and political dynamics. This adaptive nature of institutions underscores their role in shaping long-run economic growth, where the evolution of property rights and the enforcement of institutional rules are key factors. The interplay between institutional design and political dynamics highlights the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, where the benefits of institutional development are not always straightforward or universally beneficial.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion of persistent African poverty by examining historical economic performance and real wage levels. They show that African poverty is not an enduring historical phenomenon, and that the economic performance of some African countries was better than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this perspective by demonstrating that the patterns of economic growth and decline in African countries were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. This highlights the role of external factors in shaping economic performance. This nuanced view suggests that while external factors played a significant role, local conditions also influenced economic performance. The role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes is also evident, as they could impact the market for exports and the overall economic performance of African countries. This perspective underscores the complex interplay between external and local factors in shaping African economic performance, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of the determinants of economic growth in the region.

Mokyr, along with Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49), contend that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. Kuznets emphasizes that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth, and its impact varies across regions and centuries. This technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Mokyr builds on this by highlighting the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge. This perspective underscores the importance of technological advancements in driving economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role in facilitating this process. Mokyr also acknowledges that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets further elaborates on this by noting that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments suggests that while institutions are important, they are not the primary driver of economic growth. Instead, they facilitate the efficient use of technological innovations, which are the mainspring of economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a response to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained as a norm within African societies. The slave trade's effect on trust operates through both external environmental factors and internal norms, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. Nunn elaborates on this point by highlighting the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, noting that these trades spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, and were thus more enduring than the period of official colonial rule. This historical context is crucial for understanding the long-term impact of the slave trade on African economic development, as it shaped the continent's economic and social institutions in profound ways. The enduring mistrust fostered by the slave trade continues to hinder economic growth by reducing the effectiveness of institutions and impeding the development of robust economic networks. This mistrust undermines the ability of institutions to function effectively, thereby limiting their role in shaping long-run economic growth. This analysis suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is not just a historical artifact but a persistent barrier to economic development in Africa, highlighting the need for policies that can address and overcome this deep-seated mistrust.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the roots of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies lie in distributional conflicts, which shaped key economic institutions in ways that favored elites and maintained inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by the power struggles between different social groups. These institutions often served to protect the interests of the elite, even if they were not the most economically efficient arrangements. Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this point by examining New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. The persistence of these institutions highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to the entrenchment of inequality, with significant implications for long-run economic growth. This dynamic is particularly evident in societies where elites have the power to shape institutions in their favor, creating barriers to economic development for the broader population. However, Ogilvie cautions that while institutions are crucial, they must be integrated into a broader research program that considers their complex interactions with other economic factors.

Institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and property rights. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly influenced their economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster long-term growth, highlights how institutional frameworks can perpetuate economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North argues that secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further underscore the importance of institutions governing public choice, demonstrating that credible commitment to property rights was pivotal in fostering economic development in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships between institutions and economic outcomes by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other economic factors. Ogilvie cautions that institutions must be integrated into a broader research program that considers their complex interactions with other economic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective aligns with North's emphasis on the importance of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights (North 1989, p.6). The shared finding is that institutions matter, but the disagreement remains over the precise mechanisms by which they influence economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparison of institutional frameworks in former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, experienced significantly lower levels of economic development compared to those with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This empirical comparison shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had a profound and lasting impact on long-run economic growth.
