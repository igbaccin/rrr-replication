The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development studies. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure political, economic, and social interactions, are widely acknowledged to influence economic outcomes. The intellectual stakes involve understanding the mechanisms through which institutions drive growth, and the extent to which these mechanisms are universal or context-dependent. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and culture, or if they are endogenous and can be altered through political and economic processes. On one side, some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and culture playing equally significant roles. Another debate focuses on the nature of institutions themselves: are they efficient responses to economic challenges, or are they shaped by distributional conflicts and power dynamics? This debate is crucial for understanding historical economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote development in contemporary settings. The interaction between institutions and growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of this relationship. Institutions matter because they provide the framework within which economic activities occur, influencing everything from property rights to market regulations. This framework sets the stage for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth, and how different theoretical perspectives contribute to this understanding.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as the primary drivers. North (1989, p.9) highlights the significance of the English financial revolution, noting that the establishment of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by creating a more secure environment for economic exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutions established by colonial powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in their former colonies. The type of institution established depended on European policies, which were influenced by the mortality rates of Europeans in these colonies. When mortality rates were high, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, leaving behind a legacy of poor economic performance. Where Europeans settled in large numbers and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, the economic outcomes were more favorable. This pattern is evident in the stark differences between the economic trajectories of colonies like the United States and Australia, which experienced high levels of European settlement and developed strong institutions, and those like the Congo and the Gold Coast, which were characterized by extractive institutions and low levels of European settlement. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) together argue that the institutional evolution of different historical periods and paths is crucial in understanding economic development. This perspective challenges simplistic causal narratives by highlighting the complexity of institutional change. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, adapting their institutional prescriptions to local incentives. This adaptive behaviour suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged commodity production, illustrating how institutions can be shaped by the need for economic stability. Furthermore, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) posits that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective underscores the role of power dynamics and social conflicts in shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement.
