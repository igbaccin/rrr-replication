The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One side argues that institutions matter by setting the rules that govern economic interactions, thereby determining the incentives for investment and growth. The other side posits that the effects of institutions are contingent on other factors, such as geography, culture, or the specific historical context. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions can inform policy interventions aimed at fostering economic development in less prosperous regions. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they operate in conjunction with other factors. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in former European colonies significantly affected their current income per capita. Austin, however, suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, implying that institutions alone cannot explain economic outcomes. This tension sets the stage for a nuanced exploration of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments were crucial in this regard, as they provided a more secure time dimension of property rights. This development reflects a broader pattern of institutional evolution that facilitated market exchanges and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the commitment of the regime to these rules underpins economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights leads to falling transaction costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the economy. The development of these institutions was critical in fostering a long-term capital market, which is essential for economic growth. This institutional framework fostered economic growth and created a stable environment for capital markets to flourish. However, the role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to reducing transaction costs; North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore the importance of credible commitment by the regime to the rules governing economic exchange, which is a critical political factor in sustaining economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from the status quo, further entrenching the extractive nature of these colonial regimes. The persistence of these institutions post-independence has been a significant barrier to economic development in many former colonies. Despite the role of geography, the institutional framework established during colonial rule has had a lasting impact on economic outcomes in these regions.

Frankema (2012, p.1) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This is evident in the development of formal education systems, which were more robust under British rule. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, indicating a level of engagement with the educational initiatives introduced by the British. This engagement was facilitated by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further support this by showing that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied owing to a range of factors, including political institutions. This suggests that the institutional framework established by British colonial rule played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa.

This focus on poverty reduction, while important, often overlooks the broader economic context necessary for sustained growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the perspective that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon. They argue that the economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at poverty reduction, particularly by providing incentives for investment and development. The new economic history of Africa has drawn attention from development specialists by emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) focus on the long-term picture of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income, to assess future growth possibilities and the impact of historical conditions on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This focus on historical economic conditions and the role of institutions in shaping them underscores the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. The historical perspective on African poverty and the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes provides valuable insights into the potential for long-run economic growth in the region.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The authors argue that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channels, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin also notes that the behaviour of older industrial economies can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that the mistrust caused by the slave trade has deep historical roots and has significantly impacted the institutional evolution of African economies.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that these conflicts, not the institutions themselves, were the fundamental drivers. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, it posits that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and the craft and merchant guilds, were shaped by the political and economic struggles between different social groups. These struggles influenced the distribution of power and resources, leading to institutional arrangements that reflected the interests of the dominant groups. The distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe were marked by a struggle for control over resources and political power. This dynamic led to the emergence of institutions that served to maintain the status quo and the interests of the ruling elite. For instance, serfdom and guilds were maintained because they helped to preserve the power of the landed aristocracy and the merchant class. This perspective suggests that the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe was deeply influenced by the power dynamics and distributional conflicts of the time. These distributional conflicts have implications that reach beyond pre-industrial Europe. For example, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be traced back to the institutional arrangements that were put in place during the colonial period, which advantaged the elite through various government policies and restricted competition. This suggests that the legacy of distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges, as North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). Institutions specify property rights and provide effective enforcement, leading to falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is crucial for economic growth, as it encourages investment and provides the necessary incentives for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Moreover, the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). These findings are echoed by Acemoglu et al. who argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In the other extreme, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe was deeply influenced by the power dynamics and distributional conflicts of the time.

The persistence of inequality in Latin America can be traced back to the institutional arrangements that were put in place during the colonial period, which advantaged the elite through various government policies and restricted competition (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This suggests that the legacy of distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic development. The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the relative incomes of former European colonies have reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, provides further evidence of the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The thesis emphasizes the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by the Europeans, documenting the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This reversal was related to industrialization, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreement is that the legacy of distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical evidence of the reversal of fortune in former European colonies settles the disagreement, as it shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
