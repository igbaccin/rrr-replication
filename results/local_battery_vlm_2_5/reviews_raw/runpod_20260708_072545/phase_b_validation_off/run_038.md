The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economics and economic history. At stake is the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, determine a society's economic trajectory over the long term. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. This debate intersects with the broader question of whether economic growth is primarily shaped by endogenous or exogenous factors. The crux of the matter is whether institutions can be transformed to foster economic growth, or if they are merely a reflection of deeper, more intractable forces. This question is particularly salient for understanding the economic trajectories of different regions and the potential for policy interventions to promote growth. The debate over institutions and economic growth has profound implications for policy and development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can be understood through the lens of different scholarly streams, each offering distinct perspectives on the causal mechanisms at play.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) share the view that institutions are critical for long-run economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that primarily focus on population and savings. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this through the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework enhanced the security of property rights, which is essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further develop this idea by showing that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutions, which in turn influenced economic outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive states that rapidly transferred resources to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that the design of institutions often maximizes rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the institutional frameworks established during colonial times were often oriented towards extracting resources for the benefit of the colonizers.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework allowed for the emergence of capital markets and the development of a stable economic environment. The political commitment to these rules was a critical factor in ensuring that the regime was bound by them, thereby fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in England secured property rights and created a political environment where the rules governing economic exchange were enforced. This political stability was essential for the long-term economic development of the country. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was influenced by the institutional frameworks that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The different factor endowments in these societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. This highlights that while secure property rights and political stability were key for economic growth, the initial conditions and the distribution of power within societies also played a significant role in determining the trajectory of economic development. The institutional legacies of colonial times, therefore, had a profound impact on the economic outcomes of these societies.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2) share a perspective that Africa's slave trades have had a profound impact on economic development, shaping the continent's trajectory in ways that extend beyond colonialism and recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). The slave trades disrupted the development of strong, centralized institutions that could support economic growth and trade. The lack of centralized governance and economic institutions meant that Africa was ill-equipped to support trade with Europeans. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that regions with sustained trade, such as the Kongo kingdom, had centralized governments and well-developed markets, which facilitated foreign trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This pattern of resource allocation has led to high levels of military spending and debt service payments, which have displaced capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). The legacy of slave trades and the subsequent neopatrimonial governance structures have thus created a vicious cycle where economic development is continually hindered by institutional weaknesses. This cycle has been particularly marked in countries like Kenya and Tanzania, where high debt burdens and military expenditures have diverted resources away from productive investments.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting trust levels within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade had a profound impact on social trust, with its effects persisting even after accounting for interethnic slave exports. This mistrust is deeply ingrained and manifests in various aspects of social and economic life. This culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Akyeampong and Fofack illustrate this by documenting how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, a pattern reinforced by colonial rule. This marginalization, alongside the codification of customary law that further disadvantaged women, highlights how historical injustices have shaped contemporary economic structures. These patterns of mistrust and marginalization have created a vicious cycle where economic development is continually hindered by institutional weaknesses. The legacy of the slave trade and subsequent neopatrimonial governance structures have thus created a vicious cycle where economic development is continually hindered by institutional weaknesses. This dynamic is particularly evident in the marginalization of women, who were often relegated to less productive economic activities due to a combination of colonial policies and customary laws that disadvantaged them. This pattern of resource allocation has led to high levels of military spending and debt service payments, which have displaced capital and recurrent spending.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) share a finding that the colonial legacy played a significant role in shaping economic institutions, which in turn influenced development outcomes. For example, colonial policies often directed local institutions towards resource extraction, which could stifle economic diversification and long-term growth. The colonial administration's influence on economic institutions was profound, as it shaped local governance structures and resource allocation mechanisms. This institutional framework often prioritized the interests of the colonizing power over those of the indigenous population, leading to long-term economic distortions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This does not negate the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic development. The legacy of colonial rule continues to influence African economies through the persistence of institutions that were designed to serve the interests of the colonizers. This institutional inheritance has had lasting implications for economic growth and development, as it has shaped the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share the finding that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal pathways of economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the institutional evolution of European economies, shaped by settler presence and indigenous economic action, deviates from a universal path (Austin 2008, p.15). The dynamics of colonial governance reveal how institutions adapted to local conditions and incentives. This perspective underscores how the adoption of institutions favourable for economic growth is contingent on complex socio-political factors. The colonial experience in Africa illustrates this complexity, where institutions designed to serve the colonizers' interests persisted and influenced economic development trajectories. This historical institutional analysis suggests that the long-term effects of colonial rule on African economies are a result of the enduring impact of institutions shaped by specific historical contexts and power dynamics. This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with local conditions over time.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. North argues that institutions matter by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. build on this by showing that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted current economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They argue that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, with high-mortality colonies often receiving extractive institutions that persist to this day (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical institutional analysis underscores the enduring impact of colonial rule on African economies, illustrating how specific historical contexts and power dynamics shape institutions over time. Austin further elaborates on this, emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15) highlights the importance of understanding how institutions evolve and interact with local conditions over time.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings underscore the complexity of institutional development and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions are shaped by power dynamics and shared beliefs. Hopkins, by suggesting that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development, reinforces this understanding (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
