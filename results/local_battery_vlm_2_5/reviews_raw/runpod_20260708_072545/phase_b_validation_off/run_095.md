The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins sustained economic development. One side of the debate posits that institutions, by defining the rules governing economic interactions and enforcing property rights, are pivotal in fostering economic growth. The other side contends that institutions are less critical, with other factors such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks playing more significant roles. The central disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors. This debate has significant implications for understanding historical economic trajectories and informing contemporary policy. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, highlighting the political factors underpinning economic growth. (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, with Acemoglu et al. agreeing that institutions shape economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are essential for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) describes how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate this dynamic in colonial contexts, showing how institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment led to significant economic development in colonies like the United States and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that support long-term economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their impact on economic growth depends on their design and the interests they serve.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). Credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that political actors adhere to rules that support market development. This commitment is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic activity. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that the institutional frameworks established during this period were not only about economic rules but also about the political structures that enforced and adapted these rules. This adaptation was crucial in responding to changing economic and political landscapes, as seen in colonial administrations that adjusted their policies based on local incentives and actions. However, Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the institutional changes were not universally revolutionary; instead, they were part of a broader historical context where different paths and patterns of institutional evolution played out. This highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic growth, where the specific historical and political contexts shape the outcomes.

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23) both examine long-term structural changes in Europe, but do not directly address how institutions influence these processes. Despite this, their work provides valuable insights into the economic development and structural changes in Europe. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) focuses on the period between 1500 and 1800, using wage data to study geographical patterns in silver and grain wages and their development over time. This analysis reveals that economic development, measured by the urbanisation ratio, did not consistently correlate with improvements in the standard of living. Pamuk (2007, p.23) argues that the Black Death initiated a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, highlighting the significance of institutional flexibility in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. This divergence in institutional responses to economic shocks underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic development. However, van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the economic development shift to the North Sea countries after 1700 led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating that economic development was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including institutional changes and market dynamics (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This interplay suggests that while institutions play a crucial role, they are just one piece of the puzzle in understanding long-term economic growth.

This influence is particularly evident in the context of colonial rule in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial powers had varying impacts on African institutions, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education. This variation in colonial policies altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which was linked to new social and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence. This economic performance was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that while institutions matter for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development, the quality of public goods provided by African governments was not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This suggests that while colonial policies shaped institutional frameworks, the effectiveness of these institutions in promoting long-term economic growth was influenced by complex interactions within African societies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) explore the influence of historical factors such as the role of women and the impact of the slave trade on African economic development. Their work highlights that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, a view that challenges the marginalization of women's roles. This is particularly evident in the economic revolution driven by cocoa production in the Gold Coast, where women played a crucial role in migrating for farmland. This dynamic was influenced by patriarchal alliances and customary law, which codified the subordinate status of women under colonial rule. The slave trade also played a significant role in shaping African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon note (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), has been shown to be at least twice as large as the external channel of mistrust, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Thus, historical factors such as the role of women and the impact of the slave trade have profoundly influenced African economic development, shaping the institutional frameworks and social norms that continue to affect growth and development.

Economic growth is fundamentally propelled by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) assert. Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth, highlighting how the growth of economic reasonableness complements technological progress. Kuznets, similarly, argues that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Kuznets notes that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, which may require substantial institutional and ideological shifts. For example, the transition from a rural mode of life to an industrialized society necessitates significant adjustments in societal structures and norms, which can be facilitated by the sovereign state. Without such adjustments, the sustainability of economic improvement is at risk. This dynamic is evident in the early periods of industrialization, where countries that successfully managed these transitions experienced sustained economic growth. However, Kuznets also acknowledges that the pace and particular production sectors affected by technological progress vary across regions and centuries, suggesting that the institutional and ideological adjustments must be tailored to specific contexts. Mokyr adds that coordination failures can arise from technological progress, necessitating policies to rectify them. This underscores the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman share a finding that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They argue that these institutions were shaped by the interests of elites, who used them to maintain their power and wealth. The mechanism by which this occurred is through the use of government policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality. For example, in Latin America, institutions were structured to protect the interests of the elite, who controlled the political and economic systems. This resulted in a lack of property rights and contract enforcement, which hindered economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how these institutions persisted over time, influencing the course of development. Ogilvie cautions that the role of institutions should not be oversimplified. She argues that institutions are complex structures that reflect the underlying social and political dynamics of a society. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions can be easily transformed to promote economic growth, as they are deeply embedded in the social fabric (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The implications of this view are significant, as it suggests that efforts to promote economic development through institutional reform must take into account the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts that shape institutions.

A key finding across multiple studies is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonizers established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, which had lasting impacts on economic outcomes. These institutions, shaped by the underlying social and political dynamics, reflect the power structures and distributional conflicts of the time. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, for example, significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is reinforced by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how secure property rights and effective enforcement can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Ogilvie's work further underscores that institutions are not merely tools for economic efficiency but are deeply embedded in the social fabric, reflecting underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize their own interests over long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared understanding highlights the critical role of institutions in economic development.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, is a key point of agreement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is corroborated by Hopkins's (2009, p.8) emphasis on the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Thus, the specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the variation in colonial policies and their long-term economic impacts, as well as the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment.
