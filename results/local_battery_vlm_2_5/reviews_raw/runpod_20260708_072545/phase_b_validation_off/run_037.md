The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions drive economic development or merely reflect other underlying factors. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, while others contend that they are secondary to other factors such as geography or culture. The debate hinges on whether institutions can be seen as independent variables, or if they are simply proxies for other causal forces. This disagreement is critical because it influences how economists and policymakers approach economic development. For instance, if institutions are the key drivers of growth, then policy should focus on institutional reform. If institutions are mere epiphenomena, then other factors, such as geography or culture, should be the primary targets of policy intervention. The disagreement also shapes how historians understand the past, as the role of institutions in economic growth is central to interpreting the rise and fall of empires and civilizations. The central question, then, is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely a reflection of other underlying factors, and if they are primary drivers, what specific institutions matter. The answer to this question has profound implications for both economic theory and policy.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided increasingly effective enforcement reduced costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, allowing the economy to benefit from increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional change was both an economic and a political phenomenon, as it involved the commitment of the regime to these rules, which was critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implications of this institutional framework are profound, as it suggests that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). These changes were deeply rooted in the European strategies of colonization. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework is key to understanding the economic trajectories of former colonies. For instance, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, the extractive nature of these institutions persistently hindered economic development. By contrast, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, economic growth was more robust. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that this institutional difference has lasting effects on current economic performance. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies suggests that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development. The persistence of these institutional legacies means that the economic impact of colonialism is a continuing influence on contemporary economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) present a counterargument, suggesting that colonial institutional legacies do not uniformly determine economic outcomes in Africa. This nuanced view of institutional evolution in Africa underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and contextual factors that shaped colonial institutions and their economic impacts.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economic performance was not as dire as previously thought, and that the region's historical economic development has been misunderstood. They contend that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates has obscured the true picture of African economic development. The authors reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, revealing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's work, which demonstrates the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era in Africa. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This suggests that economic growth in Africa was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, not just persistent poverty. Broadberry and Gardner note that the patterns of growing and shrinking were in most countries tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports. This qualification underscores the importance of external factors in shaping African economic development during the colonial period.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, as argued by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This divergence was driven by structural differences in economic development, particularly in the role of the service sector and institutional changes. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that the service sector, through merchant distribution and finance, played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This sector facilitated the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe, contributing to the region's economic development. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) further supports this by noting the shift in economic development to countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England. This shift was marked by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a change in economic dynamics that favored these regions. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) also points out that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period. This qualification suggests that while economic development was occurring, its benefits were not uniformly distributed across the population. The implications of this uneven distribution are significant, as they indicate that the economic growth experienced in Europe during this period was a complex process influenced by various factors, including institutional changes and sectoral productivity. The economic divergence between Europe and Asia, therefore, was a reflection of broader structural and institutional changes that shaped long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This approach posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were the result of political struggles between different social groups. These struggles led to institutions that reflected the interests of the dominant groups. For example, the persistence of serfdom can be seen as a means for landowners to maintain control over labor, while the craft guilds served to restrict competition and protect the interests of established artisans. The implications of this perspective are significant, as they suggest that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were the outcome of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. This perspective is further supported by the work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) who argue that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutions that perpetuate inequality. The distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe, therefore, were a key factor in shaping the economic institutions that influenced long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa directly hindered economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impeding economic growth. This mistrust manifests both externally, through factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internally, through individuals' norms of mistrust. This internal channel is notably more influential than the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, far exceeds the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This prolonged impact suggests that the slave trades had a profound and lasting effect on Africa's economic institutions, shaping them in ways that continue to influence development trajectories. The centralised governance and well-developed markets of the Kongo kingdom, for instance, facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how institutional structures can either support or hinder economic interactions (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both argue. Technological advancements, in particular, have been a significant force in economic development, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These technological changes have varied over centuries and regions, shaping the growth trajectories of economies. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology often conflicts with traditional modes of life and family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This tension requires resolution through institutional changes to facilitate economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional progress, as the latter ensures the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by emphasizing that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts must occur without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Without such institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic growth remains limited.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law or created extractive states. This variation in institutional frameworks significantly influenced the long-term economic outcomes of these colonies. North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional progress was crucial for the growth of a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. The creation of these institutions reflects a broader trend where political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement contribute to economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional design. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design them to maximize rents. This perspective highlights that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to benefit specific groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) supports this view by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This includes institutions like serfdom, the community, and guilds, which were shaped by the interests of powerful groups. The historical evidence from colonial settings and pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions designed to maximize rents can hinder long-term economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutional design in economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa shows that institutional reforms can inform policy and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This highlights the ongoing relevance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.9; Hopkins 2009, p.8) is that institutions matter for economic growth. The disagreement is whether the institutions that matter are those that maximize rents or those that promote efficiency. The historical evidence of the British industrial revolution (North 1989, p.9) settles the disagreement by showing that institutions which promote efficiency and lower transaction costs are more conducive to long-term economic growth.
