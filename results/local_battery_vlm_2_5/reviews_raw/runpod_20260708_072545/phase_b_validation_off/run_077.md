The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic prosperity over generations. One position argues that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, shaping the incentives and constraints that guide economic activity. This view emphasizes the importance of institutional quality, particularly the security of property rights and the rule of law, in fostering investment and innovation. Another position posits that economic growth is primarily driven by exogenous factors such as geography and climate, which influence the types of institutions that emerge. This perspective posits that institutions are a byproduct of these underlying factors, influencing economic growth. Furthermore, some scholars propose that the role of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, play a more significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The debate hinges on the interplay between institutions and other factors, with scholars disagreeing on the extent to which institutions can be credited with long-run economic growth. The question of whether institutions are the driving force behind economic growth or merely a reflection of deeper underlying factors remains unresolved.

The distributional conflicts that arose from these power struggles were more fundamental in shaping economic institutions than any considerations of efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the establishment of institutions that favored the elite. This inequality was perpetuated through institutions that restricted competition and limited opportunities for the lower classes. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that understanding these distributional conflicts is crucial for economic historians. She posits that institutions should be viewed as tools that reflect the power dynamics of the societies that created them. The persistence of these institutions over time has significant implications for economic development. Ogilvie also notes that the focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the importance of efficiency considerations. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) provide a detailed analysis of Europe's economic development, highlighting the regional shifts and changes that characterized this period. Van Zanden's (1999, p.1) study of wage data from twenty European cities and regions reveals that economic development did not uniformly improve the standard of living across Europe. This finding is supported by Pamuk (2007, p.23), who highlights the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, which allowed for rapid economic adaptation and urban growth. Pamuk (2007, p.16) further elaborates on this by noting that urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, varied significantly across Europe during this period. This variation suggests that the institutional environment played a crucial role in determining how different regions responded to economic opportunities. The shift in economic development towards the North Sea countries, particularly Holland and England, illustrates how regional economic shifts influenced the broader European economy. This regional specialization and institutional flexibility in the northwest contributed to the rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, making England the most expensive country by 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These findings suggest that the institutional framework and regional economic dynamics were pivotal in shaping the long-run economic growth trajectory of Europe.
