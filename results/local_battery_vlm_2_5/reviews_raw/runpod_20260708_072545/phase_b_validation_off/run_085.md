The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. At stake is the explanatory power of institutions in economic development and the extent to which they can account for historical and contemporary economic disparities. One live disagreement concerns the nature and impact of institutions established during colonial periods. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions imposed by colonial powers, such as extractive versus inclusive institutions, significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies. This perspective emphasizes the enduring effects of colonial institutions on current economic outcomes. One perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural and economic factors, which drive economic growth. This view suggests that institutions are a byproduct of deeper historical and social processes, and their impact on economic growth is secondary. A third perspective contends that institutions matter, but their effectiveness is contingent on broader economic and political contexts, including the presence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. These differing viewpoints highlight the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within specific historical and cultural contexts. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth underscores the importance of examining the interplay between institutions, culture, and economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the institutional changes in England during the seventeenth century were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, which is a critical dimension of property rights that supports long-term capital markets and economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by these institutional advancements, was a direct response to fiscal crises and reflected a strategic trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution involved political factors that supported economic growth and the enforcement of rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, contributing to economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The role of secure property rights in fostering economic development is further supported by the observation that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was not significantly different from England's. This suggests that the success of England's economic institutions depended on the political commitment to enforcing and maintaining them.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonists established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and more inclusive ones in low-mortality areas. These institutional differences persisted and shaped long-run economic outcomes. For instance, the extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which hindered local economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) find that these institutional differences are robust across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. The persistence of these institutions over time suggests that the initial institutional choices made by colonial powers had lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. This reversal underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this perspective and challenge the conventional view that these institutions were designed for economic efficiency. They argue that these institutions were instead the result of power struggles and the interests of dominant groups. This perspective is significant because it offers a different lens through which to understand the origins of economic institutions, suggesting that they were designed to maintain social order and consolidate power. For example, craft guilds, which are often seen as barriers to economic growth due to their restrictive practices, can be understood as institutions that emerged from the struggles between different social groups. Craft guilds were formed to protect the interests of skilled workers and to maintain their monopoly over certain trades, which limited competition and restricted economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective highlights the importance of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions and suggests that these institutions may not always be conducive to economic growth. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is complex. For instance, while craft guilds may have restricted competition and limited economic growth in the short term, they may have also contributed to the development of skilled labor and the diffusion of knowledge in the long term (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping economic growth underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19; 1999, p.1) argue that economic performance and living standards in Europe are complex and multifaceted. They show that the skill premium, which reflects the remuneration for investing in human capital, is a crucial factor in understanding long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.3) explains that the skill premium is determined by various factors, including the costs of training, the chances of successfully finishing training, and the interest rate linking future earnings to present costs. These factors collectively shape the economic landscape by influencing the supply and demand of skilled labor. This framework helps to understand the broader economic dynamics at play. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) caution that estimates of national accounts, which are essential for understanding economic performance, are subject to margins of error. These errors can be significant, especially when studying distant historical periods, and are often based on subjective estimates of underlying data. This underscores the need for careful interpretation of economic data and the recognition that economic performance is influenced by a multitude of factors, including the accuracy of the data used to measure it.

Pamuk (2007, p.23; 2007, p.16) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death triggered significant changes in economic institutions, particularly in regions with flexible institutional structures. These changes enabled rapid responses to new economic opportunities. For instance, Holland's institutional flexibility allowed new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies. This institutional adaptability was crucial in transforming Holland into one of the most advanced, urbanised, and commercialised regions of the continent. Regions like Tuscany, which strengthened existing monopolies, experienced stagnation and a decline in urban rankings. This divergence highlights the role of institutional flexibility in shaping long-run economic growth. While Pamuk focuses on institutional flexibility, Kuznets (Pamuk 2007, p.16) emphasizes structural change as a key characteristic of economic growth in the early modern and modern eras. This structural change, driven by institutional flexibility, led to significant differences in urbanisation rates between northwestern and southern Europe. However, while institutional flexibility facilitated growth in regions like Holland, it is important to note that the overall impact of the Black Death on economic institutions was complex and varied across different regions.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2), highlighting the importance of considering historical contexts beyond institutional frameworks. This prolonged exposure to slave trades disrupted African societies, leading to economic underdevelopment. Frankema and van Waijenburg support this perspective by highlighting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to assess the long-term effects of slave trades on African welfare (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Austin qualifies this downstream implication by noting that colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged indigenous economic and political action, which sometimes led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that while the slave trades had a profound impact, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is more complex and varied than Nunn's thesis might suggest.

British rule in Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This is evidenced in the British educational policy, which fostered a greater demand for missionary education among African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British approach to education in Africa differed from other colonial powers, who often prioritized extractive policies over investment in human capital. This institutional framework influenced educational outcomes, leading to more developed educational systems in British colonies compared to those under other colonial administrations. Hopkins notes that the role of institutions in shaping economic development is complex. While good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development, the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while British rule may have been more conducive to educational development, the overall impact on economic growth was influenced by a multitude of factors, including the quality of institutions and the incentives they provided for investment.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly show that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental to economic growth than institutional frameworks. The development of useful knowledge is pivotal to economic growth because it enables the creation of new technologies and the improvement of existing ones. This process requires institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) identifies the sovereign state as crucial in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This implies that while institutions play a role in managing the social and political tensions that arise from economic growth, the underlying driver of that growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while institutions are important, they are not the primary cause of economic growth. Instead, they serve as enablers that facilitate the application of new knowledge and technologies. The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, is the mainspring of economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they facilitate the application of new knowledge and technologies. Institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they help create secure property rights, which are critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The development of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, such as those established in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, has led to significant economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is because institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment create an environment conducive to economic growth. These institutions provide the necessary framework for economic transactions, reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). This shows that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends beyond their ability to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. Institutions also play a crucial role in shaping the distribution of economic gains. Institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists were likely to have been extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
