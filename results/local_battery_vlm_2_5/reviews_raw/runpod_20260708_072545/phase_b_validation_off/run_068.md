The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense debate among economists and historians. The stakes are high because the question of how institutions influence growth can inform policy decisions and shape our understanding of historical economic development. Central to this debate is the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that other variables, such as geographical factors, cultural beliefs, or colonial legacies, may play a more significant role. For instance, while North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The tension between these views underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth. The debate is far from settled, and the search for a definitive answer continues.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are fundamental to economic growth, with North emphasizing the role of transaction costs and Acemoglu et al. focusing on the extractive nature of colonial institutions. North (1989, p.9) highlights the financial revolution in England, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs. These institutional developments reflect increased security in property rights, which are crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This security enabled a growing proportion of the economy to be devoted to transactions, facilitating market exchanges and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) build on this by showing that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions aimed at rapid resource transfer to the metropole, which persisted and affected current income per capita. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that benefit from maintaining the status quo. This suggests that even when institutions are not designed for economic development, they can still persist due to the interests of those in power. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where institutional design and political power significantly influence long-term economic outcomes.

This interplay extends to the governance of public choice, where North and Weingast, along with Ogilvie, propose that the evolution of institutions is pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, challenging views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast’s analysis of seventeenth-century England illustrates this dynamic, where political factors underpinning economic growth were crucial. They argue that the success of England’s economic development was tied to the regime's commitment to enforcing rules that secured property rights. This commitment was a critical factor in the development of markets and the enforcement of economic exchange rules. Ogilvie, on the other hand, underscores the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, suggesting that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This includes institutions like serfdom, the community, and the craft guild. Ogilvie’s emphasis on shared beliefs as part of the institutional structure of a society complements North and Weingast’s focus on political factors, highlighting that the evolution of institutions is not just about efficiency but also about the distributional outcomes that these institutions produce. Ogilvie also observes that incorporating institutions into economic history research requires careful consideration of how these institutions shape and are shaped by economic outcomes. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact is mediated by the broader social and political context in which they operate. The evolution of institutions governing public choice involves securing property rights and managing distributional conflicts within society.

Technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, as the growth of useful knowledge underpins sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both stress that technological progress involves innovation and the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate efficient use of these innovations. This perspective highlights that technological advancements require complementary institutional changes to ensure that new knowledge is effectively integrated into the economy. This integration is crucial because it addresses coordination failures and ensures that technological progress is sustained over the long run. The historical record shows that societies that successfully managed these adjustments experienced significant economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that these adjustments must occur without excessive costs or long-term weakening of society’s political fabric. This qualification underscores the importance of political stability in sustaining economic growth driven by technological advancements.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. These changes were not merely institutional but also economic, as Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic context reinforced the value of education, as families saw increasing returns from investing in their children's schooling. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic outcomes was complex. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that in other contexts, institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This suggests that while British rule in Africa may have promoted education, the broader institutional framework could still limit economic mobility and growth.

Long-term economic performance is influenced by various factors, including skill premiums and wage levels, which are examined by van Zanden and Broadberry and Gupta (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The guilds in Western Europe, for instance, managed to facilitate the intergenerational transfer of skills, which was crucial for economic development. These institutions were also criticized for creating local cartels that restricted trade entry and kept relative pay above market-clearing levels. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) complement this by showing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose from merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a critical role in economic growth during the early modern period. The implications of these findings are that while institutions like guilds could enhance skill development, their restrictive practices might limit overall economic growth by suppressing competition and innovation. This tension between the beneficial and restrictive aspects of institutional frameworks underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-term economic performance. These factors collectively influence the supply and demand dynamics of skilled labor, which in turn affect long-term economic performance.

The complexities of historical paths and the role of African initiatives in shaping institutional change are further illuminated by the literature on colonialism and institutional reforms in Africa. Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both argue that simplistic causal narratives, such as the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, fail to capture the nuanced dynamics of institutional evolution in Africa. Austin notes that the 'new institutionalist' approach, which emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, struggles with quantitative testing and explaining the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions in some countries but not others. This limitation is particularly relevant in the African context, where colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production. This adaptation indicates that institutional change in Africa resulted from both external powers and local initiatives. Hopkins builds on this by noting that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing that 'good' institutions provide essential incentives for investment and development. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding historical paths and local initiatives in shaping institutional frameworks that can drive long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These differences in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by the persistence of these institutions and their impact on current income per capita. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This highlights the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic development. Austin notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, demonstrating that institutional change was not solely an imposition from external powers but also a result of local initiatives (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that institutional frameworks can evolve to provide essential incentives for investment and development, aligning with Hopkins' emphasis on the importance of 'good' institutions in driving long-term economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
