The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. This question has sparked a live disagreement among scholars, with some emphasizing the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, while others point to rival causes such as geographic factors or cultural beliefs. The former argue that institutions, by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, create a stable environment conducive to investment and economic growth. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can lead to extractive institutions that do not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Those who advocate for rival causes contend that geographic conditions and cultural beliefs play a significant role in shaping economic trajectories (Author, Year, p.N). The debate underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that drive long-run economic growth.

Building on this complexity, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework was crucial for the long-term capital market and overall economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction, which in turn facilitated market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The political commitment to these rules, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight, was a critical factor in ensuring the regime's adherence to these institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of these institutions varies across different economic contexts. This suggests that the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can vary, underscoring the importance of understanding the unique historical and political contexts in which these institutions operate.

Extending this idea, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, with the specific nature of these institutions varying based on European mortality rates in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, hindered investment and economic development. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The differing institutional frameworks set in motion by colonial powers thus had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Moreover, Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). The presence of poor institutions is linked to Africa's economic struggles, and the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework was crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that colonial policies could foster economic growth under certain conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors indicate that while colonial rule could spur economic growth, the specific institutional and political contexts played a critical role in determining the extent and sustainability of this growth.

Institutional adjustments and technological progress are interdependent drivers of economic growth, with technological advances necessitating corresponding institutional changes for efficiency (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both emphasize the role of technological progress in economic growth, but they also highlight the importance of institutional adjustments. Mokyr similarly highlights the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr also notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and might be eliminated by rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is crucial for economic growth, it must be accompanied by institutional adjustments to ensure long-term sustainability. For instance, the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The pace and extent of these adjustments can vary, and their effectiveness depends on the specific institutional and political contexts (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets and Mokyr's insights suggest that while technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own. Institutional adjustments are also required to ensure that technological progress is used efficiently and sustainably.

This divergence is a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1). These scholars emphasize that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, challenging views that attribute this divergence solely to later industrial developments. This economic growth was not uniform, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled laborers, indicating that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly. Van Zanden (1999, p.1; 1999, p.9) complicates this narrative by showing that economic development in Europe did not necessarily translate into improved standards of living, as there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards between 1500 and 1800. This implies that while merchant distribution and finance were important, they were not sufficient to ensure broad-based economic growth and improved living standards.

This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2), who argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to favor elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. A key mechanism in this process is the codification of customary law, which can reinforce existing power structures and limit economic opportunities for marginalized groups. These institutions, in turn, affected the course of development by influencing property rights, the financial sector, and social capital. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that the slave trades in Africa had a profound impact on economic development, with the centralized governments and well-developed markets of kingdoms like Kongo facilitating trade with Europeans. This suggests that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule could have long-lasting effects on economic growth and development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming. This indicates that while institutions may favor elites, they do not completely exclude other groups from contributing to economic development. Institutions can be both a barrier and a facilitator of economic growth, depending on how they are structured and who they benefit.

This critique is furthered by Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argue that simplified institutional theories in economic history compress different historical periods and paths in ways that obscure the complexities of long-run economic growth. They note that existing formulations often fail to capture the nuances of institutional evolution and the varied paths of economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, which posits that institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, oversimplifies the causal mechanisms. This critique is particularly relevant when considering the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective suggests that the adoption of institutions is influenced by power dynamics and the distribution of resources. A downstream implication of this perspective is that institutions may not always be adopted for their growth-promoting potential. Austin (2008, p.2) further qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions can be shaped by local contexts and the actions of those within them (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights the importance of considering the local and contextual factors that influence institutional development and their impact on economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and securing property rights. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, and that institutions such as the Bank of England, which facilitated market transactions and secured property rights, contributed to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This highlights how institutional development can lower transaction costs and promote economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize this point by illustrating how the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly impacted current income per capita, showing that institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in economic growth is not straightforward. Austin and Hopkins point out that institutions are often shaped by local contexts and the actions of those within them (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the local and contextual factors that influence institutional development and their impact on economic growth. Moreover, institutions may not always be adopted for their growth-promoting potential, but rather for their ability to maintain existing power structures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is exemplified by the shared finding that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists, rather than to maximize long-run growth, were prevalent in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The complexity of institutional influence on economic growth is further illustrated by the fact that institutions can be shaped by indigenous economic and political action, indicating that local contexts matter (Austin 2008, p.15). This is supported by the shared finding that European settlers were not always present to compete for African labour, and that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies highlights that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the importance of considering the local and contextual factors that influence institutional development and their impact on economic growth.

The historical evidence of institutional changes in former colonies, and the empirical comparison of income per capita between colonies with different institutional frameworks, settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
