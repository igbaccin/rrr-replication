The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal constraints (laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms, conventions), are central to this debate. At the heart of the disagreement lies the question of whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts, and whether their impact on economic growth is mediated through these factors. On one side, some argue that institutions evolve to maximize efficiency and economic growth, while others contend that they are often the result of power struggles and distributional conflicts, which can hinder growth. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding historical economic trajectories and for designing effective policies to promote economic development. The stakes are high because the answer determines whether economic growth can be systematically engineered through institutional design or whether it is more contingent on historical and political factors. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus intrinsically linked to the broader debate on the determinants of economic development and the potential for policy intervention to foster growth.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional change was pivotal in enhancing the security of property rights, which is crucial for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional evolution reflects a broader pattern where political stability and secure property rights are interconnected with economic growth. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England facilitated market exchanges and ensured that the costs per transaction were falling, even as the transactions sector grew as a proportion of aggregate GNP. This dynamic highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The broader implication that institutional changes can drive economic growth is qualified by other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, which may also influence economic performance. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV, for instance, was not a significant hindrance to economic growth in France, suggesting that institutional changes are necessary but not sufficient conditions for economic development.

The impact of these institutions varied widely, with some colonies experiencing significant economic growth and others stagnating. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European settlers established institutions that varied widely depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth. This institutional design had profound implications for the economic development of these regions, as extractive institutions hindered investment and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) further illustrate that the persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era significantly affected current income per capita. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. However, the role of institutions is not isolated from other factors; the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights that while institutions are crucial, they operate within a broader context of economic and historical factors.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The thesis, which posits that colonial institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic outcomes, does not account for the diversity of historical experiences and the dynamic nature of institutional change. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over extended periods. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight that the legacy of the slave trade, including the development of a culture of mistrust, has significant implications for economic development. This culture of mistrust, which persists to this day, can be traced to the historical context of the slave trade and its impact on social norms and economic behavior. The colonial experience was not uniform, and institutions evolved in response to local conditions and external pressures. For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, as seen in the case of Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior suggests that institutions respond to changing circumstances, which in turn influence long-run economic outcomes. The adaptive behavior of colonial administrations highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and its interaction with local conditions. This dynamic interaction challenges the notion that institutions are the sole determinant of economic growth, as it underscores the importance of considering the broader historical and economic context in which institutions operate. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as the external channel, suggest that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape economic development in Africa. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, one that takes into account the complex interplay between historical experiences, institutional evolution, and economic outcomes.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) asserts that the quality of public goods is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, suggesting that effective governance can transcend societal divisions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct real wage levels in British African colonies to challenge the notion of persistent poverty, arguing that historical and geographical conditions may have had temporary effects. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who notes that the new economic history of Africa has gained attention by highlighting the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment. This broader view suggests that while institutions are crucial, they are part of a complex web of factors influencing economic development, including historical experiences and contemporary policy frameworks.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) focus on the economic performance and divergence between Europe and Africa, shedding light on historical patterns of growth and income levels. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further note that growth in export production varied between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) suggest that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe may have arisen through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This indicates that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. This pattern suggests that the economic performance and divergence between Europe and Africa can be understood through the lens of historical patterns of growth and income levels. The role of merchant distribution and finance in economic growth during the early modern period highlights the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms that drove economic development in different regions.

Long-term economic changes in Europe, such as shifts in urbanization rates and economic development, are marked by a lack of clear correlation between these shifts and changes in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). These structural changes included shifts in urbanization rates, which are a key indicator for levels of productivity and economic development in the pre-modern period. This suggests that the economic development of Europe during this period was characterized by complex and varied patterns of growth and decline, which were not uniformly experienced across the continent. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) notes that, after 1740, England became the most expensive country in terms of grain prices, indicating a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. This price level rise suggests that economic development in England led to increased demand for basic foodstuffs, which in turn drove up prices. This pattern of economic development and price level changes highlights the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms that drove economic development in different regions of Europe during this period.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that gender-based access to resources significantly impacts economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This pattern was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that further marginalized women's access to land. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that the demand for missionary education in Africa was influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were closely tied to economic opportunities. Frankema (2012, p.1) further argues that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This educational development, in turn, had downstream implications for economic growth, as it empowered women and men to engage more productively in the economy. The codification of customary laws under colonial rule often led to the perpetuation of patriarchal structures, which limited women's economic participation. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between gender, education, and economic outcomes in Africa, where institutional changes under colonial rule had varying impacts on different segments of the population.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The process of technological progress is not uniform; it requires institutions to facilitate the diffusion of innovations and resolve conflicts that arise from growth-induced changes. This is because technological progress requires institutional adjustments that are compatible with the social fabric of society. This is because institutions facilitate the diffusion of technological innovations and reduce access costs to useful knowledge. This complexity is further highlighted by the fact that technological progress can sometimes outpace institutional adjustments, leading to coordination failures and rent-seeking behavior that can erode economic gains. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating technological progress and sustaining long-run economic growth, even as the primary driver of growth remains the accumulation of useful knowledge.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, better explain core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They propose that pre-industrial Europe's institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by power struggles, not efficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns. The institutions that emerged from these conflicts often favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth and political power led to institutional structures that advantaged elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies, in turn, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Ogilvie argues that institutions are deeply rooted in the social and political contexts that shape them, not merely the result of efficiency-driven processes. This view underscores the importance of understanding the historical and social factors that influence institutional development, as these factors can have lasting impacts on economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally rooted in the security of property rights and the efficiency of transactions they facilitate. North argues that institutions, by defining and enforcing property rights, reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes. Moreover, the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, highlights how secure property rights and efficient transactions can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The historical context of institutional development further illustrates their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced economic development paths. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often shaped core economic institutions, not efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Austin, who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). These insights suggest that the social and political contexts in which institutions emerge are crucial in determining their impact on economic growth.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts and the influence of colonial policies. Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the colonial context, where European settlers introduced institutions that maximized rents for colonizers, not promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin's analysis of colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous commodity production and changed institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives further highlights the complexity of institutional development and its economic impacts (Austin 2008, p.15). These findings demonstrate that institutions are deeply rooted in social and political contexts that shape their development and impact on economic growth, not merely efficiency-driven.
