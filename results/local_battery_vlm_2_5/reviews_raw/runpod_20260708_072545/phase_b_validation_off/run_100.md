The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role. Institutions, in this context, refer to the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and political interactions. The stakes are high: if institutions are indeed the key, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could be transformative. If other factors exert greater influence, institutional reforms may be misguided or insufficient. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) both engage with the question of institutional drivers of economic growth, but they differ in their approaches to the topic. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while Acemoglu et al. focus on how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. Both streams of thought, however, acknowledge the importance of institutions in economic development, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of how these institutions shape long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) focus on how specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, reduced transaction costs and thereby facilitated economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly lowered the cost of transacting, which was pivotal for economic growth. This institution facilitated the development of long-term capital markets and enhanced the security of property rights, which are crucial for sustained economic development. North (1989, p.9) argues that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which in turn led to the creation of institutions that specified and enforced efficient property rights. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that as political institutions evolve to specify and enforce efficient property rights, the costs per transaction fall, and the volume of exchanges increases, reflecting a growing proportion of GNP. This dynamic highlights how institutional developments can lower transaction costs and foster economic growth by enhancing the efficiency and security of property rights. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that the degree to which a regime is committed to these rules is a critical political factor, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on the credibility of the governing regime's commitment to them.

This perspective shifts the focus from the efficiency of institutions to the political dynamics that shape their design and effectiveness. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are more critical than efficiency in shaping institutions. They show that the power dynamics between elites and other groups drove institutional change in ways that did not necessarily maximize economic efficiency. This focus on distributional conflicts highlights the importance of political and social dynamics in shaping institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this analysis to the New World, showing how initial inequalities in factor endowments led to persistent institutional differences that favored elites. These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The role of distributional conflicts extends beyond pre-industrial or colonial contexts. The influence of these conflicts on institutional design and economic outcomes is a recurring theme in economic history, highlighting the need to consider the political and social contexts in which institutions operate.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita in the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth. This institutional divergence was not merely a function of geography or initial conditions, but a direct result of colonial policies that maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-run development trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions is not solely attributable to their initial design. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they are shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo.

The literature on colonial rule in Africa highlights how institutional changes were influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that colonial rule in Africa had a complex impact on economic and educational institutions, with African agency playing a significant role in shaping these outcomes. This suggests that African initiatives were crucial in shaping the educational landscape during colonial rule. The economic impacts of colonial rule varied across Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the importance of considering the diverse experiences of African colonies under colonial rule. Despite these variations, the shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) is that the impact of colonial rule on African institutions was multifaceted, influenced by both African agency and exogenous factors.

This multifaceted impact suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is influenced by a variety of factors. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) share the posture that innovation and technological progress are pivotal for economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that the establishment of institutions to reduce access costs to knowledge is a key mechanism that complements technological progress. This reduction in access costs allows for the widespread dissemination and application of new knowledge, thereby fostering economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) qualifies this by noting that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary to efficiently use innovations. Without these adjustments, the benefits of technological progress can be limited or even eliminated by rent-seeking behaviors. The implication is that while the growth of useful knowledge is essential, it must be accompanied by institutional changes that facilitate its effective use. This underscores the importance of a coordinated approach to technological and institutional development in achieving long-run economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mechanism operates through both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust. The internal norms of mistrust, in particular, are at least twice as large as the external factors, suggesting a deeply ingrained cultural impact. This finding is significant because it highlights the enduring effects of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) adds nuance by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which could mitigate some of the detrimental effects of mistrust. The persistent mistrust rooted in the slave trade creates a challenging environment for building the trusting relationships necessary for economic growth. This interplay between historical mistrust and contemporary economic development underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic advancement (North 1989, p.9). The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further complicated by the influence of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize their own interests over long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The design of institutions to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than to promote long-run growth, illustrates how institutional frameworks can be extractive and hinder economic development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) highlights the importance of understanding the motivations behind institutional design and the potential for institutions to be shaped by distributional conflicts.
