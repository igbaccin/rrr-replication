The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. At stake is the understanding of how economic growth is sustained over extended periods, and the mechanisms that facilitate or hinder this process. The debate centers around the causal impact of institutions on economic growth, with some scholars arguing that institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic development, while others propose that institutions are shaped by broader historical, geographical, and cultural factors. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the cause or the consequence of economic growth, and whether their impact is direct or mediated through other variables. This debate is crucial for informing policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth in developing countries. The debate is also important for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development in different regions of the world.

North argues that the emergence of new financial institutions in 17th-century England, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This reduction in transaction costs was crucial for fostering long-run economic growth, as it facilitated market exchanges and supported the development of a long-term capital market. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by political factors, notably the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights and maintaining secure markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of these institutions varied across Europe. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that France, despite having a sophisticated bureaucracy, did not experience the same level of economic growth as England, suggesting that the specific institutional arrangements in England were particularly effective in promoting growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This underscores the significance of both the presence and the effectiveness of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated, such as the development of new financial instruments, played a critical role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic efficiency. The effectiveness of these institutions in England underscores the broader historical significance of institutional development in driving economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional frameworks established by European powers in their colonies varied widely, ranging from settler colonies with strong property rights and rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional development was driven by differences in European mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies. These institutions were not just passive reflections of geographic factors; they actively shaped economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and productivity growth. The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended highlights their enduring impact on economic development. This institutional design had lasting effects on economic performance, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. European colonizers' policies were influenced by population density and prosperity, which in turn shaped the institutions they imposed (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This underscores the critical role of institutional quality in determining economic trajectories, as institutions that encourage investment and productivity growth foster sustained economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Broadberry and Gupta, examine specific historical economic performances, such as the per capita income levels in Anglophone African economies and the divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). These studies underscore the importance of understanding historical economic data to contextualize modern economic development. This data reveals that growth patterns in these economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta note that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers yielding a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that institutional changes, such as those in agriculture, were key drivers of economic development in both Europe and Asia. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by Broadberry and Gupta's assertion that institutional change brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This highlights the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth by encouraging investment and productivity.

The literature on economic performance and standard of living in Europe focuses on factors such as wage data, economic development patterns, and collaborative research methods (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.1; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The skill premium, which compensates for the costs of training and other expenses related to the training period, is influenced by institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades. van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that in Western Europe, guilds emerged as formal institutions that managed the intergenerational transfer of skills, though they were also criticized for monopolizing these skills and restricting entry to maintain high wages. This institutional framework suggests that the presence of guilds, which regulated apprenticeship contracts and ensured fair execution, played a critical role in determining the skill premium and, by extension, long-term economic performance. The beneficial effects of guilds in skill transfer were sometimes accompanied by their attempts to form local cartels, which could restrict economic growth. This implies that while institutions like guilds may have influenced the skill premium and economic performance, their impact on the standard of living was not straightforward. Moreover, the shift in economic development towards countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England, indicates that other factors beyond institutional frameworks also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and that patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced gender inequalities. Customary law, codified under colonial rule, often marginalized women's access to land, particularly for cash crops, which in turn limited their economic opportunities. This marginalization was exacerbated by the central role men played in shaping these laws. This dynamic was not unique to Africa; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) illustrate that in the New World, societies with greater inequality also saw elites advantage themselves through government policies. These policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, highlighting how institutional frameworks can both reflect and reinforce existing social hierarchies. However, the persistence of inequality is not solely a function of institutions; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that differences in factor endowments, such as the abundance of land relative to labor, also played a critical role in shaping the economic trajectories of these societies. The implication is that while institutions are crucial, they operate within a broader context of economic and social factors that can either amplify or mitigate their effects. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, influenced by both the legal frameworks in place and the broader economic conditions under which they operate.

Institutional reforms are pivotal for poverty reduction in Africa, as argued by Hopkins (2009, p.18); (Simson 2019, p.2). A core mechanism through which institutions shape economic growth in Africa is by altering incentives for investment and development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that the implementation of new social and political institutions in Africa influenced the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn affected investment in human capital. This suggests that institutional changes can create a more conducive environment for investment, which is essential for long-term economic development. Simson (2019, p.11) qualifies this by noting that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were influenced by factors such as military spending and debt service payments, indicating that the effectiveness of institutional reforms can be constrained by broader economic and political conditions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development. This mistrust is a persistent legacy of the slave trade, rooted in the exploitation and betrayal that characterized the era. The slave trade's impact on trust operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. This pervasive mistrust hinders economic development by undermining social cohesion and cooperation, which are crucial for productive economic activity. Nunn underscores that the slave trade's adverse effects are not just historical artifacts but have lasting implications for contemporary economic development. This indicates that the impact of the slave trade on economic development varies depending on pre-existing institutional and economic conditions.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are critical for harnessing technological progress and achieving sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from the integration of new technologies into existing economic systems. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also points out that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, ensuring that economic growth does not weaken the political fabric of society. This underscores the importance of a strong and adaptable institutional framework in supporting technological progress and sustaining economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) both argue that distributional conflicts are a more compelling explanation for the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency-driven factors. This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are adopted solely based on their growth-promoting potential. In this view, institutions often emerge as a result of power dynamics and the need to manage conflicts among different social groups. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this by showing how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that institutions evolve in response to changing incentives and power structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further emphasizes that institutions are indispensable tools for economic historians, suggesting that a deeper understanding of institutional dynamics can reveal how power and distributional conflicts shape economic outcomes. This nuanced view highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need for a more contextualized understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

This wage gap is the result of institutional flexibility in northwestern Europe, which enabled the region to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. This flexibility allowed new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies. The political authorities in Tuscany worked to strengthen the powers of existing monopolies. This dynamic reflects a broader pattern of economic divergence between regions with different institutional structures. The wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 highlights the significant role of institutional flexibility in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the shared finding that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is reinforced by the observation that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes remain a subject of debate. For instance, some argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This debate highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical and institutional contexts that shape economic growth. The case of the Bank of England and the financial revolution in England illustrates how institutions can reduce transaction costs and facilitate economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, the experience of Africa shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence of institutions facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs provides a clear pathway to understanding the role of institutions in economic growth.
