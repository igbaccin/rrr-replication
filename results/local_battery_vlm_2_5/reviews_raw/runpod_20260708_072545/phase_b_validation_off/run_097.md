The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate. A central disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for fostering economic growth. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that factors like initial endowments or historical trajectories are more determinative. Additionally, there is debate over the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some focusing on the role of colonial institutions and others on the endogenous development of institutions. The interplay between these factors and the causal pathways they create is complex and multifaceted, yet essential for comprehending the dynamics of long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) both emphasize the critical role of institutions in economic growth, but they do so from different angles. Acemoglu et al. focus on the impact of colonial institutions, arguing that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. They highlight that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth. North, on the other hand, underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are fundamental for economic development. North's work on the English financial revolution illustrates how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, fostering economic growth. Both scholars agree that institutions matter, but they differ in their emphasis on the specific types of institutions and the historical contexts in which they operate. This divergence highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutional frameworks can shape economic outcomes over the long run.

North's study of the English financial revolution demonstrates that the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This decline in transaction costs is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. However, North and Weingast note that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets-not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. The implications of these institutional changes are profound, as they not only facilitated economic growth but also shaped the political landscape of England.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show. The nature of these institutions was determined by the European settlers' mortality rates in the colonies, which in turn dictated the type of institutions they established. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, has had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these former colonies. The economic outcomes in these colonies were not merely a result of geographic factors but were significantly shaped by the institutional legacies of colonial rule. These institutions, designed to extract resources, hindered economic development and perpetuated poverty in many former colonies. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even in the face of potential economic gains from institutional change, highlights the entrenched nature of colonial legacies. This is because politically powerful groups may resist institutional changes that could undermine their political power or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Ogilvie maintains that distributional conflicts, more than efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This is shared by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). These authors demonstrate how economic institutions, such as serfdom, the community, and guilds, were shaped by the political and economic interests of powerful groups. This pattern is evident in the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women in accessing land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This codification was a key mechanism through which patriarchal alliances were maintained, ensuring that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The implications of these distributional conflicts extend beyond pre-industrial Europe, as seen in the colonial era where customary law was used to codify and reinforce the subordinate status of women. This highlights the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share a finding that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a unique impact on educational development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn 2008, p.2). The demand for missionary education in Africa was shaped by new institutions implemented by the British, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, who saw education as a means to navigate the changing social and political landscape. However, the legacy of British rule was not uniformly positive; Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades, which predated colonial rule, had a lasting adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical context suggests that while British institutions may have fostered educational development, they did so within a broader framework of economic and social constraints that limited overall growth. The long-term implications of this institutional legacy are complex, as the educational gains under British rule must be weighed against the enduring economic impacts of the slave trades.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that institutions determine long-term economic growth, oversimplifies causation by overlooking the complexity of historical paths and institutional evolution. This limitation is partly due to the challenge of quantitatively testing institutional theories and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on indigenous economic activities and political dynamics, rather than adhering to a fixed set of institutional rules (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this by highlighting that the quality of institutions in Africa is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but also by the capacity of societies to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective suggests that institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa must consider the local context and adapt to the unique historical and political dynamics of each region. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) also acknowledges that the new economic history of Africa has gained prominence due to its focus on institutions, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The implication is that while institutional reforms are necessary, they must be tailored to the specific needs and contexts of African countries to be effective.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The role of technological progress in economic growth is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where technological changes necessitated significant adjustments in institutions and ideologies. Kuznets highlights that technological progress, particularly epochal innovations, has been a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods, spreading across substantial parts of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process is not merely about the introduction of new technologies but also involves the institutional and ideological adaptations required to efficiently utilize these innovations. Mokyr supports this view, emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Mokyr also notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the institutional frameworks that support these advancements are subject to fluctuations and may require continuous adaptation. Kuznets adds that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community, plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights the importance of stable political institutions in supporting technological progress and economic growth. The implication is that while technological advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth, the role of institutions in providing the necessary framework for these advancements cannot be overlooked.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutions in different colonies. In some cases, settlers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States. In others, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights how the nature of colonial rule directly influenced the economic trajectories of these regions. Austin agrees with this, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work underscores that the institutional legacies of colonialism are complex and multifaceted, affecting economic development in nuanced ways. Austin's perspective aligns with the argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6) demonstrates that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have had lasting impacts on economic growth, shaping the development paths of former colonies.

The importance of stable political institutions is further emphasized by North, who argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change facilitated economic growth by providing a secure framework for market exchanges. Ogilvie extends this argument by noting that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the role of institutions in managing conflicts and ensuring economic stability. Austin agrees, noting that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous economic and political action, thereby influencing institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.15) highlights the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic growth and managing conflicts that arise during development. In particular, the role of institutions in managing conflicts is crucial for ensuring economic stability and facilitating growth. This is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where the nature of colonial rule directly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies.

Institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic development in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.18) demonstrates that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic development in former colonies, highlighting the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement is that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This is settled by the measurement of income per capita across former colonies, showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting impacts on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
