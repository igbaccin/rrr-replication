The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. Institutions are the game itself, shaping economic outcomes through incentives and constraints, and thus are central to growth. The key disagreement is whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position is that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial for economic growth. Another position argues that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to other factors, such as the distribution of power and the resolution of conflicts. A third position suggests that institutions are shaped by historical and cultural contexts, which in turn influence economic growth. This debate is crucial because it determines whether policy interventions should focus on institutional reforms or address other underlying factors. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions that affect billions of people. The central question is: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth?

This foundational role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights is further highlighted by the work of North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29). The development of these institutions reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both capital markets and economic growth. These institutions facilitate market exchanges, leading to a growing proportion of transactions in the economy, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The emergence of these institutions involves the rules governing economic exchange and the mechanisms enforcing and changing these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the political commitment to these rules is a critical factor, underscoring the role of credible commitment in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic growth is complex. The historical evidence from capital markets suggests that institutions alone may not be the sole determinant of economic performance. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's, despite differences in their institutional frameworks. This implies that while institutions are significant, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political stability also play a role in economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that a multifaceted approach is necessary for a comprehensive understanding.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) extend this idea by showing that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in colonies with lower mortality rates, institutions were set up to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks resulted in stark differences in economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time has had profound implications for the economic development of former colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, a finding that underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This suggests that while colonial institutions were pivotal, their impact was mediated by other factors such as industrialization opportunities, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth. This thesis, shared with Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18), highlights the complex interplay between colonial rule and indigenous initiatives in shaping African economic outcomes. Colonial administrations often tailored their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which challenges the notion that colonial institutions uniformly hindered economic growth. For instance, in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was contingent on local conditions and indigenous agency. The role of institutions in economic development goes beyond colonial legacies. Hopkins underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective underscores that while colonial institutions played a significant role, the evolution of African economies was also influenced by broader institutional frameworks that emerged post-colonially.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economic development is better understood by examining real wages and export markets, which show periods of growth and divergence. The divergence of wages across British Africa was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This real wage divergence is a key indicator of economic development, as it reflects changes in living standards and economic conditions. The growth of exports, tied closely to foreign trade, varied significantly both between and within countries. This variation was driven by factors such as local resource endowments, transport infrastructure, and the institutional framework governing economic activity (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This data reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more complex economic landscape than previously acknowledged. The availability of this data has also facilitated comparative historical work on economic growth and development in Africa. The structural effects of these historical conditions on long-term African welfare development remain a subject of ongoing debate.

The early modern period saw significant economic divergence between Europe and Asia, with the roots of this divergence already evident before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that this divergence was driven by differences in productivity and economic development, with merchant distribution and finance playing crucial roles in Europe's growth. This productivity was due to agricultural advances and the service sector's contributions, which facilitated the distribution and financing of goods. The uneven spread of these developments meant that skilled laborers benefited more than unskilled ones, highlighting the unequal distribution of economic gains. This pattern of economic divergence was further complicated by geographical factors, such as climate, which influenced the need for shelter, fuel, and clothing. Parthasarathi's claim that high productivity in southern Indian agriculture resulted from high investment levels rather than geographical factors raises logical difficulties, as institutional changes typically drive investment in agriculture, leading to high productivity. van Zanden (1999, p.9) shows that economic development in Europe led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries after 1740. This shift in economic development to the North Sea countries, particularly Holland and England, underscores the complex interplay between economic growth and changes in the standard of living.

Distributional conflicts are argued to better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective suggests that economic institutions are shaped more by the struggle over resources and power than by a desire for efficient outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further support this by noting that the institutions in New World societies were shaped by the initial conditions of inequality, which were maintained through policies that favored elites. This dynamic led to institutions that restricted competition and reinforced inequality. The persistence of these institutions, even in the face of potential efficiency gains, underscores the primacy of distributional conflicts in institutional development. The role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions is significant.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, posit that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels, offering an alternative explanation for Africa's economic underdevelopment. The slave trade's impact on trust is multifaceted, with both internal and external channels playing crucial roles. The internal channel, which involves individuals' norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internalization of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in various economic activities, including trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The adverse effects of the slave trade on African economic development extend to broader economic institutions. The slave trade disrupted local economies and social structures, hindering the development of sustainable economic institutions. This disruption was particularly pronounced in regions with high levels of slave exports, where the institutional framework was weakened, and economic growth was stifled. For instance, the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans, highlighting the importance of institutional strength in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of the slave trade continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as the mistrust and weak institutions resulting from the slave trade persist and impede economic growth. This enduring impact underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of the historical factors shaping current economic conditions.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for harnessing economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. The rise of new technologies and the diffusion of innovations necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate efficient use and integration. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that technological progress requires significant institutional changes to resolve conflicts and maintain social cohesion, as the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that coordination failures can hinder economic improvement, necessitating institutional progress to sustain technological gains. The growth of economic reasonableness, however, is not as linear or irreversible as technological progress, suggesting that institutional adjustments must be continuously adapted to maintain economic growth. These findings highlight that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the effectiveness of these advancements depends on the ability of institutions to adapt and support new technologies. These insights underscore the importance of institutional adaptability in fostering long-term economic growth, as technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional developments.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of institutional frameworks. Institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, are crucial for economic growth. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists but also created an environment conducive to economic development.

The persistence of institutions over time is another critical factor. Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes in these regions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic growth. Hopkins further supports this by noting that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shows that understanding the historical context of institutions is essential for designing effective policies to promote economic growth.

The adaptability of institutions to new technologies and economic changes is also crucial. North argues that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GDP (North 1989, p.6). This highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their ability to adapt to changing economic conditions. Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions, arguing that these conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective adds another layer to the understanding of how institutions evolve and influence economic growth.
