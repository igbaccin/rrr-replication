The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, with substantial intellectual stakes. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One camp emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic growth, arguing that institutions shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. This perspective posits that institutions, such as property rights and rule of law, are critical for fostering investment, innovation, and overall economic development. Another camp highlights the indirect effects of institutions, suggesting that they operate through mediating factors such as human capital, technology, and political stability. This view contends that institutions create an enabling environment that facilitates the accumulation of these factors, which in turn drive economic growth. A third camp focuses on the historical and contextual factors that shape institutions, arguing that the evolution of institutions is influenced by a complex interplay of cultural, political, and economic forces. This perspective emphasizes the importance of understanding the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate. The debate centers on whether institutions directly cause economic growth, indirectly influence it through other factors, or are themselves shaped by broader historical and cultural contexts. The stakes are high, as resolving this debate could provide insights into how to design effective policies for promoting economic development. This understanding is crucial for policymakers seeking to foster sustainable economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue.

North and Weingast, along with other scholars, assert that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This development was critical in reducing the costs associated with market exchanges, facilitating economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement led to a decline in transaction costs per exchange, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GDP (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs enabled a more efficient allocation of resources, which is a key driver of economic growth. The role of institutions in reducing transaction costs extends beyond financial institutions. North and Weingast emphasize the importance of political institutions that govern the enforcement of property rights and the rules of economic exchange, highlighting that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpins the stability and predictability of the economic environment, which in turn fosters long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) document that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The specific institutions established by European colonizers were shaped by mortality rates and the resulting settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth. This pattern highlights that the institutional legacy of colonialism is a critical factor in understanding current economic disparities among former colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions, which were not designed to encourage investment or enforce the rule of law, has had lasting effects on economic development. This distinction is crucial because it underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, even after the end of colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this point by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with current economic outcomes. This evidence suggests that the institutional framework established during colonialism continues to influence economic performance, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical context in which current economic policies are embedded.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the finding that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies. These institutions were not merely the result of efficiency considerations, but rather were driven by the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain their status and control resources. This institutional framework restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms through which these distributional conflicts operated varied, but the common thread was the influence of powerful groups in shaping institutions to their advantage. This distributional conflict model provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions can perpetuate inequality. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that the role of beliefs and culture in shaping institutions should be considered. Beliefs about political legitimacy, individual rights, and cultural values can influence the institutional framework, even if they are not the primary driving force behind distributional conflicts. This interplay between distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs adds complexity to the understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. This complexity underscores the need for a multifaceted approach to studying the role of institutions in economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth. This is because technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that the growth of modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural life patterns and veneration of undisturbed nature. This suggests that technological progress creates a need for institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the transition from traditional to modern economic systems. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be unsustainable. This implies that institutions play a role in sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. This suggests that while technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own. Institutions must adapt to technological changes to ensure that economic growth is sustained over the long run. Therefore, technological advancements and institutional adjustments are complementary processes that drive economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) share a finding that historical factors, particularly the legacy of colonialism and the slave trade, have profoundly shaped economic development in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These historical legacies have left deep imprints on African societies, influencing contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial governments' institutional prescriptions were often responsive to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that local agency played a role in shaping colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of mistrust, rooted in the historical context of the slave trade, continues to hinder economic development. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has been shown to reduce trust within African societies, a critical component for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust can impede economic cooperation, investment, and overall development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the notion that poverty in Africa is a persistent historical phenomenon (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They argue that pre-1950 poverty in Africa was not as pervasive as often portrayed, with some regions achieving significant economic growth. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic growth was closely tied to foreign trade and the export market, with local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions playing crucial roles in shaping these patterns (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left many questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. To address this gap, Frankema and van Waijenburg reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a more nuanced understanding of economic development in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This research highlights the importance of historical data in assessing long-term economic trends and the potential for future growth in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the economic environment and the incentive structures that govern economic activities. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes over the long term. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This shows that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their research provides a striking indication of this, showing that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This historical evidence from England demonstrates that the institutional changes were revolutionary and played a critical role in economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in economic growth, taking into account the complex interactions between institutions and other factors. Austin also points out that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, and tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutions evolve in response to changing economic and political conditions. Furthermore, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is determined by efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts that shape institutional outcomes.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate. While there is agreement that institutions matter, the specific mechanisms by which they influence economic outcomes are still being debated. Ogilvie shows that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that these beliefs can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
