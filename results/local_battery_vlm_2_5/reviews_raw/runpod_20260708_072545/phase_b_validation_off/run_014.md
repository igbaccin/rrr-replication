The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies develop and sustain economic prosperity. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure social interactions, drive economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in fostering growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity. Another position argues that institutions are merely one factor among many, with other variables such as geography, culture, or technological innovation playing equally significant roles. The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of economic development and the need to understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Institutions evolve over time, shaped by political, social, and economic forces. The evolution of institutions and their impact on economic growth is a dynamic process that requires a nuanced understanding of historical contexts and the interplay of various factors. The historical record of colonialism provides a rich context for examining how institutions shape economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. These frameworks ranged from settler colonies with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This perspective highlights how initial institutional setups, influenced by factors like mortality rates, can have long-lasting effects on economic development. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrate the enduring impact of historical institutional choices. North and Weingast also emphasize the critical role of institutions in economic growth, focusing on the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. They argue that the development of secure property rights and the enforcement of these rights were crucial for fostering economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which is essential for long-term economic growth. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in providing a stable framework for economic activity, which in turn fosters growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of colonialism on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes. Hopkins argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the presence of progressive institutions. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. By reducing transaction costs, financial institutions like the Bank of England played a crucial role in facilitating market exchanges, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This reduction in transaction costs is closely linked to the increased security of property rights, which is essential for a functioning capital market and sustained economic growth. This institutional development was a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, and involved trading certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North and Weingast further elucidate that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for creating secure property rights and economic growth. These institutions governed economic exchanges and enforced rules, determining how they could be changed. A critical factor in this process was the regime's commitment to these rules, which underpinned the success of economic growth and market development. The creation of these institutions involved ensuring that these rules were enforced and could be adapted over time. This institutional framework was essential for the long-term stability and growth of the economy. North (1989, p.9) adds that as economies grow, the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement leads to a growing proportion of transactions in the economy. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are fundamental for economic development.

Building on this, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutional changes introduced during colonialism had lasting effects on economic development. They identify two distinct colonization strategies: settler colonies, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, and extractive colonies, where institutions were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This dichotomy in institutional frameworks had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that in settler colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, the establishment of robust institutions fostered economic growth. These institutions, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, reduced transaction costs and facilitated market exchanges, aligning with North's emphasis on the role of institutions in economic development. Extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on this point, noting that these extractive institutions were equilibrium institutions shaped by politically powerful groups, which did not encourage economic development. This institutional legacy persists, influencing current economic outcomes. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is further qualified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutions on long-run economic development, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies in profound ways.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the stance that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This highlights the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments in driving economic growth. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, ensuring that economic growth is sustained over the long run. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, it must be supported by institutional progress to ensure long-term sustainability. This highlights the importance of both technological and institutional progress in driving long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is a deeply ingrained internal norm. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, indicating that the legacy of the slave trade has shaped African societies' internal norms in a way that hinders trust. This internal mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activity. Nunn and Wantchekon qualify this by noting that while the slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, it is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic underdevelopment. This nuanced perspective underscores the complex interplay between historical factors and contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that while the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is a critical factor, it operates in conjunction with other institutional and economic variables to shape long-run economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa, by highlighting the role of institutions, has gained the attention of development specialists. Hopkins (2009, p.18) explains that good institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, and the persistence of poverty in Africa remains a contentious issue. This underscores the need for further research into the specific mechanisms through which institutional reforms can drive long-run economic growth in Africa.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. She repudiates the game-theoretic view that beliefs are institutions, instead arguing that shared beliefs are part of the institutional structure of a society. These distributional conflicts influenced the development of institutions that favored certain groups over others, leading to institutional arrangements that reflected power dynamics. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) argue that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The specific focus on how different environments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality highlights the role of factor endowments in shaping institutional development. This perspective suggests that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset influenced the institutions that evolved, perpetuating inequality over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need for a nuanced approach to understanding institutions, advocating for their transformation into an indispensable tool for economic historians. This nuanced approach is crucial for understanding how institutions shaped long-run economic growth, as it acknowledges the complex interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional development, and economic outcomes.

Understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires examining how different institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes. Institutions, as defined by North (1989, p.9), are the rules of the game in a society, and they shape economic performance by providing a structure to human interaction (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonialism significantly affected the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive institutions fostering it (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 exemplify how institutional changes can lead to economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The presence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), are critical for fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in the success of capital markets, where credible commitments underpin growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further emphasizes the role of institutions in economic development, noting that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The evidence from Africa supports the broader claim that institutions matter for economic growth, as they provide the necessary framework for investment and development.

The shared finding that institutions matter for economic growth is grounded in the historical evidence of colonial institutions and their long-run effects (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of distributional conflicts versus efficiency in shaping institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes distributional conflicts as the primary driver, while North (1989, p.9) focuses on the role of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth, settles this disagreement by demonstrating the critical role of institutional changes in reducing transaction costs and providing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9).
