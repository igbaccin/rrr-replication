The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and they shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives and constraints that individuals and firms face. The live disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions that foster growth and the mechanisms by which they operate. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce secure property rights and the rule of law are crucial for economic growth. Others contend that the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve are equally important. Additionally, there are debates about whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous factors such as political struggles. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting economic development. One key question is whether the institutions that promote growth are those that maximize long-term economic benefits or those that serve the interests of politically powerful groups. The answer to this question has significant implications for how policymakers should approach institutional reform.

The specific institutional changes in 17th-century England, highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), were not isolated events but part of a broader pattern of institutional evolution that significantly influenced long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, such as financial contracts, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This decline facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by increasing the security of property rights. These institutions, in turn, fostered secure property rights by ensuring that the regime was committed to the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was crucial for the development of capital markets, which North (1989, p.9) identifies as a critical component of economic growth. The reduction in transaction costs and the development of capital markets had a significant impact on economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction. The aggregate volume of exchanges increased as specialization and division of labor multiplied. This pattern suggests that the institutional changes in 17th-century England were pivotal in fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and establishing secure property rights.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European settler colonies, with their extractive institutions, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies was driven by European policies aimed at rapid resource extraction. This institutional framework, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not foster long-run economic development. The divergence in economic outcomes among former colonies can be attributed to the differing institutional frameworks established during colonial rule. European policies shaped these institutions to extract resources efficiently. This pattern suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies hindered economic development, as these institutions were not conducive to long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. showed that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which had lasting effects on economic development.

African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, as Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue. This perspective challenges the notion that African economies were stagnant and underdeveloped throughout their history, instead highlighting periods of growth. The economic development in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century was driven by foreign trade and the market for exports, which were influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This growth was not uniform across all regions or countries, but it did show that African economies were capable of development under certain conditions. However, the timing and scale of this growth varied significantly, indicating that the institutional framework and external factors played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as the institutional framework can either facilitate or hinder economic development.

The slave trades in Africa significantly impeded economic development, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) demonstrate. These trades had lasting consequences on African societies. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly five centuries, was considerably longer than the period of official colonial rule. This prolonged exposure to the slave trades disrupted African societies' economic and social institutions, leaving a lasting imprint on their development trajectories. This mistrust is deeply ingrained within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the importance of trust for economic development, political institutions, and international trade, underscoring the far-reaching implications of the slave trades' impact on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered Africa's economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for economic growth. The slave trades' impact on African economic development was a consequence of the long-term effects on social institutions and cultural norms.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both posit that technological advancements, specifically the growth of useful knowledge, drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that economic growth is fundamentally propelled by innovations that require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The implications of this view are profound, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, contrasting with the view that institutions are the fundamental cause. This perspective is further supported by Kuznets' (1973) observation that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to resolve growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) underscores this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the specific institutional and ideological frameworks that support them must be tailored to the unique historical and regional contexts in which they occur.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These scholars contend that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography, which emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors. Austin's work highlights the limitations of this approach in quantitatively testing institutional theories and explaining why some countries adopted institutions favorable for economic growth while others did not. This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve in response to local economic and political dynamics. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by economic efficiency. Ogilvie further suggests that the shared beliefs and cultural values, often cited as foundational for economic institutions, may not be as exogenous as previously thought. This implies that a more comprehensive understanding of institutional evolution requires not only recognizing the role of distributional conflicts but also acknowledging the complex interplay of cultural, economic, and political factors.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment and innovation. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6) by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges. This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who emphasize the importance of credible commitments in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis of seventeenth-century England shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and supporting economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework allowed for the emergence of a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of European colonization strategies on the economic outcomes of colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was robust. In colonies where extractive states were established, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, economic growth was stunted. This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic performance. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and economic dynamics that shape institutional development.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of cultural and political factors. Ogilvie argues that institutions should be considered indispensable tools for economic historians, emphasizing the need for a deeper integration of institutional analysis into long-term research programs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Austin, who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's analysis shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between cultural, economic, and political factors in shaping institutional development and economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous cultural factors or endogenous political and economic dynamics. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional frameworks across European colonies. This variation shows that institutions are shaped by both exogenous cultural factors and endogenous political and economic dynamics, with the latter playing a more significant role in determining long-run economic growth.
