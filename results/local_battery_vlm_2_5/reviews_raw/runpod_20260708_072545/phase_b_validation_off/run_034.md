The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue with significant implications for understanding historical development and contemporary economic policy. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play a more decisive role. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions matter in economic growth and development. Some argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while others contend that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions. Additionally, there is a debate over whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. These positions conflict over the mechanisms through which economic growth is achieved and the relative importance of institutions versus other factors. The significance of this debate lies in its potential to inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. As such, understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is essential for both historical analysis and contemporary economic policy.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North emphasizes that institutions can lower transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly affected their long-run economic development. They demonstrate that colonies with extractive institutions, where Europeans aimed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, experienced lower income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that the nature of institutions established during colonization has lasting effects on economic outcomes. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic growth, where the interests of powerful groups can influence the type of institutions established and their impact on economic development.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, challenging views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors. This institutional evolution, they contend, was driven by a credible commitment to secure property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the institutional changes in England, particularly the establishment of a credible commitment mechanism, were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. This mechanism ensured that political actors were bound by the rules governing economic exchange, thereby creating a stable environment conducive to long-term economic development. The credibility of these commitments was essential in reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment, as it provided a reliable framework within which economic actors could operate. This framework addresses the rules of economic exchange and the institutions that enforce and alter these rules. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that institutions should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programmes. This underscores the importance of understanding the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the evolution of these institutions can have profound implications for economic development. The impact of institutions on economic growth varies. The effectiveness of these institutions in fostering economic growth depends on the specific historical and political context in which they operate. For instance, the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were successful in fostering economic growth, but this may not be the case in other historical contexts where different political and economic factors are at play. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the impact of these institutions can vary significantly depending on the specific circumstances.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in Africa and the Americas by influencing the expansion of commodity production and the distribution of wealth and power. Colonial institutions in Africa often encouraged indigenous commodity production, as colonial governments sought fiscal security and responded to local economic and political dynamics. This institutional framework fostered economic activities that aligned with colonial interests, such as commodity production, which could generate revenue and stabilize colonial rule. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to adapt their policies in response to indigenous economic actions, opting for higher production equilibriums to maximize revenue. This dynamic illustrates how colonial institutions evolved in response to local conditions and the interests of colonial powers. The same pattern is visible in the New World, where colonial institutions reflected the interests of European settlers and local elites, shaping the distribution of wealth and political power. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that societies with greater inequality often had institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework had long-term implications for economic development, as it influenced the security of property rights, the prevalence of corruption, and the structures of the financial sector.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Frankema, examine the specific impacts of colonial rule on African development, focusing on the roles of women and the educational policies under British rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). The colonial impact on African development was multifaceted, with significant implications for gender roles and economic structures. This codification of customary law under colonial rule had profound effects on economic activities, as women were frequently relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed the cost-benefit calculations for African families and political leaders, making formal education more attractive. This shift in educational policies had long-term implications for African development, as it influenced the skills and knowledge base of the population. However, the benefits of formal education were not uniformly distributed, and the economic gains from education often accrued to those already in positions of power, reinforcing existing inequalities (Frankema 2012, p.15). Consequently, while British educational policies under colonial rule did contribute to the development of formal education, the overall impact on long-term economic growth was complex and contingent on the broader institutional framework.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) both highlight that economic reforms and policy impacts in Africa show how institutional changes can address poverty. The literature does not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth. The mechanism by which institutions shape economic outcomes is through their influence on incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Institutions in Africa often function according to a patrimonial logic, where government resources are treated as private property and used to buy loyalty rather than deliver broad-based public goods and services. This results in policy-making that focuses on consumption (Simson 2019, p.2; Simson 2019, p.11). The economic reforms in East Africa, influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments, illustrate how institutional changes can lead to significant shifts in expenditure patterns. For example, in Kenya, subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. This shift highlights how institutional changes can redirect resources away from productive investments, thereby affecting long-term economic growth. The impact of these institutional changes on long-run economic growth depends on the broader institutional framework and the specific context in which they occur.

The slave trade has left an enduring legacy of mistrust within African societies, which continues to influence contemporary economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust, which persists today and is an important factor in explaining the lower levels of trust in Africa. This mistrust, in turn, impedes economic development by hindering cooperation and undermining social capital. The mechanism through which the slave trade affects trust is multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and through individuals' internal norms of mistrust. The internal norms of mistrust are found to be at least twice as significant as the external environmental factors. Nunn further illustrates that the adverse effects of the slave trade extend beyond historical periods and continue to shape economic development in contemporary Africa. The slave trade's impact on economic development is more pronounced than that of colonialism, which lasted a relatively shorter period. The enduring effects of the slave trade on trust levels in Africa highlight the deep-seated nature of these historical legacies.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonial powers established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to systems that enforced the rule of law. These institutional differences had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are cited as examples of institutional changes that reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. The historical legacies of these institutions continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as seen in the enduring effects of the slave trade on trust levels in Africa.

The role of institutions in economic development is further underscored by the impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. These adaptations highlight the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic outcomes. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This dynamic is evident in the historical record, where institutional changes have been shown to have significant and lasting effects on economic development.

The institutional framework also plays a crucial role in shaping economic behavior and outcomes in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the craft guild. These institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which often prioritized their own rents over long-run economic growth. This perspective is consistent with the view that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to the adoption of extractive institutions that hinder economic development. The historical record shows that institutions that were designed to maximize the rents to powerful groups often had adverse effects on long-run economic growth. This is evident in the case of serfdom and the craft guild, which were shaped by the interests of powerful groups and had adverse effects on economic development.
