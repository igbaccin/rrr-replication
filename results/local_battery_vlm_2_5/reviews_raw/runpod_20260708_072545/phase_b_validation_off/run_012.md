The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding whether and how political, legal, and social frameworks drive prosperity over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. One camp posits that institutions facilitate economic growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. Another camp argues that institutions are shaped by political and economic power dynamics, and that these institutions can be extractive, hindering long-run growth. A third camp suggests that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs play a significant role in economic growth, with institutions being secondary drivers. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the cause or the consequence of economic development, and whether they primarily serve to facilitate growth or to entrench power. This question is crucial for policymakers seeking to understand the root causes of economic stagnation or growth in different regions. This review will explore the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms proposed by different scholars.

North's work, along with that of North and Weingast, focuses on specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, and their impact on transaction costs, rather than directly addressing how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They argue that the development of new financial instruments and the increased security of property rights led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast further elaborate on this by emphasizing the role of political factors in underpinning economic growth, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is crucial for creating a stable environment where economic institutions can thrive. However, while North and Weingast acknowledge the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering growth, they also note that the impact of these institutions can be nuanced. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, indicating that while institutions play a significant role, their impact is mediated by a variety of factors. This nuanced view aligns with the broader debate on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, also play a significant role.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional changes were driven by the differential mortality rates of Europeans in various colonies, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. This institutional framework was maintained by European colonial powers to ensure the rapid transfer of resources to the metropole, often at the expense of the colonies' economic development. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these extractive institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended, continuing to hinder economic growth in the affected regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This dynamic highlights the complexity of institutional change, where both historical legacies and contemporary political dynamics interact to shape economic outcomes.

This historical context is important because Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon by examining historical data and re-evaluating the impact of colonial policies. They argue that historical data can reveal the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that colonial institutions were not monolithic and that their impact varied significantly across different regions. This is because colonial governments adapted their policies in response to local economic and political dynamics. Austin (2008, p.15) adds that colonial administrations, particularly those seeking fiscal security, often modified their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. Colonial institutions were not always extractive; they could also foster economic development when aligned with local interests. This nuanced view suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was context-dependent and varied across different regions and historical periods. The persistence of extractive institutions is not a given; instead, it is shaped by the interplay between colonial policies and local economic and political dynamics.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the source of technological progress, the sectors it affects, and the pace of growth vary over time and region, necessitating different institutional and ideological adjustments. The growth of useful knowledge is about technological advancements and the institutional frameworks that support and disseminate these innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for long-term economic growth. This underscores the importance of ongoing institutional development in complementing technological advancements, ensuring that economic growth is not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors or institutional failures. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, requiring continuous adaptation and support for technological innovations.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These internal norms are at least twice as large as the external factors, highlighting the deep-seated nature of mistrust ingrained by the slave trade. This mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical component for economic transactions and growth. However, the extent to which this mistrust can be mitigated or overcome through institutional changes remains an open question, as the persistent nature of these internal norms suggests that addressing this issue may require more than just economic reforms.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts are central to shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These authors highlight that institutional structures are deeply influenced by the underlying power dynamics and wealth disparities within societies. For instance, Ogilvie critiques the efficiency-driven explanations of institutions, arguing that they overlook the distributional conflicts that arise from the unequal distribution of resources. She posits that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the struggles between different social groups to maintain or enhance their economic and political power. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. This focus on distributional conflicts reveals that institutional structures are not merely efficient responses to economic challenges but are fundamentally shaped by the power dynamics within societies. The persistence of inequality suggests that addressing these distributional conflicts requires more than just institutional reforms.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth through their impact on property rights, transaction costs, and the distribution of political power. North argues that institutions governing property rights and economic exchange are crucial for economic growth. He shows that the development of institutions that facilitate market exchanges and enforce property rights leads to falling transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were critical for economic growth, as they provided a framework that encouraged investment and facilitated market exchanges. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the type of institutions established during colonization has long-lasting effects on economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. He shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political dynamics.

The persistence of inequality suggests that addressing these distributional conflicts requires more than just institutional reforms. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. She shows that these institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This focus on distributional conflicts reveals that institutional structures are not merely efficient responses to economic challenges but are fundamentally shaped by the power dynamics within societies. The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic growth, as they shape property rights, transaction costs, and the distribution of political power (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The disagreement lies in whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence of colonial policies and the measurement of economic outcomes in former colonies show that institutions are fundamentally shaped by the power dynamics within societies.
