The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has long been a contentious issue among economists and economic historians. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development. One central debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, is crucial for long-term economic growth. These institutions create an environment where investment is encouraged, and economic activities can flourish. Some scholars argue that institutions are just one factor among many, and that geographic endowments or cultural beliefs may be more influential in shaping economic outcomes. This debate is not merely academic; it has practical implications for policy-making and development strategies, as understanding the role of institutions can inform how to design effective policies for economic growth. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, are more decisive. This literature review synthesizes the findings from various streams of scholarship to elucidate the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The specific institutional changes in England during the seventeenth century, as highlighted by North and Weingast, along with North (1989, p.9) (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), illustrate how secure property rights can be fostered through effective governance. This institutional evolution was marked by significant developments such as the creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. These financial innovations reflect an increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights should manifest in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. Consequently, costs per transaction decrease, while the size of the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP due to increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6).

North and Weingast also emphasize the role of political factors in underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, focusing on the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is crucial for the stability and predictability necessary for economic growth. North and Weingast recognize that the effects of institutional changes can be intricate. This qualification underscores the importance of considering the broader context and comparative performance of different economies when evaluating the impact of institutional changes.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) arguing that the type of institutions established during colonialism was determined by European mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were created to maximize rents for European colonists, promoting long-run economic growth. Colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, followed different economic paths. These institutions, which fostered investment and economic development, were more conducive to long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a lasting impact on the economic performance of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is attributable to the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the 19th century. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions influence economic growth are complex and multifaceted. This qualification underscores the nuanced role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting the need for further research to fully understand the long-term effects of these institutional legacies.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) enrich this historical context by arguing that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, drive core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions primarily arise from efficiency-driven motives, instead highlighting how power dynamics and the interests of elites shape institutional structures. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) note that New World societies with greater inequality saw institutions that advantaged elites, such as restrictive government policies that limited competition. These distributional conflicts were central drivers of institutional design, leading to structures that perpetuated inequality and shaped long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time indicates that while distributional conflicts may initiate institutional formation, other factors such as path dependency and institutional inertia also play crucial roles in maintaining these structures. This interplay between initial formation and long-term maintenance underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic outcomes.

Building on this, Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) jointly re-evaluate colonial impacts on African development by examining how different historical periods and paths influenced economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, contrary to the assumption that they uniformly imposed unfavorable institutions. This adaptive behavior indicates that colonial institutions evolved in response to local conditions. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity challenges the notion of colonial institutions as uniformly detrimental to African development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further illustrates the importance of institutional reforms by highlighting how the new economic history of Africa can inform policies that eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. The focus on institutional reforms underscores the potential for African governments to develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize that understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth.

This historical legacy is further complicated by the profound adverse effects of the slave trade in Africa on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collaboration, which are essential for economic growth. Nunn contends that the slave trade's impact on economic development extended beyond its direct effects, also influencing institutions and social norms. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011) finding that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, indicating that the slave trade's impact on trust is primarily through individuals' internal norms of mistrust. Nunn also observes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the slave trade's impact on economic development was not uniform across Africa and that the presence of certain institutions and social norms could mitigate its adverse effects.

This historical context is crucial for understanding how the growth of useful knowledge, as a primary driver of economic growth, interacts with technological progress and institutional adjustments in long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The institutional adjustments needed to support technological progress are ongoing processes that evolve with technological change. This implies that the institutions supporting the growth of useful knowledge must also adapt continuously. This suggests that while technological progress may be steady, the institutional adjustments required to support it can be more volatile. The implication is that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on the ability of institutions to adapt to technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further underscores that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that strong, adaptive institutions are essential for managing the challenges posed by technological progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, with scholars highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic development. Institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by the shared finding that institutions shape the economic landscape by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, which are essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported economic growth by reducing costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). Additionally, the political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The Bank of England’s establishment and the development of new financial instruments reflect the creation of efficient institutions that support economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

The historical evidence shows that the creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment has been pivotal in economic development. European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, setting up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in some colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states were established in other colonies, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This indicates that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Furthermore, the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
