The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the causal role of institutions versus those who highlight other factors. The stakes are high, as the debate influences policy recommendations and our understanding of economic success and failure. Institutions are seen as the key to unlocking economic growth by some scholars, who argue that they provide the necessary framework for investment, property rights, and economic stability. Some scholars argue that institutions play a secondary role, with geography, culture, or population density being more influential. This debate underscores the need to carefully examine the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, and to consider the complex interplay between institutions and other factors. The institutions literature offers a compelling perspective on the causal mechanisms through which institutions shape economic growth, providing a foundation for understanding the broader debate.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and secure property rights in 17th-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments were pivotal in this transformation, as they increased the security of property rights over time. This institutional change facilitated a long-term capital market, which is critical for economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specified and enforced property rights led to a decline in transaction costs, as economic institutions evolved to facilitate market exchanges. This dynamic is evident in the growth of the transactions sector, which became a larger proportion of GNP as specialization and division of labor increased. North (1989, p.9) argues that these institutional developments were a product of fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated trade-offs between rights and revenues. This political context shaped the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which in turn underpinned economic growth. The economic performance of France, which had a different institutional structure, suggests that other factors also played a role. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other determinants of economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions imposed by European colonizers was determined by the mortality rates they experienced in these regions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, while lower mortality rates resulted in more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law. This institutional framework persisted long after the colonial period, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) provide empirical evidence supporting this claim, showing that the quality of institutions in former colonies is strongly correlated with current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their profound impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that the design of these institutions was often driven by the interests of politically powerful groups, who sought to maximize their rents. This qualification indicates that the economic outcomes of colonial institutions were influenced by both their design and the political dynamics that shaped them. Consequently, the long-run economic growth of former colonies was intrinsically linked to the nature of the institutions imposed during colonial rule, with extractive institutions often hindering development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental to economic growth than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies epochal innovations as dominant sources of sustained growth. These innovations, such as the Industrial Revolution, are pivotal because they constitute major breakthroughs in human knowledge. They spread across substantial parts of the world, driving economic growth. This complementarity ensures that economic improvements are sustainable and not undermined by rent-seeking behaviors. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also observes that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional adaptations, is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while technological progress can drive economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt efficiently to harness this progress fully. This underscores the importance of institutional adaptability in supporting technological advancements and ensuring sustained economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were forged in the crucible of distributional struggles. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this perspective to New World societies, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that underpin institutional development. This points to a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve, where the primary driver is the need to manage conflicts over resources, with shared beliefs playing a secondary role in legitimizing these institutions. This perspective offers a deeper understanding of how institutions are shaped by the underlying power dynamics in society, and how these institutions in turn shape economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The mistrust cultivated by the slave trade has profound implications for contemporary economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both external and internal channels, with the internal channel of mistrust being at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalized mistrust can hinder cooperation and collective action, which are essential for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) further emphasizes the importance of historical legacies in shaping contemporary economic institutions, noting that the paths of institutional evolution are complex and often oversimplified (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of mistrust in Africa, as a legacy of the slave trade, can therefore be seen as a significant barrier to the adoption of institutions that facilitate economic growth. This mistrust can make it difficult for African societies to build the strong, inclusive institutions necessary for sustained economic development.

This narrative is challenged by Broadberry and Gardner, who argue that many Anglophone African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era in Africa has enabled recent historical national accounting work to demonstrate that the region's economic performance was more robust than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner highlight that the economic growth of these economies was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, with the timing and scale of growth in export production varying due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The significance of these exports in driving economic growth underscores the importance of institutions that facilitate trade and market integration. This pattern of growth, however, was not uniform across all African economies. Broadberry and Gupta further illustrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arising through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, highlighting the importance of institutions that support merchant activities and financial services. This divergence suggests that the institutional frameworks in Europe were more conducive to economic growth and development than those in Asia, even before the Industrial Revolution. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they demonstrate that institutions that support trade, finance, and merchant activities can drive economic growth and development, even in the absence of technological advancements.

This suggests that the literature on institutional reforms in Africa, while focusing on specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty, does not directly address the broader implications for long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also emphasize that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The new economic history of Africa has enabled development specialists to focus on institutions and organizations, recognizing that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This focus on institutions aligns with broader findings on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it underscores the importance of institutional quality in driving economic development. This attention to institutions is crucial for understanding how specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The specific mechanisms through which these institutional reforms translate into long-run economic growth remain under-explored. This gap in the literature suggests that while institutional reforms are recognized as important for poverty reduction, their broader implications for long-run economic growth in Africa need further investigation.

This perspective is further enriched by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Frankema 2012, p.15), who argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This shared posture underscores the importance of women's roles and the impact of colonial education policies on African economic development. The colonial period introduced a dual legal system of European courts and native courts, which codified customary law and reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops. This pattern of land allocation and gender-based division of labor had significant implications for economic growth, as it limited women's participation in the cash crop economy, which was a key driver of economic development during the colonial period. Frankema (2012, p.1) suggests that the demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This altered perception of education's benefits, coupled with the marginalization of women, shaped the economic landscape by influencing who had access to education and, by extension, to economic opportunities. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes, such as the codification of customary law and the introduction of formal education, interacted with gender roles to influence long-run economic growth in Africa.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities. The establishment of extractive institutions, for instance, can significantly impede economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists implemented extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of Africa, where colonial powers introduced institutions that often prioritized resource extraction over local economic development. Austin notes that colonial administrations sometimes altered their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the interactive nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction between external impositions and local responses shaped the economic landscape, influencing who had access to education and economic opportunities, thereby affecting long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the development of financial instruments and the creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced property rights security (North 1989, p.9). North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs, thereby fostering economic development. The historical evidence from Europe shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is particularly relevant in the context of colonial Africa, where institutions were often designed to benefit European colonists.

The long-run impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The prominence attached to the role of institutions has enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, as 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth and development. The shared finding that institutions matter (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8) is tempered by disagreement over whether institutions are shaped by efficiency or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of colonial Africa, where institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, settles this disagreement by showing that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shaped these institutions.
