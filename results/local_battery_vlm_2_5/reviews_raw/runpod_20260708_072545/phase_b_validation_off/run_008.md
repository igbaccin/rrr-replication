The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists and economic historians. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and why some economies thrive while others stagnate. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic development. Others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, with cultural beliefs and geographical factors also playing pivotal roles. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. For instance, if institutions are the key drivers, then policy should focus on institutional reform. If cultural beliefs or geographical factors exert more influence, policies addressing these areas may prove more effective. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as economic policies and historical contexts, further complicates the issue. For example, some argue that European institutions were shaped by cultural beliefs and political factors, while others emphasize the role of financial innovations and secure property rights. The debate continues, with some scholars proposing rival causes that do not operate through institutions. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, for instance, can dramatically reduce transaction costs, fostering economic growth by facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and supported economic growth. This institutional framework is pivotal because it encourages investment and economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this by showing that extractive institutions, which prioritize rent extraction over economic development, can persist and significantly affect long-run economic outcomes. They demonstrate that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with high-mortality areas receiving extractive institutions that hindered long-term growth. This historical pattern underscores the enduring impact of institutional choices on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the persistence of these institutions is not inevitable; changes in political dynamics can lead to shifts in institutional frameworks, potentially altering economic trajectories. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is dynamic, subject to historical and political contexts that can either reinforce or alter institutional pathways.

North and Weingast, together with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This institutional evolution was driven by the development of credible commitment mechanisms, which ensured that rulers would uphold property rights and enforce contracts. North and Weingast highlight that the Glorious Revolution in England led to a significant shift in political dynamics, where the monarchy's power was constrained by parliamentary oversight. This political transformation created a stable environment for economic growth by ensuring that property rights were secure and that contracts were enforced. The credibility of these commitments was further reinforced by the emergence of a strong legal system and independent judiciary, which provided a framework for resolving disputes and protecting property rights. Ogilvie builds on this by suggesting that the institutional framework in pre-industrial Europe was shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective challenges the notion that economic institutions are primarily driven by efficiency goals, instead emphasizing the role of political dynamics and power struggles in shaping institutional frameworks. Ogilvie also acknowledges that the institutional changes in England were influenced by broader cultural and ideological shifts. Ogilvie notes that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights played a significant role in shaping the institutional landscape. This highlights that while political dynamics are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of institutional evolution. The interplay between political dynamics and cultural beliefs creates a complex environment where institutions evolve in response to both internal and external pressures. This evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights in seventeenth-century England underscores the dynamic nature of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that institutions in societies with greater inequality have been shown to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. These inequalities were reinforced by institutions that favored elites, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. The persistence of inequality is a result of these institutions, which often include policies that protect elite interests and stifle competition. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this view by highlighting the broader implications of inequality, noting that it can also hinder the development of trust, a crucial element for economic growth. The internal norms of mistrust, exacerbated by historical inequalities, further impede economic development by reducing the trustworthiness of others and undermining the rule of law. This internal mistrust, in turn, creates a self-reinforcing cycle where economic stagnation and social distrust feed into each other, perpetuating the cycle of inequality and underdevelopment. The historical legacy of inequality thus shapes institutional frameworks in ways that continue to affect long-run economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This process is not merely about the invention of new technologies but also involves the efficient use and diffusion of these innovations within societies. This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from modernization. For instance, the transition from a rural mode of life to an industrial one necessitates significant adjustments in social structures and values. Mokyr highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of economic reasonableness, which Mokyr defines as the ability to make rational economic decisions, is essential for maintaining the benefits of technological progress. However, Mokyr also notes that this growth is not monotonic or irreversible, suggesting that the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors if not properly managed. This underscores the importance of technological innovation and the institutional frameworks that support and sustain it. Kuznets elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully, thereby ensuring that modern economic growth is possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The state's authority, based on loyalty and a shared sense of community, is pivotal in managing the tensions that arise from technological and economic changes.

Frankema shows that the role of British colonial rule in fostering formal education challenges the notion that all colonial powers had uniformly negative impacts on African development (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). British colonial rule implemented a dual legal system that included European courts based on common and civil law, alongside native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This system altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, reflecting the broader institutional changes that affected educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, the extent to which these educational benefits translated into economic growth varied across regions and was influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share the view that colonial experiences in Africa were not uniform and that indigenous economic and political actions influenced institutional reforms (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, especially when European settlers were absent. This occurred because colonial administrators, seeking fiscal stability, adapted their institutional policies in response to local economic activities. This adaptation was a strategic response to the incentives generated by indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic reveals a more complex picture of institutional change in Africa, where local initiatives played a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by highlighting the broader implications of colonial legacies on African institutions. He notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the capacity of societies to develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity. This suggests that while indigenous actions influenced institutional reforms, the broader historical context and the capacity of societies to adapt and develop institutions are crucial for long-term economic growth.

This perspective is supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the skill premium is influenced by factors such as training costs, institutional barriers to entry, and the interest rate.
