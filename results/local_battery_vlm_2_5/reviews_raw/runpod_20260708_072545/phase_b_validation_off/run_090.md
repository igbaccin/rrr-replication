The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, by defining the rules of the game and enforcing property rights, are crucial for economic development. Another position suggests that institutions are merely one of many factors, and their impact is often overstated. A third position contends that the impact of institutions is mediated by other variables, such as the historical context or the specific characteristics of the institutions themselves. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Institutions, by shaping the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior, can either foster or hinder growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of institutional choices made in the past on present economic outcomes. The question of how and why institutions matter in economic growth is central to understanding the divergent paths of development taken by different societies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in 17th century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This security was crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself, as it facilitated market exchanges and multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets of the time, which indicates the commitment of the regime to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). France, with a different institutional framework, showed comparable economic performance before the French Revolution, qualifying the extent to which these institutions fostered economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in 17th century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by the mortality rates of European settlers, had a lasting impact on economic development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The colonial institutions established by European powers varied significantly depending on the settler mortality rates. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. In contrast, in areas with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled permanently and developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences, driven by mortality rates, had profound implications for long-term economic growth. The persistence of these institutions highlights how initial colonial policies can have enduring effects on economic outcomes. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is complex. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics in colonial settings, where political power and rent-seeking behavior can shape institutions in ways that do not necessarily foster economic development. The long-run economic impact of colonial institutions is further complicated by the interaction between these institutions and other factors, such as the opportunity to industrialize. This interaction played a central role in the development trajectories of former colonies, illustrating the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Colonial rule in Africa significantly shaped institutional change through the negotiation and imposition of policies, a process that Hopkins (2009, p.18); (Frankema 2012, p.15) and Frankema (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) both highlight. This institutional shaping was not uniform but varied across different colonial powers and regions, with British rule in particular having a distinctive impact on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the demand for missionary education was influenced by new social and political institutions implemented during colonial rule, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This institutional framework created by colonial powers thus played a crucial role in structuring the educational landscape. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further elucidate that the growth and decline of economies in colonial Africa were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, highlighting how colonial institutions facilitated or hindered economic activities. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth was complex. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding suggests that while colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes, their effects were mediated by a complex interplay of factors.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth, focusing on the efficient use of innovations rather than institutions as the fundamental cause (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies, ensuring that economic growth does not weaken the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological innovations drive growth, the efficiency of their use depends on concurrent institutional developments. This interplay is not always straightforward. While technological progress can lead to economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt to support these innovations. If institutions fail to keep pace with technological changes, the benefits of these innovations may be lost, leading to a less efficient economy.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Austin, argue that the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has shaped contemporary economic outcomes through a persistent culture of mistrust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment of distrust among communities, which has had enduring economic consequences. This mistrust impedes collective action and reduces the effectiveness of institutions, hindering economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that this cultural legacy explains a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, suggesting that the historical trauma of the slave trade continues to influence social and economic behaviors in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is not merely a response to external factors but is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. Austin highlights that colonial policies, which often prioritized fiscal security over long-term economic development, exacerbated these issues. Colonial administrations that encouraged material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic further complicates the relationship between historical legacies and current economic outcomes, as the institutional frameworks established during colonial times continue to influence contemporary economic policies and practices (Austin 2008, p.15). The enduring legacy of mistrust, coupled with the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, creates a complex interplay that shapes Africa's economic development trajectory.

Distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue. They contend that these conflicts, not merely institutional efficiency, drive the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds. The mechanism through which distributional conflicts influence institutions involves the political power of elites, who shape institutions to maintain their advantages. This power dynamic is evident in New World societies, where elites leveraged their influence to create institutions that reinforced inequality. For example, in societies with extreme inequality, elites used government policies to restrict competition, thereby maintaining their privileged status (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). However, the persistence of these institutions over time is not merely a function of elite power but also of the broader social and economic context. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) suggests that shared beliefs, while part of the institutional structure, do not solely determine institutional outcomes. Instead, the interplay between distributional conflicts and these beliefs creates a complex environment where institutions evolve to reflect both elite interests and broader societal values. This interplay has significant implications for economic development, as institutions that emerge from distributional conflicts may not always be efficient or conducive to long-term growth. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the underlying power structures and conflicts that shape institutions, as these factors can have lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in economic history, with scholars highlighting the critical importance of institutional frameworks in fostering development. Institutions, as defined by North, are the rules of the game in a society, and economic growth is fundamentally about the ability to generate and adopt new technologies and organizational forms (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. support this view, demonstrating that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth, illustrate how institutional structures can either facilitate or hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of such institutions underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional arrangements on contemporary economic outcomes, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity.

A related stream of scholarship emphasizes the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is echoed by Austin, who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further illustrated by the persistence of guilds in pre-industrial Europe, which, despite their potential inefficiencies, were supported by distributional conflicts that ensured their continuation (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of institutions on economic outcomes.

The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth also involves the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on development. Hopkins emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are associated with higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century illustrate how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and promote economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering long-term economic development. The historical evidence from the colonial experience and the financial revolution in England shows that institutions matter for economic growth. This evidence settles the debate by demonstrating that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, rather than promote long-term growth, hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9).
