The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding how political, legal, and social structures influence the trajectory of economic development over centuries. The live disagreement centres on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. One position argues that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. A rival position contends that the efficiency or extractive nature of institutions is less critical than their role in mediating distributional conflicts. Another perspective posits that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on the specific historical and geographical context. These debates underscore the complexity of institutional effects and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive long-run economic growth.

Institutional quality is crucial for economic growth, as institutions determine the incentives for investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, while others set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional quality had lasting effects on the economic development of these colonies, with those having more extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita. North similarly emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These arguments highlight how institutional quality can shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives for investment and the efficiency of market exchanges. Austin suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which might affect the institutional evolution and economic outcomes of different regions (Austin 2008, p.2).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering economic growth. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and encouraged investment. This institutional evolution was driven by the need to address fiscal crises, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This political model underscored the importance of credible commitments in securing property rights. The institutional changes in England, particularly the establishment of the Bank of England, were both economic and political, as they involved the creation of mechanisms to enforce rules and ensure that the regime was committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was critical in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which are essential for long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates. In regions with low mortality, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In regions with high mortality, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework persisted over time and significantly affected the current income per capita of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further emphasize the robustness of their findings by using various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all of which showed similar results. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that while extractive institutions may not encourage economic development, they can persist as equilibrium institutions because they benefit politically powerful groups. This persistence of extractive institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on the economic trajectories of former colonies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the view that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). One specific mechanism through which these factors interacted is the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has emphasized the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The persistence of poverty in Africa cannot be solely attributed to structural impediments, as recent literature has suggested. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to check for the persistence of poverty, revealing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This finding underscores the complex interplay between endogenous factors, such as local institutions and cultural practices, and exogenous factors, such as colonial policies and global economic conditions, in shaping African economic development.

Austin's work, along with Ogilvie's, examines specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena without directly addressing the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments, when not competing with settlers for African labour, often encouraged indigenous commodity production, indicating a dynamic response to local economic and political actions. This suggests that institutions in pre-industrial Europe evolved in response to both internal and external pressures. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective underscores that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were deeply rooted in social and political dynamics, which could either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on the specific context. The implications of these findings are qualified by Ogilvie's (2007) call for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be treated as an indispensable tool in the economic historian's toolkit. This call underscores the need for a deeper, more contextualized analysis of how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long run.

The slave trade in Africa, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.2); (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), had a profound adverse effect on long-term economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. This adverse effect is mediated through a culture of mistrust, which the slave trade instilled within African societies. This mistrust has persisted over time and continues to influence economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon document that the slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external channels, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust mechanism is pivotal in understanding the long-term economic impact of the slave trade. This extended duration highlights the deep-rooted nature of the institutional changes brought about by the slave trade. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are further qualified by the specific institutional contexts of African societies. For example, the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the impact of the slave trade on economic development was not uniform across Africa and was influenced by pre-existing institutional structures. The enduring legacy of mistrust, shaped by the slave trade, continues to influence economic outcomes in Africa.

The growth of useful knowledge, as seen by Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49), suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Mokyr and Kuznets argue that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, and that institutional adjustments are necessary to integrate these technological advancements into society. Mokyr further elaborates on this, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be limited and unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by appropriate institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary to support it may be more complex and subject to greater variability. Kuznets' observation that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts further underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where the establishment of different institutional frameworks significantly influenced economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to divergent institutional setups in colonies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persist and have significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on economic development. This perspective is supported by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The long-term effects of these institutions are evident in the economic performance of these regions, underscoring the critical role that institutions play in shaping economic trajectories.

The institutional framework is also crucial in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.6). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for reducing costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic growth. This argument is reinforced by the work of Hopkins, who emphasizes the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of distributional conflicts on core economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups that designed them to maximize rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, where the design of institutions is influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which in turn affects long-run economic growth.

There is a shared finding across these sources that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic activity, reduce transaction costs, and create incentives for investment (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. focus on the extractive nature of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of income per capita in former European colonies, which shows a clear correlation between the type of institutions established during colonialism and current economic outcomes. This empirical comparison demonstrates the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.
