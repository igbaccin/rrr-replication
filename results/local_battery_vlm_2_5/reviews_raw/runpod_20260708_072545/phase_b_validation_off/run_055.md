The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. The stakes are high, as understanding this role can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who argue for alternative explanations, such as cultural or geographical factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) assert that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are crucial for economic development. They contend that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions play a key role in economic growth and development, complementing neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. This perspective is echoed by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The debate extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some scholars focusing on the role of distributional conflicts, as seen in Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), while others highlight the impact of colonial policies on institutional development, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8). The interplay between these positions underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, making it a rich area for further exploration.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in colonies with low mortality rates, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional development had long-lasting effects on economic growth. One specific mechanism through which these institutions influenced growth was through the differential impact on property rights and the rule of law. Acemoglu et al. show that the efficiency of the judiciary and constraints on the executive were crucial factors in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The endurance of these institutions over time results from both their initial design and the political dynamics that sustain them. Acemoglu et al. highlight that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may resist changes that could undermine their rents or political power, even if such changes could promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This political dimension underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic development. The long-run effects of colonial institutions are further qualified by the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that this reversal was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not linear but depends on the broader economic context in which they operate.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) focus on specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, and their impact on transaction costs. These developments reflect increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, which was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This decline in transaction costs is a direct result of the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. The English financial revolution, for example, significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs is a critical factor in the long-run economic growth of a country. The success of these institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth is evident in the remarkable evidence from capital markets.

This scrutiny reveals that colonial policies were not merely imposed but often negotiated, shaping the institutional landscape in complex ways. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) highlight that colonial rule led to the introduction of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education and other public goods. For instance, the demand for missionary education was shaped by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, reflecting the broader institutional shifts imposed by colonial powers. This dynamic highlights that the impact of colonial rule on African institutions varied based on specific policies and their interactions with local contexts. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century was tied to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings suggest that the legacy of colonial rule on African institutions is complex and multifaceted, with enduring effects on economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that African women made significant contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as the challenges posed by patriarchal alliances and customary law. These contributions were often overlooked or marginalized by colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women through customary law. Frankema and van Waijenburg note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of historical economic conditions to assess the long-term impact of structural impediments on African welfare development.

The literature on economic policies and land conflicts in Africa highlights the complex interplay of political-economic processes and social dynamics (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Simson argues that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments, shaping the region's economic trajectory. This expenditure pattern was marked by high subsidies and interest payments, peaking at 30% of spending in Kenya during the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.2). This economic strategy, driven by neopatrimonial tendencies, prioritised consumption over public goods, with vertical ethnic and regional ties influencing policy-making. These tendencies were not confined to expenditure patterns but also extended to military spending, which grew significantly in response to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.2). Peters adds that the perception of 'custom' as static within official circles, coupled with longstanding prejudices against African landholding practices, further complicated the economic landscape. This perception was reinforced by agricultural officers who viewed 'communal' landholding practices as inhibiting individual investment in agriculture. The push for agricultural development during the final decade of colonial rule exacerbated these tensions, as schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement were designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming (Peters 2004, p.5). This dynamic reveals a deeper pattern of exclusion and deepening social divisions, driven by both colonial legacies and post-independence policies.

van Zanden (2009, p.19; 1999, p.1) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) focus on economic performance and standard of living in Europe, with skill premiums, wage data, and collaborative research methods as their primary analytical lenses. van Zanden (2009, p.19) points to the role of guilds in skill transfer, noting that their institutional framework facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills by guaranteeing fair execution of apprentice contracts. This institutional support for skill development is crucial for long-term economic performance, as it directly influences the supply of skilled labor. Van Zanden also points out that guilds' efforts to form local cartels and restrict entry into trades could offset these benefits, potentially keeping relative pay above market-clearing levels. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in refining economic measurements, particularly in the context of long-term economic growth. This collaborative approach is essential for improving the accuracy of national accounts and understanding the nuances of economic performance over time. van Zanden (1999, p.9) highlights the shifting focus of economic development in Europe, noting that the rise in relative price levels of basic foodstuffs followed economic development in countries bordering the North Sea. This dynamic suggests that economic growth can lead to increased costs of living, which in turn may affect the distribution of wealth and standard of living across different regions.

Distributional conflicts, more so than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were not necessarily efficient but served to concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the craft guilds, while ostensibly promoting quality control and training, primarily functioned to restrict competition and maintain the economic dominance of guild members. This mechanism of institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had a significant impact on economic outcomes. This perspective is nuanced by the recognition that while distributional conflicts are a powerful explanatory factor, they do not operate independently. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges that cultural beliefs also play a role in shaping institutions, although they are not the primary driver. This interplay between distributional conflicts and cultural factors highlights the complexity of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade has left a lasting legacy of mistrust within Africa, which continues to impact contemporary trust levels. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, which fostered a pervasive sense of betrayal and suspicion among African communities. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is multifaceted, involving both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors. Austin (2008, p.15) further qualifies this by highlighting that colonial governments' institutional prescriptions were influenced by indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that the legacy of mistrust is actively shaped by ongoing interactions. This interplay between historical trauma and contemporary institutional dynamics reveals that the mistrust engendered by the slave trade is an evolving phenomenon that continues to influence African societies today.

This interplay suggests that technological progress is both a driver of economic growth and a catalyst for institutional change. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) agree that technological advancements, not institutional frameworks, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Mokyr highlights the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth, whereas Kuznets identifies the accumulation of knowledge as the primary source of sustained growth over long periods. Kuznets (1973, p.6) observes that the successful integration of technological progress into the economy requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets further notes that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is essential in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in sustaining economic growth.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450, a development that was further reinforced by the unusual degree of institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, which allowed for rapid urbanisation and economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This institutional flexibility facilitated the emergence of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies. Regions like Tuscany, which strengthened existing monopolies, experienced stagnation and a decline in urban ranks. While Pamuk highlights the role of institutional flexibility in driving economic divergence, Broadberry and Gupta note that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This sector's contribution to economic development was significant, as merchant distribution and finance were as important as production in driving high productivity in the traded goods sector. This development was uneven, with skilled labourers benefiting more than unskilled labourers.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of institutional frameworks. One stream of research highlights the importance of institutional flexibility and the service sector in driving economic growth. Pamuk demonstrates that institutional flexibility is crucial for economic divergence. Meanwhile, Broadberry and Gupta underscore the significance of the service sector, noting that merchant distribution and finance were pivotal in enhancing productivity in the traded goods sector. This sector's contribution to economic development was substantial, as it facilitated high productivity through efficient distribution and financial services (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Another stream of research focuses on the impact of institutions on long-term economic growth and development. North argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as primary determinants. He notes that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder long-run growth. They argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may not prioritize economic development, leading to extractive institutions that do not encourage growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between institutional changes and economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interactions between different historical periods and paths. He notes that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to the adoption of institutions that serve the interests of these groups (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a profound impact on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He argues that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. North shows that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development, as they provide the framework for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder long-run growth. They show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which highlights the importance of institutional changes in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This measurement type, used by Acemoglu et al., provides a clear picture of how institutional changes can influence economic growth and development.
