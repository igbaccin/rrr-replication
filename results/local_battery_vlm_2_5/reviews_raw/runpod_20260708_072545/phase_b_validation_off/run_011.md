The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental question: how do institutions, understood as the rules of the game that shape human interactions, drive economic growth over extended periods? One position emphasizes the direct causal role of institutions in fostering growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and economic exchange. This view is often linked to credible commitment, which underpins growth by ensuring that economic actors can trust in the stability and enforcement of rules. Another position argues that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables, such as cultural beliefs or distributional conflicts, may be more decisive in shaping economic institutions and outcomes. This disagreement highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions operate. This understanding is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at promoting sustainable economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, highlighting the impact of property rights and their enforcement on long-term economic development. He notes that these institutions reflect increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. These institutions shaped the economic trajectories of colonies, with some experiencing rapid growth due to stable property rights and others stagnating under extractive regimes. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and dependent on the specific context and power dynamics at play.

North and Weingast, along with Austin, extend this idea by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). The development of credible commitments to property rights in England was a pivotal factor in ensuring long-term economic growth. This was achieved through a series of institutional changes that included the establishment of a strong central government and the creation of a legal system that protected private property. North and Weingast highlight that these changes were critical in creating a stable environment where economic actors could invest with confidence, knowing that their property rights would be protected (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Austin elaborates on this by noting that the institutional evolution in England involved both economic rules and the political factors that supported their enforcement. This included the development of a regime that was committed to these rules, ensuring that they were not easily changed or manipulated by powerful interests (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin qualifies the impact of these institutional changes by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.15). This implies that while secure property rights were essential for economic growth, the specific historical and political contexts in which these institutions evolved also played a significant role. For example, in colonial contexts, the presence of European settlers and the fiscal policies of colonial governments influenced the adoption and effectiveness of these institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's analysis suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth varies based on the specific historical and political conditions of different regions.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that skill premiums are a significant predictor of long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) asserts that the skill premium reflects the costs and benefits of investing in human capital, including training expenses and the likelihood of securing a skilled job. This framework highlights the role of institutions in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, as seen in the guilds of Western Europe, which ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This suggests that while skill premiums are important, their impact on economic performance can be influenced by various factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that African economic development is influenced by various factors, including the role of women and the impact of colonial rule. Their work highlights the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This perspective challenges views that marginalize women's roles in economic development. This segregation was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men's access to land and economic opportunities. Hopkins (2009, p.8) elaborates on the broader institutional context, noting that by the 1990s, development policy had widened to incorporate governance and the role of institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) stresses the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The implications of these institutional changes are complex. This nuance is further illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who discuss how patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule codified the subordinate status of women, limiting their economic contributions. This interplay between colonial institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development highlights the multifaceted nature of African economic history.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on African development highlight how the legacy of mistrust persists and affects contemporary social dynamics (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which continues to influence current social dynamics. This mistrust is deeply ingrained in the internal norms of African societies, not merely an external response to past injustices. The slave trade's impact on trust is significant, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, highlighting how colonial policies interacted with pre-existing social structures to shape contemporary African development. The legacy of mistrust is a persistent force that continues to shape African societies today, not just a historical artifact.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, not efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were maintained because they benefited the powerful elites who controlled them, even if they were not the most efficient economic arrangements. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to the New World, where institutions in societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The implications of this perspective are profound: if institutions are primarily shaped by distributional conflicts, then efforts to promote economic growth through institutional reform must address these underlying power dynamics. This nuance suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they are not the sole determinant of institutional outcomes, and other factors such as cultural beliefs and technological changes may also play a role.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and by influencing the incentives for investment and innovation. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), is critical for economic development. The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century, for instance, dramatically declined the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). These institutions and consequent falling transaction costs are a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years War (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This development of economic institutions is essential for long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This argument highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, showing that the legacy of colonial rule continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today. The reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies was related to industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The new economic history of Africa, as argued by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This is because societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shows that good institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
