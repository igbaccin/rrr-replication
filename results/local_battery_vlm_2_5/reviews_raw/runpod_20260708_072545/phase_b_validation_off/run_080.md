The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate, and the extent to which economic outcomes can be influenced by institutional design. A key disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they provide the framework for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth. Others posit that the impact of institutions is mediated through complex historical and political processes, and that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural and distributional conflicts. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy, as it informs whether and how institutional reforms can drive economic development. The complexity of the institutional landscape, and the myriad ways in which institutions interact with economic outcomes, make this a rich and challenging area of study. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is not a straightforward one, as institutions themselves are products of historical, political, and social processes.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North's seminal work highlights the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) build on this framework by demonstrating that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. These strategies determined whether colonies established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The long-term implications of these institutional differences are profound, as they significantly affect current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on this by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their impact on economic development. The historical context and the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes remain critical areas of study.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, suggesting that the institutional framework played a pivotal role in economic development. This commitment to secure property rights and predictable rule enforcement created an environment conducive to investment and economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further underscore the importance of institutional frameworks by examining the differential impacts of inequality in New World societies. They argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality often advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic highlights how the institutional framework can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how property rights and political power are distributed. The persistence of inequality in these societies illustrates the long-term implications of institutional design on economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, the community, and craft and merchant guilds, were fundamentally influenced by distributional conflicts among different social groups. These conflicts often led to the creation of institutions that maintained or redistributed power and resources, rather than those that maximized economic efficiency. For example, guilds in medieval Europe were not merely efficiency-enhancing organizations but were shaped by the power struggles between craftsmen and other social groups. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. This insight underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, as these factors can have long-term implications for economic development.

Similarly, the legacy of the slave trade in Africa has fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to impact contemporary levels of trust, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2). The slave trade's impact on trust is primarily through internal norms of mistrust rather than external environmental factors, such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust, which is rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, continues to shape social interactions and economic behaviors in Africa. Nunn demonstrates that the slave trades lasted for nearly 500 years, significantly longer than the period of official colonial rule, and that they had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). The long duration of the slave trades suggests that their institutional legacy is deeply ingrained, affecting economic institutions and development trajectories over centuries. This historical mistrust can hinder the formation of effective economic institutions, as it affects the ability of individuals to cooperate and engage in productive activities. Nunn provides evidence that the Kongo kingdom's centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade with the Europeans, highlighting the importance of institutional structures in supporting economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This underscores that the absence of such institutions, exacerbated by a culture of mistrust, can impede economic growth.

This is particularly evident in the role of women and colonial education in Africa, which have significantly influenced economic activities, especially in agriculture, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15). The colonial era introduced a dual legal system that codified customary law, often reinforcing patriarchal structures that marginalized women's access to land and resources. This codification was particularly evident in the context of cash crops, where women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated the more profitable cash crop sectors. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where women played a crucial role in cocoa production, highlights this dynamic. Colonial rule also shaped educational policies, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This educational development altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing African political leaders' and families' decisions to pursue it. However, the implementation of these policies varied widely, and the benefits of education were not uniformly distributed. This variation underscores the complex interplay between colonial policies, gender dynamics, and economic development in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) together argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not directly address the fundamental causal role of institutions in long-run economic growth. They focus on the institutional evolution in Africa under colonial rule, highlighting that the presence of European settlers influenced the adoption of institutions that encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This dynamic is evident in cases where colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). One implication of this institutional evolution is that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but by the broader institutional landscape. This institutional context can either facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on the incentives it creates for investment. Despite these insights, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not fully capture the complex interplay between colonial policies and institutional development in Africa. This complexity underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth in the region.
