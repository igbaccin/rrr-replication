The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. The mechanisms driving economic development are at stake, particularly the role of institutions in comparison to other factors such as geography or culture. The disagreement centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they are merely one of many factors. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a society, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth. Others posit that institutions are secondary to other factors, such as population density and prosperity. This debate is significant because it informs policies aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing countries. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for designing effective economic policies. The debate over institutions and economic growth is particularly intense when considering the historical context of colonialism and its lasting effects on economic development. This is where the live disagreement lies: the extent to which colonial institutions, shaped by European strategies, have influenced the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was a pivotal moment in economic history, as it provided a stable foundation for long-term capital markets and economic growth. They argue that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured the enforcement and stability of the rules, which were critical for economic growth. These institutional developments have implications that go beyond economic growth. This highlights how the interplay between political and economic institutions can shape long-run economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions did not foster long-run economic growth, as they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not to promote economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to more robust economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these equilibrium institutions, though extractive, were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo. This institutional framework highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) support this by documenting the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial in shaping the economic paths of these colonies.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had distinct impacts on educational development compared to other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial administration's emphasis on indirect rule and the introduction of formal education systems shaped local institutions in ways that fostered educational development. This approach altered the perceived benefits of formal education, as it provided new opportunities for social mobility and political engagement (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education, driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, further stimulated educational development. African political leaders and families recognized the value of formal education in navigating the new institutional landscape, leading to increased investment in education. The extent to which these educational benefits translated into broader economic development differed across regions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was influenced by institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and hindering economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This pattern suggests that while British colonial rule may have promoted education, the broader institutional framework and its interaction with local conditions significantly influenced the long-term economic outcomes. The legacy of colonial education policies continues to shape contemporary educational systems and economic trajectories in sub-Saharan Africa.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This perspective is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011) finding that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted contemporary economic outcomes. This mistrust, as a mechanism, is a crucial factor in shaping economic development, as it affects both intergroup trust and individual norms of mistrust. This mistrust is not merely an artifact of the slave trade's effect on the trustworthiness of others, but rather, it operates through a more profound internalization of mistrust within individuals. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) note that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, underscoring the deep-seated nature of this issue. The adverse effects of the slave trade are not limited to the past but persist through the legacy of mistrust, which continues to hinder economic development. This mistrust, rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, remains a significant barrier to economic progress in Africa.

Technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that sustained economic development is closely tied to the growth of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This implies that while technological advancements are vital, they must be complemented by institutional changes to ensure sustained economic growth. For instance, the development of new technologies often necessitates changes in production sectors and societal structures, which can lead to conflicts that need to be resolved peacefully. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in resolving such growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that the effectiveness of technological advancements in driving economic growth is contingent on the ability of institutions to manage the associated societal changes. This underscores the importance of institutions in ensuring that the benefits of technological advancements are not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Thus, while technological advancements are a key driver of economic growth, their impact is significantly influenced by the institutional framework in place.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis has oversimplified the role of institutions in shaping economic development, and that distributional conflicts are a more compelling explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This is particularly evident in colonial contexts, where the interests of colonial governments and indigenous populations shaped institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political action on the ground, not through a simple, linear process of institutional transfer (Austin 2008, p.15). These institutions were not merely the outcome of rational, efficiency-driven processes but were deeply rooted in the power dynamics and conflict resolution mechanisms of the time. This critique underscores the importance of considering the historical and contextual specificities of institutional change. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) qualifies this by emphasizing the need to integrate institutions more deeply into the long-term research programme of economic historians, transforming them from a mere fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument. This suggests that while the critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is valid, it does not diminish the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes but rather calls for a more sophisticated understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors. This nuanced perspective on institutional change highlights the need for a more dynamic and contextualized approach to understanding the role of institutions in economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights, which in turn affect investment and economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, with some promoting economic growth through secure property rights and others extracting resources with little regard for long-term development. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) emphasizes that the evolution of institutions, such as the Bank of England, reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby fostering economic growth. Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6) shows that institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs are crucial for economic development. This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic development is further highlighted by the impact of colonial policies on the institutional frameworks of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. This institutional legacy persists, influencing economic outcomes in the long run. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and enforced, which is essential for economic development. Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) shows that the institutional frameworks established during colonialism have long-lasting effects on economic growth. This points to the enduring impact of institutions on economic development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations. The underlying point of agreement among these studies is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) emphasize the role of colonial policies in shaping institutional frameworks, while Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) highlights the importance of distributional conflicts in determining institutional outcomes. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the long-term impact of colonial policies on economic development, as shown by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison demonstrates the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
