The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with implications for understanding the wealth of nations and the persistence of poverty. At its core, this question probes the mechanisms through which societal rules and norms influence economic outcomes over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on the causal pathways from institutions to growth. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic performance, while others point to indirect effects mediated through other factors such as cultural beliefs or geographic conditions. Additionally, there is contention over whether institutions are endogenous to economic growth or exogenous determinants that shape economic trajectories independently. This debate is crucial for policy, as it informs whether institutional reforms can catalyze economic development or if other factors must be addressed first. The institutions-development relationship is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions matter more for economic growth than other factors, while others contend that institutions are shaped by underlying socio-economic dynamics. This interplay between institutions and economic growth is central to understanding why some countries prosper while others stagnate.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). He traces the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, as pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional framework, by specifying and enforcing efficient property rights, facilitates market exchanges and reduces costs per transaction (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) reinforce the significance of institutions by demonstrating that European colonization strategies, which established different institutional frameworks, had long-lasting effects on economic development. For instance, colonies with extractive states, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, experienced significantly lower income per capita. This pattern underscores the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elucidate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also caution that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic development, where institutions can either catalyze growth or perpetuate economic stagnation depending on their structure and the interests they serve. The enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic outcomes underscores the need for careful consideration of institutional design in policy-making.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This institutional change was not merely about establishing rules for economic exchange but also about creating mechanisms to enforce and adapt these rules. A key factor in this process was the commitment of the regime to these rules, which North and Weingast describe as a critical political factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment was essential for the development of capital markets and the overall economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for investment and trade. Kuznets further elaborates on this by highlighting the role of technological progress and the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for its efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.1). He notes that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires a society to adapt its institutions and ideologies. However, Kuznets also points out that these adjustments must be made without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the delicate balance required in institutional design, where the benefits of economic growth must be carefully managed to avoid social and political instability. The interplay between institutional evolution and technological progress is crucial for sustained economic growth, as it ensures that societal adjustments do not hinder the development of new technologies and innovations.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both highlight that the structure of economic institutions is deeply influenced by the distribution of power and resources within society. Ogilvie shows that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be attributed to the interests of ruling elites, who used these institutions to maintain their control over the labor force and resources. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily designed for economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The institutional arrangements in these societies were shaped by the initial distribution of factor endowments and the resulting power dynamics, which led to policies that favored the elite at the expense of broader economic growth. Ogilvie points out that the efficiency argument for institutions is often overstated, and that a more nuanced understanding of institutional design must consider the political and social contexts in which they operate. This suggests that the distributional conflicts inherent in societal power structures play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are pivotal drivers of long-term economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). He notes that the development of scholarly societies and periodicals in Europe significantly enhanced the dissemination of useful knowledge. This institutional progress is crucial because it complements technological advancements, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. They argue that the service sector played a crucial role in early modern economic growth, suggesting that institutional changes can lead to high agricultural labor productivity. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) also note that the benefits of economic development were spread unevenly, indicating that the distribution of these gains is influenced by the specific institutional context. This uneven distribution highlights the importance of considering how institutional frameworks shape not only economic growth but also the equitable distribution of its benefits. The role of institutions in supporting technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge is therefore multifaceted, encompassing both the facilitation of economic growth and the distribution of its fruits.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) establish that the impact of British colonial rule on African education systems varied significantly across sub-Saharan Africa, with British rule fostering development of formal education in some areas. The demand for missionary education was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The development of formal education was influenced by the centralization of power and the establishment of markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This pattern of educational development was closely tied to the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets, which facilitated trade and economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied between countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation in educational development highlights how different colonial powers influenced the educational landscape of sub-Saharan Africa, with British rule often being more conducive to the development of formal education. However, the legacy of British colonial rule was not uniformly beneficial, as the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development also lingered.

Simson argues that economic policies and land conflicts in Africa are deeply intertwined with political-economic processes and social dynamics (Simson 2019, p.2). Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) show that these processes are driven by the interplay of patrimonial governance structures and competition over land. Simson explains that patrimonial governance structures in Africa often lead to the misuse of government resources for buying political loyalty rather than delivering public goods. This results in a focus on consumption rather than investment in public goods and services. This dynamic is particularly evident in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 percent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern of expenditure highlights how political dynamics can divert resources away from productive investments, thereby hindering long-run economic growth. Peters adds that the perception of 'custom' as static can advantage groups seeking to keep officials ignorant of their actions (Peters 2004, p.5). This dynamic can further complicate land conflicts and economic policies, as it creates a environment where traditional practices are either misinterpreted or manipulated for political gain. This interplay of patrimonial governance and land conflicts underscores the complex nature of economic policies in Africa, where institutional frameworks often fail to support sustained economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), challenge the narrative of persistent poverty in Africa by highlighting the significant economic contributions of African women during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This perspective is crucial for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it underscores the impact of gender dynamics within institutional frameworks. Akyeampong and Fofack describe how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production under colonial rule. This historical context is essential for recognizing the structural impediments that have affected African economic development. For instance, the dual legal system introduced during colonial rule, which included European courts and native courts, further entrenched patriarchal alliances and customary laws that disadvantaged women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional framework not only limited women's economic contributions but also shaped broader economic policies that hindered long-run growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg’s reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies highlights the variability in economic conditions across different regions, suggesting that poverty in Africa was not a uniform or persistent phenomenon (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This variability indicates that institutional changes could have significant impacts on economic outcomes, depending on the specific historical and geographical context.

Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal pathways of economic growth and decline across different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). He suggests that the thesis, as formulated by Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn, overcompresses the complexity of institutional change, investment, and policy experience across different regions. This is especially relevant to Africa, where the presence or absence of European settlers, and the specific incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, shaped colonial governments' institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Austin notes that in some cases, colonial administrations responded to local incentives by altering their institutional frameworks to encourage commodity production and foster a sense of material interest in the status quo. Hopkins extends this analysis, arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He posits that 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. However, Hopkins also notes that the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is not straightforward, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view suggests that institutional reforms must be tailored to specific historical, geographical, and cultural contexts to be effective.
