The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of wealth disparities across regions and over time. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, driven by European settlers' strategies, significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Others contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The debate also extends to whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Shared beliefs and cultural values are sometimes cited as drivers of economic institutions, but this perspective is not universally accepted (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The debate is further complicated by the role of colonialism in shaping institutional changes, which some argue were designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The stakes are high because understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering development and reducing poverty, particularly in regions with a history of extractive institutions. To address this, we must examine the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes, and how these mechanisms interact with other factors such as geography and culture.

The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was a critical factor in the long-term growth of the English economy. This success was underpinned by political factors that committed the regime to these rules. Their research shows that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules.

These institutions were extractive in nature, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This pattern of institution-building was not uniform across colonies but varied based on European mortality rates and the resulting settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This extractive approach contrasts with colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The long-term economic impacts of these differing institutional frameworks are profound, as evidenced by the persistence of these institutions and their effects on current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the enduring influence of colonial policies on long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) contend that the role of women in African economic growth is critical, especially in the context of institutional changes brought about by colonial rule. Women's contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, often overlooked in mainstream narratives. The colonial era introduced a dual legal system that codified customary laws, which often marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional change, while not directly addressing long-run economic growth, illustrates how colonial rule reshaped social and economic structures. Nunn and Wantchekon, Nunn, and others argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had profound implications for economic growth. The slave trade contributed to the development of a culture of mistrust, which persisted long after the trade itself had ceased. This mistrust, in turn, had a significant impact on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade's effect on trust is primarily internal, rooted in individuals' norms of mistrust, rather than external factors such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust mechanism is at least twice as large as the external channel, underscoring the deep-seated nature of the problem. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates that the economic and social institutions in Africa were too underdeveloped to facilitate foreign trade until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). The Kongo kingdom's ability to support trade highlights the importance of centralized governance and robust economic institutions in fostering economic development. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade, combined with the lack of robust institutions, has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic trajectory.

These gains were unevenly distributed, benefiting skilled labourers more than unskilled ones. This disparity in wages and productivity suggests that the economic benefits of early modern development were not uniformly shared across all sectors of society. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that institutional explanations for economic development are insufficient on their own, as they do not account for the complexity of historical paths and the role of distributional conflicts. Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that institutions are the primary driver of economic development, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This oversimplification ignores the nuanced interplay between institutions and other factors such as distributional conflicts. For instance, Austin points out that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, rather than imposing a uniform set of institutions. This adaptation highlights the dynamic nature of institutional development and the influence of local contexts on economic outcomes. She contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by power struggles and the distribution of resources, rather than purely by efficiency concerns. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by economic efficiency and underscores the importance of considering the broader social and political context in which institutions operate. The interplay between institutions and distributional conflicts suggests that economic development is a multifaceted process influenced by a variety of factors beyond institutions.
