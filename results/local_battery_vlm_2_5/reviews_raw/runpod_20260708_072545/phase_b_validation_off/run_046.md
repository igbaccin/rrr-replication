The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. The extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can drive economic development over the long term is at stake. The debate centres on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play more significant roles. One position argues that institutions that enforce property rights and provide a stable political environment are crucial for sustained economic growth. Another position contends that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their impact is often overstated. Some scholars even propose that institutions are a secondary factor to economic growth, with geography or culture being the primary determinants. The live disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors in fostering long-term economic development. Building on this, North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, with different institutions leading to different economic outcomes. He illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional development was a response to fiscal crises and reflected a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. In some cases, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors. They argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists, and to promote long-run growth. This institutional perspective underscores the importance of property rights and political stability in fostering economic development, as these factors can shape the long-term trajectory of economic growth in colonized regions.

Institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). For instance, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be attributed to institutions that were shaped by the initial factor endowments of these societies. These endowments, characterized by an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality, in turn, influenced the development of institutions that favored the elite, such as those governing property rights and political structures. The resulting institutional framework restricted competition and hindered economic growth, as the elite sought to maintain their privileged status. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further elaborate on how these institutional effects can persist over time, noting that the impact of historical inequalities on contemporary economic development is significant. They argue that the legacy of inequality can shape societal norms and institutions in ways that perpetuate economic disparities. The specific mechanisms through which these historical inequalities translate into contemporary economic outcomes can vary widely across different regions and contexts. For instance, the internal norms of mistrust stemming from historical inequalities can have a profound effect on economic development, as these norms can undermine social capital and trust, which are crucial for economic growth.

North and Weingast, along with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Their analysis focuses on how political factors, particularly the commitment of regimes to enforce and uphold rules, significantly influence economic development. This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of economic rules, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth. Ogilvie extends this perspective by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. She argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped more by distributional conflicts, and efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie suggests that these institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic needs, and were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts within society. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which institutions evolve. Ogilvie also notes that institutions should be viewed as indispensable tools in economic analysis, suggesting that their role in economic development is multifaceted and complex (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This complexity implies that while secure property rights are crucial, the broader institutional framework, including the political and social contexts, plays a significant role in shaping economic growth.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements, which require complementary institutional and ideological changes. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress necessitates institutional changes, such as adapting to new production sectors and resolving conflicts that arise from these changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement is limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutions play a critical role in facilitating and sustaining this progress. The relationship between technology and institutions is complex. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that the political and institutional framework is essential for managing the social tensions that technological progress can generate (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment where technological advancements can thrive and contribute to long-term economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that British colonial rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, challenging the view that all colonial powers uniformly stunted African development. This educational development was driven by the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family considerations. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational legacy had significant downstream implications for economic development, as formal education equipped Africans with skills that enabled them to engage more effectively in the colonial economy. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that while formal education was promoted, it was often accompanied by patriarchal alliances and the codification of customary law that reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to educational opportunities and economic participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This reveals that British colonial rule had a complex impact on African economic development, fostering formal education but also reinforcing gender inequalities that constrained broader economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex historical paths and institutional evolution that have shaped African development. This simplification obscures the nuanced interplay between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, demonstrating that institutional change was a dynamic process influenced by indigenous agency. This adaptability suggests that institutional evolution in Africa was more responsive to local conditions than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis acknowledges. However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) qualifies this by noting that the emphasis on 'good' institutions, those that provide incentives for investment, has become a central tenet in development policy. This highlights that while local adaptations were crucial, the broader institutional framework still played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes. The implication is that African economies, despite their unique historical paths, are fundamentally influenced by the quality of their institutions, which can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is undeniable. The institutional framework, which includes the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic interactions, fundamentally influences economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of their colonies. This is consistent with North (1989, p.6), who argues that the development of efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic growth. The creation of secure property rights and the reduction of transaction costs are key factors that institutions must address to facilitate long-term economic development. This is evident in the historical example of the Bank of England, where the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutional framework, therefore, is a dynamic force that shapes economic development over the long run.

The historical paths of different regions reveal that the institutional frameworks that emerged were often shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the institutions established in former colonies were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, and to promote long-run growth. This aligns with Austin's (2008, p.15) observation that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. The historical evidence shows that institutions evolve in response to the interests and actions of various groups, highlighting the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional change.
