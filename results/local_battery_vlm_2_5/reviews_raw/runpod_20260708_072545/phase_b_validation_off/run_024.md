The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding whether institutions are the fundamental drivers of growth or merely secondary factors that complement other determinants. A live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. One camp highlights the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Another camp argues that institutions are shaped by deeper historical and cultural factors, which in turn influence economic growth. A third perspective focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in determining institutional structures. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are shaped by other underlying factors. This question is crucial for policy, as it determines whether institutional reform can be a panacea for economic stagnation or if deeper structural changes are necessary. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of both historical contexts and contemporary economic policies.

North and Weingast assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were key to this process. North argues that these developments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's analysis underscores the importance of political institutions in specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which are critical for market exchanges and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic efficiency and political stability also play significant roles in economic growth. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is thus complex, with institutional changes being just one of the many factors at play.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is a fundamental driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both emphasize the pivotal role of technological advancements in fostering economic development. Kuznets describes the interplay between technological changes and the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for their efficient use. He highlights that the pace and sectors affected by technological progress varied over centuries and regions, requiring corresponding adjustments in institutions and ideologies. This dynamic interplay is crucial for sustaining economic growth, as it ensures that technological innovations are integrated into the economy without excessive conflict or political destabilization. Mokyr supports this view, asserting that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth. He notes that the growth of useful knowledge requires institutional progress to complement it, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable. Mokyr also acknowledges that economic reasonableness grows in a less consistent and irreversible manner than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are essential, the institutional framework must evolve to support and sustain these advancements, otherwise the benefits of technological progress may be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Therefore, the relationship between technological progress and economic growth is symbiotic, with each reinforcing the other in a complex interplay of knowledge, institutions, and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies, influenced by mortality rates, led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). High mortality rates in certain regions, such as Africa and parts of Asia, prompted Europeans to implement extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach differed from settlement strategies in regions like North America, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The resulting institutional differences have had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further underscore that these institutions were designed to extract maximum rents for European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritized short-term gains over sustainable economic development. The legacy of these colonial institutions persists, influencing current economic outcomes and highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa have had a profound adverse effect on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trades led to a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, which in turn hinders economic development. This mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms, not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others. This internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, underscoring the depth of its impact (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical context is crucial for understanding the enduring effects of the slave trades on contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and other scholars (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) highlight that the literature on African economic development and colonial impact underscores various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. A key mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes was the codification of customary law. This process often reinforced patriarchal structures and marginalized certain groups, such as women, from accessing productive resources. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and were de-privileged in access to land for cash crops under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization had significant economic implications, as it limited the productive capacity of a large segment of the population. The impact of these institutional changes varied across Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while colonial institutions had profound effects, their long-term impact on economic growth varied depending on local conditions and historical contexts, necessitating a more nuanced understanding of African economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenge simplistic views of causation in economic development by re-evaluating historical economic institutions. They contend that institutional evolution is a complex process, shaped by multiple factors over extended periods. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that while the 'reversal of fortune' thesis provides insights, it oversimplifies the diversity of historical experiences by compressing different paths into a single narrative. This critique aligns with Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that institutional structures are deeply embedded in societal beliefs and distributional conflicts, not merely efficient responses to economic conditions. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this complexity by showing how colonial institutions in Africa evolved in response to both external pressures and local economic and political actions. This nuanced view suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth depends on the specific historical and contextual factors at play. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that while institutions are crucial, their role must be carefully integrated into economic history research to avoid oversimplification. This highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of institutional dynamics, one that considers the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its varied effects on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical aspect of economic development, as institutions facilitate the enforcement of property rights and contractual obligations. This is directly supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). North also shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Hopkins (2009, p.18) expands on this by showing that 'good' institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins also demonstrates that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the development of progressive institutions, as societies can be ethnically heterogeneous and still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing effective institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8) shows that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth by shaping property rights, contractual obligations, and the overall economic environment. Disagreement remains over the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, particularly regarding the balance between extractive and inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
