The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. The central question is how institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules and informal norms, influence economic performance over extended periods. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives economic development and how policies might be designed to promote growth. One key debate centers on whether institutions matter primarily because they facilitate efficient economic outcomes, or whether they are shaped by distributional conflicts and power dynamics. Another debate concerns the extent to which institutions are endogenous to economic development or exogenous factors like geography and culture. For example, some scholars argue that institutions are a product of historical and political processes. Other scholars posit that institutions are shaped by the need to secure property rights and enforce contracts, which in turn drive economic growth. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and for designing effective policies to promote development. The resolution of this debate could significantly impact our understanding of how to foster long-run economic growth in both developed and developing economies.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, and this argument is supported by Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The creation of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is central to economic development. North (1989, p.9) highlights the role of the English financial revolution, which significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development was driven by fiscal crises and the need for political stability, which in turn fostered economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by showing that European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes in colonies, with some colonies benefiting from institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were burdened with extractive institutions that hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, a pattern that Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate by emphasizing how population density and prosperity influenced European policies in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes, suggesting that the long-term effects of institutions depend on the interplay between political power and economic incentives.

North and Weingast, along with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights was pivotal for economic growth, especially in the context of seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), highlighting the importance of institutional stability. The institutional changes in England during this period were crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic development. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks. North and Weingast highlight that the success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie, similarly, advocates for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie also notes that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes, suggesting that the long-term effects of institutions depend on the interplay between political power and economic incentives. The evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights was pivotal for economic growth, particularly in the context of seventeenth-century England.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Frankema (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15), argue that colonial institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality, illustrating the impact of institutional design on economic outcomes. This inequality was not merely a result of economic factors but was deeply entrenched through institutional frameworks that favored the elite. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in these societies was virtually present from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The institutional design in these colonies, which often included policies that restricted access to education and economic opportunities for the majority, played a crucial role in maintaining this inequality. For example, Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic outcomes, suggesting that while some colonial institutions may have supported economic growth, they also entrenched inequality. The persistence of these institutions, which often favored the elite, had long-term implications for economic development, as they restricted the economic mobility of the broader population. The long-term effects of these institutions are evident in the enduring economic disparities observed in many former colonies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) jointly highlight the substantial role of institutions in shaping economic growth, particularly in Africa. This perspective underscores the importance of inclusive institutions that recognize and integrate diverse economic actors, such as women, into the productive economy. Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can address poverty by focusing on the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Akyeampong and Fofack document how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, which marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework had long-term implications for economic development, as it restricted the economic mobility of women and their contributions to the economy. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further qualifies this by noting that while institutional reforms are crucial, the effectiveness of these reforms depends on the broader socio-political context and the ability of institutions to adapt to changing circumstances. The role of women in economic development is a critical factor that institutions must address to foster sustainable growth and eliminate poverty.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800, a period marked by significant shifts in economic development within Europe, is a key area of study that helps us understand the broader dynamics of long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This sector's contribution extended beyond production to the distribution and financing of goods, which significantly impacted economic development. The high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe, for instance, was driven by merchant activities as much as by production processes. This productivity was further reinforced by the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates, which allowed for a more efficient allocation of resources. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) notes that the focus of economic development shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, with Holland and England emerging as key players. This shift was accompanied by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader economic transformation. This transformation did not uniformly improve living standards across Europe. This highlights the complex interplay between economic development and living standards, where institutional changes and resource allocation played pivotal roles.

Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, but they do not directly address institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This is because technological advancements are not inherently compatible with existing social structures and ideologies. For instance, modern technology challenges rural lifestyles and large family patterns. To manage these changes, the national state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This is because technological progress alone can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors if not complemented by institutional changes (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are necessary for long-run economic growth, they are not sufficient without the appropriate institutional framework.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, shared by Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), examines the historical roots of African poverty by focusing on structural impediments. This approach highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between historical periods and colonial policies. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to understand the long-term picture of economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This implies that the effectiveness of colonial policies was not uniform and could vary based on local conditions and responses. This nuanced view of colonial impacts suggests that the institutional framework imposed by colonial powers was not monolithic and could be adapted or resisted by local populations. Austin's analysis underscores the need for a more detailed understanding of how colonial policies interacted with local economic and political contexts, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic actors. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies based on mortality rates, leading to extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and more growth-oriented institutions in low-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had a lasting impact on economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth. Austin's analysis supports this view, highlighting that colonial policies were not uniform and could be adapted or resisted by local populations, influencing long-term economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin's work shows that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that the effectiveness of colonial policies varied based on local conditions and responses.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's argument that institutions matter because they define and enforce property rights, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9). North argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges reduces transaction costs, which is essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie extends this perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions operate. Ogilvie's work shows that institutions are shaped by broader social and political factors, which in turn influence economic growth.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al.'s argument that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional perspective is supported by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins shows that good institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.8) highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Austin's analysis of colonial impacts and Hopkins' discussion of institutional reforms in Africa both underscore the need for a detailed understanding of how institutions interact with local economic and political contexts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underpinned by the idea that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9) shows that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was related to industrialization, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The key to understanding long-run economic growth lies in examining the specific historical evidence of institutional changes and their impact on economic outcomes. The detailed analysis of colonial policies and their interaction with local economic and political contexts, as well as the measurement of institutional quality and its effects on investment and development, provides the empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
