The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty. One live disagreement is between those who argue that institutions are the primary cause of economic growth and those who posit that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasise the importance of secure property rights and the rule of law in fostering economic development, while others point to the impact of historical events and cultural norms on institutional development. Still others focus on the evolution of political institutions and their role in enforcing property rights and promoting economic growth. This disagreement underscores the need to understand how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic outcomes. The extent to which institutions can be shaped to promote growth is a question that lies at the heart of development economics.

The efficiency of property rights and enforcement mechanisms, as highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), is crucial for understanding how institutions shape economic trajectories. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs by increasing the security of property rights. This development facilitated a long-term capital market and economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand resulted in significant economic growth. This contrasts with extractive institutions in other colonies, where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the design of institutions is crucial for economic development, as they can either facilitate growth or hinder it depending on the interests they serve. The interaction between institutions and economic opportunities, such as industrialization, played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The impact of institutions on economic growth is about their efficiency and their alignment with economic opportunities and the interests of powerful groups.

The establishment of secure property rights through public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, as detailed by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), exemplifies how institutional changes can foster economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement, which secured property rights and encouraged economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was crucial for the development of markets and economic growth, as it ensured that economic rules were enforced and could not be arbitrarily changed. The commitment to these rules was a critical political factor that enabled sustained economic development. The divergence in institutional frameworks between England and its colonies illustrates the broader impact of institutional design on economic growth. The secure property rights and stable institutions in England contrasted sharply with the inequality-driven institutions in the colonies, where elites used their power to restrict competition and maintain their dominance. This institutional divergence had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as secure property rights and stable institutions in England facilitated sustained economic development, while the unequal and restrictive institutions in the colonies hindered growth.

The literature on economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa highlights specific historical and educational developments, but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, illustrating how institutional changes can influence educational outcomes and, by extension, economic development. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where institutional factors are just one piece of a larger puzzle.

The literature on colonial impact on African economic development focuses on how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2009) (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) share this focus on poverty reduction through institutional change. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that new economic history can inform institutional reforms in Africa, highlighting that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This emphasis on institutional quality aligns with the broader literature on economic growth, which posits that strong institutions can foster long-term economic development. The specific mechanism through which institutions affect growth in Africa is complex. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) suggest that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This implies that institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty must also address historical and geographical conditions that have influenced African welfare development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies to check for the persistence of poverty, indicating that historical data is essential for designing effective institutional reforms. This historical perspective underscores the importance of long-term institutional changes in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that African poverty is not solely a result of persistent structural impediments but can be addressed through targeted institutional reforms.

The causal relationships identified by Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) reveal a more complex interplay between institutions and economic growth than initially suggested by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. In particular, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is a significant impediment to economic development, as it undermines the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Austin's analysis of colonial governments' responses to indigenous economic and political action shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This finding suggests that the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments were not monolithic and that they could be influenced by local conditions. The implication is that institutional change in Africa is not merely a matter of adopting the right policies, but also of understanding the local context and the incentives that shape institutional evolution. This highlights the importance of considering the historical and cultural context in which institutions operate, and the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This highlights the interdependence of technological progress and institutional adaptation, as both are essential for sustained economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, it must be supported by appropriate institutions to ensure long-term sustainability. The implication is that technological advancements alone are insufficient for long-run economic growth; they must be accompanied by institutional changes that facilitate their integration into the economy.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate the integration of technological progress into the economy. Acemoglu et al. show that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutions, with some fostering investment and rule of law while others were extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North elaborates on this by highlighting that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is essential for managing the societal changes brought about by technological progress, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable over the long term.

Institutions also play a critical role in managing the economic and political dynamics that arise from technological advancements. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). North and Weingast support this by emphasizing that credible commitment underpins growth, as it ensures that economic rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon procedures (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment is essential for maintaining long-term economic growth, as it provides a stable framework for investment and development.

The interaction between institutions and economic development is further illustrated by the experiences of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by North, who shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were crucial for fostering economic growth and development, as they provided the necessary framework for investment and market exchanges. The historical evidence from capital markets shows that institutions per se are revolutionary, as they underpin economic growth and the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This historical evidence settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it shows that credible commitment and secure property rights are essential for long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
