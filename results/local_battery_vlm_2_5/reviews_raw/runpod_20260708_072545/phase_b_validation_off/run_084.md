The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics and economic history. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One position posits that institutions are pivotal in determining long-term economic outcomes. This perspective is based on the premise that well-functioning institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, foster economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and economic activity. Another position proposes that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs are more influential in shaping economic institutions than efficiency considerations. A third position suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The debate hinges on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as cultural beliefs and historical paths, in driving long-term economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, and their impact on secure property rights, is a key area of contention. The question of whether institutions are more important than other factors in shaping long-term economic growth remains unresolved. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and for informing policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, as they influence the transaction costs and property rights that underpin economic activity. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the establishment of institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment in colonies with low mortality rates led to significant economic development. These institutions fostered investment by securing property rights and lowering transaction costs, which are critical for long-term economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that institutions can also be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. This highlights that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth depends on their specific design and the interests they serve.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They note that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment to rules is central to the development of markets. This suggests that the design of institutions and the interests they serve are critical in determining their impact on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This indicates that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth can be influenced by the specific incentives and actions of the local population, highlighting the importance of adaptive institutional frameworks.

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development explores the historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa but does not explicitly focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These authors underscore the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. 'Good' institutions, as the literature calls them, matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This emphasis on institutional quality suggests that the effectiveness of colonial institutions in promoting economic growth can be influenced by the specific incentives and actions of the local population, highlighting the importance of adaptive institutional frameworks. The downstream implication is that colonial institutions may have had a profound impact on African economic development, but their effectiveness was contingent on local conditions and adaptive responses. This perspective is qualified by the observation that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Mokyr argues that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets supports this perspective by noting that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are pivotal for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements. Mokyr's analysis underscores that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets further emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, highlighting the importance of a strong political fabric in society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt to support and sustain these advancements. Mokyr also recognizes the possibility of coordination failures, suggesting that policies to rectify these failures are essential for maintaining economic improvement. This implies that the growth of useful knowledge, while a primary driver of economic growth, requires a supportive institutional environment to fully realize its potential.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust creates a pervasive environment where cooperation and trust are severely hindered, which are crucial for economic development. This extended period of exploitation disrupted the development of robust economic and social institutions, leaving Africa with a legacy of weak governance and limited economic growth. The historical context of the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, highlights the potential for economic growth when institutional frameworks are strong and supportive. This suggests that the institutional damage wrought by the slave trade is a persistent barrier to contemporary economic development.

Distributional conflicts are the primary drivers behind the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this view, arguing that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts. These conflicts arose from the struggle between different social groups to maintain or enhance their economic power. In this context, economic institutions were the outcomes of power struggles among elites and other social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) elaborate on this by showing that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were structured to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in the formation of institutions that protected elite interests at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which institutions emerge, as these contexts can significantly influence their long-term impact on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate efficient economic transactions and enforce property rights. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This perspective is corroborated by Acemoglu et al. who highlight that European settlers established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, which were crucial for long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions not only provided a stable framework for economic activities but also ensured that the benefits of economic growth were distributed in a manner that encouraged further investment and development.

The efficiency of institutions is not the sole determinant of their persistence. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts play a significant role in shaping institutions, often leading to the maintenance of inefficient systems that benefit powerful groups at the expense of broader economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s observation that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which they emerge, as these contexts can significantly influence their long-term impact on economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. provides a clear empirical comparison that highlights the significance of institutions in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
