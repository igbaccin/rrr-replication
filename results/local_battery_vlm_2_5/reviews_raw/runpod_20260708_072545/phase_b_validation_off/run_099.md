The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among economists. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development over extended periods. One live disagreement centres around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to geographic factors or cultural beliefs, drive economic growth. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established in a region, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacts long-term economic performance. Others posit that the reversal of economic fortunes among former European colonies is primarily due to the institutional changes introduced by colonial powers. Additionally, there is a debate on whether the efficiency of institutions or distributional conflicts better explains the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The debate on institutions and economic growth is crucial for understanding the historical trajectories of different economies and for informing policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable development. This debate hinges on the interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, with scholars offering varying explanations for how and why institutions matter.

This interplay is vividly illustrated by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, was a significant milestone in this evolution. This revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This security was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change facilitated the development of economic institutions that enhanced market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction fell, while the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate grew as a proportion of GNP. This trend was driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutional changes was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. They emphasize that the political factors underpinning economic growth included the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor was the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to or bound by these rules. North (1989, p.6) extends this idea by profiling transaction costs in a growing economy, noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges.

These changes were not limited to Europe, as colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that European colonizers implemented different strategies in their colonies, which led to the establishment of either extractive or growth-enhancing institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with lower mortality rates, they created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, thus fostering long-run economic growth. This institutional divergence was driven by the mortality rates experienced by European settlers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional framework established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic development, as these institutions persisted even after independence. Notably, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that the long-run development of former colonies was influenced by the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic trajectories, as colonies with growth-enhancing institutions were better positioned to industrialize and achieve higher levels of economic development. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, even long after the colonial period has ended.

Similarly, Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes leading to significant economic divergences within Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The Black Death caused a massive decline in the population, which led to increased wages and a shift in the balance of power between labor and capital. This demographic shock created an environment where institutional changes could take root and flourish, particularly in regions like northwest Europe. Institutional flexibility was a key factor in enabling regions to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. For instance, in Holland, new towns could emerge and rural manufacturing could thrive under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional flexibility allowed regions to adapt to the post-Black Death economic landscape, fostering growth and development. However, Broadberry and Gupta note that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, as merchant distribution and finance contributed significantly to productivity in the traded goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This highlights that while institutional flexibility was important, the growth of the service sector also played a critical role in economic development. The interaction between institutional changes and sectoral shifts underscores the complex nature of economic growth during this period.

This period saw Ogilvie and Austin (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped primarily by distributional conflicts and efficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are merely efficiency-enhancing mechanisms. Ogilvie contends that institutions like serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by power struggles between different social groups, each seeking to secure their interests. Austin extends this logic by highlighting that colonial administrations, driven by fiscal security and material incentives, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic underscores the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, as local power structures and interests influenced institutional outcomes. Austin also notes that where European settlers were absent, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This qualification suggests that the role of distributional conflicts varied across different colonial contexts, influenced by the presence or absence of European settlers and the specific economic interests at play. The influence of distributional conflicts on economic institutions extends beyond pre-industrial Europe, shaping the institutional landscape in colonial settings as well.

These conflicts are evident in the findings of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) who share a finding that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional bias towards elites was facilitated by the concentration of political power and the lack of competitive pressures that could have fostered more inclusive economic policies. These societies were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception, which shaped the institutions that evolved. The persistence of inequality was reinforced by policies that protected elite interests, such as those that restricted land ownership and access to education. Moreover, the lack of competitive pressures in these societies allowed elites to maintain their dominant positions without significant challenges. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the broader implications of such institutional arrangements, emphasizing the impact of mistrust on economic development. The culture of mistrust that developed in these societies, partly due to the exploitative nature of their institutions, further hindered economic growth by undermining social capital and cooperation. This mistrust, in turn, affected the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that perpetuated inequality and stunted economic development.

This dynamic is further illustrated by the colonial impact on African economic development, which highlights how external governance structures influenced local institutional frameworks (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). This educational infrastructure, in turn, affected the broader institutional environment by fostering a more educated populace capable of engaging in economic activities that require higher levels of skill and knowledge. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this by pointing out that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were heavily influenced by factors such as industrial policy, capital investments, and military expenditure. This indicates that while colonial institutions laid the groundwork, post-colonial economic policies and expenditures significantly shaped the trajectory of African economic development. The interplay between colonial institutional legacies and post-independence economic policies underscores the complex nature of institutional influences on long-run economic growth in Africa.

This institutional influence is evident in the finding that Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This challenges the prevailing narrative that African economies were uniformly underdeveloped during this period. One specific mechanism driving this economic performance was the growth in export production, which was closely tied to foreign trade. This pattern was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Their reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over the period of colonial rule provides a more nuanced picture of economic development. This work highlights the importance of considering the distribution of income when assessing long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The implication of this historical perspective is that the economic performance of African economies was more dynamic and complex than previously thought, with significant variations across regions and time periods. This complexity suggests that African economic development should be studied through a lens that accounts for these variations and through a single, monolithic narrative.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on their ability to adapt to technological innovations, as Kuznets and Mokyr argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets posits that economic growth is driven by technological progress, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is crucial for sustained growth. Mokyr extends this idea by highlighting the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that institutional progress complements technological progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for managing the social and political tensions that arise from technological advancements. Mokyr cautions that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while technological innovations can drive economic growth, the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for sustained growth are more complex and less predictable. The coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors that Mokyr identifies as potential obstacles to sustained economic growth underscore the importance of robust institutional frameworks in managing the social and political dimensions of technological change. Kuznets and Mokyr's emphasis on the interplay between technology and institutions highlights the need for adaptive institutional frameworks that can support long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework necessary for sustained development. The establishment of robust institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment is pivotal for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.9). The historical development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, illustrates how secure property rights and low transaction costs can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutions create an environment where long-term capital markets can flourish, which is essential for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The presence of such institutions fosters a climate of trust and predictability, which is necessary for investment and economic activity (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Austin's work highlights that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This nuance is crucial because it shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political changes, and that different historical contexts can lead to different institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the fact that they are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize their own interests over long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic is evident in the context of colonialism, where institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of extractive institutions in some former colonies highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The importance of institutions is also evident in the new economic history of Africa, where the quality of institutions is directly linked to economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Good institutions provide the necessary incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is consistent with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and that the creation of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie's work shows that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, emphasizing the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The shared finding across these sources is that institutions are central to economic growth, and that the specific nature of these institutions can significantly impact long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations versus distributional conflicts. Austin and North agree that institutions are crucial for economic growth, but they differ in their emphasis on the role of efficiency versus distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth settles this disagreement by showing that efficient institutions can drive long-term economic development (North 1989, p.9).
