The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with the stakes being the fundamental understanding of economic development. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. These scholars often emphasize the importance of institutions that protect property rights, enforce contracts, and provide a stable political environment. Others, however, contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that geographic, cultural, or technological factors may play equally or more significant roles. These competing views highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic growth over the long term. To understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it is crucial to examine the mechanisms through which institutions operate and the conditions under which they are most effective. This is particularly important for understanding the historical and contemporary factors that have influenced economic development in different regions of the world. Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, but they do so from different perspectives.

The reduction in transaction costs and establishment of secure property rights in England during the 17th century, as argued by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), set a precedent for how institutional changes can foster long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This decline reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional development facilitated market exchanges and lowered costs per transaction. The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate grew as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and that the rules governing economic exchange are effectively enforced. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling. The institutional changes in England during the 17th century were revolutionary. They significantly impacted economic growth by reducing transaction costs and establishing secure property rights.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies that led to the establishment of diverse institutions in their colonies. These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The type of institution established was influenced by European mortality rates; high mortality led to extractive institutions, while low mortality encouraged more inclusive institutions. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth. This suggests that the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers could lock countries into paths of underdevelopment if not accompanied by subsequent institutional reforms.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the finding that African economic development is influenced by institutional changes that affect public goods provision (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes is by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This institutional framework can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing the distribution of income and the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The persistence of poverty in Africa is due to historical or geographical conditions and the structural effects of colonial institutions on long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Therefore, the institutional legacies of colonial rule play a critical role in shaping the long-run economic growth of African countries.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) further illustrate the enduring effects of historical institutional legacies, finding that the slave trades in Africa have had a direct adverse effect on economic development. The slave trade disrupted African societies and fostered a culture of mistrust. This culture of mistrust, which persists to this day, is a significant factor in the reduced economic development of African countries. The cultural effects of the slave trade, particularly the erosion of trust, are at least as important as the economic and political disruptions caused by the slave trade. This mistrust undermines economic development by hindering cooperation and investment, both of which are crucial for long-term economic growth. The slave trade's impact on trust is so significant that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, illustrating the profound and lasting effects of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.19) supports this by noting that the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This highlights the importance of strong institutions in facilitating economic development, even in the face of historical disruptions.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) traces how the accumulation of knowledge over time has been the engine behind economic development. This technological progress requires institutional adjustments to facilitate its efficient use. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological progress and ensuring that economic growth does not weaken the political fabric of society. Institutions in this context support and complement technological progress. This relationship between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby supporting the sustainability of economic growth. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth, even if they are not the primary driver.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) build on this by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They propose that distributional conflicts offer a more compelling explanation for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the adoption of growth-favorable institutions was a function of exogenous factors and endogenous processes driven by local dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on this by arguing that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency goals, highlighting instead the role of power struggles and distributional concerns. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also acknowledges the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they are not the sole determinant of institutional outcomes, and a comprehensive analysis must consider multiple factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with significant contributions from both historical and contemporary analyses. Institutions fundamentally influence economic outcomes by defining the rules that govern economic interactions. North argues that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s work, which highlights that institutions shape economic growth by determining the incentives for investment and innovation (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Thus, the establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering economic development.

The specific nature of institutions matters greatly. Acemoglu et al. show that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies adopting extractive institutions that hindered long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly emphasizes that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in determining economic performance. The historical evidence from colonial experiences illustrates that the type of institutions implemented can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also shaped by the interplay between power dynamics and distributional concerns. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin builds on this by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). These insights highlight that institutions are tools for economic efficiency and are shaped by power struggles and the need to manage distributional conflicts. The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and colonial administrations demonstrates that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including power dynamics and distributional concerns.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the historical evidence from colonial experiences and pre-industrial Europe. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for fostering economic development (North 1989, p.6). The precise impact of institutions on economic growth remains a subject of debate, with some scholars arguing that distributional conflicts are a key determinant of institutional outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from colonial experiences shows that the type of institutions implemented can have lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory, with extractive institutions often hindering long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
