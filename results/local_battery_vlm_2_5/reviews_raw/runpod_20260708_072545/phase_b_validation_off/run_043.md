The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, as the rules of the game, can either facilitate or hinder economic progress. This debate pits those who emphasize the importance of institutions against those who point to other factors, such as geography or cultural attitudes. A central disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, particularly those introduced during colonial periods, have influenced economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the economic trajectories of colonies, while others contend that the reversal of economic fortunes among former colonies is better explained by other factors. A key point of contention is whether the impact of institutions is direct or mediated by other variables, such as cultural attitudes or geographic endowments. This review will examine how institutions have shaped economic growth, focusing on the mechanisms through which these effects occur. Institutions matter in economic growth because they can either facilitate or hinder the development of markets and economies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutions imposed by colonial powers had a profound impact on the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. The nature of these institutions was largely determined by the European colonizers' strategies, which varied significantly based on factors such as mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as in parts of Africa, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. For example, in the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states that focused on resource extraction rather than fostering local economic development. In contrast, in colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth and development. The institutional legacy of colonialism, therefore, played a crucial role in determining the economic fortunes of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the institutions established during colonialism were not necessarily designed to promote economic development but rather to maximize the benefits for the colonizers. This institutional design had long-lasting effects on the economic trajectories of the colonies, influencing factors such as industrialization and overall economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th-century England exemplifies this dynamic. These institutions dramatically declined the cost of transacting by increasing the security of long-term property rights. This financial revolution enabled economic growth by facilitating a long-term capital market. The political model that underpinned this transformation involved trading certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, a strategy born out of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) explain that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets of the time. The regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also ensured the enforcement and adaptability of these rules. The extent to which these institutional changes were revolutionary is debated. Recent research has upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that institutional changes in England might not have been as groundbreaking as previously thought. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not a significant hindrance to economic growth. This challenges the notion that specific institutional developments in England were uniquely responsible for its economic success.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on formal education compared to other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of these institutions was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa, but it was particularly effective in areas where British rule was established. The development of formal education under British rule was not merely a byproduct of colonial exploitation but a deliberate policy aimed at creating a local elite that could support the colonial administration. This policy had long-term effects on the educational landscape of sub-Saharan Africa, as it laid the foundation for the development of modern educational systems in the region. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide historical national accounting data that show the economic impact of these educational developments, highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that the institutional changes brought about by British rule had a tangible economic impact, contributing to the development of a more educated and economically productive population. The persistence of inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that the benefits of formal education were not evenly distributed (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) both focus on the period from 1500 to 1800, examining economic development through wage data and urbanization rates, rather than the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This finding challenges the notion of an 'industrious revolution' in Western Europe during this period. Pamuk attributes this shift to Holland's institutional flexibility, which allowed for the rapid response to new economic opportunities. This suggests that while institutional factors may have played a role in regional economic shifts, the broader relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth remains complex. Institutional flexibility, as seen in Holland, facilitated economic development by enabling new towns and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living, as noted by van Zanden, indicates that institutional factors alone do not guarantee sustained economic growth. Instead, the interaction between institutional flexibility and other economic factors, such as urbanization and commercialization, may be crucial for long-run economic development.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are mutually reinforcing drivers of economic growth, with institutions facilitating the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both underscore that technological advancements require complementary institutional changes to sustain economic growth. Mokyr elaborates that without these institutional progressions, the benefits of technological advancements may be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, thus limiting the sustainability of economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological innovations drive economic growth, the institutional environment must remain flexible and adaptive to fully realize the potential benefits of these innovations.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust within African societies (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, in turn, has had profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering economic growth. However, Austin also notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that the impact of historical legacies is not deterministic but rather subject to local agency and adaptation (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view suggests that while the slave trade has left a lasting imprint on African societies, the path to economic development is not preordained but can be shaped by contemporary policies and actions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent economic trajectories of countries. Institutions, as argued by North, are the rules of the game in a society that shape economic performance (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as seen in the development of the Bank of England, illustrates how institutions can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al., who contend that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current economic outcomes. For instance, colonies with high European mortality rates often developed extractive institutions that persisted and hindered economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, often maximized rents for colonists rather than promoting long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical legacies of colonial institutions continue to influence contemporary economic development. Austin argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that the impact of historical legacies is not deterministic but subject to local agency and adaptation (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these historical institutions underscores their enduring impact on economic development. For example, the slave trade left a lasting imprint on African societies, contributing to a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, highlighting the deep and enduring influence of historical institutions on contemporary economic and social outcomes. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development by undermining the trust necessary for market exchanges and political stability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The extractive institutions established in high-mortality colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al., provide a clear example of how institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists hindered long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence, coupled with the enduring impact of mistrust on economic development, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, demonstrates the critical role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories. The empirical comparison of former European colonies, as presented by Acemoglu et al., further highlights the institutional factors that contributed to the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
