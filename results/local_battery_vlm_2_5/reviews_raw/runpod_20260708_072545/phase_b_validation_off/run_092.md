The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating both the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth and the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors. One live disagreement centres on the nature and significance of institutions in economic development. On one side, some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are pivotal for fostering economic growth. These institutions provide the necessary framework for investment and market exchanges, reducing transaction costs and encouraging long-term economic activity. On the other side, other scholars contend that the institutional framework alone does not determine economic outcomes, but rather that the distribution of resources and power within societies plays a more critical role. This debate highlights the complexity of economic growth, where institutions are just one piece of a larger puzzle that includes cultural beliefs, political structures, and economic policies. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one of many interdependent factors. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for long-term economic growth, with their impact extending beyond neoclassical theories' focus on population and savings. North illustrates this dynamic through the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments reduced transaction costs and enhanced property rights' security. This institutional development facilitated a long-term capital market, which was critical for economic growth. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional setups in colonies, with some establishing extractive states that hindered growth. These findings suggest that the type of institutions established during critical historical periods can have lasting effects on economic development. For example, colonies with high mortality rates tended to develop extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This underscores the enduring influence of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting that the initial institutional framework can shape a country's economic trajectory for centuries.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that economic growth is deeply influenced by the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights. They propose that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering economic growth. This institutional framework ensured that individuals could confidently invest in and reap the benefits of their endeavors, thereby stimulating economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate this with evidence from capital markets, showing that the success of these markets was underpinned by a political regime committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. This commitment to institutional stability and property rights created an environment conducive to long-term economic growth. While North and Weingast focus on the political factors that supported economic growth, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that a comprehensive understanding of institutional evolution is essential for economic historians. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions against overemphasizing shared beliefs as institutions, noting that distributional conflicts often drive the development of core economic institutions. This highlights a nuanced view of institutional evolution, where both political commitment and distributional dynamics play crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie's perspective underscores the complexity of institutional change, suggesting that while secure property rights are important, the broader social and political context in which they operate must also be considered. This interplay between institutional frameworks and broader social dynamics is critical for understanding the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share a view that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to restrict competition and contribute to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This inequality is not just a matter of wealth distribution but also extends to political power and human capital. The nature of these institutions is shaped by the initial factor endowments of the societies. For instance, New World societies that were characterized by an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, often saw the emergence of institutions that reinforced the power of elites, limiting opportunities for broader economic participation. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often disadvantaged marginalized groups. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, reinforcing their subordinate status through legal frameworks that favored men (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This pattern of institutional development, which favors elites, has significant downstream implications for economic growth. For example, in Africa, the slave trades had a profound impact on the development of institutions that hindered economic progress. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the slave trades disrupted social and economic structures, leading to institutional frameworks that were less conducive to growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional legacy persists, affecting contemporary economic development.

Technological and knowledge-driven advancements, Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue, are crucial for sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress has required institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments have been necessary to resolve conflicts arising from the introduction of new technologies, ensuring that economic growth is not hindered by social or political disruptions. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further qualifies this by suggesting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks underscores the importance of a coordinated approach to economic development.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as articulated by Austin and Hopkins, explores the historical paths and institutional evolution of African development without directly linking to the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin's work highlights the complexity of institutional evolution, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This complexity is further illustrated by Hopkins, who emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective suggests that the institutional frameworks in Africa, shaped by historical paths and colonial impacts, play a crucial role in economic development. However, Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that the impact of colonial institutions on African development is not uniform. This nuanced view of institutional evolution in Africa highlights the need for a more contextual understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) found that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This positive impact was driven by the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This institutional framework fostered a more conducive environment for education, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were shaped by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. This pattern suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule continued to influence economic performance even after independence, as the new governments had to manage the resources and infrastructure left behind by the colonial powers. The high levels of military spending in the post-independence period indicate that the new governments were dealing with regional instability and domestic dissent, which were often rooted in the institutional frameworks established during the colonial era. This suggests that the institutional impacts of colonial rule on African development were not limited to the colonial period but continued to shape economic performance in the post-colonial era.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with van Zanden, examine the economic divergence between Europe and Asia, noting that the Great Divergence was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This divergence was driven by differences in economic development patterns, with north-western Europe experiencing high productivity in the traded goods sector due to merchant distribution and finance. This sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. The uneven distribution of early modern development's fruits is evident in the larger surplus over basic food needs yielded by the wages of skilled labourers compared to unskilled labourers. This pattern suggests that institutional changes, such as those in north-western Europe, led to investment in agriculture, which in turn resulted in high agricultural labour productivity. Van Zanden shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This implies that while institutional changes may have driven productivity, they did not necessarily translate into a uniform improvement in living standards across Europe. The shift in economic development towards the countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England, further underscores the regional variability in economic growth and its impacts. The focus on north-western Europe and the North Sea countries highlights the role of geography and institutional factors in shaping economic development, with England becoming the most expensive country after 1740 due to changes in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical trajectories of European countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate that European settlers established varying institutions based on mortality rates in colonies, with some enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment while others created extractive states. This institutional variance directly influenced current income per capita. Institutions are not merely static frameworks but dynamic entities shaped by political power and economic incentives. North (1989, p.6) highlights that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. These institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering a more robust economy. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 exemplify how institutional changes can lead to significant economic transformations (North 1989, p.9).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further underscored by the historical evidence from colonial settings. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local dynamics. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic opportunities highlights the complex nature of institutional influence on economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective is shared by Ogilvie (2007, p.3), who advocates for incorporating institutions into the long-term research program of economic historians. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding is that institutions matter for economic growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not solely about efficiency but also about the distribution of power and resources. The historical evidence from colonial settings and the new economic history of Africa settles this disagreement by showing that institutions shaped by political power and economic incentives can either foster growth or hinder it, depending on how they are designed and enforced. The measurement of institutional quality and the empirical comparison of different institutional frameworks across regions provide a clear resolution to the debate.
