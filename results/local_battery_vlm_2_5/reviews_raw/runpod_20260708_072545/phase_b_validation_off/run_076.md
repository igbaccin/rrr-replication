The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with implications for understanding both historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules and informal norms, significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, constraining behavior, and facilitating or hindering economic activity. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in creating stable property rights and enforcing contracts, and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or cultural differences, are more determinative. Additionally, there is a debate about the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate, with some scholars focusing on the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions. This debate is crucial for understanding historical patterns of economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth in developing economies. Institutions matter because they determine the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior, and understanding their role is essential for crafting policies that can foster sustainable economic growth. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to debates about the drivers of economic development and the effectiveness of policy interventions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. For instance, low mortality rates in regions like the United States and Australia encouraged settlers to establish inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment. High mortality rates in areas like the Congo and the Gold Coast resulted in extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These colonial institutions persisted over time, shaping the long-term economic trajectories of these regions. This persistence is crucial because it highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to affect economic development long after the colonial period. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are consistently linked to current economic outcomes. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic outcomes is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role, other factors such as industrialization and the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities also contributed to long-run economic development.

The institutional developments in Europe, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional evolution was a critical driver of economic change. This security was essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. North and Weingast further emphasize that the political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement were crucial for economic growth. The English financial revolution, with its new financial instruments, facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction. This institutional framework supported a growing economy by enabling increased specialization and division of labor. The impact of these institutions varied across Europe. Recent research has challenged traditional interpretations by showing that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was stronger than previously thought. This suggests that while institutional changes in England were significant, they were not the sole determinant of economic growth. The elaborate bureaucratic structure in France, often seen as a hindrance, did not necessarily impede economic development. This implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and influenced by a variety of factors, including the specific institutional arrangements and the broader economic context.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations, driving long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) share this view. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to accommodate technological changes. These adjustments are essential for sustaining economic growth over long periods. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by noting the importance of reducing access costs to useful knowledge through formal institutions. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments involves adopting new technologies and creating an environment where these technologies can be effectively utilized. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological advancements do not lead to social or political instability. Mokyr (2005, p.54) extends this idea by suggesting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological advancements are the driving force behind economic growth, they need to be supported by a robust institutional framework that can adapt to and integrate these advancements. This dynamic relationship underscores the importance of both technological progress and institutional adjustments in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hopkins argues that colonial rule in Africa has had a profound impact on the continent's economic development, influencing local institutional frameworks that shaped long-term socio-economic trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins and Frankema both note that the governance structures imposed by colonial powers altered the incentives and opportunities for investment, which in turn affected the development of formal education and other institutions. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa has underscored the importance of institutions in development, showing that "good" institutions provide incentives for investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema adds that the implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule changed the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and incentives created by the colonial administration (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic illustrates how external governance structures can reshape local institutions, with Hopkins noting that these institutional changes are crucial for understanding Africa's economic trajectory. For example, the role of colonial powers in shaping education systems reveals how external influences can either facilitate or hinder institutional development, with Frankema observing that British rule was more conducive to formal education development in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This finding suggests that the specific characteristics of colonial governance can have lasting effects on institutional quality and economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how different colonial experiences influenced African development.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on African economic development highlight how the legacy of mistrust persists and affects contemporary trust levels, which in turn influences economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2)). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply rooted in individuals' internal norms of mistrust. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, significantly outlasting the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to 1960. This prolonged period of slave trades had profound and lasting effects on Africa's economic development. Nunn further argues that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the presence of strong institutions facilitated trade and economic development. The legacy of mistrust from the slave trade may have hindered the development of such institutions in other parts of Africa, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) explore how institutions in societies with high inequality in the New World favored elites through policies that restricted competition, thus perpetuating inequality. They show that differences in factor endowments across New World societies led to varying degrees of inequality from the outset. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) highlight that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was underway before 1800 and was driven by factors including merchant distribution, finance, and the role of the service sector in economic growth. The uneven distribution of economic gains in early modern Europe suggests that the benefits of development were not uniformly shared. This uneven distribution likely reinforced existing inequalities, as those with greater wealth and power were better positioned to capitalize on new economic opportunities. Consequently, the institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies tended to favor the elite, perpetuating a cycle of inequality.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) jointly argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that Africa's poverty is a result of long-term historical factors. The colonial period, in particular, saw varied institutional responses to local economic and political dynamics. For instance, colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, and adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local actions. This adaptive behavior, however, was not uniform across Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this view by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, showing that economic conditions varied significantly over time and across regions. This variability challenges the notion of a persistent, uniform poverty driven by structural impediments. This suggests that while African poverty may not be a persistent historical phenomenon, the legacies of colonial institutions continue to influence economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonial rule and its enduring effects. Institutions established during colonial periods often favored European interests, which had long-term implications for economic development. These institutions were not merely neutral frameworks but were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective aligns with the broader argument that institutions matter significantly in economic growth and development. The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as seen in the development of the Bank of England, illustrates how institutional changes can foster economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from colonial rule and the financial revolution in England both underscore the critical role of institutions in economic development. This shows that the legacies of colonial institutions continue to influence economic outcomes, and that institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder long-run economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, as seen in seventeenth-century England, further highlights how secure property rights and credible commitments underpin economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets and the political factors underpinning economic growth both show that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa also informs institutional reforms and helps eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, reinforcing the idea that good institutions matter for economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence from the reversal of fortune thesis and the institutional changes introduced by colonialism both show that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significant, and that the legacies of colonial institutions continue to influence economic outcomes.
