The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions, as distinct from other factors like geography or culture, are the primary drivers of economic development. The disagreement hinges on two key positions. One position holds that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they shape the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior. This perspective emphasizes how institutions, such as property rights and legal systems, can either facilitate or hinder economic development. The other position argues that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geographic, cultural, or other variables may be more determinative. This perspective often highlights the role of exogenous factors like climate or natural resources in shaping economic outcomes. The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy, as understanding the role of institutions can inform strategies for economic development and poverty reduction. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers of economic growth, then policies aimed at institutional reform could be pivotal in fostering development. If other factors are more important, different policy approaches might be necessary. The crux of the matter is whether institutions are the key to unlocking long-term economic growth, or whether they are secondary to other determinants.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and new financial instruments was pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this process, as it led to a significant decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional change was a response to fiscal crises and involved a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is crucial for economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and increases the efficiency of transactions. This institutional framework underpins the development of a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic growth. The institutional changes in England were critical in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security is a key mechanism through which long-run economic growth is achieved. The implications of this mechanism are profound, as it suggests that institutional reforms aimed at reducing transaction costs and securing property rights can be pivotal in fostering economic development. The effectiveness of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange, as well as the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that Europeans established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks was driven by differences in European mortality rates, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of the colonies. The extractive institutions established in regions with high mortality rates hindered long-term economic development by discouraging investment and failing to protect property rights. This institutional legacy persists, influencing current economic outcomes. The impact of these institutions varies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic growth. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic development trajectories, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional changes. The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic opportunities reveals how the long-term effects of colonialism are deeply rooted in the institutional legacies it left behind.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths and that African poverty is a more recent development. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that colonial administrators adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which shaped the development of institutions in Africa. Colonial administrators, such as Governor-General Clifford, opted for higher production equilibriums to encourage indigenous commodity production where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior shows that institutional changes were a response to local conditions, challenging the idea that colonial institutions were uniformly detrimental to economic growth. The historical paths of institutional evolution in Africa reveal that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more nuanced than a simple reversal of fortune thesis suggests. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for British African colonies to assess the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional changes and their impact on economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) explain that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. The slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is substantial, with internal norms of mistrust playing a crucial role. This mistrust, in turn, impedes economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. Nunn highlights that the slave trade's duration and scale dwarfed that of colonial rule, spanning nearly 500 years compared to colonial rule's approximately 75 years. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted African societies' economic and social institutions, hindering their ability to develop and sustain trade networks. The absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets, as seen in regions like the Kongo kingdom, further exacerbated the economic decline. Trust, a critical component of economic development, is significantly reduced by the historical legacy of the slave trade, creating a persistent barrier to growth.

Technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. The growth of useful knowledge, a critical component of economic development, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that the accumulation of useful knowledge is essential for sustained economic improvement. This growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. The advancement of human knowledge, particularly epochal innovations, constitutes dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spreads to a substantial part of the world. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and on a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. The growth of useful knowledge necessitates institutional progress to complement technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. The growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress.

Distributional conflicts are argued to better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is most evident in the structure of guilds, which restricted competition and preserved the privileges of elites. The guild system, for instance, served to limit market entry and control prices, thereby protecting the interests of established artisans and merchants. This dynamic is also observed in the New World societies, where institutions were crafted to reinforce existing inequalities. These mechanisms were deeply rooted in the political and social structures that maintained the status quo. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the entrenched interests of those in power, who used institutional frameworks to secure their positions. This interplay between initial conditions and institutional design underscores the complexity of economic development, where distributional conflicts are a key driver but not the sole determinant.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they establish the rules and structures that govern economic interactions and incentivize investments. Acemoglu et al. show that the institutions Europeans established in their colonies were instrumental in determining the economic trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The type of institutions that were established, whether inclusive or extractive, directly influenced the economic outcomes of the colonies. North and Weingast similarly argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic development.

The role of institutions extends beyond economic factors alone. Distributional conflicts also play a significant role in shaping institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that the initial factor endowments of colonies, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, played a crucial role in shaping these institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions were shaped by the interests of powerful groups who sought to maximize their rents, often at the expense of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interplay between initial conditions and institutional design underscores the complexity of economic development, where distributional conflicts are a key driver but not the sole determinant. In the case of colonial institutions, European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions, depending on the mortality rates and the initial factor endowments of the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had significant long-run effects on the economic trajectories of these regions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. While institutions are crucial for providing the necessary framework for economic development, they are also shaped by distributional conflicts and initial conditions. The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that the type of institutions established had significant long-run effects on economic outcomes. The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which initial conditions, such as mortality rates and factor endowments, versus distributional conflicts, shaped the institutions that were established. The empirical comparison of colonial institutions, using measures of income per capita and the efficiency of the judiciary, settles the disagreement by showing that the institutions established by European settlers had significant long-run effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2).
