The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they operate in conjunction with other factors. One position holds that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts, are crucial for fostering economic growth. This view emphasizes the importance of credible commitment and the rule of law in creating a stable environment for investment and economic activity. Another position argues that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on broader historical and geographical factors, suggesting that institutions alone cannot explain long-run economic growth. The disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they are shaped by or shape other determinants of growth. The stakes are high because understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting economic development and reducing poverty. The central question is whether institutions are the driving force behind economic growth or if they are merely one piece of a complex puzzle. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. and North suggests that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth, but the nature of this role and its interaction with other factors remain subjects of ongoing debate.

Acemoglu et al. and Frankema argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutions established by European colonizers were shaped by mortality rates and the settlers' intent to extract resources, which in turn determined the economic trajectory of the colonies. This extractive approach was not conducive to long-run growth, as it did not encourage investment or enforce the rule of law. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than promoting long-run growth in the colonies. The downstream implication is that the long-run economic growth of former colonies was significantly affected by the extractive institutions established during colonial rule. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a position that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This stream proposes that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. The role of institutions in this context is to facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the effective integration of technological progress into the economy. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes and for ensuring that economic growth is sustainable. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that the growth of useful knowledge, while essential, requires a supportive institutional framework to translate into long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts peacefully. This suggests that strong, stable institutions are necessary to manage the social and political challenges that accompany technological advancements. This indicates that while institutions are important, their effectiveness in supporting economic growth can vary over time and may be subject to setbacks. This suggests that the relationship between technological advancements and economic growth is complex and dependent on the institutional environment.

This was evident in north-western Europe, where high productivity in the traded goods sector arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the institutional environment, shaped by historical events like the Black Death, significantly influenced economic development trajectories. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) caution that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This uneven distribution of economic benefits highlights the complex interplay between institutions, historical events, and economic growth.

The literature on economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa highlights that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). One specific mechanism through which this economic growth was achieved was the expansion of foreign trade and export production. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that the growth patterns in these economies were closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. This suggests that access to international markets played a significant role in driving economic development during the colonial period. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, indicating that the economic conditions were not uniformly poor and that there was variability in economic performance across different regions. This variability suggests that while foreign trade was a significant driver of economic growth, the effectiveness of this driver was mediated by local conditions and institutional frameworks. The implications of this finding are that policies aimed at fostering economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa should consider the specific local conditions and institutional environments of different regions.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share a finding that colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a unique impact on educational development, distinct from other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn 2008, p.2). This impact was mediated through the introduction of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African leaders and families. The British colonial administration, in particular, fostered an environment where formal education was seen as advantageous, leading to a higher demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This demand was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both the macro and micro levels, influenced by the new institutional frameworks implemented under British rule. The British colonial administration's emphasis on formal education contrasts with the experiences in other colonial societies, where institutions often favored elites and restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). However, while British rule did create conditions favorable for educational development, the persistence of inequality and the legacy of the slave trades meant that these benefits were not uniformly distributed. Nunn highlights that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on economic development, creating conditions that hindered the widespread benefits of formal education (Nunn 2008, p.19). These findings suggest that while British colonial rule had a more benign impact on educational development compared to other colonial powers, the broader historical context, including the legacy of the slave trades, complicated the pathway to economic growth through education.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay of historical factors shaping African economic outcomes. They stress that the thesis conflates distinct historical periods and trajectories. This simplification obscures the nuanced roles of institutions and economic policies. This is evident in colonial administrations that adjusted their policies in response to local incentives. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments, in some cases, encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This dynamic illustrates how institutions can evolve in response to local economic conditions, challenging the notion that colonial rule uniformly hindered African economic development. This institutional flexibility is crucial for understanding the varied economic outcomes across Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this perspective by emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. He argues that 'good' institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they create an environment conducive to investment. This perspective is supported by the new economic history of Africa, which highlights the importance of institutional reforms in eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This means that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth is contingent on their design and implementation. This contingency underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth, one that considers the specific historical and economic contexts in which they operate.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly lowered transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional change reflected increased security in property rights, which is crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself.
