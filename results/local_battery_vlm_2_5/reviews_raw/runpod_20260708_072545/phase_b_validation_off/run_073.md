The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At its core, the debate revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic performance, arguing that institutions governing property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability are crucial for sustained growth. This view is supported by scholars who highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating long-term economic development. Another position, however, suggests that the influence of institutions is mediated by other factors, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, which shape the effectiveness of institutional frameworks. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and the need to consider a broader range of factors in understanding economic growth. The interaction between institutions and other determinants of economic development, such as geography and population density, adds further layers to this debate. A key point of contention is the extent to which institutions operate independently or in conjunction with other variables to drive economic growth. This interplay is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where the institutions imposed by colonial powers had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled, with ongoing research seeking to unravel the complex web of factors that influence economic development. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one piece of a larger puzzle remains a critical area of inquiry.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both highlight the transformative role of new financial instruments and political institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering long-run economic growth. England's 17th century saw the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which "led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting" (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was underpinned by increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework was crucial for economic growth, as evidenced by the remarkable success of capital markets in England during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political factors underpinning this success included the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and ensured that transaction costs were falling, even as the transactions sector's share of GNP grew. The emergence of political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided increasingly effective enforcement was a critical factor in this process. The success of England's capital markets during this period underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions established by colonial powers varied based on the colonists' mortality rates and their intentions for resource extraction. This variation in institutions led to divergent economic outcomes. High mortality rates in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while lower mortality rates in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand resulted in institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, in turn, shaped the long-run economic growth trajectories of these regions. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) show that the persistence of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies significantly affected current income per capita. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the notion that poverty in Africa has been a persistent historical phenomenon (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They highlight that periods of economic growth in African economies during the early twentieth century were significantly tied to foreign trade and export markets. Broadberry and Gardner identify that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors influenced the economic performance of African economies, demonstrating that growth fluctuated in response to external market conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg further emphasize the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the impact of historical conditions on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg acknowledge that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered, highlighting the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the economic dynamics of the period.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a persistent culture of mistrust, which has had lasting economic effects (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust persists as a result of the slave trade's influence on both the external environment and internal norms, with the latter being at least twice as impactful as the former. This mistrust is a persistent cultural legacy of the slave trade, which continues to affect African economic development by influencing both the external environment and internal norms. The paper finds that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the deep-seated nature of this cultural legacy (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Despite the importance of this cultural legacy, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's argument complements Nunn and Wantchekon's findings by suggesting that the effects of the slave trade on African economic development are complex and multifaceted, and that a more nuanced understanding of these effects is needed. This implies that the legacy of the slave trade on African economic development is a complex interplay of cultural, historical, and institutional factors.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the pivotal role of technological advancements in fostering economic development, emphasizing that institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to integrate new technologies into existing economic systems. This process can generate conflicts that must be resolved to sustain economic growth. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that coordination failures can impede technological progress, necessitating policies to rectify these issues. The growth of economic reasonableness, or the ability to make rational economic decisions, is crucial for sustaining technological progress. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community, plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr qualifies this by pointing out that the growth of economic reasonableness is not monotonic or irreversible, like technological progress. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on the ability to make rational economic decisions and the effectiveness of institutions in resolving conflicts. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is complex, and the success of economic growth hinges on the ability to manage this interplay effectively.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations. Instead, it posits that institutions are the outcome of political struggles between different social groups. This argument is grounded in the idea that elites have the power to shape institutions in ways that benefit them, even if these institutions are not efficient. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were maintained because they served the interests of the elite. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate this mechanism by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to the creation of institutions that perpetuate inequality, even if they are not optimal for economic growth. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is not without its complexities. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they are not the only one. Other factors, such as cultural beliefs and technological progress, also play a role in shaping institutions. Therefore, while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor in shaping institutions, they are part of a larger web of factors that interact in complex ways to shape economic institutions.

Distributional conflicts play a significant role in shaping institutions that influence long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the extraction of resources by European colonists led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as these institutions underpinned the credibility of commitments to property rights and market enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This shows that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor in shaping institutions, they interact with other factors such as the credibility of commitments to property rights and market enforcement.

The role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions is another important factor. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and these beliefs can influence the adoption of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This indicates that cultural beliefs and the security of property rights are intertwined in shaping institutions that influence long-run economic growth.

The interaction between institutions and technological progress is also a key factor in shaping long-run economic growth. This shows that technological progress and institutional evolution are interconnected in shaping long-run economic growth.

The historical evidence of the English financial revolution shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al., who emphasize the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions, and Austin, who argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The English financial revolution provides a clear example of how the interaction between institutions and technological progress can lead to long-run economic growth, even in the presence of distributional conflicts.
