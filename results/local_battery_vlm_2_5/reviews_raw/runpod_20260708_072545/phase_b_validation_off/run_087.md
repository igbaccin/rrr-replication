The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some countries prosper while others stagnate, and how historical and political contexts influence economic trajectories. One live disagreement centres on the nature of institutions that drive growth. Some scholars posit that institutions promoting secure property rights and contract enforcement are crucial for economic development. Others suggest that the efficiency of institutions is less important than their distributional effects, which can either foster or hinder growth. Some scholars attribute the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, with geographic factors playing a lesser role. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as cultural beliefs or colonial legacies play a more significant role, remains contentious. This debate is particularly salient in the context of African economic development, where the interplay between institutions and growth has profound implications for policy and economic reform. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not just academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic development strategies.

The development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was pivotal because it increased the security of property rights over time, a factor essential for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, particularly those governing public choice, were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The evolution of these institutions was not just about the rules governing economic exchange but also about how these rules were enforced and could be changed. This commitment to rules was a critical political factor that underpinned economic growth and the development of markets. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the political factors supporting economic growth were as important as the economic rules themselves. The role of institutions in economic growth presents complexities. This variation underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions, a finding shared by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These authors argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by the interests of the elite, who sought to maintain their power and wealth through institutional arrangements that restricted competition and preserved inequality. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the creation of barriers to entry into the elite, which limited competition and allowed the elite to maintain their economic advantages. This mechanism was particularly effective in societies with high levels of inequality, where the elite had the power to shape institutions in their own interests. However, the persistence of inequality was not inevitable, as shown by the example of the Protestant Reformation, which led to a decline in guilds and a reduction in economic inequality. Ogilvie's analysis of the role of institutions in shaping economic growth highlights the importance of considering the distributional implications of institutional arrangements. The impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is not limited to pre-industrial Europe. In the New World, the institutions that evolved in societies with high levels of inequality also reflected the interests of the elite, who used their power to shape institutions that restricted competition and preserved inequality. This highlights the importance of considering the distributional implications of institutional arrangements in shaping economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. highlight that Europeans established different institutions in their colonies depending on mortality rates, creating either extractive or growth-enhancing institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development. This institutional framework, shaped by the interests of the colonizers, persisted and had long-lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy has been a significant factor in the economic trajectories of former colonies, with those that inherited extractive institutions experiencing slower economic growth. Acemoglu et al. also note that these institutions may have been shaped by politically powerful groups who feared losing their political power if there was institutional development, or who were reluctant to initiate change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, shaped by the interests of the colonizers, persisted and had long-lasting effects on economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. also document the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization, providing evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Colonial impacts on African economic development were not uniformly negative, particularly in the domain of education. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This phenomenon can be attributed to the interplay between missionary activities and local demand for education. African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were linked to new social and political institutions introduced by British colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This dynamic suggests that the institutional framework established by British colonial rule, which included support for missionary education, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. However, the extent to which this educational development translated into broader economic growth is qualified by the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates, leaving some questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Thus, while British colonial rule had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa, the long-term economic effects remain a subject of ongoing research.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, and institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of innovations. This institutional support is necessary for technological progress to translate into sustained economic growth. Mokyr's work underscores the importance of the growth of useful knowledge, suggesting that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress must accompany it to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Mokyr also acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic and irreversible as technological progress, implying that institutional progress is subject to setbacks and may require continuous adaptation. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates on the role of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, emphasizing that the political fabric of society must remain strong to avoid excessive costs. This suggests that the stability of the state is a critical factor in enabling the institutional adjustments necessary for technological progress to drive economic growth.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, and the economic performance of Europe and Asia during this period was shaped by regional shifts in economic development that were not fundamentally determined by institutions (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This sectoral shift was accompanied by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating that economic development led to increased living standards for some segments of the population (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This uneven distribution of benefits raises questions about the extent to which economic development led to improvements in the standard of living for all segments of the population.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) establish that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The slave trade's effect on trust operates through both internal and external channels, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This internal channel includes individuals' internal norms of mistrust, which are deeply ingrained and resistant to change. The external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, is also important but less significant. Nunn (2008, p.19) further supports this by noting the importance of centralized government and well-developed markets in facilitating trade and economic development. This suggests that the mistrust generated by the slave trade may have hindered the development of strong institutions and markets in Africa, further impeding economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade continues to affect African societies today, with mistrust undermining social cohesion and economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both highlight the intricate interplay of historical and institutional factors in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, challenging simplistic causal narratives about long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience of older industrial economies provide a more nuanced understanding of economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the specific historical contexts and institutional developments in Africa, rather than applying universal models. Austin (2008, p.2) further notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, indicating that institutional prescriptions could adapt to local economic and political actions. This adaptive capacity challenges the notion of rigid, externally imposed institutions and suggests that indigenous economic activity can influence institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that institutional quality can vary independently of ethnic diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that effective institutional reforms in Africa must consider the complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors, rather than relying on simplistic ethnic or cultural explanations.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives for investment and the provision of public goods. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is because the institutions introduced by European settlers varied widely, with some colonies receiving extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, while others were granted institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, which posits that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He contends that the institutional evolution of older industrial economies today is best understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's argument underscores the complexity of institutional development, highlighting that the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth varied across countries due to historical, economic, and political factors (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is further illustrated by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development by specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North's work shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This decline in transaction costs reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that exogenous cultural theories are applied on the micro-level to explain the behavior of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). She contends that the superior performance of European economies derived from distinctive European beliefs in changing the world through human intervention, and European values for diligence, thrift, tenacity, honesty, and tolerance, which were not shared by non-European cultures, and which gave rise to European institutions that were distinctively 'capitalistic' (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This argument is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They argue that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is debated, but there is agreement that institutions matter. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which varied widely across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that institutional quality can vary independently of ethnic diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that effective institutional reforms in Africa must consider the complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors, rather than relying on simplistic ethnic or cultural explanations. The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their impact on current income per capita, as well as the empirical comparison of institutional quality across different ethnic and cultural contexts, settles the debate by showing that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth.
