The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanation of persistent economic disparities between nations. One prominent position emphasizes the causal role of institutions in economic development, arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained growth. This perspective is grounded in the idea that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect individual and collective behavior. Alternative explanations challenge this position by highlighting other factors, such as geography, culture, and distributional conflicts. These rival explanations propose that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural or geographical factors and are not the primary drivers of economic growth. The debate centers on whether institutions are the cause or the effect of economic development, with implications for policy and theoretical understanding. The resolution of this debate requires a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic growth over the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the impact of colonial institutions on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates. In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality rates, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were set up. This institutional framework significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin suggests that the behavior of older industrial economies can be understood within the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors over time. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction is crucial for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.

This institutional framework, which secured property rights and lowered transaction costs, was pivotal in the long-run economic growth of 17th-century England, as argued by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29). This institutional development was driven by fiscal crises that necessitated trade-offs between rights and revenues, ultimately leading to more efficient property rights and effective enforcement. This institutional change involved political mechanisms that enforced and adapted economic rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) underscore that the degree to which a regime commits to these rules is a critical political factor. The English financial revolution's development was a complex process that involved economic institutions and the political framework that supported them. This institutional evolution was not merely a British phenomenon; France's pre-Revolutionary economic performance highlights that institutional changes were pivotal in shaping economic growth, even when bureaucratic structures were complex.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) extend this analysis to colonial contexts, showing that the institutions established by European powers significantly shaped the long-run economic trajectories of their former colonies. These institutions varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates. Where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. For example, in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans implemented institutions that prioritized resource extraction over local economic development. The resulting institutions were extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-run growth. This extractive institutional framework persisted and significantly influenced the economic outcomes of these colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was tied to the industrialization policies introduced during colonial rule. The legacy of these institutions continues to affect economic performance in many former colonies today.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. The economic growth in Europe was not driven by agricultural productivity alone. Instead, it was influenced by merchant distribution and finance, which played a crucial role in the traded goods sector. This growth was also unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled laborers. This uneven distribution of growth aligns with the findings of van Zanden (1999, p.9), who notes that economic development in Europe shifted towards the countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England. This shift led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader economic transformation.

Frankema and van Waijenburg challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa by examining historical economic data and colonial impacts (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments and that this view contrasts with recent literature emphasizing such impediments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) explore the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, highlighting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered. They reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further support this view by demonstrating the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. This work underscores that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. The data reveal that economic growth in these regions was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This historical perspective suggests that the economic development of African countries was dynamic and responsive to various factors, and was not uniformly constrained by structural impediments. The specific historical or geographical conditions that influenced long-term African welfare development remain a subject of ongoing debate.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) further complicate this picture by highlighting the adverse effects of the slave trades on African economic development. The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, left a lasting impact on African societies. This impact was both economic and cultural, fostering a pervasive mistrust that continues to influence social and economic interactions today. The slave trades disrupted social cohesion and undermined the development of strong, trust-based institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust, and is not fully explained by the trustworthiness of others. Nunn further elaborates on this point by highlighting how the slave trades impeded the development of robust economic and social institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African societies can be traced back to the disruptive effects of the slave trades, which prevented the establishment of stable trading networks and economic institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical legacy of mistrust and institutional weakness continues to shape African economic development, making it challenging for contemporary African economies to achieve sustained growth.

Technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, as technological improvements increase the stock of useful knowledge, which in turn enhances productivity and fuels economic expansion. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress requires institutional adjustments to facilitate the efficient use of new innovations. He notes that the pace and sectors affected by technological progress differ across regions and periods, necessitating corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological improvements might be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the importance of coordination failures. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where technological improvements spur productivity gains, and institutional frameworks facilitate the efficient use of these innovations.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenge institutional theories by arguing that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They posit that these conflicts shape institutions in ways that are not always conducive to economic growth, highlighting the importance of power dynamics and political interests. Austin (2008, p.2) points out that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, indicating that institutional prescriptions were responsive to local economic and political actions. This suggests that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adapted their policies in response to the incentives created by indigenous actions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates that institutions are deeply rooted in the social and political fabric of societies and are not merely efficiency-enhancing mechanisms. She argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts. These institutions were a product of power struggles and the need to maintain social order, and were not efficient. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional development, where institutions are shaped by the political and social contexts in which they emerge and are not just tools for economic growth. This complexity is further illustrated by Austin (2008, p.2), who notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary. This nuanced understanding is crucial for grasping how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth, depending on the specific historical and political context.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between political contexts and institutional design. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in colonies with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights how the political and social contexts in which institutions emerge significantly influence their nature and impact on economic growth. Austin further elaborates on this complexity by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He notes that European settlers' presence or absence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations, thereby shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding indicates that institutions evolve in response to historical and political contexts, which in turn affect economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast illustrate this through the development of the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War and reflected increased security of property rights, critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie complements this by emphasizing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape institutions and their impact on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and that their evolution is driven by political and social contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional framework was shaped by the political power dynamics of the time, where influential groups sought to maintain their control and extract resources (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North similarly highlights that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for economic growth. He shows that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges leads to falling costs per transaction, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The shared finding is that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, underscoring the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the empirical comparison of former European colonies and the specific historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as evidenced by the development of the English financial revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).
