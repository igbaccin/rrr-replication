The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can significantly influence economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior. The live disagreement centers around the nature of institutions that promote economic growth and the mechanisms through which they operate. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, while others focus on the role of distributional conflicts and the political dynamics that shape institutional outcomes. Additionally, there is debate over the extent to which colonial institutions have persisted and influenced contemporary economic development. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions can provide insights into why some countries achieve sustained economic growth while others remain mired in poverty.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, both of which were part of the English financial revolution, led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This decline was a direct result of increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political model outlined by North (1989, p.6) posits that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This model suggests that the development of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. This implies that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary and played a significant role in fostering economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, and this had a profound impact on the development of capital markets and the overall economic performance of the country.

The legacy of these institutional changes, however, is still felt today, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers was influenced by the mortality rates they faced in the colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth. This institutional framework, characterized by weak property rights and lack of investment incentives, persisted long after the colonial period, hindering economic development in these regions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the impact of these institutions can be measured across various dimensions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These institutional legacies continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, as they are deeply entrenched and resistant to change. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive not only because they do not encourage economic development but also because they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic growth, where the interests of colonizers and local elites can align in ways that perpetuate underdevelopment.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema, highlight that British colonial rule in Africa was more conducive to developing formal education than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This was driven by the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, creating a foundation for economic development. This educational investment, in turn, contributed to higher per capita incomes in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, Frankema qualifies this by noting that the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa were complex, involving a range of factors that went beyond the simple presence of British rule. The benefits of formal education were related to new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This underscores the intricate relationship between colonial institutions, education, and economic growth, where the interests of colonizers and local communities can align in ways that foster development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight that simplistic views of causation in African economic history often overlook the complex interplay of institutions and economic development. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions were the primary determinant of Africa's economic trajectory, oversimplifies the causal mechanisms at work. This perspective is shared by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to local economic and political actions. Colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on the incentives created by indigenous economic activities. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between colonial institutions and local economic actors underscores the importance of contextual factors in shaping institutional outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, but rather a result of specific historical and geographical conditions that have had a temporary effect on long-term welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view of institutional evolution challenges simplistic narratives and highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the factors driving economic growth in Africa.

This perspective, however, shifts the focus from efficiency to the power dynamics within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argues that these institutions were primarily driven by power struggles and the interests of elite groups, rather than by efficiency or productivity gains. For example, the persistence of serfdom can be attributed to the ability of landowners to maintain their control over the labor force, despite its economic inefficiency. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were designed to maximize economic output. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) provide a complementary perspective by showing that societies with greater inequality tended to develop institutions that advantaged elites, such as restrictive government policies that limited competition and maintained elite control. However, the implications of this inequality were not limited to economic outcomes; they also had profound social and political effects. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that economic historians should integrate institutions more deeply into their analyses, treating them as indispensable tools rather than mere accessories. This approach would allow for a more nuanced understanding of how distributional conflicts shaped economic institutions and, ultimately, long-term economic growth.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, suggesting that technological progress, alongside institutional and ideological adaptations, drives long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These adjustments are essential for resolving conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable without weakening the political fabric of society. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in harnessing the full potential of technological innovations for long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the diffusion of useful knowledge and mitigating coordination failures. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) present the argument that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks, which in turn significantly influenced current income per capita. This underscores the importance of institutions in harnessing the full potential of technological innovations for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and contributed to economic growth by reducing costs per transaction. These findings are consistent with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for long-term economic growth.

However, the role of institutions in economic growth is not straightforward. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with economic and political factors over time. The argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe is compelling (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the belief that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the importance of incorporating institutions into the long-term research programme of economic historians, which is crucial for understanding the dynamics of economic growth. The historical evidence shows that institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that influence institutional adoption and their impact on economic growth.
