The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others posit that institutions are merely a secondary factor, with other variables such as culture or geographic endowments playing a more significant role. This debate is crucial for policy, as it informs whether institutional reforms are the key to fostering economic development. In the 1980s, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argued that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Some scholars posit that institutions play a secondary role, with other variables exerting more significant influence.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) claim that institutions shape economic growth by influencing the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges. This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) elaborate on this mechanism by showing that European colonization strategies varied based on mortality rates, leading to different institutional outcomes. In colonies with high mortality, Europeans established extractive institutions that hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by noting that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were not conducive to long-run growth. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, persists and significantly affects current income per capita.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. They propose that technological progress and innovation are fundamental to sustained economic development, highlighting that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. This technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on the role of institutions in complementing technological progress, noting that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological and institutional progress is crucial for long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, which are inevitable during periods of rapid technological change and economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that the ability of institutions to adapt and manage conflicts arising from technological progress is essential for sustaining economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) illustrate how the effects of institutional changes introduced during colonialism varied widely across different regions and historical periods. This variation is evident in both the extent and the nature of the institutional changes that occurred in different colonial contexts. For example, in regions where European settlers were not present to compete for local labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of local conditions and colonial policies in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional framework established by colonial powers did not always foster long-term economic growth. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that the adaptability of institutions to local conditions is crucial for sustainable economic development. The success of colonial administrations in fostering economic growth depended on their ability to create a stable institutional environment that could respond to local needs and incentives. This adaptability is essential for long-run economic growth, as it allows institutions to evolve in response to changing economic conditions and technological progress.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa has fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to affect trust levels and potentially long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) identify that the slave trade's detrimental effects on African economic development stem from both recent events and colonialism, as well as the slave trade's enduring impact on trust. The slave trade's cultural impact on mistrust is rooted in the historical disruption of social and economic institutions, which reduced the incentives for cooperation and trust-building. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's effect on trust is primarily internal, working through individuals' norms of mistrust rather than external factors such as the rule of law. This internalization of mistrust can be traced to the slave trade's disruption of traditional African societies, which often relied on strong social networks and cooperative institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) further illustrates this point by highlighting how the slave trade targeted areas with well-developed markets and trading networks, such as the Kongo kingdom. The destruction of these institutions left a lasting impact on the ability of African societies to rebuild trust and foster economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the long-term effects of historical events on contemporary economic development, as the legacy of mistrust continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa.

The literature on colonial impacts reveals how African initiatives and exogenous factors influenced institutional change during colonial rule. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) illustrate that colonial rule was a complex interplay of African agency and external influences. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities. This is because the demand for missionary education depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. Frankema (2012, p.15) finds that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This finding is significant because it challenges the notion that all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts on African education systems. This is because British rule introduced new economic opportunities that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. By the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence, as shown by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9). This economic performance was driven by the growth of export production, which was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports. The timing and scale of this growth varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by influencing the investment in agriculture and the productivity of the traded goods sector. The benefits of this economic growth were not evenly distributed, as skilled laborers saw larger surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This uneven distribution of benefits highlights the complex interplay between institutions, economic growth, and social inequality.

The long-term structural changes and economic development in Europe were influenced by a complex interplay of factors, without directly addressing how institutions influenced these processes. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) both emphasise that long-term structural changes in Europe were driven by economic development and shifts in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. These structural changes were driven by shifts in economic development, which led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. This is evidenced by the fact that after 1740, England became the most expensive country, thanks to the abundant supply of Baltic grain. The institutional flexibility of regions like Holland enabled them to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, as evidenced by the emergence of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies. The implication is that while economic development led to structural changes, the institutional environment played a crucial role in shaping these changes, particularly in regions with high levels of institutional flexibility. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic development is not uniform and can vary significantly across different regions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutions themselves, are the fundamental drivers behind the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is particularly evident in the case of serfdom and guilds, where the institutional structures were shaped by the need to maintain social order and control, rather than by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights blocked the institutions that might have guaranteed property rights and contract enforcement. This mechanism highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to the persistence of institutions that favor the elite, even if they are not efficient. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument by showing that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were shaped to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective is qualified by the recognition that the specific conditions of each society, such as factor endowments, also played a role in shaping institutions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor, they are not the sole determinant of institutional formation.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. and North. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North posits that institutions matter in economic growth and development, emphasizing that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges shows up in terms of falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the shared finding that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9). The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with institutions being both a cause and an effect of economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by European colonizers. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
