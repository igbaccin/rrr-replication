The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with far-reaching implications for understanding why some societies prosper while others stagnate. At the heart of this debate lie differing views on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One school of thought emphasizes the direct impact of institutional frameworks on economic growth, arguing that well-designed institutions can foster an environment conducive to investment and innovation. These scholars point to historical examples where the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures has led to sustained economic development. Another perspective suggests that institutions are one of many factors influencing growth, with geographical endowments, cultural factors, and technological advancements also playing significant roles. These scholars contend that institutions alone cannot explain the vast disparities in economic performance observed across different regions and time periods. This tension underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to drive long-term economic growth. The key to resolving this debate lies in identifying the specific institutional features that are most conducive to economic development and understanding the conditions under which these features emerge and persist.

These institutional features were crucial in facilitating the growth of financial markets, which in turn supported long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was underpinned by the increased security of property rights, which is crucial for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflects the broader economic growth facilitated by secure property rights. The evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering economic growth, as it provided a stable framework for economic exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also note that the political commitment to these rules was a critical factor in ensuring their effectiveness. This commitment is evident in the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. North (1989, p.9) adds that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This underscores the importance of efficient property rights and their enforcement in fostering long-term economic growth. The development of financial institutions and the creation of new financial instruments were part of a broader institutional framework that supported economic growth.

This broader framework, however, is not limited to financial institutions and must address systemic issues that hinder growth and poverty reduction, as shown by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). The role of institutions in fostering economic growth includes the quality of public goods provided by governments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) cites Englebert's finding that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods. This implies that even ethnically heterogeneous societies can develop progressive institutions and uphold the rule of law. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further underscore this point by emphasizing the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This understanding is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The implication is that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) adds that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact is contingent on broader development policies. The role of institutions in African economic development is thus multifaceted, requiring a nuanced approach that considers both the quality of institutions and the broader policy environment.

The literature on sub-Saharan Africa's economic performance during colonial periods highlights the region's economic conditions and educational developments under different colonial powers, but does not directly address how institutions influenced long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The educational landscape in sub-Saharan Africa was significantly shaped by the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) demonstrate that British rule was particularly conducive to the development of formal education, altering the perceived benefits of education for African families and political leaders. This institutional shift was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which created a demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This demand was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping educational outcomes. The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is complex. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability suggests that while institutions played a role in educational development, their influence on economic growth was mediated by a complex interplay of factors. The educational developments under British rule, for instance, did not uniformly translate into economic growth across all regions, indicating that the institutional framework alone is insufficient to drive long-run economic growth.

Thus, institutional adjustments are necessary to efficiently harness these innovations, as technological advancements are pivotal for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets argues that technological progress is the driving force behind economic growth, but it requires significant institutional and ideological adaptations. For instance, modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating substantial changes in social organization and values (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr similarly emphasizes the role of useful knowledge in economic growth, highlighting that institutional progress is crucial for sustaining technological advancements. Without complementary institutional developments, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technology and institutions is not straightforward; technological progress alone does not guarantee sustained economic growth. Kuznets points out that the pace and impact of technological change vary across regions and periods, influenced by the specific institutional and ideological contexts (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Moreover, the resolution of growth-induced conflicts, such as those arising from technological disruptions, is essential for maintaining political stability and ensuring long-term economic growth. Kuznets underscores the critical role of the modern national state in peacefully managing these conflicts, thereby facilitating sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The dynamic relationship between technological advancements and institutional frameworks underscores the complexity of achieving long-run economic growth.

This dynamic relationship is further complicated by distributional conflicts, which shaped key economic institutions in early societies, as asserted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World were driven by power dynamics and inequality. In pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by the distribution of power among different social groups. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiencies, can be attributed to the elite's ability to maintain control and extract resources. Similarly, in the New World, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate that societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework, rooted in distributional conflicts, created a pathway for long-term economic development that was fundamentally shaped by power imbalances. The impact of these institutions on long-term economic growth varied. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, suggesting that while distributional conflicts shaped institutions, the efficiency of these institutions varied across different contexts. This variation in institutional efficiency highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how power dynamics and inequality influence economic development. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional efficiency in pre-industrial societies underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply influenced by the historical and political contexts in which they develop. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks in colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita. This is evident in cases where Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, resulting in either the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law or the creation of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Hopkins (2009, p.18) similarly emphasizes the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This is shown by the new economic history of Africa, which highlights that good institutions are crucial for fostering economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which the regime is committed to or bound by the rules governing economic exchange. This is evident in the success of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) further argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as seen in the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments that facilitated market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutional framework that ensures the enforcement of property rights and provides incentives for investment is essential for long-run economic growth, as it fosters a stable environment for economic activities.
