The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes and the mechanisms through which they operate. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Institutions can be understood as the rules of the game in a society, shaping economic incentives and constraints. One key position argues that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contractual arrangements are crucial for fostering economic growth. Some scholars highlight the role of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions, challenging this perspective. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions in economic growth has implications for policy-making and development strategies. The debate centers on whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or if they are exogenous factors that can be manipulated to achieve desired economic outcomes. This question is at the core of the debate: do institutions drive economic growth, or are they merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographic factors? Institutions matter for economic growth, and securing property rights is a critical factor.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the English financial revolution of the 17th century, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This period saw the evolution of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and lowered costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). The political factors underpinning this economic growth were crucial, as the regime's commitment to enforcing these rules played a pivotal role (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the institutional changes in England were pivotal for economic growth. The success of these institutions highlights the importance of credible commitment in economic development. However, the implications of these institutions extend beyond economic growth; they also shaped the political landscape by influencing the distribution of power and resources. This institutional framework not only reduced transaction costs but also ensured the stability and predictability necessary for long-term economic planning and investment.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrated that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In contrast, colonies with high mortality rates, like the Congo and the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. showed that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. They found that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. also emphasized that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, as these extractive institutions can persist and hinder growth even after the colonial period.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments together are critical drivers of economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. Kuznets posits that technological progress is the fundamental cause of economic growth, but it requires institutional adjustments for its efficient use. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and this is only sustainable when coupled with institutional progress. The successful integration of these innovations into the economy hinges on the ability of societies to resolve growth-induced conflicts without weakening their political fabric. Kuznets highlights the role of the modern national state in peacefully resolving these conflicts, thereby facilitating economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further underscores that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is necessary for economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own; institutional adjustments are essential for harnessing the full potential of technological innovations. Therefore, the efficient use of innovations, rather than institutions as the fundamental cause, is crucial for economic growth. This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the necessary framework for the efficient use of technological innovations.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This simplification can obscure the nuanced ways in which historical events and institutions shape contemporary economic conditions. This limitation is significant because it highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptive behavior suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this view by showing that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and influences economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical mistrust can impede the formation of trust-based institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. The internal norms of mistrust, developed through historical experiences, can be at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Therefore, understanding the historical roots of institutional development is essential for grasping the long-run economic growth patterns in regions affected by such historical events.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) jointly argue that the British colonial rule in Africa had a distinct impact on the development of formal education systems, which was often more positive than the influence exerted by other colonial powers. The British administration's approach to education was not merely a neutral or uniformly negative force; rather, it actively shaped the demand for missionary education through the implementation of new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family calculations (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that while British rule facilitated some economic growth, its impact on long-term African welfare development remains complex (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The persistence of poverty in Africa is not solely attributable to structural impediments imposed by colonial rule; instead, it is influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The British colonial legacy in Africa, therefore, must be understood as a multifaceted influence on economic development, with both positive and negative effects that extend beyond the immediate post-colonial period. Understanding the nuanced impact of British colonial rule on African education systems and economic development is crucial for grasping the broader historical context of institutional development in the region.

Distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. This perspective highlights how the power dynamics between elites and the broader population can influence the development of institutions. Ogilvie's work underscores that the institutions of serfdom, the community, and the craft and merchant guilds were shaped by conflicts over the distribution of resources rather than by efficiency considerations alone. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of these institutions despite their potential inefficiencies. For instance, the craft guilds, which restricted competition and limited economic growth, endured because they served the interests of the elite craftsmen who controlled them. This pattern of institutional development was not unique to Europe. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that similar dynamics played out in the New World, where institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutional outcomes. The persistence of inequality does not always follow the same trajectory. The downstream implications of these institutional arrangements can be qualified by the specific historical and political contexts in which they emerge. For example, the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development may vary depending on the nature of the conflicts and the relative power of the groups involved. In some cases, these conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that promote economic growth, while in others, they can result in stagnation.

Institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are critical for long-term economic development. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, which are essential for economic growth. This is evident in the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth.
