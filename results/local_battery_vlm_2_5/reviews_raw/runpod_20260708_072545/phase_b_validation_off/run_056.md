The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the efficacy of contemporary policy interventions. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement: to what extent do institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, drive economic outcomes over the long term? One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in fostering economic growth, arguing that secure property rights and effective governance are crucial for sustained development. Another position challenges this view, suggesting that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle that includes cultural and historical factors. The disagreement hinges on the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many interrelated factors.

This question is particularly salient given the historical evidence that economic growth has been uneven across regions and time periods. For instance, the divergence in economic performance between Europe and its colonies has been a focal point of scholarly inquiry. The institutional perspective posits that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which prioritized resource extraction over local development, significantly impeded economic growth. This argument is supported by evidence showing that European settlers in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, implemented extractive states that transferred resources rapidly to the metropole, fostering local investment and growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework contrasts with the experience of settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutional differences has had enduring effects on current income per capita, underscoring the long-term impact of institutional choices (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further complicated by the interplay between institutional design and cultural factors. Some scholars argue that distributional conflicts explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective suggests that the institutional landscape was shaped by power dynamics and the interests of ruling elites, a desire for economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view is supported by evidence that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often shaped by indigenous economic and political action, a purely extractive logic (Austin 2008, p.15). The interplay between institutions and culture highlights the complexity of economic development, suggesting that institutions alone cannot fully explain economic outcomes.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as they create the rules that govern economic interactions and incentivize growth-enhancing behavior (North 1989 (North 1989, p.9); Acemoglu et al. 2001 (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)). This institutional framework supported long-term economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, promoting long-run growth. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional impact, suggesting that the nature of institutions, whether growth-enhancing or extractive, is crucial in determining their effect on economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that the institutional framework in England, which included secure property rights and effective governance, played a pivotal role in economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to enforce and maintain these rules, ensuring stability and predictability in economic transactions. This institutional framework was about the rules governing economic exchange, the mechanisms that enforced and adapted these rules. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that institutions should be rigorously incorporated into economic history research, moving beyond mere fashion and into indispensable instruments. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering both the formal rules and the underlying beliefs that shape institutional effectiveness.

Technological advancements are essential for sustained economic development, as the growth of useful knowledge drives economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay is about adopting new technologies, resolving conflicts that arise from these technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in managing the social and political implications of technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own; institutional progress is also required to complement and sustain these advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine the economic performance of African economies under colonial rule, revealing that poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon, a result of specific historical and geographical conditions. They argue that the economic performance of these regions was significantly influenced by the market for exports, which in turn was tightly linked to foreign trade and the availability of economic data. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight the role of these political institutions in shaping the economic landscape. They show that these patterns of growing and shrinking were influenced by a range of factors, which included local endowments and transport costs, the political institutions that governed the region. This suggests that the institutional framework under colonial rule played a crucial role in determining the economic outcomes of African economies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, particularly regarding the long-term picture of economic development and the distribution of income. This underscores the need for further research to fully understand the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events. The slave trade created a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and impacts economic outcomes, such as the trustworthiness of others. This mistrust is not merely a result of the slave trade's effect on the external environment, like the rule of law, but also stems from internal norms of mistrust that were ingrained during the slave trade era. Nunn (2008, p.19)  argues that the slave trade had a direct negative effect on the region's economic development. For instance, African societies with centralized governments, like the Kongo kingdom, were better equipped to support trade with the Europeans. Smaller societies with undifferentiated economic and social institutions struggled to facilitate foreign trade. This suggests that the slave trade directly impacted economic development, shaping the institutional frameworks that influenced Africa's economic trajectory.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to have advantaged elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). A specific mechanism by which this occurred was through the adoption of government policies that favored elites. In societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, institutional frameworks were designed to protect the interests of the elite class. This elite capture of institutions restricted competition and hindered economic growth. This suggests that while institutional changes often favored elites, there were instances where colonial governments responded to local pressures, potentially mitigating some of the restrictive effects on competition.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been extensively studied, with scholars highlighting the importance of both initial conditions and institutional adaptability. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development, as seen in the differing trajectories of countries like the United States and the Congo. Austin argues that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political pressures (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that while initial institutional frameworks were often extractive, they could evolve in ways that influenced long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts, efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, highlighting that institutions are tools for economic efficiency, shaped by societal power dynamics.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice has been crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the political institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed are critical for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and that economic rules are consistently enforced. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design and how these dynamics can shape economic outcomes over the long term.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is grounded in their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate economic exchanges. North and Weingast show that secure property rights and consistent enforcement of economic rules are critical for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is supported by North's argument that the development of institutions to facilitate market exchanges leads to falling costs per transaction, which in turn supports economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Austin shows that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local pressures, which could mitigate some of the restrictive effects on competition (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
