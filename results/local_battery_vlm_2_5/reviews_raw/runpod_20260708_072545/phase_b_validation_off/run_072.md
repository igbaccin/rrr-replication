The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions themselves are shaped by exogenous factors versus being endogenous to economic processes. Another key point of contention is the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some scholars arguing for the primacy of property rights and legal frameworks, while others emphasize the role of political stability and governance. Institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6).

North and his peers (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are critical for economic growth, a perspective that contrasts with neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings. The institutions that North (1989, p.9) discusses are those that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. This security is crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide a specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes: European colonization strategies. This variation in institutional design led to significant differences in current income per capita among former colonies. This institutional design had lasting effects on the economic development of these colonies, illustrating how initial institutional frameworks can have persistent impacts on economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are profound: the initial design of institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, can have long-lasting effects on a region's economic trajectory.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework ensured that property rights were secure and that the government was committed to enforcing these rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The evidence from capital markets shows that the political factors underpinning economic growth were not merely the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as it ensured that property rights were protected and that the government was committed to enforcing these rights. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate that the differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recently attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally. These scholars have highlighted the variation across societies in conditions relevant to growth such as the security of property rights, prevalence of corruption, structures of the financial sector, investment in public infrastructure and social capital, and the inclination to work hard or be entrepreneurial. The persistence of inequality in societies with greater inequality can restrict competition, which in turn contributes to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as it ensured that property rights were protected and that the government was committed to enforcing these rights.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, focusing instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) share this posture, using wage data and urbanisation rates as proxies for economic development and structural change. This suggests that institutional factors, which are not directly measured by these proxies, may have played a more nuanced role in shaping economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.23) finds that institutional flexibility was a key factor in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, contrasting with the stagnation in regions like Tuscany where existing monopolies were strengthened. This institutional flexibility allowed for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing, contributing to Holland's transformation into one of the most advanced regions of the continent. The shift in economic development towards the countries bordering the North Sea, as noted by van Zanden (1999, p.9), further underscores the importance of regional institutional dynamics. However, Pamuk's (2007, p.23) emphasis on institutional flexibility qualifies this pattern, suggesting that the specific institutional arrangements in different regions were crucial in determining their economic trajectories. The implication is that the long-term economic growth in Europe during this period was influenced by the interplay of regional institutional frameworks and economic opportunities.

Colonial rule in Africa shaped institutional change through a combination of negotiation and imposition, with African initiatives playing a significant role alongside exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic is evident in the development of formal education systems, where African political leaders and families made strategic decisions based on perceived benefits. For instance, the implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift highlights the agency of African actors in shaping their own development trajectories, even within the constraints of colonial rule. The impact of colonial institutions on long-term economic growth varied. The variation in institutional outcomes across different colonial powers suggests that the specific mechanisms of colonial rule, such as the imposition of formal education systems, played a crucial role in determining economic trajectories. For example, British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, indicating that the type of colonial institution mattered significantly (Frankema 2012, p.1). This finding underscores the importance of institutional flexibility and the role of African initiatives in shaping long-term economic growth, as different colonial powers imposed varying institutional frameworks that influenced economic development. The implication is that the long-term economic growth in Africa was influenced by the interplay of colonial institutional frameworks and African agency.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and knowledge-driven factors are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both emphasise that technological progress is a key determinant of economic growth. Kuznets explains that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for the efficient use of technology, they are not the primary driver of growth. Mokyr, meanwhile, asserts that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting the role of technological progress in driving economic development. Mokyr points out that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This highlights that technological and knowledge-driven factors are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets further elaborates that modern technology is incompatible with traditional modes of life and family patterns, indicating that technological progress can drive significant economic changes. The implication is that technological and knowledge-driven factors are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development, as they drive significant economic changes that require institutional adjustments.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on Africa highlight how the legacy of mistrust and underdevelopment can persist long after the initial event, offering a rival explanation to institutional frameworks for understanding economic stagnation. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to influence economic outcomes today. This mistrust is an internal norm, deeply ingrained. This internal mistrust is at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law. The slave trade's legacy is not limited to cultural mistrust; it also had a profound adverse effect on economic development. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets. This suggests that the slave trade disrupted the development of such institutions, further hindering economic growth. The implication is that the long-term effects of the slave trade are multifaceted, encompassing both cultural and institutional dimensions.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). The conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns is challenged by Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts, which she argues were the driving force behind the development of institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. For instance, colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to have a material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions. Austin (2008, p.2) observes that the expansion of indigenous commodity production often aligned with the interests of colonial governments where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This suggests that the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions can vary depending on the specific historical and geographical context. The implication is that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development is complex and multifaceted, encompassing both local and global dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce contracts. North shows that the development of institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets and economic expansion. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions, which prioritize resource transfer to the metropole, can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights that the type of institutions established during colonization can have lasting impacts on economic development. Moreover, North argues that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are essential for reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Austin supports this view by noting that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions can evolve based on local dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15) underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity.

The specific historical and geographical context plays a crucial role in determining the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates was a strategic response to the challenges of colonization (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This strategic response was influenced by the presence or absence of European settlers, who competed for African labor. Austin extends this idea by noting that in regions where European settlers were not present, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions can vary depending on the specific historical and geographical context. Additionally, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This indicates that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development is complex and multifaceted, encompassing both local and global dynamics. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is that the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions can vary depending on the specific historical and geographical context, which is complex and multifaceted (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical evidence shows that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development can be nuanced, depending on the presence or absence of European settlers and their competition for local labor.
