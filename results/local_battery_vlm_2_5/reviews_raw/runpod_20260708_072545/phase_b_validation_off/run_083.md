The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as institutions can influence economic outcomes by determining the incentives, constraints, and opportunities available to economic actors. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth, with scholars debating whether institutions matter primarily through their role in enforcing property rights, facilitating market exchanges, or mediating distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that institutions govern the enforcement of economic rules and the allocation of resources, thereby fostering economic growth. Others contend that institutions shape economic outcomes by managing distributional conflicts and determining the incentives for investment. The interplay between these factors is complex, and the relative importance of each can vary across different historical and cultural contexts. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is ongoing, with scholars continuing to explore the underlying mechanisms and the conditions under which institutions exert their influence. For instance, some scholars focus on the role of institutions in enforcing property rights and facilitating market exchanges, while others emphasize the importance of institutions in managing distributional conflicts and determining the incentives for investment. This debate underscores the importance of understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth, as well as the conditions under which these mechanisms operate. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is intrinsically tied to the broader question of how economic systems evolve over time.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the security of property rights, which in turn affects investment and transaction costs. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this through the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth by securing property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further highlight that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly impacted their economic trajectories. This differential institutional setup influenced current income per capita, underlining the enduring effects of historical institutions on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth, leading to the persistence of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the political and economic forces that shape institutional development and their implications for long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that colonial institutions, particularly those introduced by British rule, played a significant role in shaping the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The British colonial administration fostered a unique set of social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African communities. This institutional setup created incentives for investment in education, which was crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The persistence of these colonial institutions also had implications for the long-run economic growth of African countries. The institutional legacies of British rule, while fostering education, also entailed the persistence of extractive institutions that prioritized short-term rents over long-term growth (Frankema 2012, p.1).

North and Weingast, alongside Austin, discuss how specific historical institutional changes influenced economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in England fostered secure property rights and economic growth, but also that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as they seemed. The institutional framework in England allowed for a commitment to secure property rights in a way that was not universally adopted across Europe. This commitment was critical for long-term economic growth. Austin's work highlights that colonial administrations encouraged indigenous economic and political action, which led to institutional changes that fostered economic growth. This suggests that the specific historical context and the nature of colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping economic development. However, Austin also notes that colonial institutions could change their prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, which complicates the notion of a linear path to economic growth. This implies that the impact of institutions on economic development is not uniform and can vary based on the local context and historical circumstances.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue for the economic divergence between Europe and Asia, highlighting the role of economic development before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) assert that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose from merchant distribution and finance, as much as from production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) identifies a shift in economic development towards the countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England, during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift indicates that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming the most expensive country after 1740. This pattern of economic development and its impact on living standards highlights the complex interplay between economic growth and institutional changes. This complicates the notion of a direct link between economic growth and improvements in living standards, suggesting that other factors, such as institutional changes, may have played a more significant role.

Mokyr and Kuznets share the perspective that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements are not merely tools for economic growth but are also dependent on the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. Kuznets elaborates that technological progress often requires significant societal changes, noting that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures. This incompatibility necessitates institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes, which in turn can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Mokyr supports this view by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress are not only drivers of economic growth but also require institutional support to be sustained. Mokyr further argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be supported by institutional changes to ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of political stability in supporting technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr qualifies this by pointing out that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress can be sustained, the institutional changes required to support it may be more fragile and subject to reversals.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were shaped by the power dynamics between elites and the masses, leading to structures that favoured the former. Similarly, in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutions, despite their potential inefficiencies, underscores the role of power dynamics in shaping economic structures. However, Ogilvie acknowledges the challenge of integrating institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that the study of institutions requires a nuanced understanding of their historical and political contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific outcomes depend on the interplay between power dynamics and historical contingencies. The enduring influence of these institutions on economic development highlights the need to consider power dynamics in understanding long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities. The enduring impact of institutions is evident in their ability to shape economic structures over time. Shared beliefs and cultural values, which are part of the institutional structure of a society, significantly influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of secure property rights and their enforcement are critical to economic development, as they provide the necessary incentives for investment and innovation. This is a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). However, the specific outcomes of these institutions depend on the interplay between power dynamics and historical contingencies, as seen in the case of colonial institutions in Africa (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, often did not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial institutions in Africa shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the need to consider power dynamics in understanding long-run economic growth.
