The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy debates. At the heart of this question lies a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the causal role of institutions and those who propose alternative explanations. Some scholars argue that the establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Others point to the impact of colonial institutions on long-run development, suggesting that the extractive nature of these institutions has significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Additionally, there are those who contend that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs play a more significant role in shaping core economic institutions, challenging the efficiency-based explanations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This debate underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the institutional factors at play. The interaction between institutions and economic growth is multifaceted, with various factors influencing the establishment and persistence of institutions that either foster or hinder economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution was driven by the need to manage the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. The establishment of secure property rights, particularly the time dimension of these rights, was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This development reflects a broader political model where efficient property rights and their enforcement are crucial for facilitating market exchanges and reducing costs per transaction.

They argue that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets. The impact of these institutional changes is complex. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be context-dependent and influenced by other factors such as bureaucratic structures and economic performance. The evolution of these institutions in England highlights the importance of credible commitment in economic development, as the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange is a critical factor in fostering growth. The development of these institutions in England during the 17th century laid the foundation for long-run economic growth by creating a stable environment for market exchanges and reducing transaction costs.

The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied widely, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The impact of these institutions on economic development was profound, with extractive institutions leading to persistent economic underdevelopment. This pattern highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, as these institutions often persisted even after independence, continuing to hinder economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) underscore that the equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This finding is particularly relevant for understanding the economic trajectories of former colonies, as it illustrates how institutional legacies can have lasting effects on economic development.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths, which complicates understanding the economic experience of various continents (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This critique underscores the importance of recognizing that the economic trajectories of different regions cannot be reduced to a single, overarching narrative. Austin highlights how the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences of older industrial economies are deeply rooted in their historical contexts, which is often overlooked in simpler models of economic growth. This nuanced view is supported by Austin's observation that colonial administrations, driven by fiscal security and the need to manage indigenous economic and political actions, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives. This adaptation shows that the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers evolved in response to local conditions, indicating a dynamic relationship between colonial institutions and economic development.

Austin's work is complemented by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today and significantly impacts contemporary economic outcomes. This mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon show is at least twice as large as the external channel of trust, underscores the deep and enduring effects of historical institutions on modern economic behavior. The persistence of this mistrust highlights how historical legacies can shape contemporary economic institutions and behaviors in profound ways, even long after the original institutions have been dismantled. This finding is particularly relevant for understanding the economic trajectories of former colonies, as it illustrates how institutional legacies can have lasting effects on economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating that per capita incomes and real wages were higher than previously thought. Their work reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, which contradicts the prevailing narrative of persistent poverty. This re-evaluation suggests that African economies were more dynamic and integrated into global markets than commonly assumed. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations highlight how different institutional contexts shaped economic outcomes in colonial Africa. Broadberry and Gardner note that economic growth was closely linked to foreign trade and the market for exports. This dependency on external markets underscores the importance of global economic conditions in shaping African economic development during the colonial era. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which could provide insights into the long-term economic development of the region. This focus on real wages and their determinants is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which historical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development. The shared finding of higher per capita incomes and real wages in colonial Africa suggests that historical institutional frameworks played a significant role in shaping economic trajectories, even in the face of structural impediments.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) share the view that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective is anchored in the idea that technological advancements are the dominant source of sustained growth over long periods, spreading to substantial parts of the world. The growth of useful knowledge is not just about the accumulation of scientific discoveries but also encompasses the development of formal institutions that facilitate the dissemination and application of this knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, noting that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving conflicts arising from these advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These adjustments are crucial because they ensure that technological progress does not lead to excessive costs or weaken the political fabric of society. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Thus, technological progress and institutional adjustments are interdependent, with the latter playing a pivotal role in sustaining the benefits of the former. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the complex dynamics of economic growth, suggesting that while technology is the mainspring, institutions are essential for harnessing its full potential.

Distributional conflicts are the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the structure of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World was fundamentally shaped by the underlying distributional conflicts. These conflicts arose from the unequal distribution of wealth, political power, and human capital, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. For instance, in pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the power of craft and merchant guilds can be attributed to the interests of the ruling elites who benefited from maintaining these institutions. Similarly, in the New World, the extreme inequality in wealth and political power led to the establishment of institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. These institutions, which were shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic development. The specific mechanisms through which these distributional conflicts shaped institutions varied across different contexts. For example, Ogilvie argues that the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe can be attributed to the interests of the landed aristocracy, who benefited from the labor of serfs. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the extreme inequality in the New World led to the establishment of institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. One downstream implication of this perspective is that the persistence of inequality in many societies can be attributed to the institutions that were shaped by distributional conflicts, which in turn perpetuated inequality. This perspective is further supported by the analysis of the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions in other historical contexts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue that involves the interplay of various factors including the creation and enforcement of property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) discuss how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states that prioritized resource transfer. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound effects on the economic trajectories of colonies, with those that adopted extractive institutions experiencing significantly lower income per capita in the long run. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, reflected increased security of property rights, which in turn supported long-term economic growth. The establishment of secure property rights and the reduction of transaction costs are thus shown to be essential for economic development.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are also pivotal in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2), who emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the economic impact of institutional changes. The creation of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is thus shown to be a key factor in economic growth.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is another important aspect of this discussion. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who discusses how colonial administrations responded to indigenous economic and political action, often changing their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by these actions. This highlights the importance of understanding the political and economic dynamics that shape institutional frameworks and their impact on long-run economic growth. The persistence of inequality in many societies can be attributed to the institutions that were shaped by distributional conflicts, which in turn perpetuated inequality. This perspective is further supported by the analysis of the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions in other historical contexts. The historical evidence and empirical comparisons show that the institutional frameworks established by colonial powers had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of their colonies.
