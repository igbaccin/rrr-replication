The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and the extent to which institutional frameworks either facilitate or hinder this process. A live disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, in driving economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Others posit that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that geographic factors or cultural beliefs may play equally significant roles. This debate is critical because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing nations. The institutions that govern economic activity can significantly influence the incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic growth is essential for designing effective policies. The debate extends to the specific types of institutions that matter most, with some scholars focusing on formal institutions like legal frameworks and others highlighting the importance of informal institutions such as cultural norms and social structures. The interplay between these different types of institutions and their collective impact on economic growth is a key area of inquiry. This interplay is particularly evident in the historical context of European colonization, where different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks in colonies. The enduring effects of these institutions on current economic outcomes highlight the long-term significance of institutional design.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) share a fundamental claim that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) extend this argument by showing how different European colonization strategies led to varied institutional frameworks in colonies, which in turn influenced current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These frameworks were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, leading to either inclusive institutions that promoted growth or extractive ones that hindered it. For example, in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European colonizers set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources to the metropole rapidly (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, emphasizing the long-term impact of these historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical perspective underscores the enduring effects of institutional design on economic development, highlighting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to influence economic trajectories today. The interplay between historical institutional frameworks and current economic outcomes demonstrates the profound and lasting impact of institutional design on long-run economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that this institutional evolution was pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories. A key mechanism through which this occurred was the development of credible commitment institutions that ensured the enforcement of property rights. These institutions provided a stable framework that encouraged investment and economic activity. This was a pattern that could be seen in the broader context of the original industrialisation of Europe. The institutionalist approach encounters challenges in explaining why similar institutions were adopted in some countries. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography, which emphasises the political delivery of secure private property rights, struggles to account for the variability in institutional adoption across different contexts. This variability suggests that the path to secure property rights and economic growth is not uniform and is influenced by a complex interplay of historical, political, and economic factors. Understanding these factors is crucial for comprehending the long-run economic growth trajectories of different regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Hopkins (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18), highlight that African economic development is deeply intertwined with the contributions of women and the potential of institutional reforms to drive growth. The role of women in African economic development has been significant, yet often overlooked. During both pre-colonial and colonial periods, women made substantial contributions to economic growth. For example, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, but they also played crucial roles in the economic revolution of the Gold Coast, where they migrated in search of farmland for cocoa production. This highlights the importance of understanding the contributions of women to economic development and the need for institutional reforms that recognize and support their roles. The new economic history of Africa, which focuses on institutional reforms, can inform policies that eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions that provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that the role of institutions in African economic development is complex and multifaceted, involving not just the provision of incentives for investment but also the broader governance framework that supports economic growth. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth in Africa.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic growth, highlighting that the diffusion of innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. For Kuznets, the introduction of modern technology necessitates significant shifts in societal structures and values. This includes changes in family patterns, rural-to-urban migration, and the veneration of nature (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These changes are essential for the efficient use of new technologies. Mokyr also underscores the importance of institutional progress, arguing that without it, technological advancements may not lead to sustainable economic growth. He notes that coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors can erode the benefits of technological progress, making institutional progress crucial for economic sustainability (Kuznets 1973, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth, they must be complemented by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic improvement.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) posit that the slave trades in Africa have had a direct adverse effect on economic development, a perspective Nunn (2008, p.2) shares. Both argue that the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, played a more significant role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory than colonialism, which lasted approximately 75 years. The mechanism through which the slave trades affected economic development is the erosion of trust. The slave trades created a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has impeded economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The implication is that the legacy of the slave trades continues to hinder Africa's economic progress, a finding that is supported by the historical evidence of the Kongo kingdom's ability to support trade with the Europeans due to its centralized government and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that the institutional framework established by the slave trades has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development, one that continues to shape its trajectory today.

Distributional conflicts drove the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts led to the establishment of institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the control of elites over economic resources and labor, which allowed them to maintain their power and influence. This control was maintained through various government policies that restricted competition and advantaged elites, contributing to the persistence of inequality. The downstream implication of this is that these institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, had a lasting impact on economic development. This suggests that the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is complex and multifaceted, with shared beliefs and norms playing a significant role in their persistence.

Institutional frameworks play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the historical development of property rights and economic institutions. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and subsequent financial innovations were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional development was driven by the need for secure property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and economic expansion. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrate how colonial policies shaped economic outcomes in the long run. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, underscores their enduring influence on economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is multifaceted, involving both efficiency considerations and shared beliefs and norms. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were also supported by shared beliefs and norms that reinforced their persistence. This perspective is echoed by Austin, who notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to local contexts, further emphasizing their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The shared finding is that institutions matter for growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The remaining disagreement is whether the persistence of institutions is due to efficiency considerations or shared beliefs and norms (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence of the Bank of England's creation and the financial innovations it brought shows that institutions that facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical case settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutions designed to enhance efficiency and reduce transaction costs have a lasting impact on economic development.
