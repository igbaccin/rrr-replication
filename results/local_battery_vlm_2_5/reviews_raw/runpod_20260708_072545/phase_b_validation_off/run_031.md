The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement over the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture. One camp argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective governance foster investment and innovation. Another camp posits that institutions are merely one piece of the puzzle, with geography and cultural factors playing equally significant roles. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies, as understanding the true drivers of economic growth can inform effective interventions. The stakes are high, as misdiagnosing the root causes of economic stagnation can lead to misguided policies. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the key to unlocking economic growth or if they are overshadowed by other factors. The answer to this question can shape how economists and policymakers approach development, making it crucial to understand the nuances of institutional impact on long-run economic growth. This question will shape the debate between institutions and other factors as the primary drivers of growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions, particularly those governing financial markets and property rights, play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution was a direct result of political institutions that specified "efficient" property rights and provided effective enforcement. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) contends that as economies grow, the emergence of political institutions that facilitate market exchanges leads to a decline in transaction costs. This reduction in costs is not just about individual transactions becoming cheaper; it is also about the growing proportion of the economy devoted to transactions as specialization and division of labor increase. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) qualify this by emphasizing the critical role of credible commitment in economic growth, stating that the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange is a key political factor. This commitment ensures that the rules are enforced and can be changed in a predictable manner, which is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions in England highlights the broader implication that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) extend this idea by demonstrating how colonial institutions, shaped by historical contexts, significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies. The nature of these institutions was heavily influenced by the mortality rates experienced by European colonizers, which in turn determined whether they established extractive or inclusive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach was driven by the colonists' desire to maximize short-term gains rather than fostering long-term economic development. In contrast, in colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These inclusive institutions were more conducive to economic growth and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide evidence that the institutional differences established during colonial rule were a direct result of the strategies adopted by European colonizers. This institutional framework, shaped by mortality rates, had lasting effects on the economic outcomes of these colonies. The enduring impact of these institutions is evident in the current income per capita of these regions, highlighting the profound influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) reinforce this by showing that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly affected subsequent economic performance, as measured by various indicators of institutional quality. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their critical role in shaping economic development. This legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes today, emphasizing the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Nunn, further illustrate this point by showing how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The impact of inequality on economic development is evident in how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites. This occurred through various government policies that restricted competition, thus contributing to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms of elite advantage are not limited to economic factors but also involve political power and human capital, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The impact of this inequality was not merely economic but also institutional, as it shaped the development of property rights, financial structures, and social capital in ways that favored the elite. This institutional framework, in turn, influenced economic development by limiting opportunities for broader segments of the population, thereby stifling growth and innovation. Nunn's work on Africa's slave trades complements this perspective by showing how the slave trades disrupted economic development through their adverse effects on institutions. The slave trades created conditions that hindered the development of centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed markets and trading networks, which are crucial for sustained economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). The absence of these institutional foundations meant that Africa struggled to support trade and development, further illustrating how historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks and influenced long-term economic trajectories.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific changes aimed at improving governance and economic conditions, but does not directly address how these reforms relate to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The focus on institutional reforms in Africa is driven by the recognition that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This mechanism is supported by the historical context of Africa, where the presence of poor institutions has been linked to the continent's dismal economic record. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not straightforward. Understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a perspective that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. They argue that technological advancements independently fuel long-run economic growth without necessarily relying on institutional adjustments. Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by institutional changes to resolve conflicts and ensure sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.49) echoes this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be limited. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is dynamic. Kuznets (1973, p.6) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, which suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, institutional frameworks are essential for managing the social and political implications of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, indicating that while technological advancements are more predictable, institutional adjustments may face more variability and challenges. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is complex, with technological progress driving economic growth and institutional adjustments ensuring its sustainability.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were influenced more by the struggle over resources and power than by efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency, as seen in the work of North and others. Austin's analysis of colonial governance in Africa further illustrates how institutional prescriptions were often shaped by the need for fiscal security and the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, rather than by efficiency alone. Austin's work, for example, shows that colonial governments sometimes opted for higher production equilibria contrary to expectations, highlighting the role of local dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they structure incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide a stable framework for economic transactions. This stability is essential for fostering investment and economic development. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the long-term economic outcomes of colonized regions. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, for example, led to persistent economic underdevelopment. This shows that the institutional framework set during colonization had long-lasting effects on economic growth. Moreover, Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) highlights that institutions are not merely efficiency-driven but are also shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies. This perspective underscores the multifaceted nature of institutions and their role in economic growth.

The role of local dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes is further illustrated by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15). Colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, rather than purely efficiency considerations. This adaptive behavior shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to local contexts and incentives. The historical evidence from colonial governance in Africa provides a clear example of how local dynamics can influence institutional outcomes and, consequently, economic growth. Furthermore, Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) emphasizes that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. This highlights the practical implications of studying institutional dynamics for policy-making and economic development. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18) is that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of local dynamics, historical context, and economic incentives, all of which significantly influence long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are fundamental to economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by local dynamics and distributional conflicts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence of colonial governance in Africa, as documented by Austin (2008, p.15), settles this disagreement by showing that colonial governments often opted for higher production equilibria contrary to expectations, highlighting the role of local dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes. This evidence demonstrates that institutions are not solely driven by efficiency but are also influenced by the complex interplay of local dynamics, historical context, and economic incentives.
