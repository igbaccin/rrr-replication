The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity over generations. At stake intellectually is understanding the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic outcomes, and whether these pathways are universal or context-dependent. The live disagreement centres on the relative importance of institutional frameworks versus other factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. Others contend that institutional factors are less decisive, suggesting that cultural beliefs and historical paths play a more significant role. Still, some scholars propose that the effectiveness of institutions depends on the broader economic and political context in which they operate. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to shape economic trajectories. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and political contexts in which they operate.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and supported a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by examining how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutional frameworks. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutions significantly influenced current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) build on this by highlighting that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Changes played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This qualification suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic growth depends on the broader political and economic context in which they operate. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial for the long-run development of former colonies, illustrating the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that a critical political factor in economic growth is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to enforcing rules and binding themselves to them. This commitment underpins the development of markets and the enforcement of economic exchange rules. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by noting that the specific environments in which European colonies were established led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. These institutions, influenced by the initial factor endowments and power dynamics, determined the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption, both of which are crucial for economic development. The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic trajectories is evident in the contrasting records of growth in different societies, where the security of property rights and the structures of the financial sector played pivotal roles. The persistence of inequality and the impact of initial factor endowments suggest that the evolution of institutions is shaped by complex interactions between political, economic, and social factors. This complexity underscores the importance of understanding the broader context in which institutions operate, as it directly influences their effectiveness in fostering economic growth.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe shaped core economic institutions in ways that efficiency considerations could not (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These conflicts were pivotal in the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which were influenced by power dynamics and social structures. Ogilvie's analysis underscores that the evolution of these institutions was driven by the distribution of power and the interests of different social groups. This perspective is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate how the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring impact of these historical events on contemporary trust levels highlights the lasting effects of institutional legacies. However, Frankema qualifies this by noting that the impact of colonial rule on education varied significantly across different regions, with British rule in sub-Saharan Africa being more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.1). This variation in institutional outcomes suggests that the legacy of colonial rule is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both the nature of colonial governance and the pre-existing social structures. The differential impact of colonial rule on education systems in Africa illustrates how institutional legacies can be both enduring and varied, shaped by the specific historical and social contexts in which they emerge.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective underscores the critical role of technological advancements in shaping long-run economic growth, positing that the development and diffusion of new technologies necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets highlights that the interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is pivotal for sustaining economic growth over extended periods. He notes that epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, require significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr complements this view by emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. He suggests that coordination failures can hinder economic growth, and policies to rectify these failures are essential for maintaining long-term economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Kuznets also points out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is crucial for modern economic growth, and the sovereign state plays a vital role in peacefully resolving such conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This highlights the complex interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks in driving economic growth, suggesting that while technological advancements are fundamental, the institutional environment must evolve in tandem to support sustained economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that historical accounts of economic performance in Africa and Europe reveal significant divergences in growth patterns and income levels. This pattern of growth was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta further highlight that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by production, merchant distribution, and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. The implications of these findings are significant, as they underscore the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic performance. For example, Broadberry and Gupta argue that institutional change in Europe led to investment in agriculture, resulting in high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This highlights that the institutional environment is pivotal in driving economic growth, as it facilitates the allocation of resources and the adoption of new technologies.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, focusing instead on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This finding suggests that economic growth in early modern Europe was not uniformly experienced across regions, and that factors beyond institutions, such as regional economic shifts, played a significant role in shaping economic development. This institutional flexibility allowed new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies, as seen in Holland, while regions like Tuscany, with stronger monopolies, struggled to adapt. The shift in economic development to countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England, further confirms that regional economic shifts and institutional flexibility were key drivers of economic growth during this period. The role of institutions in facilitating these shifts remains unclear.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge the notion that African poverty is solely due to structural impediments. They show that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The role of African women in economic development is evident in their involvement in food farming and cash crop production. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men were more involved in cash crops. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men's access to land for cash crops. For example, in the Gold Coast, women's involvement in cocoa production was crucial to the region becoming the world's leading producer by 1911. The gendered division of labor and the codification of customary law under colonial rule created a system where women's economic contributions were marginalized, despite their significant role in agricultural production. The implications of this gendered division of labor are profound. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This includes examining how specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the economic contributions of African women and the structural impediments they faced. The interplay between gendered labor divisions and colonial institutions shaped economic development in Africa, underscoring the need for a nuanced view of historical economic dynamics.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its implications for African development are explored through historical and institutional lenses (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The thesis, which posits that Africa's current poverty is a result of its colonial legacy, can be critically examined through the lens of institutional evolution. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, thereby missing the nuanced interplay between colonial institutions and indigenous economic and political actions. This is a significant qualification to the thesis, because it suggests that the development of institutions in Africa was not a straightforward process of imposition by colonial powers, but rather a dynamic interaction between external forces and local contexts. Austin further illustrates that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction is crucial because it highlights the agency of local actors in shaping institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. Hopkins builds on this by emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The complexity of institutional evolution in Africa means that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not deterministic. Englebert's findings, as cited by Hopkins (2009, p.18), suggest that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that the impact of institutions on economic growth in Africa is mediated by a range of factors, including the specific historical and political contexts in which these institutions operate. The nuanced view of institutional evolution in Africa underscores the need for a more context-specific approach to understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
