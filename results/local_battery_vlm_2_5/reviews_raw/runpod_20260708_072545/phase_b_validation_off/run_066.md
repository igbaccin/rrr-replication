The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, as the rules of the game in a society, can significantly influence economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints that individuals and firms face. This debate is central to understanding why some countries prosper while others remain mired in poverty. At the heart of this debate lie two main positions: one emphasizes the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth, while the other attributes economic development primarily to other factors. The former position argues that institutions shape the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior, thereby fostering growth. The latter position, however, points to factors such as geography, culture, or initial conditions as the primary drivers of economic development. This debate underscores the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. It is clear that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a range of factors that interact in dynamic ways.

North's framework suggests that institutions are not just static structures but dynamic forces that continuously reshape economic behavior. North and Weingast, for instance, argue that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework was crucial in creating a stable environment for economic exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the commitment to rules and their enforcement was a critical factor in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The extent of revolutionary change in these institutions is debated. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and dependent on various factors, including the broader political and economic context.

Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that these institutional changes can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes, even across generations. The colonial powers established different institutions depending on their mortality rates and settlement strategies. In regions with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In areas with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had long-lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate that these institutional differences were directly tied to the extractive or inclusive nature of the institutions imposed. This approach suggests that the persistence of these institutions is the primary determinant of current economic disparities. The dynamic nature of institutions means that they can evolve over time, potentially altering their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for the colonizers. This perspective highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, even as other factors may change.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) indicate that before the 19th century, economic divergence was more about productivity and development patterns. This productivity was due to high levels of investment in agriculture. This regional variation in economic development suggests that while institutions may have played a role, other factors such as geographic location and trade networks were also significant. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further elaborate that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was due to high levels of investment, highlighting the complex interplay between geography, investment, and productivity. This implies that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is nuanced and cannot be isolated from other economic and geographical factors.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) reveal that the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond economic factors, deeply influencing social dynamics and trust levels. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this by showing that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. The slave trade disrupted social and political institutions, leading to a lack of centralized authority and well-developed markets and trading networks. This disruption was a significant obstacle to economic growth, as it hindered the development of sustainable trade networks and economic institutions. While the slave trade had a significant negative impact on some regions, it had a less pronounced effect on others. This variation suggests that the impact of the slave trade on economic development was mediated by other factors, such as pre-existing economic and social institutions.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight the growth of useful knowledge as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores that technological progress is a fundamental force driving economic growth. This institutional support facilitates the dissemination and application of new technologies, thereby enhancing productivity and economic output. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the pace and impact of technological progress vary across regions and periods, indicating that the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for efficient use of innovations are context-dependent. This variability suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the effectiveness of this driver is contingent on the specific institutional and ideological framework of a society. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about creating an environment where technological advancements can be sustainably integrated into the economic fabric.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest that the distribution of power and resources within societies shapes economic institutions, which in turn influence how economies are organized. This perspective posits that institutions are shaped by the struggles between different social groups over the distribution of resources. In pre-industrial Europe, the institutional structures of serfdom, the community, and craft and merchant guilds are better understood as outcomes of these distributional conflicts. These institutions, far from being efficient, often served to entrench the power of elites and maintain existing social hierarchies. This dynamic is evident in New World societies as well, where initial conditions of extreme inequality led to the development of institutions that protected elite interests and restricted competition. The persistence of inequality in these societies is thus linked to the institutional frameworks that emerged from these early distributional conflicts, which in turn shaped long-term economic trajectories. However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) qualify this by noting that the specific factor endowments of these societies, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, played a crucial role in shaping these initial conditions of inequality. This highlights that while distributional conflicts are a key driver, the broader economic context also influences the specific institutions that develop.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, facilitated by institutions like the Bank of England, were crucial for economic growth. These findings highlight that institutions are active shapers of economic outcomes, influencing everything from capital markets to long-term economic development.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is also significant. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups often hinder economic development. These insights underscore that the distributional implications of institutions are central to understanding their impact on economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide further evidence, showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

While there is broad agreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, the precise mechanisms and historical contexts remain areas of active debate. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, influencing economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) show that institutions and culture are deeply intertwined, with cultural beliefs and historical experiences shaping the institutions that govern economic activity. The empirical comparisons of former European colonies and the measurement of trust levels in Africa provide specific evidence that settles the debate, showing that institutional frameworks and cultural beliefs are inextricably linked in shaping long-run economic growth.
