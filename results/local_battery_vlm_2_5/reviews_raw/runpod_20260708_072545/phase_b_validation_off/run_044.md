The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of economic development and the mechanisms by which societies achieve sustained prosperity. One position argues that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, directly influences economic outcomes. Extractive institutions, which concentrate power and wealth in the hands of a few, hinder growth by discouraging investment and innovation. Inclusive institutions, on the other hand, foster growth by protecting property rights and encouraging broad-based participation in economic activities. Another position emphasizes the importance of historical and geographical factors in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic growth. This view suggests that the initial conditions set by geography and historical events create pathways that either facilitate or impede the development of growth-enhancing institutions. A third position focuses on the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, arguing that the distribution of power and resources among different groups influences the design and effectiveness of institutions. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions are crucial for economic growth, but they diverge on the specific mechanisms by which institutions exert their influence. The debate centres on whether institutions are primarily shaped by historical and geographical factors, by the interests of powerful groups, or by the broader economic and political context. This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other factors to drive economic growth. The resolution of this debate could provide insights into the mechanisms underlying economic development and inform policies aimed at promoting sustainable growth.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They argue that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor in economic growth. The development of these institutions was a significant driver of economic performance. The political factors underpinning economic growth were the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing their enforcement and change. The political context of England during this period was conducive to the development of institutions that supported economic growth. This period saw the establishment of credible commitment mechanisms, which were essential for fostering long-term economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be context-dependent. The nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms by which institutions exert their influence.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates and extractive strategies. They argue that European settlers in high-mortality regions set up extractive institutions designed to transfer resources back to Europe. Where mortality rates were lower, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional legacy has persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is not guaranteed. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes can be modified by other factors, including changes in property rights and the rule of law. This suggests that while colonial institutions have had a profound influence, their effects can be mitigated or even reversed through institutional reforms, thus offering a pathway for economic development in former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The institutional frameworks in New World societies that emerged from unequal factor endowments, such as abundant land and natural resources relative to labor, tended to favor elites. This elite advantage was reinforced through government policies that limited economic opportunities for the broader population, thereby restricting competition and maintaining inequality. This institutional setup can be traced back to the early stages of colonial development, where the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power shaped the evolving institutions. The persistence of these institutions over time had a profound impact on the course of development, as they created barriers to economic growth by limiting the participation of the majority of the population in productive activities. The impact of these institutions varies across different societies. Nunn (2008, p.2) also demonstrates that the effect of inequality on economic development can be mitigated by the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets and trading networks. These institutions can facilitate trade and economic activity, even in the face of high levels of inequality.

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development explores how colonial rule shaped institutional change through both negotiation and imposition, highlighting the role of African initiatives alongside exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Instead, the nature of colonial institutions and their impact on economic development varied significantly across different regions and periods. Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrates that the quality of public goods provided by African governments was not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, suggesting that the presence of progressive institutions could mitigate the effects of ethnic heterogeneity. This highlights the role of African agency in shaping institutional outcomes. The benefits of formal education were related to new social and political institutions, which were implemented in response to the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The development of formal education was a result of negotiation and adaptation by African societies. This adaptation was crucial for the development of progressive institutions, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was not uniform and was influenced by the specific historical and political contexts in which they were implemented.

The economic performance of post-colonial Africa was significantly influenced by the expenditure patterns and policy trends shaped by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). This neopatrimonial tendency is evident in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. This pattern was not unique to Kenya; Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure. These trends underscored the challenges faced by post-colonial African economies in balancing their expenditure priorities. The variability in the timing and scale of growth in export production across different countries suggests that local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions played critical roles in determining economic performance. This variability underscores the complex interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes in post-colonial Africa, where the effectiveness of industrial policy and capital investments was contingent on the broader political and economic context.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These authors show that the evolution of core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, was deeply influenced by the power struggles between different social groups. This perspective highlights that the adoption of institutions was driven by political and social dynamics. This is evidenced in the colonial context where local economic and political actions influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie underscores the need to integrate institutional analysis into economic history, emphasizing that institutions are essential tools for understanding long-term economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The complexity of institutional evolution means that the relationship between distributional conflicts and economic growth is intricate. The variability in institutional outcomes across different regions and periods suggests that while distributional conflicts were a significant factor, they were not the only determinant of long-run economic growth.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as they establish the rules and incentives that guide economic behaviour. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had lasting effects on economic development. This is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that institutions that secure property rights and reduce transaction costs are crucial for economic growth. Together, these findings underscore the importance of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the complexity of institutional evolution and the variability of institutional outcomes across different regions and periods. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) emphasizes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These studies highlight that while distributional conflicts were a significant factor, they were not the only determinant of long-run economic growth. The variability in institutional outcomes across different regions and periods suggests that the relationship between distributional conflicts and economic growth is not straightforward.

The precise impact of institutions on economic growth is further complicated by the role of shared beliefs and cultural factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Together, these findings suggest that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including distributional conflicts, shared beliefs, and cultural values. Institutions remain the essential tool for understanding long-term economic development.

Shared findings (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) show that institutions are essential for economic growth. Remaining disagreement (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2) centers on the role of shared beliefs and cultural values in shaping institutions. The historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in former European colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), settles this disagreement. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This empirical comparison shows that institutions, not shared beliefs or cultural values, are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth.
