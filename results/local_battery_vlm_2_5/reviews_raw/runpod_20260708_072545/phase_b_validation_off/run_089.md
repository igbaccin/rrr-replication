The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the very nature of economic growth: is it driven by the underlying institutions that structure economic interactions, or by other factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments? This question has sparked a live disagreement among scholars. Some argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of property rights, rule of law, and effective governance. Others posit that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geography, climate, and initial endowments play a more significant role. Still others suggest that cultural beliefs and values are the underlying determinants of institutional quality and, by extension, economic growth. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and development strategies aimed at fostering economic growth. The extent to which institutions matter determines whether policy should focus on institutional reform or on other factors such as infrastructure development or technological innovation. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they are shaped by economic factors or by other social, cultural, or political forces. This question is particularly relevant in the context of former European colonies, where the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes. In this context, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is both an academic question and a practical one with real-world implications for development policy.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights the critical role of the Glorious Revolution in establishing credible commitments to property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth by reducing transaction costs. This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself, was thus underpinned by these institutional changes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.6) further support this claim by showing how the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) note that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets-not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. The development of these institutions not only facilitated economic growth but also set the stage for long-term economic development by establishing a stable framework for property rights and market exchanges. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, and its legacy continues to influence economic outcomes today.

This influence is evident in the significant impact colonial institutions had on the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. The authors show that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This pattern is evident in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, leading to the establishment of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development. The institutional framework set up by colonial powers, therefore, played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional changes are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The authors show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of these colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions maximized the rents to European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which they were established and their long-term effects on economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that these institutions were designed to distribute resources and power among different social groups, rather than to promote economic efficiency. The craft guilds, for instance, were not merely about regulating production and maintaining quality, but also about controlling access to markets and resources, thereby protecting the interests of established artisans. This perspective highlights that the institutions' primary function was to manage conflicts and maintain social order. The persistence of these institutions over time had significant implications for economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies, institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic underscores how the institutional legacy of distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes, shaping patterns of inequality and growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that institutions are not just about economic efficiency but are deeply intertwined with social and political structures, and their impact on long-run economic growth is profound (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, disrupted African societies and hindered the development of robust economic institutions. Nunn posits that the extensive period of slave trades, compared to the relatively shorter duration of colonial rule, had a profound impact on Africa's economic trajectory. This perspective is supported by Austin, who notes that the economic paths of industrialized economies are deeply rooted in their institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences over centuries (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that long-term economic development is shaped by enduring factors, such as the slave trades, which can have lasting effects on institutional development and economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg challenge this view by emphasizing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). They argue that a better understanding of real wage levels and trends in British Africa can help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which historical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This implies that while the slave trades may have had an adverse effect on economic development, the impact of historical conditions on African welfare development is complex and multifaceted.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was instrumental in fostering the development of formal education, a factor that significantly influenced long-term economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. These educational advancements were not uniformly distributed; they were tied to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, which varied both between and within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local conditions in shaping educational outcomes. The economic growth patterns in Anglophone African economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, which were influenced by these educational developments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The impact of British rule on economic development varied across Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) suggest that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust persisted, influencing the development of trust and economic institutions. This mistrust, rooted in historical experiences, had a significant impact on the effectiveness of colonial educational policies and their long-term economic benefits. Therefore, while British rule positively influenced the development of formal education, its impact on long-term economic growth was mediated by historical legacies of mistrust and varying local conditions.

Institutional reforms are essential for addressing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by fostering economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Institutional reforms can promote economic development by fostering "good" institutions that enhance incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the effectiveness of these reforms is contingent on the quality of governance, which can be influenced by historical legacies of patrimonial governance and the distribution of state resources. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that post-independence expenditure trends in East Africa were shaped by factors such as industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. These factors can divert resources away from productive investments, thereby limiting the impact of institutional reforms. For example, Kenya's high subsidies and interest payments in the early 1990s displaced both capital and recurrent spending, which could have hindered economic growth (Simson 2019, p.11).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800. This divergence was driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. This productivity was not solely due to production but also to the service sector, which facilitated economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.16) supports this view by highlighting the structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This early divergence suggests that institutional changes, such as those in merchant distribution and finance, were already influencing economic growth long before the Industrial Revolution. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) further qualifies this by noting the shift in economic development to countries bordering the North Sea, such as Holland and England, which became economic leaders after 1700. This shift indicates that the institutional framework in these regions was conducive to economic growth, reinforcing the idea that early institutional changes were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the importance of technological and intellectual advancements in shaping long-run economic growth. This institutional support facilitated the dissemination of technological advancements, which in turn drove economic progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. He notes that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, and this growth necessitates adjustments that resolve growth-induced conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is fundamental, the institutional framework must adapt to sustain economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are important, the fundamental driver of long-run economic growth is the growth of useful knowledge.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate and support economic activities. Institutions matter because they create the framework that determines the incentives and constraints under which individuals and organizations operate. North asserts that institutions reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which highlight that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions provide the stability and predictability necessary for long-term economic planning and investment, which are essential for sustained growth.

The historical context shows that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run economic growth in many colonies. These institutions were not conducive to the development of local economies and often perpetuated poverty and underdevelopment. The persistence of these institutions, even after colonial rule ended, underscores the long-term impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The historical evidence from these studies shows that the reversal of fortune among former European colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe also played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, it highlights the importance of power dynamics and political struggles in shaping institutional frameworks. Austin further elaborates on this by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution is necessary to fully grasp the role of institutions in economic growth.
