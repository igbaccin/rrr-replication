The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At stake is the explanation for persistent economic disparities across regions and countries. A live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic activity, while others highlight the influence of exogenous factors that shape institutional outcomes. The debate also extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some focusing on the role of property rights and contractual enforcement, and others on the impact of distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. One key point of contention is the extent to which institutions are endogenous to economic and political processes or exogenous factors that shape institutional outcomes. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, involving multiple feedback loops and reinforcing mechanisms that can either promote or hinder development. The question of whether institutions are a cause or a consequence of economic growth remains central to this debate. Institutions can be both a cause and a consequence of economic growth, but the direction of causality and the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth are areas of active research.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. They highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, showing a remarkable commitment to rules governing economic exchange. This institutional framework governed economic exchanges and enforced and adapted these rules, underpinning economic growth. The development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement reduced transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and boosting economic growth. The impact of these institutions is nuanced. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that while institutional changes were significant, they were not revolutionary in isolation. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV in France, for instance, was not a hindrance to economic growth, suggesting that the specific political and institutional context matters. The development of secure property rights and efficient institutions is a complex process that depends on a variety of factors, including the commitment of the regime to these rules. This underscores the importance of credible commitment in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) showing that European policies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. The specific colonization strategies adopted by Europeans were critical: in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of these institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering development and inclusive institutions fostering growth. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic outcomes is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) acknowledge that the influence of these institutions is nuanced, and that the specific economic and political contexts of former colonies play a crucial role in determining the long-run effects of colonial institutions.

Colonial legacies in Africa were both imposed and negotiated, shaping institutional change through complex interactions between exogenous factors and local initiatives (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education was influenced by African political leaders and families, who calculated the benefits of formal education within the new institutional frameworks introduced by colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8). These calculations were not uniform across Africa, and the specific economic and political contexts of former colonies played a crucial role in determining the long-run effects of colonial institutions. In particular, the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly affected the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The economic outcomes for African countries were thus shaped by both the type of institutions established during colonial rule and the specific economic and political contexts of each colony. The influence of these institutions was complex. This insight is crucial for understanding how colonial institutions shaped economic development in Africa, as it underscores the importance of local initiatives in negotiating the institutional legacies of colonial rule.

The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This legacy is evident in the institutional frameworks that emerged from colonial practices, which often favored elite groups and perpetuated inequality. This institutional bias toward elites contributed to the persistence of inequality over time. Nunn (2008, p.2) further illustrates this point by examining the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, highlighting that these trades had lasting impacts that were not solely attributable to colonialism or more recent events. The slave trades disrupted local economic and social institutions, creating conditions that hindered subsequent development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) extend this analysis by showing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experiences of slavery and has had significant implications for economic development. The pervasive mistrust hinders economic development by reducing trust in institutions and social interactions, thereby limiting the potential for cooperative economic activities. The legacy of slavery and inequality, as highlighted by these studies, underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutions shape long-term economic outcomes.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are complementary factors in long-run economic development, with the growth of useful knowledge being a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1)(Mokyr 2005, p.49). Institutions facilitate the absorption and diffusion of new technologies, thereby promoting economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible. This institutional framework is essential for sustaining economic improvements over the long term. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the importance of a robust institutional framework in driving long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This critique challenges the notion that institutions are always efficient and instead suggests that they often emerge from conflicts over resources and power. One specific mechanism that emerges from this perspective is the role of colonial administrations in shaping economic institutions. Austin (2008, p.15) argues that colonial governments adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, contrary to the hypothesis that they uniformly imposed inefficient institutions. This adaptation occurred because colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) extends this argument by emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. The downstream implication of these arguments is that the efficiency of institutions is a result of complex interactions between different actors and their interests. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the apparent difficulty of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively and the insufficiency of the theory for explaining why institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, underscores the need for further research into the specific conditions under which institutions emerge and evolve.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal area of study, with several streams of scholarship offering distinct but complementary insights. Institutions, as formal constraints, shape the economic environment by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, thereby influencing economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions in colonies based on mortality rates, leading to extractive institutions in high-mortality areas and growth-enhancing institutions in low-mortality areas (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how initial conditions and settlement patterns directly influence the type of institutions that emerge, which in turn affect long-term economic development. North argues that the evolution of institutions, particularly those related to property rights and financial systems, is crucial for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century illustrate how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic activity and development. The historical evidence from both Acemoglu et al. and North shows that the efficiency of institutions is a result of complex interactions between different actors and their interests (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century illustrate how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is consistent with Ogilvie's argument that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by conflicts over the distribution of resources rather than by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s findings that extractive institutions can persist because they maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if they do not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups, and that these institutions can have lasting effects on economic development, is a key insight from these studies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin qualifies this by noting the apparent difficulty of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively and the insufficiency of the theory for explaining why institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others (Austin 2008, p.2). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, contra geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of extractive institutions in some colonies and the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions in others can be attributed to the interests and actions of colonial powers, as well as the resistance and adaptation of indigenous populations (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence from these studies shows that the specific conditions under which institutions emerge and evolve are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. The measurement of institutional quality and the comparison of economic outcomes across different historical periods and regions provide a robust framework for assessing the role of institutions in shaping economic development.
