The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has long been a subject of intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic development over extended periods. One key position holds that institutions are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that well-designed institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, create a stable environment conducive to investment and economic activity. A rival position contends that institutions are shaped by broader economic and social factors, and that their impact on growth is secondary to these underlying determinants. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the cause or the effect of economic development, and whether they can be effectively manipulated to promote growth. Austin and North both engage with the idea that institutions shape economic growth, but they do so in a way that emphasizes different causal pathways. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin's critique highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its interaction with economic and political factors. North, on the other hand, argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and provide a stable framework for economic activity. North's focus on the political delivery of secure private property rights as a key to long-term economic growth underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The institutional changes in England during this period were crucial for economic growth, as they facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. This is evident in the development of economic institutions that supported a long-term capital market, a critical factor for sustained economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the performance of capital markets. However, North (1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that while transaction costs per transaction were falling, the aggregate volume of exchanges was growing, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor. This dynamic interplay between institutional evolution and economic growth underscores the complex relationship between secure property rights and long-term economic development.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) show that European colonizers established different institutions depending on their mortality rates. If mortality rates were high, Europeans set up extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In contrast, lower mortality rates led to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that these institutions persisted even after independence, continuing to shape economic outcomes. This institutional legacy helps explain the persistence of economic disparities among former colonies. The interaction between institutions and the chance to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the historical national accounting of Sub-Saharan Africa reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This accounting approach demonstrates the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. The export production patterns in these economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This pattern of economic growth and development has significant implications for understanding the long-term economic development of African economies. However, Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this by highlighting that post-independence expenditure trends in Africa were influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. These factors shifted the focus of policy-making towards consumption rather than public goods, which had a significant impact on the economic trajectories of African economies. This shift in expenditure trends underscores the complex interplay between historical economic data and contemporary economic policies in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate that economic contributions in Africa are deeply intertwined with institutional reforms, and that understanding these dynamics can inform effective policy changes. This shift has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain traction among development specialists. The codification of customary law under colonial rule played a crucial role in this dynamic, as it reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops. This codification was a key mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes, highlighting the complex interplay between gender, land access, and economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This institutional focus is essential for understanding how economic contributions and institutional reforms in Africa can be harnessed to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) extend this by noting that patriarchal alliances and customary law were instrumental in codifying the subordinate status of women, which had profound implications for economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Frankema's work (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) shows that the impact of colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa varied significantly depending on the colonial power. This variability challenges the notion that all colonial impacts were uniformly negative. British rule, for instance, was more conducive to the development of formal education. This educational development was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by the new social and political institutions implemented during colonial rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African communities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) highlight that the institutions established in New World societies with greater inequality often advantaged elites. This pattern was driven by government policies that restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The implications of these findings for sub-Saharan Africa suggest that the type of colonial institution inherited by African nations influenced educational development. The legacy of colonial education policies shaped by these institutions persists today, affecting contemporary economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development. This effect is partly mediated by the development of a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, which persists to this day, is a significant barrier to economic development. The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a profound impact on African societies. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to long-lasting adverse effects on development. For example, the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. However, the slave trades undermined these institutions. The mistrust generated by the slave trades has had lasting effects on economic development. This mistrust has been shown to affect economic development in various ways, including reducing international trade and undermining political institutions. The culture of mistrust has been shown to account for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the deep-seated nature of this issue. The implications of these findings suggest that addressing the legacy of mistrust is crucial for fostering economic development in Africa.

Broadberry and Gupta's work, shared with Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23), shows that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. This divergence was not merely a product of the Industrial Revolution but was rooted in earlier structural changes. Pamuk suggests that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These structural changes included institutional flexibility, which allowed regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. Van Zanden, however, qualifies this view by arguing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This implies that while structural changes and institutional flexibility may have driven economic divergence, their impact on living standards was not straightforward. The divergence in economic development between northwestern Europe and other regions was thus complex, involving both structural changes and institutional factors that influenced economic growth and living standards.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr posits that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting that the dissemination of useful knowledge catalyzes economic development. Kuznets elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from growth-induced changes, thereby ensuring sustainable economic growth. Kuznets further emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric of society remains intact (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutional stability in supporting technological progress and economic growth. However, Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are pivotal, the institutional frameworks that support them must be adaptable and responsive to changes, ensuring that economic growth is sustained over the long term. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is thus a dynamic process that requires continuous adaptation and innovation. The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, is not only a driver of economic growth but also a catalyst for institutional reforms that support long-term economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both critique the prevailing views on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, arguing that existing formulations oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and their relationship with economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency does. This suggests that the dynamics of power and conflict within societies play a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates this point by examining colonial administrations that adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This adaptability shows that institutions are not static but respond to the pressures and incentives within their socio-economic contexts, which can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic development, where local contexts and adaptations significantly shape institutional outcomes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus not straightforward but involves a dynamic interplay of historical paths, power dynamics, and adaptive responses.
