The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate is the conflict between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy, as understanding the drivers of economic growth can inform strategies for development and poverty reduction. The stakes are high, as the efficacy of institutional reforms hinges on a clear understanding of their impact on economic growth. The disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, are more influential. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, which significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating the enduring impact of institutional legacies. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution. This points to a broader theme: the enduring impact of institutions on economic outcomes, which can be traced through both historical and contemporary lenses. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth can be explored through the lens of institutional legacies and their impact on economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) traces this evolution through the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. These institutions reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional change, as the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues laid the groundwork for future economic development (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to enforcing rules governing economic exchange, highlighting the critical role of political factors in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework fostered a secure environment for capital markets, which in turn drove economic development. However, the implications of these institutional changes were not uniformly revolutionary, as evidenced by the comparable economic performance of France before the French Revolution. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intertwined with the broader economic and political landscape.

European colonization strategies played a pivotal role in shaping the institutions of colonies, with significant long-term economic implications. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European colonizers established extractive institutions in areas with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This institutional framework was designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. The mechanism through which these institutions affected economic growth was by discouraging investment and impeding the development of a productive economy. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the extractive institutions resulted in lower levels of investment, which in turn led to lower levels of economic growth. This pattern was observed across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and the efficiency of the judiciary. The implication is that the colonial legacy of extractive institutions continues to hinder economic development in many former colonies. This relationship is not deterministic. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions can be mitigated by subsequent economic and political changes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency concerns, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World. They contend that these institutions were crafted to maintain power and wealth within elites. Ogilvie examines the institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, asserting that these structures were designed to protect the interests of the ruling classes rather than to promote efficiency. This perspective is mirrored in the New World, where Sokoloff and Engerman show that societies with greater inequality established institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and sustaining inequality. This institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for economic development. For instance, the persistence of inequality in these societies restricted the emergence of inclusive institutions that could foster broader economic growth. This relationship is not deterministic. The interaction between these institutions and subsequent economic and political changes could mitigate their initial impact, as seen in the varying trajectories of development in different regions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying historical causation by compressing different periods and paths of development. The thesis posits that institutions set in place during colonial rule shaped long-run economic outcomes, but this view does not account for the changing dynamics of institutions over time. For instance, colonial administrations adapted their policies based on the incentives created by indigenous economic activities. This adaptation suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth was not predetermined but contingent on local responses. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further illustrate this point by showing how the slave trade influenced the development of a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects economic outcomes. This cultural legacy is an example of how historical events can have long-lasting institutional effects that shape economic development. The precise mechanisms through which these historical legacies operate remain complex and multifaceted. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments varied based on local conditions and the interests of the colonial powers. This variability underscores the importance of considering the specific historical contexts in which institutions operate. Overall, the interaction between historical events and institutional evolution highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The literature on economic development and structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 focuses on wage data, urbanization rates, and regional economic shifts, rather than directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). The shift in economic development towards the North Sea region is evident in the rise of relative price levels for basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader structural change (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This regional divergence is further underscored by the varying urbanization rates, with northwestern Europe experiencing higher rates of urbanization compared to southern Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland enabled a rapid response to new economic opportunities, facilitating the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing. This institutional flexibility contrasts with the strengthening of existing monopolies in regions like Tuscany, where economic stagnation and a decline in urban ranks were observed. The precise mechanisms through which these institutional differences influenced long-run economic growth remain complex and multifaceted. The interplay between economic development and institutional frameworks during this period suggests that while institutional flexibility may have facilitated growth, the broader structural changes, such as urbanization and regional economic shifts, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) find that British colonial rule in Africa facilitated the development of formal education systems, which were more conducive to economic growth than other colonial powers' impacts on African education systems. This influence was mediated by the colonial state's role in creating new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This institutional framework, shaped by British colonial rule, fostered economic development by enhancing human capital through education. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors including transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The implications of this institutional framework are further illustrated by the growing and shrinking of African economies tied to foreign trade and export markets. This economic growth was driven by the market for exports, highlighting the importance of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The impact of colonial institutions on African economic development, however, was not uniform across all regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) examine the real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, revealing that poverty in Africa was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This finding suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development was complex and multifaceted, with varying outcomes across different regions and time periods.

The literature on African economic development and institutional reforms highlights the significant contributions of women to economic growth and the potential of new economic history to inform policy (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Akyeampong and Fofack, for example, demonstrate that women played a pivotal role in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, but their migration in search of farm land for cash crops, such as cocoa, drove significant economic revolutions. For instance, Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farm land for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This underscores the importance of understanding the gendered dynamics of land access and economic activity in African development. However, the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities. This codification was a key avenue for the subordination of women, as colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that disadvantaged women through customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The effectiveness of these reforms depends on addressing the historical and contemporary barriers that have limited women's economic contributions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress and institutional adjustments are key drivers of long-run economic growth. The efficient use of innovations requires societal adaptations that facilitate the integration of new technologies into existing economic structures. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from the integration of new technologies, as they can disrupt existing social and economic structures. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that the political fabric of society remains intact. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that institutional progress must complement technological progress to sustain economic improvement, as the absence of such institutional support can lead to the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, the institutional framework that supports and integrates these innovations is equally important for long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that guide economic activity. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates established inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which laid the foundation for sustained economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for instance, dramatically reduced transaction costs and supported economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions is further underscored by the fact that they are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design institutions to maximize their own rents rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the extractive institutions established by European colonists in high-mortality colonies, where the primary goal was to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions significantly impacted current income per capita, highlighting the enduring effects of institutional choices on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

This perspective is supported by the observation that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).
