The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue among economists and historians. At stake is the understanding of why certain regions have prospered while others have stagnated. The debate centers around the causal role of institutions, with some scholars arguing that they are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others propose that institutions are merely epiphenomenal to other, more fundamental factors. One key position is that institutions, by providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, foster economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. This view is supported by the emphasis on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, with North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) arguing that credible commitment underpins growth. An alternative explanation suggests that institutions are secondary to factors such as geography, climate, or cultural beliefs, which are viewed as the true determinants of economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by the argument that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, which can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The resolution of this debate has significant implications for economic policy, as it informs whether institutional reforms can drive economic development or if other factors must be addressed first. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or merely secondary to other factors remains unresolved.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that the development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution reflected increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further illustrate that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets. They highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of markets involved the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. The degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to these rules is a critical political factor. This commitment underpins the credibility of property rights and the stability of economic institutions. The costs per transaction will be falling, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This dynamic highlights how the reduction in transaction costs through institutional development can drive economic growth. The evolution of these institutions involved economic efficiency, political stability, and the credibility of commitments, which are essential for long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation was driven by European mortality rates, which influenced the colonization strategies adopted. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as Europeans were less likely to settle and more likely to exploit resources for quick extraction. These extractive institutions persisted and had long-lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) underscore the robustness of this finding by demonstrating that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all yield similar results. This robustness suggests that the institutional framework established during colonialism was a critical determinant of economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic opportunities, where the presence of extractive institutions can limit the ability of colonies to industrialize and achieve sustained economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that has had lasting effects on economic development, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) jointly argue. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and eroded trust among communities, leading to persistent mistrust that hinders economic growth. This mistrust manifests in various ways, including reduced cooperation and diminished social capital, which are crucial for economic development. The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade, are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalized mistrust creates a barrier to economic progress, as it undermines the collective action and social networks necessary for sustained growth. The impact of the slave trade on economic development varies across Africa. Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, were better equipped to support trade and economic activity. This highlights that the adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development were mitigated in areas with strong institutional frameworks, suggesting that the presence of extractive institutions can limit the ability of colonies to industrialize and achieve sustained economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both highlight that technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggests that without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be complemented by institutional adjustments to ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this by noting that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that can effectively manage the societal changes that accompany technological advancements. It is clear that technological advancements and institutional adjustments play complementary roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

The skill premium is a critical predictor of long-term economic performance, with institutions that guarantee fair execution of contracts playing a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This investment in human capital is facilitated by institutions that ensure the fair execution of contracts, such as guilds in western Europe, which managed the intergenerational transfer of skills. These institutions mitigated barriers to entry into skilled trades, thereby influencing the skill premium. The beneficial effects of such institutions may be offset by their attempts to form local cartels, which could restrict entry and keep relative pay above market-clearing levels. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where the skill premium serves as a key indicator of long-term economic performance. The skill premium in Europe was influenced by the costs of training, the chance of successfully finishing training, and the interest rate linking future earnings to present costs. These factors highlight the institutional framework that supported the development of skilled labor, which in turn drove economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine how inequality influenced economic performance in New World societies, focusing on specific historical contexts and policies that shaped development paths. They argue that the varying degrees of inequality in these societies were shaped by the initial conditions of their factor endowments and the institutions that evolved from them. These initial conditions, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created an environment where inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was prevalent from the outset. This inequality was further entrenched by institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. The persistence of these institutions contributed to the long-term economic underperformance of these societies. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that export production, which was a key driver of economic growth, was heavily influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This pattern highlights how the institutional framework, shaped by inequality, directly impacted economic performance. The role of institutions in perpetuating inequality and limiting economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which these institutions developed. This dynamic is particularly evident in the New World societies, where the institutional framework favored elites and restricted broader economic opportunities, leading to long-term economic underperformance.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific changes aimed at improving governance and economic conditions, but does not directly address how these reforms relate to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This approach focuses on the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, finding that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This suggests that the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, can help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. The specific mechanisms through which these institutional reforms translate into sustained economic growth remain under-explored. Englebert's conclusion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that while institutional reforms are crucial, their impact on long-run economic growth may be mediated by other factors, such as the quality of governance and the effectiveness of public institutions. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is thus multifaceted and requires a deeper understanding of the specific conditions under which these reforms can be effective.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that the role of women in African economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods was significant and often marginalized by traditional narratives. They were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men took over the cultivation of cash crops. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men's roles in economic activities. Frankema shows that colonial education systems introduced new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift in education policy had downstream implications for women's economic participation, as it created new opportunities for women to access education and participate in the formal economy. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that the demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which could limit women's access to education. The colonial education system, therefore, had a complex impact on women's economic participation, creating new opportunities while also reinforcing existing gender inequalities. This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped women's economic roles in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the oversimplification of causal mechanisms in institutional theories, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions. Both authors note that institutional theories often compress different historical periods and paths, leading to an incomplete understanding of institutional evolution. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that this compression can obscure the complex interplay between colonial governments and indigenous economic and political action. For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives, as seen in the case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium. This dynamic highlights how local economic and political contexts can shape institutional outcomes in ways that institutional theories may overlook. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that the persistence of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds can be attributed to the power dynamics and conflicts of interest among different social groups. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this perspective by noting that the institutional responses of colonial governments were not uniform and could vary significantly based on local conditions. This variability suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they do not operate in isolation from other factors.

The standard of living in Europe from 1500 to 1800 did not clearly correlate with economic development, a finding shared by van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3). This pattern can be attributed to the variability in measuring economic performance over long periods. Maddison's pioneering work on national accounts, although subject to margins of error, spurred scholarly engagement and refinement of economic growth estimates (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This variability suggests that while economic growth may have occurred, its impact on the standard of living was not uniform across Europe. This is evident in the shifting economic focus from the Mediterranean to the North Sea region, where England's economic development led to increased grain prices, reflecting a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This price inflation indicates a complex relationship between economic growth and living standards, where growth does not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in daily life.

Pamuk 2007 argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to significant economic divergences within Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Peters 2004, p.19). The economic trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe diverged significantly in the era following the Black Death, with institutional flexibility playing a pivotal role. The northwest, particularly regions like Holland, responded rapidly to new economic opportunities due to weak monopolies and the ability of new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional flexibility allowed for a more dynamic and adaptable economic environment, fostering growth and development. Regions like Tuscany struggled with stagnation and declining urban ranks, as political authorities strengthened existing monopolies, including their own. This institutional rigidity hindered economic progress and limited the region's ability to adapt to changing circumstances. Peters 2004 highlights that pervasive competition and conflict over land in sub-Saharan Africa reveal processes of exclusion and deepening social divisions (Peters 2004, p.5). The colonial period saw significant dissatisfaction expressed by agricultural officers in charge of schemes designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming. This dissatisfaction underscores the challenges faced in implementing agricultural improvements and the complexities of landholding practices in Africa. The structural changes initiated by the Black Death in Europe and the land conflicts in Africa both highlight the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. While institutional flexibility in Europe facilitated economic development, the rigidity and conflicts in Africa impeded progress, illustrating the broader implications of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the type of institutions established by colonial powers significantly impacted the economic trajectories of their colonies. The extractive institutions imposed in regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, hindered long-term economic development. Settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, which implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, experienced sustained economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th century England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, facilitating long-term economic growth. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in England shows how institutional frameworks can support economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that economic historians see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. This suggests that the institutional frameworks established during key historical periods have enduring effects on economic growth. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.9) is that the type of institutions established during critical periods, such as colonial rule or the early modern period, have long-lasting impacts on economic development. This institutional continuity shapes economic trajectories over the long run.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional design. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were solely driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, Ogilvie suggests that these institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which often led to the creation of extractive institutions that maximized rents rather than promoting long-term growth. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than fostering economic development. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) is that the design of institutions is heavily influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which often results in extractive institutions that hinder long-term economic growth. This underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design.

The historical evidence of institutional change in Africa provides further insight into the role of institutions in economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that by the 1990s, the new economic history of Africa had gained prominence, highlighting the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.19) argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Hopkins 2009, p.8) is that institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development. This historical evidence shows that the quality of institutions is the key determinant of long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the comparative analysis of institutional frameworks in former European colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutional frameworks, not geographic factors, are the key determinant of long-run economic growth.
