The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. The stakes are high, as the answer will determine the extent to which policy can guide growth, or whether growth is driven by forces that are beyond institutional control. The live disagreement is between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, are more influential. The former camp emphasizes the importance of institutions in creating the necessary conditions for economic growth, while the latter camp suggests that institutions are merely a reflection of underlying factors that determine economic outcomes. This debate highlights the importance of understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, and whether these mechanisms are robust enough to guide policy interventions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that secure property rights are a critical factor in economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutions in creating the conditions necessary for investment and economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in the 17th century, for example, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework was pivotal in fostering a long-term capital market, which in turn drove economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this point by showing how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, which secured property rights and promoted economic development, contrast with the extractive states set up in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the primary goal was rapid resource transfer to the metropole. This institutional divergence had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with those having secure property rights and strong institutions experiencing higher levels of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) add that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impact, suggesting that while institutions are critical, their effects depend on the specific context and the interests they serve. Institutions shape economic growth by creating the conditions necessary for investment and development, but their effectiveness is contingent on the underlying political and economic dynamics.

In this vein, North and Weingast, along with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights was pivotal for economic growth, especially in the context of seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They identify a specific mechanism through which these institutions operated: the establishment of credible commitments by the state to enforce property rights and contract enforcement. This mechanism fostered economic growth by reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment. This perspective challenges the notion that economic development is primarily driven by exogenous factors such as cultural beliefs or geographical advantages. By securing property rights, these institutions created a stable environment that incentivized long-term investment and economic activity. This evidence suggests that the institutional framework was a necessary condition for the economic development observed in seventeenth-century England. Inequality and slavery have played significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of institutions that either promote or hinder economic progress. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that societies with greater inequality tend to develop institutions that protect elite interests. These institutions often restrict competition and limit opportunities for broader economic participation, perpetuating inequality over time. The persistence of inequality is further exacerbated by the codification of customary laws that reinforce patriarchal structures, as seen in colonial Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional framework not only limits economic opportunities but also affects the distribution of resources, leading to underdevelopment. However, this dynamic is not universally applicable, as evidenced by the varying impacts of slavery on different regions. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had profound adverse effects on economic development. The disruption caused by these trades weakened social and economic institutions, making it difficult for African societies to recover and develop sustainably. Therefore, the long-term effects of inequality and slavery on economic growth are complex and multifaceted, influenced by both historical and institutional factors.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are fundamental to sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These technological advancements are not merely isolated events but require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use and diffusion. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that modern technology is incompatible with traditional modes of life and family patterns, necessitating significant societal changes. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex dynamics of long-run economic growth. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) acknowledges that growth-induced conflicts must be resolved peacefully to avoid long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how technological advancements can drive economic growth, but it also highlights the need for robust institutions to manage the societal changes that accompany such progress. Technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure that the benefits of new knowledge are fully realized and sustained over time.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that the Black Death initiated a series of structural changes that culminated in a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These changes were driven by a shift in the balance of power between workers and employers, as the labor supply contracted sharply in the aftermath of the Black Death. This demographic shock led to a significant increase in wages, particularly for skilled laborers, in the northwest. The resulting wage gap was further reinforced by the institutional flexibility of regions like Holland, which allowed for the rapid emergence of new towns and rural manufacturing under weak monopolies. This flexibility enabled these regions to respond more effectively to new economic opportunities, fostering a more dynamic and productive economy. However, Broadberry and Gupta note that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. This pattern of uneven development suggests that the structural changes initiated by the Black Death interacted with preexisting institutional and geographic factors to shape long-run economic growth.

These structural changes, in turn, highlight the complexities of historical paths and the role of African initiatives in shaping institutional change, as seen in the literature on colonialism and institutional reforms in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, compressing different historical periods and paths. This view contrasts with the idea that colonialism uniformly imposed detrimental institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations, often driven by fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial powers uniformly sought to suppress African economic development. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) also points out that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous societies can develop progressive institutions. This nuanced view underscores that the relationship between ethnic fragmentation and institutional quality is not straightforward, suggesting that African initiatives and local contexts played a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes. The complexity of these historical paths means that the impact of colonialism on African institutions is not uniform but rather shaped by a multitude of factors, including local initiatives and adaptations.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that economic performance, historically, is shaped by a multitude of factors beyond institutions alone, including the skill premium, trade patterns, and economic data availability. The skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance, influenced by the costs of training, the likelihood of successful apprenticeship completion, and the interest rate linking future earnings to present investments (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This framework suggests that the economic benefits of skilled labor can be countered by institutional barriers and cartel-like behaviors, which can artificially inflate wages and restrict entry into skilled trades (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century, demonstrating that economic growth was tied to foreign trade and export markets. However, the timing and scale of this growth varied widely due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation highlights that while institutions play a role, they are not the sole determinants of economic performance. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies to explore how historical conditions influenced long-term African welfare development. The persistence of poverty in Africa is not solely due to structural impediments but also influenced by specific historical and geographical conditions. This suggests that while institutional factors are important, they are part of a broader set of determinants that shape economic performance over the long run.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they influence the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly affected long-term economic outcomes, with extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies leading to lower income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore that institutions matter because they shape the economic environment in which growth occurs.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the interplay of historical and geographical factors. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He contends that the institutionalist approach, while valuable, needs to account for the specific historical contexts and the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8) highlights that institutions are not static but evolve in response to historical and geographical conditions.

The precise role of institutions in economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie, however, contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions are shaped by power dynamics rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This debate is settled by the empirical evidence on institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by the measurement of income per capita and the documentation of the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies, which provides a clear empirical basis for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
