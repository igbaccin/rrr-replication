The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high as this debate influences policy design and implementation. Institutions can be seen as the rules of the game in a society, which can either foster or hinder economic growth. The debate revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, or initial endowments. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, arguing that institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for sustained growth. Another position acknowledges the importance of institutions but also highlights the role of other factors, suggesting that institutions alone cannot fully explain economic growth. Additionally, there is a perspective that focuses on the endogenous nature of institutions, arguing that they evolve in response to economic and political pressures, and their impact on growth is mediated by these dynamics. Institutions are crucial for economic growth, but the extent to which they shape long-run outcomes remains a subject of ongoing debate. This debate is evident in the varying perspectives on how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic development.

North argues that the establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in this transformation. These institutions decreased the cost of transacting, which is a critical dimension of property rights that underpins economic growth. This decline in transaction costs was facilitated by political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms. This dynamic is evident in the growth of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. The success of these institutions in England was remarkable, as evidenced by the developments in capital markets. This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement was a critical political factor that bound the regime to these rules, fostering a stable environment for economic growth. The impact of these institutions varies across different contexts. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can vary based on the specific historical and political contexts. The role of institutions in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a range of factors including political stability and the enforcement of property rights.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional setups. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in areas with lower mortality, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial colonial policies, continued to affect economic outcomes long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions varies across different contexts. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their effects were mediated by other economic and historical factors.

Sokoloff and Engerman show that the impact of colonialism and slavery on African development highlights how historical institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). In the New World, the unequal distribution of resources and power led to the establishment of institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is also evident in the context of African development, where the legacy of slavery and colonial rule has left enduring marks on institutional structures. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial times played a crucial role in determining the trajectory of African development. The impact of colonial institutions on African education varied across different regions and contexts. For example, British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, highlighting the nuanced effects of colonial institutions on educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.1). The legacy of these institutions continues to shape economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring impact of historical inequality on contemporary African development.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Hopkins, examine the role of women in African economic development and the impact of colonial rule on institutional changes (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This cluster highlights the role of women in African economic development, arguing that they made significant contributions during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This highlights the importance of women's economic contributions, which were often marginalized in historical narratives. The new economic history of Africa, as discussed by Hopkins, underscores that good institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that while women's contributions were significant, the institutional framework under colonial rule constrained their full economic potential. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape contemporary African development, underscoring the enduring impact of historical inequalities on economic outcomes. This underscores the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic growth in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Mokyr notes that the development of formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, played a crucial role in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating its growth. Kuznets builds on this by emphasizing that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes and to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates that the modern national state is instrumental in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by pointing out that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth is more complex and less predictable than the direct influence of technological advancements. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, while significant, is secondary to the role of technological and intellectual progress.

This critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between institutional legacies and other factors in shaping economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share the view that specific institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe can explain historical economic phenomena. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, and institutional prescriptions could change in response to economic and political actions. This nuanced view of institutional change is supported by Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that institutions must be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the institutionalist approach has difficulty explaining why institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, their impact is complex and context-dependent.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic behavior. This is evident in the colonial context, where Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, leading to extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies and growth-promoting institutions in low-mortality colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita. Similarly, North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores the importance of institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that credible commitment underpins growth. Austin further elaborates on this by noting that institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, highlighting the complex and context-dependent nature of institutional impact (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is further illustrated by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts better explain core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that institutions are shaped by political dynamics and power struggles, which can either promote or hinder economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the reversal of fortune experienced by former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Hopkins' argument that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they provide the incentives for investment and the framework for economic activity. The empirical comparison of former colonies with different institutional frameworks settles the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
