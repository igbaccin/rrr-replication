The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. Institutions are the rules of the game that shape economic interactions and are critical for economic development. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and secure contracts are essential for sustained economic growth. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and cultural beliefs are more influential. The interplay between institutions and other variables, such as geography and culture, remains a complex and unresolved issue. North and Weingast explain that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because institutions governing public choice and property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was driven by the need for credible commitment, which underpinned England's economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplified this transformation, leading to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The transaction costs in a growing economy were further reduced by the emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement. These institutions facilitated market exchanges, ensuring that costs per transaction fell while the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP. As a result, the increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges, fostering economic growth. However, North and Weingast acknowledge that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be nuanced, as evidenced by the economic performance of France before the French Revolution. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance, suggesting that institutional changes alone may not account for all differences in economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that Europeans employed diverse colonization strategies, ranging from settler colonies with institutions enforcing the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences, shaped by mortality rates and the economic incentives of colonizers, had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of the colonies. The persistence of these institutions suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had enduring impacts on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. also note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, indicating that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic opportunities, highlighting that while institutions are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth. The long-term economic impacts of colonial institutions are profound, shaping the economic landscapes of former colonies for centuries.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes leading to significant economic divergences within Europe by 1450 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). These changes were driven by the differential responses of various regions to new economic opportunities, with regions like Holland exhibiting greater institutional flexibility. This flexibility allowed new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to thrive under weak monopolies. Regions such as Tuscany reinforced existing monopolies, which impeded economic growth. This institutional flexibility was crucial in enabling regions to adapt to the post-Black Death economic landscape. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) reinforce this idea, noting that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was largely due to merchant distribution and finance (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that the service sector played a pivotal role in economic growth during the early modern period, much like it does today. This uneven distribution of economic benefits highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic opportunities, where institutional flexibility was a key factor in determining which regions thrived and which stagnated. Pamuk (2007, p.23) further illustrates this point by examining urbanisation rates, another key indicator of productivity and economic development in the pre-modern period. The Black Death led to significant changes in urbanisation patterns, with northwestern Europe experiencing rapid urban growth while other regions lagged behind (Pamuk 2007, p.16). These urbanisation patterns were closely tied to institutional structures, with regions like Holland benefiting from institutional flexibility that allowed for rapid economic adaptation.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) examine the role of institutions in pre-industrial Europe, focusing on specific institutional structures like serfdom and guilds. They both argue that the role of institutions in economic development is crucial; however, they do not directly address how these institutions contribute to long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that institutions are more than just a fashion accessory but an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. This view aligns with the idea that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the behavior of economic actors. One of the downstream implications of this view is that the efficiency of institutions is not the primary driver of economic development, but rather the resolution of conflicts within the institutional framework. This perspective is further qualified by Austin's (2008, p.15) analysis of colonial administrations, which shows that institutional prescriptions can change in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the flexibility of institutions to adapt to changing circumstances is a critical factor in economic development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted.

This complexity is evident in the varied economic performance of African regions, challenging simplistic narratives of stagnation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that significant economic data exists for the colonial era in Africa, challenging the notion of persistent poverty. The growth of export production, tied to foreign trade and market conditions, varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the role of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. The absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left many questions about long-term economic development unaddressed (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The reconstruction of real wages and trends in British African colonies offers a more granular view of economic development, suggesting that historical conditions may have had a temporary rather than a structural effect on African welfare. This perspective underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence and their relationship to other parts of colonial Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that Africa's slave trades had a profound impact on the continent's long-term economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, significantly influencing economic outcomes. This mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trades, which disrupted social cohesion and eroded trust within and between ethnic groups. The adverse effects of the slave trades are not limited to historical mistrust, but also include the destruction of economic and social institutions, which were crucial for supporting trade and development. The slave trade's impact on economic development is evident in the destruction of centralized governments and well-developed markets, which were necessary for sustaining trade with Europeans. This disruption in institutional development hindered Africa's ability to engage in productive trade and economic growth, a pattern that is also evident in the historical data on African real wages. The historical data on African real wages reveals that these wages did not keep pace with those in other parts of the world, suggesting that the slave trades had a lasting impact on economic development. The slave trades' impact on economic development varied across Africa. The effects vary depending on the intensity and duration of the slave trades in different regions. This variation highlights the complex interplay between historical events and economic outcomes, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of the determinants of long-term economic development in Africa. This variability in the impact of the slave trades on economic development suggests that the effects of historical events on economic outcomes are not straightforward and require careful consideration of the specific historical and institutional contexts.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This is not merely about the accumulation of knowledge but also about the institutional frameworks that facilitate its application. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, and these adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. The ability of societies to adapt to technological changes and to implement institutional reforms that support these changes is a critical factor in achieving sustained economic growth. This interplay between technological and institutional progress is essential for long-term economic development, as it ensures that the benefits of technological advancements are fully realized and sustained over time. The historical record shows that technological progress can be hindered by the lack of institutional support, leading to a situation where the potential benefits of technological advancements are not fully realized.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they create the framework within which economic activities occur. Institutions that enforce property rights and ensure secure transactions are crucial for economic development. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, contributing to economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Austin agrees, noting that the institutional evolution and investment paths of older industrial economies are central to understanding their long-term economic performance (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical record of the English financial revolution shows that institutions like the Bank of England were instrumental in reducing transaction costs and ensuring the security of property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth.

The specific nature of institutions introduced by colonial powers also influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of different institutions in colonies. In some cases, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in others, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks had long-lasting effects on the economic development of these colonies, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. further show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with North and Weingast's argument that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in England shows that institutional changes were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. The development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6).
