The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game, shape economic incentives and constrain behaviour, influencing growth trajectories over extended periods. The live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions directly influence economic growth by fostering secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. Others posit that institutions' impact is mediated through cultural and social norms, or that their effects are contingent on historical and geographical contexts. The debate is not merely academic; it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing nations. The stakes are high, as misguided policies can perpetuate poverty and inequality. Institutions matter, but the precise manner in which they matter remains a contentious issue.

One key stream of scholarship emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, marked by swift resource transfers to the metropole, were different from the growth-enhancing institutions in settler colonies like the United States and Australia. This perspective is supported by North's argument that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further underscore the importance of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights, noting that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This stream of scholarship suggests that the quality of institutions, particularly those governing property rights and market exchanges, is a primary determinant of long-run economic growth. The historical evidence from England and its colonies illustrates how institutions can shape economic outcomes over centuries.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. They are the primary mechanism through which a society's economic potential translates into real economic growth. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. North highlights the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth. For example, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly lowered transaction costs and facilitated long-term capital markets. This institutional framework was pivotal in shaping England's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) elaborate on this by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes in European colonies. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States, economic growth was robust. Economic growth was limited in extractive states like the Congo or the Gold Coast. This institutional framework persists over centuries, as evidenced by the enduring economic disparities between former colonies with different institutional legacies. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, where the initial conditions set by colonial institutions continue to influence economic trajectories long after colonial rule has ended.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue. This institutional evolution was marked by a shift in political commitment to rules and enforcement mechanisms, which facilitated the development of markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) describe how the success of these institutions can be seen in the evidence from capital markets. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) extends this perspective by advocating for institutions to be incorporated as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians. Ogilvie's view underscores the importance of understanding how institutions can shape long-term economic trajectories, particularly through the lens of secure property rights. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes the complexity of institutional structures, observing that shared beliefs can be part of a society's institutional framework. This suggests that the efficacy of institutions in promoting economic growth is not solely determined by their formal structures but also by the underlying cultural and belief systems they embody. This interplay between formal institutions and cultural beliefs adds a layer of nuance to the understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a common posture that technological and knowledge-driven factors are primary drivers of economic growth, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Thus, the mechanism through which technological progress drives economic growth involves the efficient use of innovations, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This is critical because it highlights the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress. The implication is that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutions play a crucial role in enabling and sustaining this progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further notes that the growth-induced conflicts that arise from technological changes must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political conflicts that can arise from technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while institutions are important, their effectiveness in managing growth-induced conflicts can vary, and their impact on long-term economic growth may not be as consistent as the impact of technological progress. This variability in institutional effectiveness adds a layer of complexity to the relationship between institutions and long-term economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-term economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the transatlantic slave trade depleted Africa's population and fostered a culture of mistrust. This mistrust, they posit, persists to this day and significantly impedes economic development. One specific mechanism through which the slave trade affected development is the erosion of social capital. The slave trade disrupted traditional social structures and institutions, leading to a breakdown in trust and cooperation among communities. The loss of social capital, in turn, hindered collective action and reduced the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.19) complements this by noting that the absence of centralized governments and well-developed markets, which were crucial for sustaining trade, further exacerbated the negative effects of the slave trade. The impact of the slave trade on economic development varied across Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) qualify this by highlighting that the internal channel of mistrust, which operates through individuals' internal norms, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which operates through the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This indicates that the legacy of the slave trade is deeply ingrained in African societies, affecting both external institutions and internal norms and behaviors.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) along with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) focus on historical economic indicators and trends, showing that the colonial impact on African economic performance is a complex interplay of factors. Their work underscores the importance of understanding historical economic data for assessing long-term African welfare development. They demonstrate that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been relatively scarce, limiting Africa's appearance in comparative historical work on economic growth and development. Recent work has shown the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. This data reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. Patterns of economic growth and decline in these economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This suggests that the colonial impact on African economic performance is multifaceted, with economic growth and development influenced by a range of factors. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further elaborate on this by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries. Thus, the colonial legacy in Africa is not uniform and its impact on economic performance is shaped by a variety of historical and institutional factors.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality shaped economic outcomes by advantaging elites and restricting competition. Inequality in these societies was deeply rooted in the colonial legacy, where European settlers established colonies with varying degrees of inequality from the outset. This inequality was reinforced through government policies that protected the interests of elites, thereby restricting economic competition. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. The persistence of inequality indicates that these adjustments were often insufficient to overcome the entrenched advantages of the elite. This dynamic shows how the institutional frameworks in these societies were actively shaped by the interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions. This reveals that the impact of institutions on economic growth was not uniform and could vary significantly based on local conditions and the specific policies implemented. This variation underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic development in New World societies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally about the interplay between political structures and economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional frameworks in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, extractive states were created to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design directly influenced economic development paths. For instance, colonies with extractive institutions experienced less economic growth compared to those with more inclusive institutions. Austin highlights that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production in the absence of European settlers, illustrating how local economic conditions could drive institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction between colonial policies and local economic interests further underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the evolution of financial systems. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was a product of increased security in property rights, reflecting a broader trend where political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic development. North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence demonstrates that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Distributional conflicts also play a significant role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie challenges the notion that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, she argues that these institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts, where powerful groups sought to maximize their rents rather than promote long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. support this view by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
