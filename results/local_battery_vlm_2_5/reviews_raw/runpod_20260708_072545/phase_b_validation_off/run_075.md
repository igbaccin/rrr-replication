The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At stake is the very nature of the relationship between economic outcomes and the political and social structures that govern societies. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, specifically those that enforce property rights and contract enforcement, is crucial for sustained economic development. Others posit that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Additionally, there is a rival perspective that emphasizes the impact of colonial policies and the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities. The interplay between these factors underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic reform.

The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, during which certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement is evident in the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and underscored the importance of credible commitments in sustaining long-term economic development. The evolution of these institutions highlights the broader implications of secure property rights and credible commitments in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) extend this perspective by demonstrating how colonialism's institutional legacies continue to influence current economic outcomes. The establishment of different colonization strategies by Europeans resulted in varied institutional frameworks across colonies. In settler colonies with low mortality rates, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities, persisted long after the colonial period and continue to hinder economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasizes that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that alternative measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, yield similar results, reinforcing the robustness of the institutional legacy argument. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was also influenced by industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities played a central role in long-run development.

Distributional conflicts are a critical factor in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that these conflicts are pivotal in determining the nature of institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) posits that the persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be better understood through the lens of distributional struggles. These institutions were maintained by elites to preserve their advantages, highlighting how power dynamics within societies shape economic structures. This perspective is reinforced by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism is evident in the policies that favored elites, such as those that restricted land ownership and access to education, thereby maintaining social and economic disparities. The downstream implications of these institutional frameworks are qualified by the specific historical and geographical contexts in which they operate.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) illustrate that the impact of colonial rule on economic development varied significantly across regions, highlighting the importance of historical and geographical contexts. While the British colonial administration introduced formal education systems and promoted literacy, the educational infrastructure developed under British rule was more extensive than in other colonial territories. This educational development was driven by the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. These calculations altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). These educational systems were often designed to serve the administrative needs of the colonial state. The legacy of this educational infrastructure is evident in the higher literacy rates and more developed educational systems in former British colonies compared to other regions in Africa. Despite the educational benefits, the economic performance of these regions was still tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that the economic development of these regions was determined by the distribution of income and the long-term picture of economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa engendered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust, stemming from the historical exploitation and betrayal inherent in the slave trade, has been passed down through generations, shaping contemporary social norms. The internalisation of this mistrust has had profound effects on economic development, as it erodes the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Nunn argues that this mistrust is a direct consequence of the slave trades that occurred over nearly five centuries (Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade dismantled existing social structures and created an environment where cooperation was risky and mistrust was prevalent. Nunn's analysis of the Kongo kingdom illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets could facilitate trade, highlighting the institutional underpinnings that were disrupted by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings underscore the importance of trust in economic development, complementing studies that highlight trust's role in various economic and social contexts (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of mistrust, therefore, continues to impact Africa's economic performance by hindering the development of institutions that foster trust and cooperation.

Skill premiums, as illustrated by van Zanden's work, are a critical factor in predicting long-term economic performance, influenced by factors such as training costs and institutional barriers to entry (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Van Zanden explains that the skill premium is determined by the costs of training, the chance of successfully completing training, the interest rate, and the number of years a skilled worker can earn a higher wage (van Zanden 2009, p.3). These factors collectively influence the supply of skilled labor and, consequently, the skill premium. The role of institutions is evident in how guilds in western Europe managed apprentice contracts, ensuring fair execution and intergenerational skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Guilds also faced criticism for attempting to monopolize skills and restrict entry, which could artificially inflate the skill premium by keeping wages above market-clearing levels. This dynamic suggests that while institutions can facilitate skill development, they can also create barriers that distort economic outcomes. The implications of these institutional dynamics extend to the measurement of economic performance, as Bolt and van Zanden note that estimates of national accounts are subject to margins of error that increase with historical distance (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). These errors are based on subjective estimates, highlighting the challenges in accurately measuring economic performance over long periods. The subjective nature of these estimates underscores the need for collaborative research to improve the accuracy of economic measurements, as Maddison's work exemplifies (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, suggesting a different pathway to long-run economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; it requires complementary institutional and ideological changes. Kuznets posits that epochal innovations, which are major breakthroughs in human knowledge, drive sustained growth over long periods. These innovations necessitate adjustments in institutions and ideologies to be effectively integrated into the economy. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to useful knowledge. This reduction in access costs facilitates the dissemination and application of technological advancements, thereby enhancing economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that institutional progress must complement technological progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. Without this complementarity, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the critical role of institutions in maintaining long-term economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where institutions play a pivotal role in harnessing the potential of innovations for sustained development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Austin (2008, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that the causal mechanisms behind Africa's economic divergence from Europe are more complex than institutional differences alone. This thesis, while influential, compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, which can obscure the complex interplay between institutions, colonial policies, and economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) also notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that the impact of colonialism on African economic development was more nuanced than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests. This perspective is crucial for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as it underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and contextual factors that influence institutional development and economic performance.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of property rights, transaction costs, and the enforcement of economic rules. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional quality directly impacted the economic trajectories of these colonies, with inclusive institutions fostering long-term growth and extractive institutions hindering it. North argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Austin, who emphasizes the importance of considering the broader socio-political context in which institutions operate, as this context can influence the effectiveness of institutional reforms and their impact on economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin further notes that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional development (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic nature is also evident in the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further underscored by Hopkins, who argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the presence of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18; North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15) shows that institutions matter for economic growth, but the precise mechanisms through which they operate and the specific historical and contextual factors that influence their development remain subjects of ongoing debate.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the question of whether institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations or distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective challenges the belief that institutions are designed to maximize economic efficiency and suggests that they are often the result of political struggles between different groups. Acemoglu et al. support this view, arguing that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North argues that institutions are shaped by the need to reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, suggesting that efficiency considerations play a significant role in institutional development (North 1989, p.6). This debate highlights the complexity of institutional development and the need to consider both efficiency and distributional conflicts in understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.3; North 1989, p.6) shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with both efficiency considerations and distributional conflicts playing important roles. The empirical evidence from Africa shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the presence of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This evidence suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is determined by the broader socio-political context in which they operate.
