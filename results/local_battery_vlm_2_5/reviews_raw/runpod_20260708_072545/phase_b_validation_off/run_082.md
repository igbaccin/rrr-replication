The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. Scholars have debated the primacy of institutions over other factors, such as geography or culture, in determining economic trajectories. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if they are merely one of many factors. Another key debate focuses on the specific types of institutions that foster growth and the conditions under which they emerge. The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the relative economic positions of countries have reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, exemplifies this debate. The institutionalist approach, championed by scholars like North, emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as crucial for long-term economic growth. Meanwhile, others argue that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or if they are exogenous factors that shape economic outcomes. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for designing effective policies to promote development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) describe the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England as pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments exemplified the English financial revolution, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. This financial evolution reflected increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political model underpinning these changes was a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast argue that the success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, highlighting the political factors that underpin economic growth. These factors include the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing these rules. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, driving economic growth. The implications of these institutional changes are significant, as they show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for fostering economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are a crucial factor in shaping economic institutions, offering a compelling explanation for pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share the finding that institutional arrangements are influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to outcomes that prioritize the preservation of inequality over economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize economic growth. The institutional arrangements that emerge from distributional conflicts can be inefficient because they are designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the elite. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the persistence of serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be explained by the power of landowners and craft guilds to shape institutions to their advantage. This dynamic is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were structured to advantage elites through various government policies, such as restricting competition and limiting access to education and political power. The impact of distributional conflicts on institutions varies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that the persistence of inequality is not inevitable. The specific historical context and the balance of power between different groups can influence the outcomes of distributional conflicts. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor in shaping institutions, they are not the only determinant. The interaction between distributional conflicts and other factors, such as cultural beliefs and technological changes, can lead to a wide range of institutional outcomes.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. identify the establishment of extractive institutions as a key mechanism through which colonialism shaped economic outcomes. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the extractive nature of these institutions was driven by the political power of European colonists, who had little incentive to foster development that would not directly benefit them. This aligns with the findings of Acemoglu et al. that the persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development. The institutional legacy of colonialism, therefore, is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent force shaping contemporary economic trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. show that the colonial institutions that maximized rents for European colonists were not conducive to long-term growth. The extractive nature of these institutions discouraged investment and stifled economic development. This pattern is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where those with extractive institutions lagged behind those with more inclusive institutions. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. These findings highlight the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers continue to influence economic outcomes today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the impact of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa was more positive than other colonial powers. This institutional change incentivized African political leaders and families to invest in education, fostering its development. This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how the specific institutional framework imposed by British colonial rule created conditions that were more conducive to educational advancement than those under other colonial powers. The long-term economic implications of this educational development are significant, as it likely contributed to broader welfare improvements in the region. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including income distribution, to assess future growth possibilities (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, suggesting that the educational advancements under British rule played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors. The slave trade's duration, spanning nearly 500 years, significantly outlasted the period of colonial rule, suggesting that its institutional legacies are more profound than previously acknowledged. The presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated trade with Europeans, but the broader impact of the slave trade on African institutions was deleterious. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust, therefore, is a critical factor in understanding the long-run economic growth of Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner note that African economies, especially Anglophone ones, demonstrated higher levels of per capita income than previously thought by the early twentieth century, challenging the conventional narrative that they were stagnant or underdeveloped. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) assert that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been underutilized. This oversight has limited Africa's visibility in comparative historical work on economic growth. Consequently, this has led to a misconception that Africa has not generated significant economic data. Recent scholarship has revealed extensive economic data available for both the colonial and post-independence periods. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) also note that the economic performance of these economies is closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. For example, export production growth varied significantly between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors influenced the timing and scale of economic growth in these regions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) elaborate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800. This divergence is evident in the high productivity of the traded goods sector in north-western Europe. This productivity arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. The service sector thus played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. The implications of this divergence are significant, as it suggests that the economic development of these regions was driven by different factors, including institutional changes and investment in agriculture.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) share the finding that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was marked by significant structural changes, including shifts in urbanization rates and regional economic dynamics. These shifts were driven by institutional flexibility, as seen in the case of Holland, where new towns and rural manufacturing thrived under weak monopolies. This institutional environment fostered economic growth by enabling rapid responses to new opportunities. van Zanden (1999, p.1) also highlights that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader impact on the standard of living. These structural changes were not uniform across Europe; for instance, Tuscany experienced stagnation and a decline in urban ranks during the same period. This divergence underscores the role of institutional factors in shaping economic trajectories. Pamuk (2007, p.16) further elaborates on this by noting that structural change, including shifts in urbanization rates, was a key characteristic of economic growth in the early modern era. This institutional flexibility and the resulting structural changes had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as regions with more adaptable institutions were better positioned to capitalize on emerging economic opportunities. The varying responses to these opportunities across Europe suggest that institutional frameworks played a critical role in determining economic outcomes during this period.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are pivotal for harnessing technological innovations, suggesting that economic growth is driven by the interplay of technological progress and institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This institutional flexibility is crucial for sustaining economic growth, as it enables societies to adapt to new technologies and economic opportunities. This suggests that while technological advancements provide the potential for economic growth, the institutional framework must continuously adapt to sustain this growth over the long run.

This adaptation was crucial for fostering local commodity production and ensuring fiscal security, as seen in the case of Governor-General Clifford's policy. This suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic but rather responsive to local dynamics, a pattern that Hopkins builds on by emphasizing the importance of understanding Africa’s unique historical context. Hopkins further argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Englebert's research shows that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impact the quality of public goods provided by African governments, indicating that effective institutions can emerge regardless of ethnic diversity. This implies that institutional reforms in Africa should focus on creating incentives for investment and development, rather than being hindered by ethnic considerations. This suggests that while institutional reforms are crucial, they must be part of a comprehensive governance strategy that addresses the specific challenges faced by African countries.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the enduring impact of colonial institutions, which persistently affect current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional legacy continues to influence income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings highlight that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has long-lasting effects on economic development. The historical evidence of institutional persistence in former colonies and the role of secure property rights in economic growth are consistent with the view that institutions matter for long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9).
