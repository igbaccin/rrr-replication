The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic progress: are institutions the primary drivers, or do other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play a more significant role? One position emphasizes the pivotal role of institutions in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment and economic activity. This view is grounded in the idea that institutions shape incentives and constraints, influencing economic behavior and outcomes over the long term. A competing perspective, however, questions the primacy of institutions, arguing that other factors, such as cultural beliefs and geographic endowments, may be more decisive in determining economic trajectories. These rival explanations highlight the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the interplay between institutions and other determinants of growth. The debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy and practice, shaping how nations and international organizations approach economic development. The resolution of this debate could guide the design of policies aimed at fostering economic growth and reducing poverty. This interplay between institutions and economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where the establishment of extractive institutions by European colonizers significantly impacted the economic trajectories of their colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were instrumental in this process. They led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This dimension is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional evolution facilitated market exchanges by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement. The result was a growing proportion of GNP dedicated to the transactions sector, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, which show that political factors underpinning economic growth are as important as the rules governing economic exchange. The regime's commitment to these rules is a critical factor in the development of markets. This institutional framework not only fostered economic growth but also ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed as needed. The implications of these institutional changes are profound, as they suggest that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as seen in the historical context of England.

The importance of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, as emphasized by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), suggests that efficiency-based explanations may not fully capture the complexities of pre-industrial Europe's institutional structures. These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the power dynamics and distributional conflicts within societies. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the ways in which they advantaged elites, who had the power to enforce rules that maintained their privileged positions. This dynamic is evident in the historical context of New World societies, where institutions evolved to reflect the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiencies, underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which they were formed. Distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping the institutional frameworks that influenced long-term economic trajectories.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of former colonies by shaping their institutional frameworks, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) showing that these institutions were often extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The mortality rates of Europeans in these colonies determined the type of institutions that were established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, which focused on rapid resource transfer to the metropole rather than encouraging investment and economic development. These institutions were not designed to maximize long-run growth but to extract resources efficiently. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that even if institutions are in equilibrium, they may still be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups who benefit from maintaining the status quo. This highlights the role of colonial institutions in perpetuating economic stagnation in former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) qualify this by demonstrating that the impact of these institutions can be measured through various indicators, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and the efficiency of the judiciary. These measures show that the institutional legacies of colonialism are multifaceted and have enduring effects on economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) both show that British rule in Africa fostered formal education more than other colonial powers did. The British colonial administration's focus on formal education and infrastructure development also contributed to the growth of per capita income in many Anglophone African economies, which was tied to the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the British colonial framework, with its emphasis on education and trade, had a profound impact on the economic development of African societies. Frankema (2012, p.15) also notes that the development of formal education was not uniform across the continent.

The new economic history of Africa offers valuable insights into institutional reforms that can alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg share the finding that the continent's economic trajectory is deeply influenced by its institutional frameworks. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This insight is crucial because it underscores the significance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, regardless of ethnic diversity. Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight the importance of understanding the long-term economic development of Africa, including the distribution of income, to assess future growth prospects. This understanding is essential for designing effective institutional reforms. However, the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates poses a challenge in accurately tracing the persistence of poverty. This gap in data underscores the need for comprehensive historical reconstructions, which can provide a more nuanced picture of Africa's economic history and inform targeted institutional reforms. Understanding these historical trends can help identify the specific conditions that have affected long-term welfare development in Africa and guide the implementation of policies that promote sustainable economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade has left a lasting impact on African societies, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experiences of enslavement and exploitation, which have shaped intergroup relationships and social norms. The mechanism through which the slave trade influences contemporary trust levels involves both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from historical experiences, is at least twice as large as the external channel. This finding underscores the profound and enduring effects of the slave trade on African societies, where historical trauma has shaped contemporary social attitudes and behaviors. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this perspective by showing that colonial administrations were not monolithic in their approach to African labor and economic development. The colonial governments' responses to indigenous economic and political actions sometimes led to higher production equilibria, suggesting that the impact of the slave trade on trust was not uniform across all regions and periods. This nuance indicates that while the slave trade has had a significant impact on trust levels, the specific outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significantly influenced by the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had lasting impacts on economic development. In colonies where Europeans settled and implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States and Australia, economic growth was robust. Conversely, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, leading to persistent underdevelopment. This shows that the institutional frameworks put in place during colonial rule were pivotal in determining the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) further argue that these institutions were not merely a product of geographical factors but were shaped by the specific policies and actions of colonial administrations. The institutional legacies of colonialism, therefore, continue to influence economic outcomes in many parts of the world today.

The historical evolution of institutions also highlights the importance of political and economic actions in shaping long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that colonial governments' responses to indigenous economic and political actions sometimes led to higher production equilibria, indicating that the impact of colonial policies was not uniform. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This nuance indicates that while colonial policies had significant impacts, the specific outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors. The colonial administrations' responses to indigenous actions show that the institutional frameworks were not static but evolved in response to local conditions.

The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The development of new financial instruments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This, in turn, facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs per transaction. However, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This highlights the importance of cultural and institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. The new economic history of Africa, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The experience of East Asia might be more relevant to Africa than that of the United States. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This indicates that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a variety of historical, economic, and political factors.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a matter of agreement and disagreement. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter. North (1989, p.9) shows that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This nuance indicates that while colonial policies had significant impacts, the specific outcomes were influenced by a complex interplay of historical, economic, and political factors. The historical evidence of colonial administrations' responses to indigenous actions, as well as the empirical comparison of different colonial policies, settles the disagreement by showing that the institutional frameworks were not static but evolved in response to local conditions.
