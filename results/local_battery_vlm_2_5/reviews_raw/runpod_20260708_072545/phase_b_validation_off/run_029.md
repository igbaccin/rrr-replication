The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central topic in economic research, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions are a primary driver of economic growth or if they are merely a secondary factor influenced by other variables. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous factors versus endogenous processes. Some scholars argue that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and climate, while others contend that institutions are the result of endogenous processes driven by political and economic forces. Another key debate revolves around the nature of institutions themselves: are they efficient responses to economic needs, or are they shaped by distributional conflicts and power dynamics? These debates highlight the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions function and evolve. This nuanced understanding is critical for developing effective policies aimed at promoting economic growth and development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) establish that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering economic growth, as the evolution of institutions governing public choice facilitated market exchanges. These institutional innovations were economic responses deeply rooted in political dynamics, reflecting a trade of certain rights to Parliament for revenues during fiscal crises (North 1989, p.9). The implications of these institutional changes reach beyond economic growth. The institutional framework that emerged in England also played a crucial role in shaping the broader political and economic landscape of Europe, influencing the development of other nations' institutions and economic policies. The degree to which a regime or sovereign is committed to these rules is a critical political factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment, underpinned by credible institutions, ensures that economic growth is sustained over the long run.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns, which in turn shaped long-term economic trajectories. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, fostering little local economic development. These institutions persisted over time, locking former colonies into patterns of underdevelopment. In areas with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to maximize resource extraction. This institutional framework had lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) reinforce this by noting that the quality of institutions, including constraints on the executive, property rights, and the rule of law, significantly influenced economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time is a function of their initial design and the political dynamics that maintain them. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may resist changes that threaten their rents or political power. This dynamic can lead to the persistence of extractive institutions, even if they do not foster long-run economic growth. Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as the external environmental factors, play a crucial role in this dynamic (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn's research underscores the prolonged impact of the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, highlighting that these trades had a profound and lasting effect on Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn also notes that the local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets (Nunn 2008, p.19). This qualification underscores that while the slave trades had a significant adverse effect, the presence of strong institutions could mitigate some of the negative impacts.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). He suggests that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not necessarily determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They assert that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth. A better grasp of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, will help to evaluate the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered, which is why they reconstructed and compared real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. This work underscores that the role of institutions in shaping economic development in Africa is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of both endogenous and exogenous factors.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered a more robust development of formal education than other colonial powers, challenging the notion that colonialism uniformly hindered African development. The British administration's emphasis on education was driven by both macro- and micro-level calculations, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This institutional shift was influenced by the implementation of new social and political structures, which made formal education more attractive to African political leaders and families. British rule's relative success in promoting formal education can be attributed to its institutional frameworks, which encouraged investment in human capital. The demand for missionary education was intricately tied to these institutional changes, as African leaders and families weighed the costs and benefits of formal education within the new colonial context. While British rule facilitated education, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) explain that extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to institutional structures that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, while fostering formal education in some contexts, could also entrench economic disparities in others. The long-term effects of British colonial education policies in Africa highlight the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial legacies shape contemporary African economies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) jointly argue that the institutional arrangements of pre-industrial Europe shaped economic outcomes in ways that are not immediately generalizable to other contexts or periods. This cluster examines specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena without directly addressing the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This suggests that, under certain conditions, colonial institutions could facilitate economic growth, even if they did not directly aim for it. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which institutions operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate economic activity by providing a stable framework for exchange and investment. Acemoglu et al. assert that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, depending on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North similarly highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shared emphasis on the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic activity highlights their fundamental importance in long-run economic development. Austin agrees, noting the importance of secure private property rights in fostering economic growth, as well as the need to understand the specific paths of institutional evolution in different historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.2).

The political and social contexts in which institutions operate are also crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, drove the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These insights underscore the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape institutions and their economic outcomes. Austin shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15).

The enduring impact of institutions on economic development is evident in the long-run effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Hopkins, who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth and development is underpinned by the evidence that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is significantly affected by the presence of poor institutions, not by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions and their long-run effects on economic development settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, showing that institutions designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups can have extractive effects and hinder long-run growth.
