The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. It addresses how institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules, informal constraints, and their enforcement, drive economic outcomes over extended periods. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policy and theory about how to foster sustained economic prosperity. There are two main positions in this debate. One side argues that institutions fundamentally determine economic growth by shaping incentives, reducing uncertainty, and facilitating transactions. The other side posits that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables such as culture, geography, or technological change play equally or more significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if they are secondary to other factors. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) address related but distinct questions, so this review does not engage their arguments.

North 1989 and North and Weingast 1989 argue that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in 17th century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. In England, the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically decreased transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The English financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, particularly the time dimension of property rights, which is crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The political institutions that specified and enforced property rights were pivotal in this process. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War necessitated the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, marking a critical juncture in the evolution of these institutions (North 1989, p.9). This development involved economic rules and the institutions governing their enforcement and change. The commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutional changes have implications that go beyond economic performance. Recent research has upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This indicates that while institutions were crucial, their impact was part of a broader context that included other factors such as economic performance and bureaucratic structures.

(2001, p.27) and (2002) share the finding that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, with the nature of these institutions being driven by the Europeans' mortality rates in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The Europeans' mortality rates determined whether they established extractive institutions, which focused on rapid resource extraction, or inclusive institutions, which promoted investment and economic growth. This mechanism highlights the critical role of institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time is not guaranteed. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the effects of colonial institutions on current income per capita are significant, indicating that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This long-run impact suggests that while initial institutional design was crucial, subsequent changes in institutions and policies could mitigate or exacerbate the effects of colonial legacies.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing with Nunn and Wantchekon that it oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). They both argue that this compression obscures the complex interplay of factors that have shaped economic outcomes across continents. One specific mechanism they highlight is the role of colonial institutions in influencing indigenous economic and political action. Austin shows that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their policies in response to local incentives, which could lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior suggests that colonial institutions could evolve in response to local conditions. Nunn and Wantchekon further illustrate the lasting impact of historical events by demonstrating that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and significantly affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This enduring legacy underscores the deep and persistent influence of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin's work shows that the effects of colonial institutions could be mitigated or exacerbated by local adaptations, indicating that the path to economic development is shaped by a complex interplay of historical legacies and local responses.

Frankema and van Waijenburg re-evaluate historical economic performance in Africa, challenging the notion of persistent poverty and highlighting periods of growth and decline tied to foreign trade and export markets (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This reevaluation is crucial because it shifts the narrative from one of uninterrupted stagnation to a more dynamic picture of economic fluctuations. The economic performance of African countries was intricately linked to global trade patterns and the demand for exports, with periods of growth and decline closely following the global market's ebb and flow. Broadberry and Gardner observe that the economic development of many Anglophone African economies was significantly influenced by export markets, which were themselves subject to global economic cycles and geopolitical shifts (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complexity of African economic history and the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors driving growth and decline. African economic development is determined by both external factors and internal dynamics and historical conditions. This highlights the need for further research into the specific historical and geographical conditions that have influenced African economic development over time.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes the role of the modern national state in peacefully resolving conflicts that arise from technological and economic changes. The shared finding is that technological progress and institutional adjustments are interdependent, with technological advancements necessitating institutional support to achieve long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not institutions themselves, fundamentally shape key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World. This perspective highlights that the distribution of power and resources among societal groups drives the development of economic institutions. The mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape institutions involves the way elites use their power to create and maintain institutions that favor their interests. This process is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by the conflicts between different social classes. This pattern of elite-driven institutional development has a downstream implication: the persistence of inequality over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the persistence of inequality is not guaranteed. The institutional structures created by elites can be challenged and changed over time, as distributional conflicts evolve and new power dynamics emerge. Thus, while distributional conflicts are a powerful driver of institutional development, the long-term effects of these conflicts depend on the specific historical and social contexts in which they occur.
