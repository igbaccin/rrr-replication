The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives prosperity: is it the efficiency of institutions that matter, or are there other factors at play? One position argues that institutions, by enforcing property rights and providing stable governance, are crucial for economic growth. Some scholars contend that other factors, such as distributional conflicts or the nature of institutions themselves, are more critical. Understanding these conflicting viewpoints is essential for crafting effective policies aimed at fostering economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one piece of a larger puzzle. This question is particularly relevant in the context of historical and contemporary economic development, where the interplay between institutions and economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted. Institutions such as secure property rights and stable governance are often cited as key factors in promoting economic growth, but the extent to which they are truly decisive remains a subject of ongoing debate.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) assert that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. This argument aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) observation that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others implemented extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for long-term economic development. The implications of these institutions extend beyond mere economic efficiency; they also influence the distribution of political power and the stability of governance structures. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize their own interests over long-term economic growth. This dynamic can lead to the persistence of extractive institutions, which hinder economic development by maximizing rents for the ruling elite. This institutional design can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, shaping the trajectory of development in former colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting how institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in their long-run development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They note that the institutional changes in England, including the development of a strong, centralised state and the establishment of a credible commitment to property rights, were instrumental in promoting economic growth. These changes led to a significant improvement in the functioning of capital markets, as evidenced by the increased availability of credit and the development of financial institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) supports this view, highlighting the importance of incorporating institutions into long-term research programs. She argues that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. The evolution of institutions in England during this period was marked by a shift towards greater economic freedom and a reduction in the power of the monarchy. This shift was accompanied by the development of a strong legal system that protected property rights and enforced contracts. The result was a more stable and predictable economic environment, which encouraged investment and innovation. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that the focus should be on how institutions actually function in practice. This practical approach is essential for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England provides a clear example of how institutional changes can have a profound impact on economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800. This divergence is evident in the structural changes that occurred in Europe, particularly in the wake of events like the Black Death. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This structural change was driven by the unusual degree of institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, which enabled new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies. This institutional flexibility allowed regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, contrasting with regions like Tuscany, where political authorities strengthened existing monopolies. The economic divergence was also influenced by the shift in economic development towards the countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift confirms that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries by the mid-eighteenth century. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through both production and merchant distribution and finance. This role was particularly pronounced in regions where the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, benefiting skilled labourers more than unskilled ones. Therefore, the economic divergence and structural changes in Europe before 1800 were shaped by a complex interplay of institutional flexibility, economic opportunities, and the evolving role of the service sector.

The colonial impact on African economic development is marked by a complex interplay of historical, social, and institutional factors. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) show that colonial rule significantly altered the economic landscape of Africa by introducing new social and political institutions that influenced the development of formal education. This was especially pronounced in British colonies, where the demand for missionary education was driven by new cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was further complicated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often reinforced patriarchal structures and marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). While the introduction of formal education and new institutions had potential benefits, the unequal distribution of these benefits exacerbated existing inequalities. This institutional framework shaped long-run economic growth in Africa by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities, thereby affecting the overall development trajectory. The implications of these institutional changes are vast, as they continue to influence contemporary economic and social structures in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. They establish that African economies, particularly in the colonial period, were more developed than existing narratives suggest. One specific mechanism that supports this claim is the availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era, which has enabled recent research to reassess Africa's economic performance. This data-driven approach reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the importance of revisiting historical economic data to gain a more accurate understanding of Africa's economic trajectory. The structural changes and political institutions that influenced export production varied both between and within countries, complicating the picture of economic growth during the colonial era (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These variations highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that drove economic development in Africa, as well as the long-term effects of colonial rule on the continent's economic welfare. This nuanced understanding is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that the complexities of post-independence economic development in East Africa are deeply intertwined with patterns of expenditure and land conflicts, which are shaped by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. These expenditure trends, which were not exceptional, reflect the broader economic and political dynamics of the region. This expenditure was particularly marked in Kenya, where subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to less access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief. This process was further complicated by the growth in military spending, as domestic military expenditure began to grow in the independence period and escalated towards the end of the 1960s and 1970s in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent. The growth in military spending was a significant shift from the colonial era, where the metropolis had effectively subsidized much of the defence of the colonies. This expenditure pattern highlights the complex interplay between economic policy and political dynamics in the region.

These expenditure trends are closely linked to the broader economic and political dynamics of the region. Peters (2004, p.19) argues that pervasive competition and conflict over land in sub-Saharan Africa reveal processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation. This competition over land is a significant factor in the economic development of the region, as it shapes the distribution of resources and the allocation of capital. The land conflicts in the region are deeply rooted in the colonial era, where longstanding prejudice against African landholding practices was fuelled by the push to agricultural development. The most overt dissatisfaction was expressed by agricultural officers in charge of schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming. This dissatisfaction highlights the complex interplay between economic policy and political dynamics in the region, as well as the broader economic and political dynamics of the region. These processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation are a significant factor in the economic development of the region, as they shape the distribution of resources and the allocation of capital. This pattern of expenditure and land conflicts highlights the complex interplay between economic policy and political dynamics in the region, as well as the broader economic and political dynamics of the region.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both share the idea that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective suggests that technological progress, particularly the introduction of epochal innovations, is crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this mechanism by noting that these innovations necessitate significant institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure that technological changes are effectively integrated into the economy (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are essential for resolving conflicts that arise from the adoption of new technologies, thereby facilitating modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by emphasizing the need for institutional progress to complement technological progress, suggesting that without such complementary progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments highlights the complex dynamics involved in achieving sustained economic growth, where the accumulation of useful knowledge is a key driver.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa generated a legacy of mistrust that continues to affect economic development. The slave trade’s impact on social trust is multifaceted, involving both external environmental factors and internal norms. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This suggests that the enduring mistrust fostered by the slave trade operates through deeply ingrained social attitudes, which are more difficult to alter than external conditions. This mistrust, in turn, can impede economic development by reducing cooperation and social capital. Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights the prolonged duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, compared to the relatively shorter period of colonial rule. This historical context underscores the deep and lasting effects of the slave trade on African societies. In the Kongo kingdom, for example, the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade with Europeans, illustrating how institutional factors could mitigate some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. The legacy of the slave trade continues to shape economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a comprehensive understanding of historical factors in economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Austin, argue that colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of wealth and power. They contend that institutions in New World societies were structured to advantage elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework, which favored the elite, was deeply rooted in the factor endowments of these societies. The abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor created an environment where inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was virtually inevitable from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality, in turn, shaped the development of institutions that reinforced the status quo, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. Austin highlights that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often responded to indigenous economic and political actions by adjusting their institutional prescriptions. This adaptive behavior contradicts the hypothesis that colonial institutions were uniformly oppressive. Austin's work also underscores that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This dynamic illustrates how colonial institutions could both restrict and facilitate economic activity, depending on the specific context and the interests of the ruling elite. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which advantaged elites and restricted competition.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme that emerges from the literature on colonial legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, depending on mortality rates and the resulting settlement patterns. Where settlers were present, institutions were designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. Where settlers were absent, extractive institutions were put in place to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional design in economic outcomes, as extractive institutions stifled long-term growth by failing to incentivize investment and innovation.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth and development. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting. This reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) also emphasizes the importance of institutions, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that good institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) shows that institutions are central to long-run economic growth. The disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists were central to the long-run development of the former colonies. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that colonial institutions could both restrict and facilitate economic activity, depending on the specific context and the interests of the ruling elite. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional design across colonies with different settlement patterns and mortality rates. This variation shows that the extractive nature of institutions was a product of specific historical and contextual factors.
