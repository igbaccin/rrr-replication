The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can significantly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, constraints, and opportunities for economic actors. The live disagreement centers around the mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, while others highlight the importance of historical and political contexts in shaping institutional outcomes. Additionally, there is a debate on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for policating effective institutional reforms that can promote sustainable economic development. The extent to which institutions matter for growth is evident in the historical experiences of various regions, where differences in institutional frameworks have led to divergent economic trajectories. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlement patterns, have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. This historical perspective underscores the enduring influence of institutions on economic growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions like the Bank of England played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) both argue that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered the cost of transacting. This financial revolution was crucial for economic growth because it increased the security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and overall economic expansion. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction, which in turn facilitated market exchanges. This institutional framework was essential for economic growth, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. The development of new financial instruments and the establishment of the Bank of England were not isolated events but part of a broader institutional framework that ensured secure property rights and effective enforcement. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the capital markets, which showed a commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange. The political factors underpinning economic growth, particularly the degree to which the regime was bound by these rules, were critical for sustained economic development. This implies that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by other factors, including the broader economic and political context.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates. In colonies with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This institutional framework was designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. The extractive nature of these institutions was a direct result of the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers. Acemoglu et al. caution that the impact of these institutions varied. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This implies that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact on economic growth was mediated by other factors, including the broader economic and political context.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that historical inequalities, shaped by institutions, have persisted over time. The roots of inequality in New World societies can be traced back to the colonial period, when institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The legacy of these institutions is evident in the enduring disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power. The impact of these historical inequalities extends to social and political institutions as well as economic outcomes. The persistence of these inequalities can be seen in the continued mistrust within African societies, which Nunn and Wantchekon note is a direct result of the slave trade. This mistrust has had a profound impact on economic development, as it undermines the trust necessary for economic exchange and cooperation. While the slave trade is a significant factor in the development of mistrust, it is not the only one. The broader economic and political context also plays a role in shaping the institutions that govern economic activity. Nunn (2008, p.19) argues that the slave trades in Africa had an adverse effect on economic development, independent of other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. The legacy of these historical inequalities is evident in the continued disparities in economic development and social outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of institutions on long-run economic growth.

Technological and intellectual advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth, with the growth of useful knowledge being the mainspring of this process, according to Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress are more fundamental to economic growth than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This adjustment is crucial because technological progress alone is not sufficient for sustained economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be limited and potentially unsustainable. This implies that while technology and knowledge are the primary drivers, institutions play a supportive role in facilitating and sustaining economic growth. This qualification suggests that while institutions are important, their role is more dynamic and subject to change compared to the steady accumulation of technological knowledge.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This interplay is crucial for formulating effective institutional reforms. Hopkins further emphasizes the significance of good institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Ogilvie cautions that institutions should be treated as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions, one that goes beyond superficial analyses and delves into the intricate dynamics that govern their formation and impact. Frankema's research on the role of British rule in the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa provides a historical context for understanding how different colonial institutions influenced educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.1). This historical context is vital for appreciating the long-term effects of institutional reforms, as it shows that the impact of institutions can be both enduring and multifaceted. The downstream implication of this historical context is that current institutional reforms must consider the legacy of past policies, as well as the cultural and economic factors that shape their effectiveness.

Broadberry and Gardner's analysis of early twentieth-century Anglophone African economies, together with Broadberry and Gupta's examination of pre-1800 European and Asian economies, shows that economic performance in these regions was shaped by complex interactions between local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This shared finding highlights the importance of institutional factors in determining economic outcomes, even in contexts where other factors such as geography and climate might be expected to dominate. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly, influenced by factors ranging from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner's work on African economies underscores that foreign trade and export markets played a critical role in shaping economic growth patterns. This pattern suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to facilitate trade and integrate economies into global markets. Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of Europe and Asia before 1800 points out that the benefits of early modern economic development were not uniformly distributed. This uneven distribution indicates that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by social and economic inequalities. The historical evidence from these studies suggests that institutional reforms aimed at promoting economic growth must consider the broader social and economic context in which they are implemented.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) identify that historical and economic contexts are critical for understanding African poverty. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the importance of local incentives and responses in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) acknowledge that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, indicating that further research is needed to fully understand the historical context of African poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This suggests that while historical and economic contexts are crucial, there are still significant gaps in our understanding of African poverty that need to be addressed.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) reinforces this idea by emphasizing that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth and development. This is because the political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement result in falling transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about the distribution of power and resources as well as efficiency.
