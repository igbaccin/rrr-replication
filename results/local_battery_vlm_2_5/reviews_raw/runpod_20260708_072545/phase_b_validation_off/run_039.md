The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to understanding the development trajectories of nations. At stake is the identification of the causal mechanisms that drive economic prosperity or stagnation over extended periods. One key disagreement centers around the role of institutions versus other factors such as geography or cultural beliefs. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are pivotal in fostering economic growth. These institutions create a stable environment that encourages investment and innovation. Other scholars, however, posit that the impact of institutions is overstated, suggesting that factors like population density, prosperity, and cultural beliefs may play more significant roles. These differing perspectives highlight the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the institutional framework. North's work underscores the critical role of institutions in economic growth, emphasizing that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for fostering a dynamic economy. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita. The interplay between institutions and economic outcomes is further complicated by the influence of population density and prosperity, which shape the policies pursued by colonial powers. This interplay suggests that the long-run development of former colonies is deeply intertwined with the institutional changes introduced during colonialism.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. In this period, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets, which shows that the political factors underpinning economic growth were as important as the rules governing economic exchange. A critical political factor was the regime's commitment to the rules and the institutions governing how these rules were enforced and changed. This institutional framework was essential for fostering a dynamic economy. The impact of these institutions varied across Europe. For instance, the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV in France did not hinder economic growth as much as traditionally believed. This suggests that while institutions were crucial, their effects varied depending on the specific historical and political context.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic growth of former colonies, as both Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) emphasize. These institutions were actively designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. The European settlers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In areas with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, designed to benefit the colonizers, had long-lasting effects on the economic development of these regions. The extractive nature of these institutions meant that they were not conducive to long-run economic growth, as they did not encourage investment or the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions post-independence further hindered the economic development of these colonies. The impact of these institutions varied across all colonies. For instance, in regions where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, the economic outcomes were significantly different. This variation highlights the critical role of institutional design in shaping economic growth. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence the economic trajectories of former colonies today, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic development.

The new economic history of Africa highlights that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This perspective is supported by Frankema (2012, p.15), who shows that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was shaped by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. These institutions were not imposed unilaterally by colonial powers but were influenced by African political leaders and families who made cost-benefit calculations (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic interplay between exogenous and endogenous factors underscores the complexity of institutional development in Africa. However, the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability highlights the importance of understanding the specific conditions under which institutions can drive economic growth in Africa.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment. Nunn and Austin both emphasize that the slave trades had a profound impact on African economic development, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn documents that the African continent experienced four slave trades over nearly 500 years, a duration far longer than the period of colonial rule. Austin elaborates on this disruption, noting that in areas where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could sometimes align with the interests of local populations. This dynamic created a complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic incentives, which could sometimes lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). The implication is that the legacy of slave trades and colonial policies created a complex web of factors that shaped African economic development, with institutions playing a more nuanced role than previously acknowledged. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that the persistence of African poverty should not be overstated, as it is not solely a result of historical impediments. They argue for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, suggesting that specific historical or geographical conditions may have had a temporary rather than a structural effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Mokyr argues that technological and intellectual advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets supports this view, noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that institutions may facilitate economic growth by accommodating technological advancements. Kuznets elaborates on the importance of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the role of political stability in sustaining economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). However, Mokyr cautions that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible, economic reasonableness and institutional progress are less so, indicating that the sustainability of economic growth depends on continuous institutional adaptation (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the complex interplay between technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, where technological advancements set the pace and direction of growth, while institutions adapt to support and sustain this growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, are the primary drivers behind the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World. These conflicts led to the formation of institutions like serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which were designed to protect the interests of dominant groups rather than maximise overall economic efficiency. For example, in New World societies, institutions that emerged from these distributional conflicts tended to favour elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This perspective underscores the role of institutional design in perpetuating economic inequality, as these institutions were often structured to maintain the power and wealth of the ruling classes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further argue that extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of these societies shaped the institutions that evolved, reinforcing the advantages of the elite. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions in economic history, suggesting that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools rather than mere accessories. This implies that while distributional conflicts shaped these institutions, their impact on economic growth was not uniform, and their effects could be mitigated by changes in institutional design over time.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. The creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and secure property rights is instrumental in fostering economic growth. For example, the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 led to a significant reduction in transaction costs, which was critical for economic growth. This financial revolution reflected increased security in property rights, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers in colonies such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which was crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The effectiveness of these institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs over time underscores their importance in long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

However, the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by various factors, including distributional conflicts and institutional design. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and the craft guild. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups that sought to maximize their rents, rather than to maximize long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to promote long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that the impact of these institutions on economic growth varied significantly, depending on the specific institutional design and the political context in which they were implemented.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the complex interplay between institutions, political power, and economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This approach highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors, such as political power and economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's argument is supported by Hopkins, who shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This indicates that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions, which are crucial for economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence from Africa shows that the presence of poor institutions is linked to Africa's dismal economic record, highlighting the importance of institutional reforms in eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Overall, institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. However, the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by various factors, including distributional conflicts and institutional design. The historical evidence from colonial institutions and the new economic history of Africa shows that the presence of poor institutions is linked to dismal economic records, highlighting the importance of institutional reforms in promoting long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the comparative analysis of institutional design and its impact on economic growth in former European colonies, which shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
