The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they are merely one factor among many. One side of the debate argues that institutions are critical for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and efficient markets. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering investment. Another side contends that institutions are not the sole determinant of economic growth and that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play significant roles. For example, some scholars argue that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the relationship between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause of long-run economic growth or if they are one of many factors that contribute to economic outcomes. This debate is central to understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. A full understanding of the role of institutions requires examining the specific mechanisms through which they influence economic outcomes and the extent to which other factors also play a role. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, with specific institutional frameworks shaping long-term economic outcomes. This institutional framework, which includes secure property rights and enforceable contracts, creates an environment conducive to investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes in European colonies. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic growth was more robust. Extractive states, designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern suggests that the type of institutions established during colonization had a lasting impact on economic outcomes, with colonies that developed institutions promoting investment and secure property rights experiencing higher levels of economic growth. This implies that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness in fostering economic growth depends on their design and the interests they serve.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) assert that the institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in promoting secure property rights and facilitating economic growth. The specific mechanism through which this occurred, as North and Weingast highlight, involved the development of institutions that ensured credible commitment to rules and policies, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic activity. This credible commitment was essential for the functioning of capital markets and the overall economic development of England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman expand on this by illustrating how the institutional framework in New World societies, particularly those with high levels of inequality, often favored elites and restricted competition, leading to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The implication is that institutions must not only exist but also be structured in a way that promotes inclusive growth rather than entrenching the interests of a few.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). According to Ogilvie, this perspective is supported by the fact that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and the craft and merchant guilds were structured to maintain the power of elites rather than to maximize economic efficiency. This approach is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The shared finding is that institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but are deeply influenced by historical and cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn further illustrates this point by demonstrating that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on the continent's economic development, highlighting how historical events can shape institutional frameworks in ways that persist over centuries (Nunn 2008, p.19). This insight is particularly relevant for understanding how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth, depending on the historical and cultural context in which they are embedded.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Broadberry and Gupta, examine specific historical cases of economic performance to illuminate the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). They show that the economic performance of regions can be significantly influenced by factors such as market access, transport costs, and political institutions, which collectively shape the institutional environment. Broadberry and Gardner illustrate this by demonstrating that the growth and contraction of economies in Anglophone African countries were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets. This pattern underscores the importance of external economic forces in shaping domestic economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta further elaborate on this by highlighting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture and other sectors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The uneven distribution of economic growth's benefits, as noted by Broadberry and Gupta, suggests that the institutional frameworks that support economic development may not always ensure equitable distribution of its fruits (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This uneven distribution can have lasting implications for economic inequality, a theme that resonates with the findings on institutional evolution and inequality.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. Both authors underscore the importance of technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge in sustaining economic development. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing the role of technological progress in driving economic improvement. This is further supported by Kuznets, who notes that epochal innovations, which are major breakthroughs in human knowledge, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, as Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) points out. He argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural modes of life and extended family patterns, necessitating significant societal changes. However, Kuznets also warns that conflicts arising from these technological changes must be resolved peacefully to avoid long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional framework must adapt to support and sustain these changes. Mokyr (2005, p.49; 2005, p.54) reinforces this idea by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that the interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is essential for long-term economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight that colonial rule shaped economic trajectories differently across continents. Both scholars argue that colonial policies were not uniform but adapted to the local economic and political contexts, shaping institutions in ways that varied significantly across regions. For example, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating a fiscal security incentive for local governments to change their institutional prescriptions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further elaborate on this by noting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered, emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the long-term picture of economic development in Africa. This suggests that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of African nations, influencing their development paths long after independence.

These institutional differences, shaped by mortality rates and settlement patterns, have lasting effects on current income per capita. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are crucial for economic growth. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), who advocates for a deeper integration of institutions into economic analysis, suggesting that institutions are essential for understanding long-term economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) highlights that colonial institutions influenced economic trajectories by shaping fiscal policies and production equilibriums, illustrating the persistence of these effects long after independence. These findings collectively underscore the profound impact of institutional frameworks on economic development paths.

The institutional legacy of colonial rule is further elaborated by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who notes that the quality of public goods and governance in Africa is intricately linked to the institutional frameworks established during colonial times. This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors. These studies show that the institutional legacies of colonial rule have shaped the economic trajectories of African nations, influencing their development paths long after independence. The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The disagreement lies in whether the persistence of poverty in Africa is due to the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates (Austin 2008, p.2) or the failure to adopt institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9).
