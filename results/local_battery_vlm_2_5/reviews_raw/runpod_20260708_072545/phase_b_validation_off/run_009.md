The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because the answer influences how policymakers and economists understand the drivers of prosperity and poverty. There is a live disagreement over whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth. One position holds that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are essential for sustained economic development. This view is supported by scholars who argue that secure property rights and effective governance foster investment and innovation. The opposing position contends that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that geography, culture, or other variables play a more significant role. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions on whether to prioritize institutional reform or focus on other areas. The resolution of this debate could guide the allocation of resources in development efforts. This review synthesizes the evidence that institutions are critical for long-run economic growth.

North's analysis of England's 17th-century financial revolution underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) together establish that the development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments were pivotal in securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. These institutional changes were not merely economic but also political, involving the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, a move driven by fiscal crises. This political dimension was crucial for establishing credible commitments that underpinned economic growth. The commitment to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges was a critical political factor that bound the regime to these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that a regime's commitment to these rules is essential for economic growth. This political commitment was evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the institutional changes' impact. The success of these institutional changes was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This institutional framework, with its secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, was key to England's economic development and sets the stage for understanding how institutional changes can drive long-run economic growth in other contexts.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, a finding shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The mechanism by which these institutions exerted their influence is tied to the strategic choices Europeans made based on local conditions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the institutional framework was designed to extract resources efficiently, with little regard for local economic development. The persistence of these colonial institutions into the post-colonial era has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further substantiate this by showing that various measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with current economic performance. The long-term impact of these institutions varies across former colonies. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies, institutional design, and economic outcomes, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic development in profound ways. The role of initial conditions, such as population density and prosperity, further influenced the policies Europeans implemented, underscoring the multifaceted nature of institutional impact on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Broadberry and Gardner argue that African economies, particularly in Anglophone regions, have historically achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding shared with Frankema and van Waijenburg (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28), demonstrating the varied outcomes of colonial policies. This challenges the conventional narrative that African economies were uniformly poor and stagnant. One mechanism that Broadberry and Gardner identify is the role of export markets, which drove economic growth in many African colonies. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation suggests that the institutional environment played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg further elaborate on this by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, showing that economic development was not uniformly poor and that real wages were linked to broader economic conditions. These findings underscore the importance of historical economic data in understanding long-term economic development in Africa. The structural impediments emphasized in recent literature may have had temporary effects on long-term African welfare development. This nuanced view of African economic history suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions, while significant, is not the sole determinant of economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the legacy of the slave trade in Africa has fostered a culture of mistrust, which has significantly impeded economic development. This mistrust, they posit, is both a direct result of the exploitative nature of the slave trade and a persistent social norm that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The slave trade created an environment where individuals could not rely on others, leading to a deeply ingrained mistrust that transcends generations. This internalized mistrust, according to Nunn and Wantchekon, is at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Austin (2008, p.15) supports this view, noting that colonial administrations often responded to economic and political actions by indigenous groups, which could influence institutional changes. However, Austin also highlights that the legacy of mistrust is not uniformly detrimental; in some contexts, it has driven indigenous economic and political actions that shaped colonial policies. This nuanced perspective suggests that while the legacy of mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, it is not an insurmountable one. The enduring effects of the slave trade on trust highlight the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the need for a multifaceted approach to understanding economic stagnation in Africa.

The primary driver of economic growth is technological progress, which is fueled by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both agree that the advance of human knowledge is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This mechanism is evident in the epochal innovations that have spread to substantial parts of the world, such as the Industrial Revolution. Technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). However, the sustainability of economic improvement is contingent upon institutional progress that complements technological advancements; without it, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex dynamics of economic growth, highlighting that while technological innovation is a primary driver, it must be supported by adaptive institutions to ensure long-term economic development.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts fundamentally shape key economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie challenges the notion that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency, asserting that institutions are shaped by the power dynamics and conflicts among different social groups. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who posit that societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, thereby perpetuating inequality. The mechanism through which these distributional conflicts operate is the capture of institutions by powerful elites, who then use these institutions to maintain their privileged status. This capture is evident in the policies and regulations that restrict competition and limit economic opportunities for the majority. For instance, in pre-industrial Europe, the craft and merchant guilds were institutions that, while ostensibly promoting efficiency, often served to protect the interests of the guild members at the expense of broader economic growth. Ogilvie's work highlights how these guilds were deeply entrenched in the power struggles of the time, reflecting the interests of the dominant classes. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this analysis to the New World, showing how initial inequalities led to the development of institutions that further entrenched these disparities. The downstream implication of this perspective is that efforts to promote economic growth through institutional reform must address the underlying distributional conflicts. However, Ogilvie cautions that the complexity of these conflicts means that institutional changes alone may not be sufficient to achieve sustained economic development, as these changes must be aligned with broader social and political transformations.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, with North and Weingast arguing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). When institutions effectively enforce property rights, they facilitate market exchanges and reduce costs per transaction, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from capital markets in England supports this view, showing that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which the regime is committed to or bound by the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Acemoglu et al. extend this institutional perspective to the New World, demonstrating that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either extractive or growth-promoting institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The presence of European settlers influenced the development of institutions that either encouraged investment or facilitated the rapid transfer of resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework was instrumental in shaping the long-run economic outcomes of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

However, the complexity of institutional change and its alignment with broader social and political transformations is highlighted by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the need for institutional changes to be aligned with broader social and political transformations to achieve sustained economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared finding is that institutions are critical for economic growth, as they shape property rights, facilitate market exchanges, and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise remaining disagreement is that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on their alignment with broader social and political transformations, as shown by the historical evidence of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the development of institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement in seventeenth-century England, as documented by North and Weingast (1989, p.2).
