The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules and informal norms, are widely recognized as crucial for economic performance. There is a live disagreement on the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth. One position argues that institutions shape growth through their impact on property rights and transaction costs. This perspective highlights the importance of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic growth. Another position contends that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs, which in turn influence economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by the interests of powerful groups and cultural norms. The stakes are high: understanding the causal role of institutions can inform policy interventions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. The disagreement extends to the historical context, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are influenced by other factors such as geography and cultural beliefs. Institutions are not just a backdrop to economic activity; they are the very fabric that shapes how economic activity unfolds over time.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that in the 17th century, England's new financial institutions and political structures significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn fostered long-run economic growth by securing property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of innovative financial instruments are key examples of how institutional changes can lead to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This reduction is critical because it reflects an increased security of property rights over time, which is essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, illustrating how political institutions can evolve to support economic development. This evolution involves the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions that enforce and modify these rules. The evolution of institutions is a complex process. North (1989, p.9) notes that as economies grow, the transaction sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, and the costs per transaction fall. This implies that while institutional changes can reduce transaction costs, the complexity of economic activity also increases, requiring ongoing institutional adaptation. The historical context of England's financial revolution underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, but it also highlights the need for continuous institutional evolution to sustain this growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) extend this analysis to colonial contexts, demonstrating how institutional legacies from European policies continue to influence economic trajectories in former colonies. The specific settlement patterns of Europeans shaped the institutions they established. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where Europeans could settle safely, they created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This difference in institutional frameworks persists and has had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of these regions. The implications of these extractive institutions are profound. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions are actively designed to benefit the colonizers at the expense of the colonized, even if this means forgoing long-term economic gains. This perspective underscores the critical role that institutional design plays in economic outcomes, highlighting that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups. Another implication of these extractive institutions is that they can be difficult to change, as they are designed to benefit those in power. This institutional inertia can perpetuate economic disparities over generations, even after the colonial period has ended. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that institutions can be measured in multiple ways, using various indices such as constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This multi-dimensional view of institutions suggests that while extractive institutions can hinder growth, a comprehensive understanding of institutional quality can reveal pathways for reform and development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) both critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) identifies the challenge of testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively, and the insufficiency of the theory for explaining why institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates that colonial administrations often altered their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this by showing that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy of mistrust can significantly impede the adoption and effectiveness of institutions designed to promote economic growth. The legacy of mistrust can hinder the development of strong institutions by reducing the willingness of individuals to cooperate and engage in collective action. This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of African economies, where the historical impact of the slave trade continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of mistrust, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), underscores the importance of addressing historical legacies in the design of institutional reforms aimed at promoting long-term economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is evident in the historical context of colonial rule, where the codification of customary law under colonial rule influenced economic activities. This institutional framework affected gender dynamics and had broader implications for economic development. The de-privileging of women in access to land for cash crops, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack, exemplifies how colonial institutions shaped economic activities by altering the incentives and opportunities available to different segments of the population. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies the impact of colonial institutions by showing that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This variation in colonial impacts underscores the importance of institutional design in fostering economic growth, as different colonial powers implemented distinct institutional frameworks that had varying effects on education and economic development. The historical legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional design on long-run economic growth.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are complementary factors in long-run economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) agree that economic growth is driven by the advancement of useful knowledge, which necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are necessary to ensure that the benefits of technological progress are sustained over the long run. Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that without these complementary institutional changes, economic improvement would be unsustainable. Kuznets (1973, p.6) echoes this by highlighting the role of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is thus essential for long-run economic development.

Distributional conflicts are the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that these conflicts are the primary determinants of institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that efficiency cannot fully explain the persistence of institutions like serfdom, the community, and the craft guild, which often hindered economic growth. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are merely the outcome of efficiency-driven processes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this argument by showing that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, reflecting the underlying distributional conflicts. These institutions restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, thereby shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) qualifies this by noting that institutions are shaped by broader societal dynamics. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the interplay between various social forces ultimately determines institutional outcomes. Distributional conflicts thus play a pivotal role in shaping institutions, but their effects are mediated by the complex interactions within society.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior. In this regard, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped the economic outcomes by either facilitating or hindering investment and growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) similarly highlights the importance of institutions in economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development. He argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were critical in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. This shared finding of the importance of institutions in economic growth across different historical contexts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) underscores the universal significance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth are subject to debate. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. He contends that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations evolved in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic view of institutions is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. She contends that these institutions were shaped by the broader societal dynamics and power struggles. This perspective aligns with Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment and development, highlighting that good institutions are crucial for economic growth. The shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) that institutions evolve in response to societal dynamics and power struggles underscores the complexity of institutional influence on economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are shown to be a key factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. They argue that equilibrium institutions were likely designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who emphasizes that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.9) that institutions designed to maximize rents for colonial powers hindered long-run growth highlights the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Institutions are shown to matter in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.9). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth. This dynamic view is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. The specific historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism and their impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18) demonstrates that institutions evolve in response to societal dynamics and power struggles.
