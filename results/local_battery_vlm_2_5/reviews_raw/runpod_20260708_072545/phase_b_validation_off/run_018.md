The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with institutions understood as the rules of the game in a society. This question is intellectually significant because it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty, and has been a focal point of debate among economists. The live disagreement centres on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, with one position arguing that extractive institutions, designed to benefit a narrow elite, impede long-run growth. A rival position attributes economic development primarily to secure property rights and effective enforcement of these rights, emphasising the role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth, while another position argues that population density and prosperity influence the policies pursued by colonizers, shaping the institutions of former colonies. The question remains: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth? The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is closely tied to the historical context in which they are established.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by the motivations and strategies of European colonizers, which varied greatly depending on the local context. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach contrasts with colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, has had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these institutional differences are a key factor in explaining current disparities in income per capita. The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continue to shape economic outcomes by influencing the quality of governance, the protection of property rights, and the overall economic environment. This implies that the legacy of colonial institutions can be a double-edged sword, providing both opportunities and constraints for economic growth in former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) show that institutions shaped by inequality in historical contexts influenced economic outcomes. They focus on how initial factor endowments led to societies characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality, in turn, affected the course of development through its impact on institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the qualitative thrust of this approach is not entirely novel, as economists have traditionally emphasized the influence of factor endowment. Pamuk (2007, p.16) supports this view by highlighting how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap is a key indicator for levels of productivity and economic development in the pre-modern period. Pamuk (2007, p.23) further illustrates that the economy of Tuscany was stagnating and Florence was quickly sliding down the urban ranks while Holland was being transformed into one of the most advanced, urbanized, and commercialized regions of the continent. This transformation was enabled by Holland's unusual degree of institutional flexibility, which allowed new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies. The political authorities in Tuscany, however, worked to strengthen the powers of existing monopolies including their own. This institutional flexibility in Holland contrasts with the rigid institutional structures in Tuscany, highlighting how differences in institutions can lead to divergent economic trajectories. The impact of institutional flexibility on economic development underscores the broader theme of how institutions shaped by inequality can influence long-run economic growth.

The role of colonial education in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa is significant, as British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the finding that the British colonial administration in sub-Saharan Africa created a more favorable environment for the development of formal education systems compared to other colonial powers. This is because the British administration's policies and institutions, which included a greater emphasis on missionary activities, created a demand for formal education among local populations. This demand was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed these calculations, making formal education more attractive. This institutional framework fostered the growth of formal education and had broader implications for economic development. For instance, the British colonial administration's focus on education helped to create a more skilled workforce, which in turn contributed to long-term economic growth. The extent to which these educational gains translated into sustained economic development varies across different regions and periods. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) show that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates makes it challenging to fully assess the long-term impact of colonial education on economic growth. This highlights the need for more comprehensive historical data to understand the relationship between colonial education policies and long-run economic outcomes. The role of colonial education in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa underscores the broader theme of how institutions shaped by colonial rule can influence economic development.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis is challenged by evidence that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding underscores that the economic performance of African countries was not uniformly dire, and that the region's economic history is more complex than often portrayed. One specific mechanism through which this complexity manifests is the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Austin shows that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could align with local economic interests. This institutional response to indigenous action suggests that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). However, Broadberry and Gardner qualify this by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation highlights that while colonial institutions played a role, they were just one piece of a larger puzzle that included economic and political factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The interplay between colonial institutions and local economic conditions reveals a more nuanced picture of African economic history, where institutional frameworks were not the sole determinant of economic outcomes.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had an adverse effect on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which has persisted and significantly impacted economic development. Nunn reinforces this perspective by noting that the duration of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, far exceeds the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This extended period of exploitation disrupted economic and social institutions, leaving a lasting impact on development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). Nunn (2008, p.2) points out that sustained trade in some regions, such as the Kongo kingdom, was facilitated by centralized governance and well-developed markets. This observation underscores that while the slave trade had detrimental effects, the presence of robust institutions could mitigate some of the negative impacts. The implications of this finding suggest that the legacy of the slave trade, including the entrenched mistrust and institutional disruptions, continues to shape Africa's economic landscape, even as some regions demonstrate resilience through effective governance and market development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of specific institutions, particularly those related to financial markets and property rights, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth in Europe. This institutional development was underpinned by increased security in property rights, which was critical for both a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This success was contingent on the regime's commitment to and enforcement of the rules governing economic exchange. These findings imply that the institutional framework that emerged in seventeenth-century England was a result of political factors that ensured the stability and enforceability of property rights. This institutional framework laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. However, North and Weingast also note that the economic performance of France before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This qualification underscores the importance of considering the broader economic context and the relative impact of institutional developments across different regions. The legacy of these institutional developments continues to shape economic trajectories, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutions on long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and property rights. North shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is reflected in the emergence of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework underpins the long-term capital market and economic growth itself.

The specific types of institutions that emerge are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al. argue that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for these groups rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, for example, were crucial for establishing a regime committed to these rules, thereby promoting economic development. This dynamic is evident in the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which underpins the development of markets and economic growth.

The legacy of colonial institutions also significantly impacts long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In others, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences persist and significantly affect current income per capita. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonialism on institutional development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The enduring influence of historical institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the persistent effects of colonial policies on current economic outcomes. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape economic trajectories, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutions on long-run economic growth.
