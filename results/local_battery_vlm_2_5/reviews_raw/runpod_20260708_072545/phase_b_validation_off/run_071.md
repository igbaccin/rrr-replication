The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the efficacy of contemporary policy interventions. At stake is the ability to explain why some countries have experienced sustained economic growth while others have stagnated or declined. A live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, suggesting that well-designed institutions foster growth by providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts. This perspective often highlights the importance of institutions that reduce transaction costs and promote market efficiency. Another position argues that the impact of institutions is mediated through other factors, such as cultural beliefs and political dynamics, which can either reinforce or undermine the effectiveness of institutional frameworks. Additionally, there is debate over whether the influence of institutions is uniform across different historical and geographical contexts, or if it varies significantly based on local conditions and colonial legacies. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional analysis and the need for nuanced understanding of the multifaceted ways in which institutions can shape economic development. The role of institutions in economic growth must be considered in tandem with the broader historical and cultural contexts in which they operate.

Building on this, North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional change increased the security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The development of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement is reflected in the falling costs per transaction, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The evidence from capital markets indicates that the institutional changes in England were crucial for economic growth, as they provided a secure environment for investment and economic activity. The role of institutions in economic growth is intertwined with other factors. This implies that the impact of institutions on economic growth is multifaceted and intertwined with broader historical and economic contexts.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) jointly establish. When Europeans faced high mortality rates in colonies, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, fostering local economic development. This institutional design was a deliberate policy choice, responding to environmental factors. The extractive nature of these institutions, which maximized rents for European colonists, promoting long-run growth, had enduring effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions persisted long after the colonial period, continuing to shape economic outcomes. The impact of these institutions varied across colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document how the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights that while extractive institutions initially hindered growth, the potential for industrialization could mitigate some of these negative effects. This suggests that the long-run impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied based on the specific historical and economic contexts of each colony.

Further, Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade induced a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels, which Nunn also documents as a fundamental factor in Africa's economic underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is deeply ingrained in the internal norms of African societies, as Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This pervasive mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Nunn further illustrates how the economic institutions of pre-colonial Africa were often too underdeveloped to support sustained trade, highlighting the deep-seated structural issues that the slave trade exacerbated (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of the slave trade continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes by inhibiting the development of trust, a critical component of economic growth.

Moreover, the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Mokyr (2005, p.49) (Kuznets 1973, p.1)). The accumulation of useful knowledge is a key factor in economic development, as it allows for continuous technological improvements that enhance productivity and efficiency. This process involves the dissemination and application of existing knowledge across different sectors. Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge highlights the importance of technological progress in driving economic growth. The institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany technological changes are crucial for the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, are termed epochal innovations. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, driving long-term economic development. Kuznets also points out that these technological changes often require institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts without excessive costs, ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. This underscores the idea that while technological progress is essential, it must be complemented by institutional progress to achieve long-term economic improvement. Kuznets' observation that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts suggests that the institutional framework is necessary for sustaining the benefits of technological advancements.

In this context, Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Broadberry and Gardner, examine how inequality influenced economic performance in New World societies, focusing on specific historical contexts and policies that shaped development paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). They argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. In New World societies, the initial abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to high living standards on average. Other aspects of their factor endowments varied in ways that meant that the great majority were characterized virtually from the outset by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was sustained through institutional frameworks that favored the elite, limiting opportunities for broader economic advancement. The persistence of these institutional structures over time meant that the initial conditions of inequality were not easily overcome, shaping the long-term economic trajectories of these societies. Broadberry and Gardner's work on Sub-Saharan Africa highlights the importance of local endowments and transport costs in shaping economic growth, showing that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This underscores the idea that while local factors are crucial, the institutional framework that supports or hinders economic activity is equally important in determining long-term economic performance.

Similarly, Hopkins and Frankema both assert that colonial rule in Africa had complex and multifaceted impacts on the continent's economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). A significant mechanism through which colonial rule influenced economic outcomes was the codification of customary law, which often reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land and economic opportunities. This legal framework, shaped by colonial rule, had profound implications for economic activity and growth. For instance, women were frequently relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cultivation of cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor was actively reinforced by colonial institutions. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched these inequalities, as it was men who were primarily involved in drafting and enforcing these laws. This legal framework thus played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by limiting the economic agency of women. Frankema argues that British rule, more than other colonial powers, was conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.1). This educational development, though, did not necessarily translate into immediate economic growth, as the benefits of education were perceived differently by African families and political leaders. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, creating a complex interplay between education and economic development. This interplay highlights how colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes directly and shaped the broader socio-political environment in which economic activity occurred.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is particularly evident in the interplay between economic policies, social divisions, and patrimonial governance structures. Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) jointly highlight that the post-colonial period in Africa was marked by significant shifts in economic policies and social dynamics, driven by the interplay of patrimonial governance structures and land competition. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that East Africa's post-independence expenditure trends were shaped by industrial policy, capital investments, military expenditure, and debt service payments. These factors were influenced by patrimonial governance, where political resources were treated as private property, used to buy loyalty and deliver public goods. This dynamic resulted in policies focused on consumption rather than public goods, leading to expenditure shifts such as the peak in subsidies and interest payments in Kenya in the early 1990s, and high debt burdens in Tanzania. These expenditure trends, coupled with the growth in military spending, reveal the complex interplay between economic policies and social divisions in Africa. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies the extent of this influence by noting that these expenditure trends were influenced by specific economic and political factors. This nuanced view suggests that while patrimonial governance and land competition are significant factors, they are part of a broader economic and political context. Consequently, understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a holistic view that considers not just the institutions themselves but also the broader socio-economic environment in which they operate.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) together argue that long-term economic performance is shaped by structural changes in the economy, including shifts in skill premiums and the distribution of income. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) focuses on the role of institutions in shaping skill premiums, highlighting that guilds in western Europe played a crucial role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite criticisms of their monopolistic tendencies. These institutions ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts, thereby facilitating the development of human capital. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this argument by showing that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by production, merchant distribution, and finance. This underscores the importance of the service sector in economic growth, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. The wages of skilled labourers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than those of unskilled labourers, indicating that institutional changes led to investment in agriculture and high agricultural labour productivity. This pattern is further supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, aiming to understand the determinants of real wage divergence and their relation to long-term African welfare development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe offer nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. European settlers' presence in Africa, for instance, influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production, and colonial governments adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also suggests that institutions should be viewed as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians, implying that while distributional conflicts were significant, they were not the sole determinants of institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including local economic conditions and political dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in economic history. Institutions, as North argues, are the rules of the game in a society, and they shape economic performance by influencing the incentives of economic agents (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. show that institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is evident in the case of European colonies where different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in determining long-run economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are complex and multifaceted. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights that institutions are products of political and social struggles, tools for maximizing economic efficiency. Austin extends this argument by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that the impact of institutions on economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including local economic conditions and political dynamics.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the experience of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, maximizing long-run growth, were a key factor in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). He shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and they shape economic performance by influencing the incentives of economic agents (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The measurement of institutional quality and the comparison of different historical paths can settle this disagreement.
