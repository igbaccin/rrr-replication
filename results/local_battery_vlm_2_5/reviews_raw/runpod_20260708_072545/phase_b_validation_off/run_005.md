The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives economic prosperity over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions directly shape economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and efficient markets. Others contend that the impact of institutions is mediated through complex historical and political processes, making it difficult to isolate their direct effects. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes driven by political and social dynamics. This review will explore these competing perspectives and their implications for understanding long-run economic growth. The central theme of the review will be the complex interplay between institutions, historical context, and economic development.

The development of new financial institutions and political structures in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These institutions facilitated market exchanges by specifying and enforcing efficient property rights, which in turn reduced costs per transaction. The evolution of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. The role of institutions is embedded within broader historical and political contexts. North notes that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This historical context shaped the development of institutions that supported economic growth. The interplay between institutions and historical contexts underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where institutions are both shaped by and shape broader socio-political dynamics.

Distributional conflicts drove the development of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. These scholars contend that the institutions that emerged in this period were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. One specific mechanism through which this distributional conflict played out was the establishment of guilds. Ogilvie argues that guilds were about restricting competition and maintaining the economic power of established artisans. This perspective challenges the notion that guilds were efficient institutions that promoted economic growth by ensuring high-quality products and training apprentices. This view is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition. The persistence of inequality in these societies underscores the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. Efficiency considerations were present. The institutions that emerged from these conflicts often had unintended consequences that could sometimes align with efficiency. For example, guilds might have inadvertently promoted innovation by fostering a culture of craftsmanship and skill development. Thus, while distributional conflicts were the primary driver, efficiency considerations could sometimes emerge as a secondary effect. This interplay between distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations highlights the complex nature of institutional development and its impact on economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) have shown. European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, far from promoting long-term growth, were crafted to maximize rents for European colonists. In the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to different economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. This dynamic is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role. This extractive nature of colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, had lasting impacts on the economic development of former colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was pivotal in shaping the long-run development of these regions. The legacy of these institutional frameworks continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today.

This perspective challenges the primacy of institutions in economic development, suggesting that the manner in which colonial rule was implemented also played a crucial role. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the legacy of colonial rule in Africa is complex and multifaceted, influencing economic outcomes through various channels beyond institutional frameworks. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods or the effectiveness of institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that institutional quality is solely derived from ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity. This suggests that the impact of colonial rule on African development varied based on local contexts and adaptations. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that the prominence of institutions in development policy allowed the new economic history of Africa to gain traction among development specialists, underscoring the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment. This emphasis on institutions, however, does not negate the influence of colonial legacies on African development. The complex interplay between colonial rule, institutional frameworks, and local adaptations continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial legacies on long-run economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the importance of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the role of political stability in facilitating economic growth. The modern national state, with its authority based on loyalty and a shared sense of community, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. This political stability is essential for the sustained growth that technological progress enables. The growth of economic reasonableness is essential for sustaining the economic improvements brought about by technological progress. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex relationship between knowledge, technology, and economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) critique the simplistic causal mechanisms of the "reversal of fortune" thesis by highlighting the importance of historical context and institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). They argue that the impact of colonial institutions is not uniform, and that historical periods and paths need to be considered in detail. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this by noting that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local incentives and indigenous economic actions, demonstrating that institutions evolved in response to local conditions and not just through exogenous impositions (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view of institutional change contrasts with the static view of colonial institutions as monolithic and unchanging. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further emphasize the importance of historical context by showing that the slave trade affected the rule of law and trustworthiness of others, and fostered internal norms of mistrust within Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of these norms today suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is complex and multifaceted, influencing contemporary economic development through both external and internal channels. This multifaceted legacy is further complicated by the fact that these institutions evolved over time in response to local conditions, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development is not straightforward.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex, with historical legacies and evolving political dynamics playing crucial roles. The persistence of colonial institutions, including extractive states, demonstrates that these institutions had profound effects on contemporary economic development. Colonial institutions evolved in response to local conditions, shaping both external and internal channels of economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to extractive states in high-mortality colonies. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This evolution highlights the multifaceted legacy of colonial institutions, which influenced economic development through various pathways. For example, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The evolution of institutions over time is also crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. North highlights that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs and increased economic growth. This is evident in the development of the Bank of England and the English financial revolution, which significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional evolution shows that the impact of institutions on economic development involves complex interactions between political and economic factors. Austin further argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is nuanced and depends on specific historical contexts and pathways (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

Institutional reforms informed by historical evidence can also play a significant role in eliminating poverty. Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical legacies of institutions and their impact on contemporary economic development.
