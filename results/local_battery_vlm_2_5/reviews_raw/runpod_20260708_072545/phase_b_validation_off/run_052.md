The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the potential paths for future growth. Central to this debate are competing explanations for why some countries have prospered economically while others have lagged behind. One prominent perspective posits that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, is crucial for fostering economic growth. This view is challenged by alternative explanations that emphasize other factors, such as geographic conditions or cultural attributes, as primary determinants of economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors in a complex web of influences. The debate is far from settled, with ongoing research continually refining our understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to shape economic outcomes. This debate is critical to unpacking the enduring puzzle of why certain regions have thrived while others have stagnated. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) demonstrate that the presence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can significantly reduce transaction costs and encourage investment, thereby promoting economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, with a specific emphasis on the role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. This institutional development was pivotal in fostering economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights, a critical factor in long-term capital markets. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this idea by showing that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to different institutional outcomes. In colonies with low mortality rates, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. High mortality colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast saw the establishment of extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita. The persistence of these institutions is not solely due to initial colonization strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive because they maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if they do not promote long-run economic development. This dynamic highlights that the path from institutions to growth is complex, involving both the initial conditions set by colonization and the ongoing political struggles that shape institutional evolution. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the historical context and political dynamics that influence institutional development and, consequently, economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They note that the success of England's economic development was underpinned by a critical political factor: the degree to which the regime was committed to the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment fostered a stable environment where property rights were secure, encouraging investment and economic activity. They argue that societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, often had institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights that the institutional framework can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how it allocates resources and power. The contrast between England's secure property rights and the institutional barriers in Latin America illustrates that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is a key determinant of long-run economic growth. The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes vary across contexts, but the overarching theme is that institutional development is a critical factor in shaping economic trajectories.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African economies during colonial times were not as economically stagnant as often portrayed. They show that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the notion that poverty was a persistent phenomenon due to structural impediments. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner, who note that the economic performance of African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation suggests that institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, highlighting that specific historical or geographical conditions may have had temporary or structural effects on long-term African welfare development. This implies that while institutions are pivotal, their impact is mediated by local contexts and specific historical conditions. Understanding these dynamics is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which historical conditions influence long-term economic development.

Mokyr and Kuznets argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that the advancement of technology and the diffusion of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic growth. Technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. Kuznets highlights that technological changes necessitate societal adaptations to resolve conflicts arising from modernization, emphasizing the role of the modern national state in peacefully managing these transitions (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates on the role of institutions in supporting technological progress, noting that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that facilitate the efficient use of technological advancements. This implies that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the institutional environment must adapt and evolve to support these advancements, suggesting a more complex interplay between technology and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth. This mistrust is deeply ingrained in the societal norms of mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon find that this internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade has left a profound impact on African societies, shaping their economic trajectories by fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust. Such mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing cooperation, investment, and innovation. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates the detrimental effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, emphasizing that the duration of the slave trades was much longer than the period of colonial rule. This historical context underscores the enduring impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic institutions and development. The slave trade's role in shaping African economic development highlights the importance of understanding historical contexts and their long-term effects on institutions and economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share the view that institutional reforms are key to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. The paper emphasizes the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts and institutional evolutions that shaped economic growth in different regions. Austin highlights that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This suggests that institutional changes were responsive to local economic and political actions, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial rule uniformly stifled African economic development. Hopkins builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Hopkins emphasizes that 'good' institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the institutionalist approach, while crucial, faces challenges in quantitative testing and explaining why favorable institutions were adopted in some countries. This indicates that while institutional reforms are necessary, their implementation and effectiveness depend on a nuanced understanding of historical and local contexts.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that encourage investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonies with settler populations developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment, leading to significant economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth. These findings underscore the importance of institutions in fostering an environment conducive to long-term economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further evidenced by the historical experiences of different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) highlights that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) underscores the enduring influence of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the political and economic transformations of seventeenth-century England. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their success in creating a stable political environment committed to property rights and economic growth was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. This historical example illustrates how institutions can shape economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity.
