The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory and history. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over centuries, and how policy might steer that evolution. One live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms: do institutions drive growth through the provision of secure property rights and enforcement of contracts, or do they emerge as a response to economic needs and distributional conflicts? Another debate focuses on the persistence of institutions: do they endure over centuries, shaping economic trajectories, or are they more malleable and responsive to changing circumstances? A third disagreement concerns the universality of institutional explanations: do institutions universally promote growth, or do their effects vary depending on cultural and historical contexts? The resolution of these debates will inform economic theory and the design of economic policies aimed at fostering sustained growth. One stream of scholarship highlights the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in colonies by European powers significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast similarly underscore the importance of institutions governing public choice in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective emphasizes the long-term effects of institutional frameworks, suggesting that once established, institutions can shape economic trajectories for centuries. The persistence of these institutions over centuries underscores their profound influence on long-term economic outcomes. The nature of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly influenced the economic development of these colonies. This institutional framework, once established, persisted and shaped the economic trajectories of these regions for centuries, reflecting the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) suggest that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are a function of the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were established.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that the political factors underpinning economic growth are the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. This institutional framework was critical in shaping economic trajectories. This institutional evolution was a function of the political and institutional frameworks that emerged during this period. The role of institutions in securing property rights and fostering economic growth is further highlighted by the differences in development paths across New World societies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.4) illustrate how societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The specific focus on how different environments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality and how these differences affected the course of development through their impact on institutions is a key insight. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency. These institutions were efficient responses to economic conditions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further illustrate this point by examining how the slave trade in Africa created a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, affecting contemporary economic outcomes. This mistrust, rooted in historical institutional legacies, has had enduring effects on social and economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) adds nuance to this perspective by showing how different colonial powers influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, highlighting that British rule was more conducive to education than other colonial regimes. These findings suggest that the specific historical and institutional contexts in which societies developed have lasting impacts on their economic trajectories. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were established.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are complementary factors in long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues, necessitates the development of formal institutions that reduce access costs to new ideas and technologies. This suggests that societies must adapt their institutions to accommodate new technologies, ensuring that the political fabric remains strong enough to manage growth-induced conflicts. If institutional adjustments lag behind technological advancements, the benefits of new technologies may be limited or even eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay underscores the importance of balanced institutional and technological progress in fostering long-run economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) assert that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwest Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. The reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in a warm climate may have reinforced this effect (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Pamuk (2007, p.16) observes that structural change, particularly the shift from agrarian to urban economies, was a key characteristic of economic growth in the early modern era. This structural change was evident in the differing urbanization rates between northwestern and southern Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that Holland's institutional flexibility allowed it to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) shows that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, confirming the view that structural changes in the economy had significant implications for living standards. This suggests that while structural changes were occurring, their impact on living standards was not uniform across Europe.

Broadberry and Gardner and Nunn together examine the historical context of African economies, highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, continuing the theme of institutional influence. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that recent work in African economic history has demonstrated the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. This data reveals that many African economies were more developed than previously thought. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. This suggests that the adverse effects of slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, were a significant factor in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn's findings indicate that the impact of these trades was profound and long-lasting. These historical factors influenced the development of local institutions and markets, which in turn affected economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further elucidate that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This variation highlights the complex interplay between historical events and institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Peters (2004, p.19) highlight significant economic contributions by African women and deepening social divisions over land. The colonial era introduced significant changes in land access and use, with women often relegated to marginal lands for food farming. This marginalization was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men's roles in cash crop production. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, for instance, was driven by male farmers migrating to secure land for cocoa production, making the region a global leader in cocoa exports by 1911. Peters (2004, p.5) reveals that this period also saw a shift in official perceptions of African landholding practices, viewed as communal and inhibiting individual investment. These perceptions fueled agricultural development schemes aimed at raising productivity through modernized farming. Peters (2004, p.5) also notes that these schemes often faced resistance and dissatisfaction from agricultural officers tasked with implementing them. This resistance underscores the complexities of land access and use in colonial Africa, where deepening social divisions and class formations were intertwined with economic development. This interplay between economic contributions, social divisions, and land access reveals the multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa, where institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping outcomes.

The measurement of economic performance and growth is critical to understanding long-term economic trends, as van Zanden (2009, p.19); (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) demonstrates. The skill premium, or the additional earnings that skilled workers receive, is a key indicator of economic performance, influenced by factors such as training costs, institutional barriers, and interest rates. For example, in western Europe, guilds played a significant role in shaping the skill premium by managing apprenticeship contracts and facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills. These institutions were also criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades. The presence of guilds thus had a dual effect: while they enhanced skill development, they also created barriers that could distort market dynamics. This duality underscores the complex role of institutions in economic growth, where their beneficial effects can be counterbalanced by their restrictive practices.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both emphasize that colonial governments in Africa implemented policies that significantly shaped economic development in the region. These policies, driven by the interests and needs of colonial administrations, often influenced the adoption of institutions that encouraged or hindered economic growth. For instance, colonial administrations that prioritized fiscal security tended to adjust their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political actions. This dynamic, however, was not uniform across all colonies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the real wage levels and trends in British African colonies varied widely, indicating that the impact of colonial policies on economic development was contingent on specific historical and geographical conditions. This variability suggests that the effectiveness of colonial institutions in promoting economic growth depended on how well they aligned with local contexts. This underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes, where the same institutional frameworks could yield different results based on local conditions.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, leading to either growth-promoting or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variability underscores that the effectiveness of institutions depends on how well they align with local conditions. Austin highlights that colonial policies could either foster or impede indigenous commodity production, depending on the presence of European settlers and the fiscal strategies of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.2). This interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes shows that the same institutional frameworks could yield different results based on local conditions.

Institutional frameworks are critical for economic growth, as they create the conditions for secure property rights and effective enforcement. North argues that institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into the long-term research program of economic historians (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6) underscores the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) shows that the design of institutions is influenced by the interests of powerful groups. This is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
