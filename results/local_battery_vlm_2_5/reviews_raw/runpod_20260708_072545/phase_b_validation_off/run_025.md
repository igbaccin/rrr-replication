Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, yet the precise mechanisms and extent of their influence remain contentious. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, are more determinative. One position emphasizes the causal role of institutions in economic growth, arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained development. This view is supported by those who contend that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, has had lasting negative effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Another position challenges the primacy of institutions by highlighting the importance of distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective suggests that institutions are not merely efficient responses to economic needs but are deeply influenced by power dynamics and shared beliefs within societies. The live disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, and distributional conflicts in driving economic growth. The stakes are high: if institutions are the key drivers, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could have transformative effects on economic development. If, however, other factors are more determinative, then institutional reforms may be insufficient or misguided. Institutions are a significant variable in long-run economic growth.

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional change was crucial for the long-term capital market and economic growth. This institutional framework was underpinned by the political commitment to enforce rules and property rights, which was critical for economic development. The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, which indicates the effectiveness of the institutional changes in promoting economic growth. Other factors, such as the bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV, also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. This nuanced view underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where institutions are significant but not the only determinant.

This extractive approach contrasts with the institutions set up in colonies where Europeans could settle safely, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, which enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The long-term economic impact of these institutions is profound, as they shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies well into the modern era. Acemoglu et al. document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than purely geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy persists, affecting current income per capita and overall economic development. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. This nuance highlights that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth depends on their design and the interests they serve.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the finding that African economic development is not solely determined by structural impediments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the provision of public goods in Africa is not merely a function of ethnic fragmentation. Instead, "good" institutions are crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the persistence of poverty in Africa is a product of historical and geographical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The specific mechanisms by which these institutions operate and their long-term effects on economic growth remain unclear. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary to assess the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that while institutions play a role in shaping economic outcomes, their impact is nuanced and depends on various factors, including historical and geographical conditions.

Moreover, the role of technological progress and institutional adjustments in economic growth emphasizes the importance of innovation efficiency. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) assert that technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Mokyr adds that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. He argues that "Without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited and in the end might have been frittered away and eliminated by the relentless erosion of rent seeking" (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr also recognises that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while technological progress can drive economic growth, the institutional adjustments necessary for sustainable growth may be more challenging to achieve and maintain. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is thus complex, with the efficiency of innovation use being a key factor in determining long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie highlights that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups, which leads to the creation of institutions that maintain their dominance. This perspective is exemplified by the persistence of serfdom and guilds in Europe, which were not efficient but served to preserve the power of elites. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. These institutions, often justified by cultural beliefs, were designed to maintain the status quo. This dynamic is evident in the contrast between European and non-European economies, where the former's institutions were shaped by beliefs that valued human intervention and certain cultural values, while the latter lacked these beliefs, leading to distinct institutional outcomes. However, Ogilvie cautions against viewing institutions as mere fashion accessories, emphasizing their indispensable role in economic history. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts that shape institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) share the claim that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They argue that this thesis, which suggests that institutions matter for economic growth, conflates various distinct historical processes. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the thesis fails to explain why some countries adopted institutions favourable for economic growth while others did not. This failure is due to its inability to account for the complex interplay between indigenous economic and political action and colonial institutional prescriptions. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this point by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives, which were influenced by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic suggests that the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions was shaped by local conditions and responses. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the importance of trust in economic development. They argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, suggesting that the legacy of historical institutions extends beyond their immediate economic impacts. Austin (2008, p.15) also notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which could lead to higher production equilibria. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay between historical institutions and economic outcomes, as well as the enduring impact of these institutions on contemporary economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating the frameworks that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. highlight that extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over investment, can persist and significantly impact current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This aligns with North's argument that institutions determine the efficiency of property rights and the enforcement mechanisms that underpin economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The legacy of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, extends beyond immediate economic impacts, influencing contemporary development trajectories. Austin notes that colonial administrations that encouraged material interest in the status quo could lead to higher production equilibria, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the financial and political developments of historical periods. North emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflects the increasing security of property rights, a critical dimension for long-term economic development. Similarly, Ogilvie argues for the importance of institutions in economic history, suggesting that they should be an indispensable tool for economic historians (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is shared by Hopkins, who notes that good institutions provide the necessary incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa, for example, highlights the crucial role of institutions in providing public goods and upholding the rule of law, which are fundamental for economic progress (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding that institutions matter for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8) is evident across various historical and contemporary contexts.

The persistent influence of historical institutions on long-run economic growth is a point of agreement among scholars. Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a lasting impact on the relative incomes of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific mechanisms by which institutions shape economic outcomes remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely shaped by efficiency considerations, highlighting the complex interplay between political power, distributional conflicts, and institutional design. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement lies in the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic growth, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This measurement shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.
