The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. The live disagreement revolves around the efficacy of institutions as drivers of economic growth versus other factors such as geography, culture, or exogenous shocks. One position posits that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are critical for sustained economic development. Another position contends that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks, play a more significant role. Additionally, there is a debate on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. This review synthesizes the evidence on the causal role of institutions in economic growth, focusing on the mechanisms through which institutions operate and the conditions under which they are most effective. A key theme is the interplay between institutions and economic growth, with particular attention to the historical context and the specific types of institutions that matter most.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) along with North (1989, p.9) establish that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This was a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The success of these institutions was remarkable, and evidence from capital markets shows that the regime was committed to enforcing these rules. The critical political factor here was the degree to which the regime was bound by these rules. This commitment to rules was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. North (1989, p.9) points out that as the economy grows, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP. This implies that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is essential for reducing costs per transaction. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with institutions shaping the economic environment and the economic environment, in turn, influencing the evolution of institutions.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that European colonial powers implemented different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from settler institutions that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The institutional framework established during colonial rule had profound implications for long-term economic development. Specifically, extractive institutions, which were more prevalent in colonies with high European mortality rates, hindered economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. This institutional legacy persisted long after the colonial period, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) finding that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional framework influenced the relative incomes of former colonies, with those that adopted settler institutions achieving higher levels of industrialization and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the impact of institutions on economic growth varies among former colonies. They find that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is mediated by other factors, such as population density and initial prosperity. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects are contingent on the specific economic and social context of each colony. Therefore, the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to influence economic outcomes in many parts of the world today.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) find that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This occurred through a complex interplay of political and economic factors that influenced local demand for education. For instance, African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that altered the perceived benefits of formal education in light of new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This dynamic was driven by the broader economic context, where patterns of growth were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors collectively shaped the trajectory of economic development in the region. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the persistence of poverty in Africa cannot be attributed solely to structural impediments, suggesting that the economic impacts of colonial rule were more nuanced and varied than often portrayed. This implies that the economic performance of African colonies was influenced by a range of factors, including the specific policies and institutions imposed by colonial powers, which in turn shaped local development trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade's impact on economic development was substantial, with Nunn noting that it spanned nearly 500 years, significantly longer than the period of colonial rule. This extensive duration allowed the slave trade to deeply embed cultural norms of mistrust, which continue to influence economic outcomes. The mechanism through which the slave trade shaped contemporary trust levels is multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon identify both internal and external channels, with the internal channel, where mistrust becomes an ingrained norm, being at least twice as large as the external channel, which involves factors like the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internalization of mistrust has far-reaching implications, affecting economic development, political institutions, and social cohesion. Nunn highlights that the slave trade's adverse effects on economic development were pervasive across Africa, suggesting a broad impact on the continent's economic trajectory. This pervasive impact underscores the deep-rooted nature of the mistrust culture, which has hindered Africa's economic growth over centuries. The long-term effects of the slave trade on trust and economic development are evident in the enduring cultural norms that continue to shape contemporary African societies.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was evident well before 1800. This divergence is reflected in the differing economic development paths of the two regions. Broadberry and Gupta attribute this divergence to the role of merchant distribution and finance in driving productivity in Europe's traded goods sector. This contrasts with Asia, where agricultural investment was the primary driver of economic development. van Zanden (1999, p.1; 1999, p.9) provides further evidence of this divergence by showing that economic development in Europe did not necessarily lead to improvements in the standard of living. This suggests that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed across European society. The uneven distribution of economic benefits in Europe was evident in the differing wage levels of skilled and unskilled laborers. The high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector may have been driven by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity. This institutional change was a key factor in the economic divergence between Europe and Asia. Institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, suggesting a pathway to long-run economic growth that complements the role of institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that technological advancements necessitate institutional changes to resolve conflicts arising from growth-induced disruptions, highlighting the crucial role of the national state in peacefully managing these conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is essential for long-term economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) builds on this by emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic gains may be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, underscoring the need for coordination to address coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is a driver of economic growth, the efficiency and sustainability of this growth depend on the institutional framework that supports and regulates it.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Austin (2008, p.2); (Hopkins 2009, p.18) share the view that a nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history can inform institutional reforms that alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. They argue that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, which posits that Africa's economic decline is due to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the complexity of Africa's economic history. In reality, colonial institutions were not uniform and evolved in response to local economic and political conditions. For example, colonial governments encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for labor, and fiscal security motivated changes in institutional prescriptions. These institutional prescriptions were often shaped by indigenous economic and political actions. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their responsiveness to local conditions and the incentives they create for economic actors. The role of institutions in Africa's economic development is complex. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that while institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, the relationship between institutions and economic development is complex and influenced by a variety of factors. For instance, the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This indicates that the impact of institutions on economic development is mediated by other factors, including the political and economic context in which they operate, as well as the incentives they create for different actors. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms that can promote economic growth and reduce poverty in Africa.
