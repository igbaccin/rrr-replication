The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from poverty to prosperity. A central disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that structure social interactions, drive economic growth. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, arguing that well-functioning institutions foster growth by providing incentives for investment and innovation. Other scholars maintain that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play a significant role in shaping economic trajectories. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors to produce long-term development. This disagreement is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote development. Institutions are not static entities; their evolution and the factors that influence this evolution are critical to understanding their role in economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) share that the development of financial institutions is pivotal to long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the advent of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating secure property rights and fostering economic growth. This financial revolution in England was instrumental in establishing a long-term capital market, which in turn drove economic development. North (1989, p.9) observes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and enforcing them effectively led to a decline in transaction costs per exchange. This decline, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP, reflects the increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). They note that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the political commitment to rules and their enforcement underpins economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutional changes in England were not merely about economic exchange rules but also about the governance of these rules and their enforcement, which bound the regime to these rules. This commitment to institutional rules is a critical factor in economic growth, as it ensures the stability and predictability necessary for long-term economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In colonies with high mortality, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences persist, and their impact can be measured through various indicators of institutional quality, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. The institutional legacy of colonialism thus shapes long-run economic growth by determining the quality of institutions that govern economic activity. The relationship between institutions and economic development is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not to promote long-run growth. This qualification highlights that the institutional impact of colonialism is complex and multifaceted, influenced by the political and economic interests of the colonizers. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the enduring significance of historical institutional frameworks in shaping contemporary economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Frankema 2012, p.15) extend this analysis by showing how societies with greater inequality develop institutions that favor elites, thereby perpetuating inequality and restricting competition. This dynamic is evident in the New World colonies, where initial factor endowments led to extreme wealth disparities and shaped the institutional frameworks that evolved. These disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power were exacerbated by colonial policies that privileged elite interests, limiting the development of inclusive institutions. For example, in sub-Saharan Africa, the demand for missionary education was influenced by the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families, which were altered by new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions changed the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby influencing economic development trajectories. The impact of inequality on economic development varies across regions. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound adverse effect on economic development, independent of colonial rule or more recent events. This historical context underscores the enduring legacy of inequality and its institutional manifestations on long-run economic growth.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments, as emphasized by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49), are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, suggesting a different pathway to long-run economic growth. This is because technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained growth; it must be accompanied by institutional progress to complement the technological progress and ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural life and extended family patterns, necessitating significant institutional and ideological adjustments.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, are key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the primary function of institutions in pre-industrial Europe was to manage conflicts over resource distribution and not to promote economic efficiency. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view by highlighting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, illustrating how institutions adapted to manage conflicts and maintain stability (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability indicates that institutions were not merely static structures but dynamic entities that evolved to address the shifting landscape of distributional conflicts. This dynamic nature also means that institutions could become entrenched and resistant to change, as seen in the persistence of serfdom and guilds despite their inefficiencies. This entrenched nature of institutions, driven by the need to manage distributional conflicts, could hinder economic growth by preventing the adoption of more efficient practices.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with van Zanden, examine the economic divergence and structural changes in Europe, focusing on periods before 1800 and the impact of events like the Black Death, without explicitly linking these to institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). Their work highlights that institutional changes, particularly in agriculture, played a crucial role in driving economic development in northwestern Europe. This regional focus underscores that economic growth was not uniform across Europe, with northwestern Europe experiencing significant agricultural productivity gains due to institutional changes. Van Zanden's analysis of wage data further illustrates that economic development in Europe was characterized by shifts in the relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, with England and the Netherlands emerging as economic leaders by the 18th century (van Zanden 1999, p.9). These price level shifts indicate that economic development was closely tied to changes in agricultural productivity and institutional frameworks. Pamuk adds to this by showing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These structural changes, including urbanization and institutional flexibility, were pivotal in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. Pamuk also notes that while institutional flexibility was a key driver, the political authorities in regions like Tuscany worked to strengthen existing monopolies, which hindered economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.16). These findings suggest that while institutional changes were crucial, their impact varied significantly across different regions, influenced by local political and economic contexts. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth was complex and multifaceted, with regional variations in institutional flexibility and political structures playing a significant role.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge the notion of persistent poverty in Africa, arguing that African economies, particularly before 1950, were more robust than previously characterized. These economies were tightly tied to foreign trade and export markets, with growth patterns influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in economic performance across different African regions highlights the significant role of local political and economic contexts in shaping growth trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) focus on the reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a nuanced view of economic development in Africa that challenges the narrative of persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The evidence suggests that African economies were more dynamic and responsive to global economic forces than previously thought, with institutional and political factors playing a crucial role in their development.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Hopkins, both assert that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods and that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Akyeampong and Fofack show that women's contributions were significant despite their marginalization. Women were often relegated to less fertile lands for food farming, and their access to land for cash crops was limited by colonial rule, which codified customary law to reinforce patriarchal structures. This marginalization was a product of pre-existing patriarchal alliances that were reinforced under colonial rule, as well as a colonial imposition (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This pattern of land access and control reveals a mechanism through which institutional reforms could have mitigated some of the economic constraints faced by women. Hopkins underscores that the new economic history of Africa has gained attention from development specialists because of its focus on institutions, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins notes that the presence of poor institutions is linked to Africa's economic challenges, suggesting that while the potential for institutional reforms to drive growth is significant, the path to effective reform is fraught with challenges stemming from deeply entrenched institutional weaknesses.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) assert that the slave trade has fostered a lasting culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust, deeply rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has had a profound impact on contemporary African societies. The slave trade not only led to the displacement of millions of Africans but also created an environment where trust between different ethnic groups was severely eroded. This erosion of trust is evident in the reduced levels of intergroup trust observed today. The mechanism through which the slave trade influenced trust levels is multifaceted. It is argued that the slave trade's effect on trust operates through both internal and external channels. The internal channel, which involves the development of norms of mistrust within individuals, is particularly significant. This internalization of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The persistence of this mistrust has far-reaching implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering economic growth and development. The historical legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape contemporary African societies, affecting their economic and social trajectories.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a matter of agreement: institutions matter (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as seen in the development of the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutional frameworks can reduce transaction costs and foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al. who argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutions, which in turn influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships between institutions and economic development, suggesting that different historical periods and paths must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape contemporary African societies, affecting their economic and social trajectories. The empirical evidence from Acemoglu et al. shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The evidence from Austin shows that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). The empirical evidence from Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
