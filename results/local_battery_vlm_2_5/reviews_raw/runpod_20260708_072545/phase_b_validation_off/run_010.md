The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of the mechanisms that drive sustained economic prosperity and the identification of policies that can foster growth. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic development. This view emphasizes the importance of institutional quality in creating an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Another position suggests that institutions are shaped by underlying factors such as geography and culture, which determine the types of institutions that emerge and their impact on economic growth. Both perspectives recognize the importance of institutions but diverge on the extent to which they are determinative or influenced by other factors. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, and understanding this relationship is essential for designing effective policies that can promote sustained economic development. The central question remains: do institutions drive economic growth, or are they merely a reflection of deeper underlying factors?

The establishment of secure property rights, as exemplified by the creation of the Bank of England, was pivotal in reducing transaction costs and promoting long-run economic growth, as North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly assert. This financial revolution was pivotal in establishing a long-term capital market, which is crucial for economic growth. The reduction in transaction costs resulted directly from institutional changes that specified and enforced property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate on this by highlighting that the success of these institutions was underpinned by a credible commitment to the rules governing economic exchange, which ensured effective enforcement and stability. This credible commitment was a critical political factor that bound the regime to the rules, fostering a stable environment for economic growth. The impact of these institutions was intertwined with other factors. This nuanced view underscores that while institutions like the Bank of England played a significant role, their impact was intertwined with other economic and political developments, illustrating the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the divergent economic trajectories of former European colonies can be largely attributed to the institutions established during the colonial period, which were shaped by European policies. The mortality rates experienced by Europeans in these colonies were a critical factor in determining the type of institutions implemented. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, whereas in areas with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound and lasting effects on the economic development of these colonies, persisting even after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these 'equilibrium institutions' were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework was a key determinant of economic outcomes, as it influenced the ability of these societies to industrialize and develop economically. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions varied across former colonies, suggesting that other factors, such as geographic conditions and local political dynamics, also played a role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa reveals that institutional change was driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This institutional framework, shaped by both local dynamics and colonial legacies, has significant implications for economic development. This variability underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and other economic factors in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman assert that institutions shaped by historical inequalities have had enduring effects on economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon's work supports this claim by showing how the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals, affecting their internal norms and behaviors. Furthermore, the slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust within Africa. The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) elaborate on this by explaining that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized many New World societies from their inception led to institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition. This institutional framework, which favored the interests of the elite, contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) underscores the long-term economic impact of slavery, arguing that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). This adverse effect has had lasting consequences that continue to influence Africa's economic trajectory. The enduring legacy of slavery and inequality, as highlighted by these studies, underscores the profound and lasting impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation and application of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) jointly assert. This perspective emphasizes that technological and intellectual advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth. The growth of knowledge, particularly in the form of technological innovations, creates new opportunities for economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) illustrates this point by noting that technological progress has required significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining economic growth over the long term. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this by highlighting the role of coordination failures and the need for policies to rectify them, suggesting that technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by pointing out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is essential for maintaining the political fabric of society, thereby ensuring the sustainability of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This qualification underscores the importance of institutional stability in complementing technological advancements, even if the latter is the primary driver. Thus, while technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, the institutional environment plays a crucial supporting role in harnessing this progress for sustained economic development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). The core institutions of the era, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by the struggles between different social groups vying for resources and power. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions emerged to maximize productivity or economic growth. Ogilvie posits that these institutions were the outcomes of political battles, where the elite classes imposed rules that secured their wealth and status. For example, craft guilds were often vehicles for restricting competition and maintaining the economic dominance of guild members. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights this dynamic by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating how institutions evolved from the need to manage social conflicts and maintain stability (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how institutions can be shaped by the need to manage distributional conflicts, which in turn can influence long-run economic growth by affecting the allocation of resources and opportunities. This perspective suggests that the institutional environment in pre-industrial Europe was a critical factor in shaping the economic landscape, with implications for understanding the broader role of institutions in economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and opportunities. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, directly impact current income levels. This finding underscores the enduring effects of institutional frameworks set during colonial periods. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights that the development of institutions like the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution were critical in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, both of which are essential for economic growth. These historical examples illustrate how institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. The shared finding is that institutions matter because they determine the efficiency and security of economic transactions (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in managing social conflicts and maintaining stability is also crucial. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the institutional environment in pre-industrial Europe was shaped by the need to manage distributional conflicts, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3), who contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The shared finding is that institutions are shaped by the need to manage social conflicts and maintain stability (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This dynamic underscores how institutions can be shaped by the need to manage distributional conflicts, which in turn can influence long-run economic growth by affecting the allocation of resources and opportunities.
