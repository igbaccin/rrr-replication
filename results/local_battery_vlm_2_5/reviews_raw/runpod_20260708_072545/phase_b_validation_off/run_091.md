The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the explanatory power of institutions in understanding economic development across different regions and historical periods. There are two main positions in this debate. The first position, supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), argues that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly affected the long-run economic growth of colonies. The second position, advocated by Austin (2008, p.2), suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complexity of historical processes and institutional evolution. Institutions have been shown to affect economic growth through various mechanisms, including the enforcement of property rights and the reduction of transaction costs. A key point of contention is whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors or by endogenous political and economic processes. This debate is crucial for understanding the broader implications of institutional design on economic performance. Institutions are a determinant of economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional evolution was marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. These developments reflect the increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for institutional changes that facilitated market exchanges. The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is evident in the falling costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This institutional framework underpins economic growth by providing a stable environment for increasing specialization and division of labor. The extent of these institutional changes being revolutionary is debated. Recent research has upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, suggesting that institutions alone may not fully account for the differences in economic growth between England and France. This perspective highlights that while institutional changes were significant, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political commitments also played crucial roles. This implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving not just the rules governing economic exchange but also the political factors that enforce and change these rules.

These changes were not uniform; European colonizers implemented different strategies based on local conditions, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In regions with high European mortality rates, colonizers established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where Europeans could settle and establish lasting colonies, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-run growth by failing to foster investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that these institutional differences persist, influencing current income per capita levels. This perspective is bolstered by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who demonstrate that the results hold across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These findings underscore the profound and lasting influence of colonial institutions on economic development, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies for centuries.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the conventional view that these institutions were shaped by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) reinforce this perspective, highlighting how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. These distributional conflicts led to institutions that were designed to protect the interests of the powerful, often at the expense of overall economic efficiency. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that maintained the status quo, even when more efficient alternatives were available. For instance, guilds in Europe were maintained because they served the interests of the guild masters in controlling access to the market and maintaining high prices. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that such institutions were not necessarily efficient but were maintained through the political power of the groups that benefited from them. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions emerge, as these factors can significantly influence their design and impact on economic growth. This is particularly evident in the case of New World societies, where initial conditions of inequality led to the development of institutions that reinforced existing power structures, perpetuating inequality and shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across time and space. Instead, the effectiveness of institutions depends on the specific historical and political context in which they operate. For instance, colonial administrations in Africa often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, contrary to the notion that institutions are fixed and exogenous factors (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that institutions can evolve and change in response to local conditions, challenging the view that they are static determinants of economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by noting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty and the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the need for more nuanced historical analysis to understand the complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

The slave trade in Africa significantly impeded the continent's long-term economic development by fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, which persists to this day, is a critical factor in understanding Africa's institutional underdevelopment. This internal mistrust, in turn, has a profound effect on economic development, as it undermines the formation of effective institutions. However, the precise mechanisms through which mistrust affects institutional development remain complex, with some scholars arguing that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how historical events shape contemporary economic institutions.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These epochal innovations are seen as dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, with technological progress being a key driver. Technological progress is essential for economic growth and can lead to coordination failures if not complemented by institutional progress. The sustainability of economic improvement is limited without institutional progress to complement technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional and ideological adjustments required to sustain economic growth are equally important. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is essential for long-term economic development.

This underscores the importance of institutional quality in fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial for economic development, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. This highlights the need for institutions that ensure secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms. Furthermore, North (1989, p.9) demonstrates that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. This emphasizes the role of financial institutions in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic development. Thus, the quality of institutions, particularly those that ensure secure property rights and efficient enforcement, is crucial for long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the impact of colonial institutions on long-run development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that extractive institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists were a key factor in the economic underdevelopment of former colonies. This underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This highlights the role of institutions in promoting investment and economic growth.
