The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern behavior, can drive economic development over extended periods. The debate centers around two main positions: those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The former group emphasizes the importance of institutions in creating a stable environment for investment and economic activity. The latter group argues that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that other variables, such as natural resources or cultural beliefs, can also significantly impact economic outcomes. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both argue that institutions shape economic growth. North (1989, p.9) also suggests that the creation of political institutions that enforce property rights influences the development of economic institutions. This interplay between political and economic institutions raises questions about the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Furthermore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complicated by the fact that institutions can be both a cause and a consequence of economic development. For instance, institutions can facilitate economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and economic activity, but they can also be a product of economic development, as economic growth can lead to the creation of new institutions or the strengthening of existing ones. This dynamic relationship between institutions and economic growth highlights the complexity of the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development. The tension between these positions is evident in the literature, as scholars debate the relative importance of institutions and other factors in shaping economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were key milestones in this process. These institutions facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This security was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, showcasing the political and institutional changes that underpinned economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. Their focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth highlights the importance of a regime committed to enforcing rules governing economic exchange. North (1989, p.6) extends this by noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement would lead to falling transaction costs and a growing transactions sector as a proportion of GNP. This increase in specialization and division of labor would multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, further driving economic growth. This dynamic underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic development, as the evolution of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms created a stable environment conducive to long-term economic growth. The evidence from capital markets provides a striking indication of this institutional impact, as the development of markets was closely tied to the political factors governing economic exchange and enforcement.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be a more fundamental driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is the political influence of elites. These elites, driven by self-interest, advocated for institutions that maintained their status and wealth, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This pattern is evident in the persistence of institutions like serfdom, which, while inefficient, were maintained because they benefited the landed nobility. The persistence of these institutions over time suggests that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers. The downstream implication is that the institutions that emerged from these conflicts were not always conducive to long-term economic growth, as they often restricted competition and innovation.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, as seen in settler colonies like the United States and Australia, fostered economic growth by encouraging investment and enforcing the rule of law. Extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, hindering long-term economic development. This institutional divergence was a direct outcome of colonial policies. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic trajectories. However, Acemoglu et al. also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape institutional development. The interaction between colonial institutions and the opportunity for industrialization further complicates the picture, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). For example, in the context of the former colonies, the reversal in relative incomes was closely tied to industrialization and the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering this process. This suggests that the long-run economic growth of former colonies was shaped by the initial institutional framework and subsequent interactions between institutions and economic opportunities.

The literature on colonial impacts on African development explores how institutional changes influenced public goods provision. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) share the posture that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) focuses on how institutions affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They aim to understand the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and explore how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, highlighting the need for further research to repair these lacunae and check for the persistence of poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This research agenda is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African development and for informing institutional reforms that can promote economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is crucial for modern economic growth, and that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about supporting technological progress and managing the social and political conflicts that arise from it.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, determine the extent to which economic growth is sustained or undermined. Austin supports this view by highlighting that the institutional paths taken by different countries are shaped by historical contexts and the interactions between colonial policies and indigenous economic actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The creation of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is essential for economic growth. North emphasizes that the development of institutions like the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution in England were critical in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This reduction in transaction costs is a direct result of institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are fundamental for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The role of institutions in managing social and political conflicts is equally important. Acemoglu et al. note that institutions that maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is because such institutions are often designed to benefit specific interests rather than promote widespread economic development. The historical evidence from colonies shows that extractive institutions were established to maximize the benefits for European colonists, which had long-lasting negative effects on the economic growth of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins argues that the quality of institutions is crucial for providing public goods and upholding the rule of law, which are essential for economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by managing conflicts and ensuring that economic benefits are distributed equitably (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Austin 2008, p.15).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. North shows that the development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by showing that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current economic outcomes.
