Institutions shape long-run economic growth by determining the incentives for investment, the security of property rights, and the overall efficiency of markets. The live disagreement centres on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, with some scholars emphasizing the role of secure property rights and credible commitment, while others focus on distributional conflicts and the efficiency of economic institutions. Some scholars argue that institutional differences are the primary driver of economic growth, while others contend that cultural beliefs and exogenous factors play a more significant role. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, as it helps to explain the divergent economic trajectories of countries and regions over time. The central question is how institutions influence economic growth, and whether their impact is direct or mediated through other factors. A key point of contention is whether the efficiency of institutions or the distribution of power within them is the primary determinant of economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) establish that institutions are critical for economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. North (1989, p.9) highlights how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn, facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework lowered transaction costs and expanded the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this mechanism by showing that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law or set up extractive states, depending on the context (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness in driving economic growth depends on whether they are designed to benefit the broader economy or to entrench the power of specific groups. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic performance, where the distribution of power within institutions can either facilitate or hinder growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that historical institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped economic outcomes in different contexts. The political commitments of colonial administrations were pivotal in shaping institutions that fostered economic growth. For example, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how colonial institutions adapted to local economic and political conditions, illustrating that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their responsiveness to contextual factors. The impact of these institutions varies across different settings. The degree to which colonial administrations were committed to secure property rights and enforceable contracts varied, influencing the long-term economic trajectories of their colonies (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development shows that historical and structural factors shaped African poverty and growth, but does not explicitly focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This is because institutions matter for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that distributional conflicts drive key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. Both authors argue that institutions in these societies were shaped by the interests of elites, who used their power to create and maintain institutions that favored their own economic interests. For instance, Ogilvie contends that the institutions of serfdom, community, and guilds in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the power dynamics between elites and the broader population. This power dynamic is also evident in the New World societies studied by Sokoloff and Engerman, where institutions were shaped by the interests of elites who had disproportionate political and economic power. These elites used their influence to create institutions that restricted competition and maintained their economic advantages, such as policies that favored large-scale agriculture and mining enterprises over small-scale, labor-intensive activities. This approach highlights that distributional conflicts have a significant impact on the development of economic institutions. Ogilvie notes that institutions reflect the broader social and political context in which they operate. This context includes the beliefs and values of the population, which can shape institutions in ways that are not always aligned with the interests of elites. For example, the Protestant Reformation in Europe led to a decline in guilds, which were seen as inefficient and restrictive by many craftsmen. This shift in beliefs and values had a significant impact on the development of economic institutions, demonstrating that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of power dynamics, beliefs, and values.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that gender dynamics and access to resources significantly influenced economic outcomes in Africa, demonstrating that women played a pivotal role in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women's access to land and resources was crucial for agricultural productivity, and their contributions were instrumental in the economic growth of regions like the Gold Coast. This was evident in the migration of farmers in search of land for cocoa cultivation, which propelled the region to become a leading producer of cocoa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified customary law, often reinforcing patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This shift in education policies and practices had significant implications for the economic development of sub-Saharan Africa, as it influenced the demand for missionary education and the subsequent economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the economic performance of the region was more robust than previously characterized. This suggests that the contributions of women and the impact of colonial education policies played a significant role in shaping the economic landscape of Africa, challenging the primacy of institutions as the sole determinant of economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, document that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are mediated through a pervasive mistrust that has become deeply ingrained in African societies. This mistrust is a crucial determinant of economic outcomes, as it influences the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The slave trade's legacy has shaped the internal norms of mistrust, which in turn affect economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the profound impact of internalized mistrust on economic behavior and outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalized mistrust has far-reaching implications, affecting economic development, political institutions, and firm management practices. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Technological progress drives economic growth by expanding the stock of useful knowledge, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a focus on technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth, with Mokyr emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge and Kuznets highlighting the need for institutional adjustments to accommodate technological changes. The growth of useful knowledge is marked by periods of significant breakthroughs that constitute epochal innovations. These innovations require institutional progress to complement technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. The institutional adjustments facilitate the efficient use of technological innovations, preventing the erosion of economic gains due to rent-seeking behaviors. This is particularly relevant in the context of technological progress, where new innovations can disrupt existing social and economic structures. The ability to manage these disruptions through effective institutional frameworks is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a well-established fact. The creation of secure property rights and the development of financial instruments have been critical in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The Bank of England's establishment and the subsequent financial revolution in England illustrate the profound impact of institutions on economic development. This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term investments, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The interaction between political and economic institutions is crucial for understanding how societies manage disruptions and sustain long-term economic growth. The specific mechanisms by which institutions affect economic growth remain a subject of debate. For instance, some argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions were established and how they evolved over time. The role of institutions in facilitating or hindering economic growth is particularly evident in the context of technological progress, where new innovations can disrupt existing social and economic structures. Effective institutional frameworks are crucial for managing these disruptions and sustaining long-term economic growth.
