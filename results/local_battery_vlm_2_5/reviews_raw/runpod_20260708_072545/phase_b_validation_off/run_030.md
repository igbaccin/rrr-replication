The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks influence economic trajectories over extended periods. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, or more formally, the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and historical paths are more determinative. This disagreement underscores the need to delineate the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and to assess the relative importance of these mechanisms compared to other potential drivers. As such, the question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to understanding the complexities of economic development.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. illustrate that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This approach contrasts with settlements where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The impact of these institutions was profound, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions for centuries. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional differences were directly influenced by the policies and strategies of European colonizers, not just geographic factors. The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended suggests that the initial institutional frameworks laid the groundwork for future economic development or stagnation. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is bidirectional. Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights the importance of both the initial institutional framework and the historical context in which these institutions operated. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

Expanding on this, North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight the development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England as pivotal moments that increased the security of property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth. This institutional evolution was a driving force, as evidenced by the dramatic decline in transaction costs and the growth of the capital market. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for a more robust institutional framework. The implications of this institutional framework are profound, suggesting that economic growth is deeply intertwined with the political and institutional environment, not solely a function of economic factors. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to factors other than institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional evolution on long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality, by advantaging elites and restricting competition, contribute to the persistence of inequality, offering a rival explanation to the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This perspective is grounded in the idea that the initial factor endowments of societies, particularly the distribution of land and natural resources relative to labor, significantly influenced the institutions that evolved. In regions where land was abundant and labor scarce, such as in Latin America, the resulting high levels of inequality led to the establishment of institutions that protected elite interests. These institutions often took the form of policies that restricted competition and maintained the status quo, thereby hindering economic growth. This uneven distribution aligns with the idea that institutions in unequal societies tend to favor elites, limiting the potential for broad-based economic growth. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) also note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic growth can vary across different regions and historical contexts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15).

In this context, technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets share a foundational belief in the power of technological advancements to spur economic development. While Mokyr focuses on the role of knowledge growth, Kuznets emphasizes the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently harness these innovations. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns, necessitating significant societal adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Similarly, Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is relatively monotonic and irreversible, economic reasonableness, the institutional progress that complements technological advancements, is less so (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, requires both technological innovation and the development of institutions that can support and sustain these advancements. This dynamic interaction between technology and institutions is essential for achieving long-term economic growth. Mokyr points out that the growth of economic reasonableness is far from linear, suggesting that the institutional environment must continually evolve to keep pace with technological changes. This evolutionary process is vital for ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. The evolution of institutions, therefore, is a critical factor in translating technological progress into long-term economic growth.

This interplay is further complicated by historical factors, such as colonial policies and the slave trade, which have significantly influenced African economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Colonial policies did not uniformly hinder economic development; instead, they could foster indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent (Austin 2008, p.15). The legacy of the slave trade extends to social norms, including mistrust, which can impede economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, affects various aspects of economic development, from international trade to political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that while historical factors like the slave trade and colonial policies are crucial, their impact on economic development is mediated by complex and evolving institutional frameworks.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework within which economic activities operate. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for fostering economic development (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is evident in the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or set up extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the long-term impact of institutional frameworks on economic development.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of colonial policies and historical factors. Austin qualifies that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that while historical factors like the slave trade and colonial policies are crucial, their impact on economic development is mediated by complex and evolving institutional frameworks. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, affects various aspects of economic development, from international trade to political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term effects. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional design, shaped by politically powerful groups, has had a lasting impact on the economic development of former colonies. The historical evidence of capital markets in England and France shows that institutions per se, and in particular, the institutional changes described, were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the difference in the development of economic institutions in England and France, which shows that the institutional changes in England were more conducive to economic growth.
