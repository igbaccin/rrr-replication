Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, a topic that has generated significant debate among scholars. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks influence economic development trajectories over extended periods. One key disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions that foster growth. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, which prioritize the extraction of resources for the benefit of a ruling elite, can hinder long-term economic development. This perspective is challenged by those who emphasize the importance of inclusive institutions, which promote broader economic participation and growth. Another contentious point is the extent to which historical events, such as colonialism, have shaped the institutional landscapes of former colonies. This debate highlights the enduring impact of past policies and institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary policy choices. Institutions are not static; they evolve over time in response to political, economic, and social pressures. Understanding this dynamic process is crucial for grasping how institutions influence long-run economic growth. The live disagreement is whether institutions that prioritize the extraction of resources for a ruling elite can foster long-term economic development or if inclusive institutions that promote broader economic participation are necessary for sustained growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argued that the development of institutions that ensured secure property rights and low transaction costs was pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the subsequent financial innovations significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional change was a response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This is a clear example of how political institutions can influence economic growth by shaping property rights and enforcement mechanisms. The development of new financial instruments and the establishment of the Bank of England were critical in reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and economic growth. This institutional framework reduced transaction costs and ensured regime commitment to these rules, fostering a stable environment for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) also noted that the transactions sector's size as a proportion of GNP would grow, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor. This growth in the transactions sector underscores the dynamic nature of economic institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth. The evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England provides a compelling case study of how institutional changes can drive economic development.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by determining the nature of economic institutions established in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement, which led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, promoting long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth varies across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the results hold across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This consistency in findings underscores the robust influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, even when considering different dimensions of institutional quality.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2) delve into how institutions rooted in inequality and slavery have shaped economic development in specific regions, emphasizing the role of government policies in maintaining social disparities. This is evident in the New World societies, where greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework, which favored the elite, was a direct response to the factor endowments of these societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical factors on economic development, even in the face of subsequent changes.

This historical lens is crucial, as Broadberry and Gardner's work (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) reveals that the economic trajectories of Africa and Asia were more nuanced than often depicted. The focus on national accounting, colonial education systems, and the great divergence between Europe and Asia reveals that the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies was better than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner show that by the early twentieth century, many of these economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence. This finding is supported by Frankema, who argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby changing the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The impact of colonial education systems is further qualified by Broadberry and Gardner, who tie the patterns of economic growth and decline in African economies to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The broader implication of these findings is that the legacy of colonial institutions, particularly in education, played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. This indicates that the economic performance of African economies was determined by colonial exploitation and by the institutional changes that facilitated economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) offer a fresh perspective by scrutinizing colonial policies and labor dynamics, which are pivotal in driving long-run economic growth. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes African poverty to institutional legacies, oversimplifies the causal pathways. For instance, colonial policies that encouraged indigenous commodity production and fiscal security influenced economic outcomes by adapting to local economic and political actions. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this mechanism by demonstrating that colonial administrations often responded to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, imposing uniform institutional prescriptions. This dynamic indicates that the economic performance of African economies was determined by colonial exploitation and by the adaptive nature of colonial institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight the importance of reconstructing real wage levels and trends to understand long-term economic development in Africa, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these dynamics can reveal the extent to which historical conditions have influenced African welfare development.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective highlights that the advancement of human knowledge, particularly through epochal innovations, constitutes a dominant source of sustained growth. These breakthroughs require technical, institutional, and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is crucial for modern economic growth, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in peacefully managing such conflicts. This implies that while technological progress is essential, institutional progress is necessary to complement and sustain it. Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on continuous institutional adaptation. This dynamic underscores the interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks, where the latter must evolve to support the former for long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the secure exchange of goods and services, which in turn fosters economic development. The evolution of institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment is critical for economic growth. This is evident in the establishment of the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, the development of institutions that govern public choice in seventeenth-century England underscores the importance of credible commitments in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These historical examples illustrate how institutions that provide secure property rights and enforce contracts are essential for long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies. European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of institutions that either enforced the rule of law or set up extractive states. These institutions significantly influenced current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, shows that institutional frameworks designed to maximize rents for colonizers often did not prioritize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the enduring effects of historical institutions on economic outcomes, as institutions initially designed for extraction can hinder long-term economic development.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is also evident in the reversal of fortune among former European colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies is attributed to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, as they shape the economic policies and opportunities available to different regions (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from capital markets further supports the idea that institutions per se, and the changes in these institutions, were critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This historical analysis demonstrates that the development of institutions that support economic growth is a complex process influenced by political and economic factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the cultural and social factors that influence trust and economic development. The slave trade in Africa, for example, caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development by undermining the trust necessary for market exchanges and economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This shows that the institutions governing public choice and the cultural factors influencing trust are interconnected and both play a crucial role in economic development. The historical and empirical evidence from Africa highlights the importance of trust in economic development and the enduring effects of historical institutions on economic outcomes.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a matter of agreement: institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are essential for economic growth. The remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions designed for extraction can be transformed to support long-run growth. The historical evidence from the Bank of England's role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth shows that institutions can be adapted to support economic development (North 1989, p.9). The case of colonial institutions in former colonies, which often did not prioritize long-run growth, highlights the challenges of transforming extractive institutions into growth-promoting ones (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
