The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods and whether institutions are the primary drivers of this evolution. The live disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. One position emphasizes the direct causal link between secure property rights and economic development, arguing that institutions facilitating these rights are crucial for sustained growth. This view is contested by another position, which posits that the relationship is more complex, involving various historical, cultural, and political factors. Additionally, there is a perspective that highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. These differing views underscore the importance of institutions in economic growth, but they diverge on the specifics of how this influence operates. The debate highlights that understanding the nuances of institutional impact is essential for formulating effective economic policies. This understanding is crucial for grasping the broader implications of institutional design and reform for long-term economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) expand on this by showing that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups in colonies, with some establishing extractive institutions that hindered economic development. For instance, in regions with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources, whereas in regions with lower mortality, they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting impacts on economic outcomes, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita in the long run. This institutional path dependency is crucial because it illustrates how initial institutional choices can have enduring effects on economic development. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics in economic growth, where the initial setup of institutions can lock in certain economic trajectories that are difficult to alter.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that the institutional framework governing public choice is pivotal for economic growth, particularly through the establishment of secure property rights. North and Weingast show that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 in England led to a significant shift in political institutions, which in turn fostered economic growth by ensuring that property rights were secure and that the government was constrained by the rule of law. This institutional change was crucial for the development of capital markets and the overall economic performance of the country. North and Weingast further illustrate that the credibility of these institutions in enforcing property rights and maintaining political stability was a key factor in driving economic growth, as evidenced by the robust performance of capital markets during this period. Sokoloff and Engerman build on this by examining how different institutional frameworks in New World societies led to varied economic outcomes. They argue that societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, had institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby hindering economic growth. This institutional framework, characterized by unequal distribution of power and resources, limited the ability of these societies to develop secure property rights and foster economic development. The comparative analysis of England and France by North and Weingast modifies this perspective. They note that France's economic performance was not significantly hindered by its bureaucratic structure, suggesting that the impact of institutions on economic growth may not be as straightforward as it seems. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, where the initial setup of institutions can lock in certain economic trajectories that are difficult to alter.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share a posture that the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development is often oversimplified. They argue that the historical paths and different periods influence economic outcomes in ways that challenge simplistic causal narratives. This complexity is seen in the institutional choices that shape long-run economic growth. This suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and actions, which influence economic trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) elaborates on this by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This indicates that the dynamics of power and interest play a crucial role in shaping institutions, which can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also points out that institutions can be transformed into indispensable instruments in economic history, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate within specific historical contexts. This nuanced understanding is essential for grasping the intricate relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both find that colonial rule in Africa had a complex impact on economic development, shaping institutions in ways that influenced growth. The literature on colonial impact on African economic development explores various factors influencing growth but does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This institutional change had profound effects on agricultural productivity and economic growth. This suggests that the institutional framework imposed by colonial powers significantly shaped the economic trajectory of African societies by altering access to resources and opportunities. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further qualify this impact by showing that the growth in export production, which was a key driver of economic activity, was tightly linked to political institutions and transport costs. This indicates that the institutional environment, shaped by colonial rule, played a crucial role in determining the success of economic ventures and the overall economic performance of African countries. Colonial institutions thus had a multifaceted impact on African economic development, influencing both short-term productivity and long-term growth trajectories.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa had profound and lasting effects on social trust, which in turn has hindered economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges institutional explanations by highlighting how historical events can shape long-term economic outcomes independently of institutional frameworks. A central mechanism through which the slave trade influenced economic development is the erosion of social trust. This mistrust is deeply ingrained in individuals' internal norms. This extended period of exploitation disrupted social and economic institutions, creating a legacy of mistrust that continues to affect economic interactions. The implications of this mistrust are far-reaching, as it undermines the very foundations of economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) points out that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the absence of such institutions in other regions, compounded by the legacy of mistrust from the slave trade, has significantly impeded economic progress. The enduring impact of the slave trade on social trust thus offers a compelling alternative explanation for Africa's economic underdevelopment, one that operates independently of institutional frameworks.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge, those that constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world, may be termed epochal innovations. The growth of useful knowledge is an active force that shapes economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggests that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the ability to coordinate and implement technological progress, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress itself. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development, the institutional environment must also be conducive to harnessing these advancements effectively. The integration of technological progress and institutional adaptability is essential for long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate the coordination and implementation of technological progress (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur, and their effectiveness in enforcing property rights and ensuring secure transactions is crucial for sustained growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence shows that the development of institutions such as the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework supported the financial revolution and laid the groundwork for long-term capital markets, which are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also critical. The credibility of commitments to property rights and economic rules significantly influences economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth are as important as the rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England's economic development can be attributed to the institutional changes that ensured secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The role of institutions shapes the political environment, which influences economic outcomes (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the experiences of European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by European colonists significantly affected the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the enduring influence of institutional frameworks on economic performance, as the institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be traced back to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which had lasting effects on their economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The historical evidence settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, as seen in the case of England, shows that credible commitments underpin growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The contrasting experiences of European colonies, where extractive institutions hindered long-run development, further illustrate the importance of institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The measurement of long-run economic growth through the lens of institutional development and the comparison of colonial experiences provide clear evidence that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes.
