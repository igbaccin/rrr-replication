The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. Institutions, encompassing the rules, norms, and organizations that structure societal interactions, are posited to influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, reducing uncertainty, and facilitating cooperation. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who argue for alternative explanations. The former group, influenced by the work of North and Weingast (1989, p.2), contend that credible commitment underpins growth. Some scholars, such as Ogilvie (2007, p.3), focus on distributional conflicts as a driving force behind institutional arrangements. The latter group, influenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), argue that mortality rates and colonial strategies significantly impacted current income per capita. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic or cultural elements, play a more significant role. This question is central to understanding the mechanisms that foster economic development and the potential for policy interventions to influence growth trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) assert that institutions are critical for long-run economic growth. North's argument rests on the idea that institutions shape the structure of incentives in an economy, influencing economic outcomes by affecting the cost of transacting. For example, the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 17th-century England led to a significant reduction in transaction costs, which was pivotal for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets, facilitating economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further explain that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced by colonialism, underscoring the enduring impact of institutions on economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The impact of institutions on economic growth depends on both their design and the context of their implementation.

These institutional arrangements, such as apprentice contracts, played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Institutions like guilds, which were responsible for the fair execution of apprentice contracts, facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions were pivotal in reducing the costs associated with training skilled workers, which in turn influenced the skill premium. The skill premium, determined by factors such as training costs and institutional barriers to entry, was a significant predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.3). The beneficial effects of these institutions were sometimes offset by their attempts to form local cartels, which restricted entry into trades and kept relative pay above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, where the same institutions that foster skill development can also create barriers to economic efficiency. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this complexity by noting that technological progress, a key driver of economic growth, requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) argues, is the mainspring of economic growth, and institutional progress complements technological progress to ensure sustained economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.49). The wages of skilled laborers in these regions yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were not evenly distributed. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that the economic development in this period did not necessarily improve the standard of living for all. The focus on the North Sea countries as economic leaders underscores the importance of regional specialization and the role of trade in driving economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner argue that Africa's economic performance is more favorable than previously thought, and Frankema and van Waijenburg support this view by showing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These findings challenge conventional views on African economic history by demonstrating higher per capita incomes and real wages than previously thought. The mechanisms driving this economic performance were complex. Broadberry and Gardner note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the economic development of Africa was not uniformly stagnant but rather influenced by specific local conditions and institutional frameworks. This perspective underscores the role of institutions in mediating economic outcomes, even if the stream does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth.

Colonial rule significantly influenced African economic development, particularly through its impact on institutions and the role of women in economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that colonial rule reshaped economic institutions and gender dynamics, altering the trajectory of African economic development. Colonial policies often marginalized women by relegating them to less productive lands and reinforcing patriarchal structures through customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization had profound implications for economic growth, as women's contributions to agricultural production and economic activities were curtailed. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched gender inequalities, limiting women's access to resources and opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). However, the impact of colonial rule on education varied significantly, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This educational development, in turn, influenced the formation of new social and political institutions, altering the perceived benefits of formal education and potentially fostering long-term economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.15). The differential impact of colonial rule on education highlights the complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development in Africa.

This historical mistrust, perpetuated through generations, affects contemporary trust levels, which are crucial for economic development. The persistence of this mistrust highlights the enduring effects of historical institutions on economic outcomes, even as other factors such as colonial rule and more recent events have played significant roles.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. In Europe, these conflicts led to the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were maintained by elites to preserve their economic and political power. In New World societies, initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the establishment of institutions that reinforced these disparities. These institutions were not merely inefficient; they were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiency, highlights the power of distributional conflicts in shaping economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further note that the initial factor endowments of New World societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created conditions for extreme inequality. This inequality was reinforced by institutions that favored elites, leading to a self-perpetuating cycle of economic and political power. The implications of these findings extend beyond historical contexts, suggesting that contemporary economic institutions may also be shaped by underlying distributional conflicts, which can hinder economic development by maintaining inequalities.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of power and resources within societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies. Extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonizers rather than the local population, hindered economic development by concentrating wealth and power in the hands of a few. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality in regions where such institutions were entrenched. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) also underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering a market economy. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which facilitated secure financial transactions, illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency. The role of institutions in economic development is further complicated by the influence of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that pre-industrial European institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions often served to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of powerful groups, even if they were not optimal for economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) thesis that institutions are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, rather than to promote long-term economic development.
