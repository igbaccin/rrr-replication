The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of development and prosperity. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who emphasize other factors, such as technological progress or exogenous shocks. Institutions, in this context, refer to the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic interactions. The debate hinges on whether institutional frameworks are the critical determinants of economic performance or if they are merely ancillary to other more fundamental forces. The stakes are high, as the resolution of this debate informs policy decisions and our understanding of historical economic trajectories. The central tension revolves around whether institutions facilitate economic growth by enhancing efficiency and reducing transaction costs, or if they are shaped by broader economic and social forces that drive growth. The implications of this debate extend to contemporary economic policy, where the effectiveness of institutional reforms hinges on their perceived role in economic development. The nature of this debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This institutional framework was not conducive to economic development, as it was designed to benefit the colonizers rather than the local population. This institutional legacy helps explain the significant fraction of current income differences between former colonies. Notably, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) propose a theory of institutional differences among countries colonized by Europeans, exploiting these differences to derive a possible source of exogenous variation in institutions. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by showing that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective emphasizes that economic growth is fundamentally fueled by advances in technology, which in turn are driven by the accumulation of knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress often involves the recombination of existing techniques, drawing on analogical thinking to transform known ideas into novel innovations. This process is not merely about individual breakthroughs but also about the broader accumulation of knowledge that informs and enables further innovation. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver, it must be supported by institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic growth.

While technological progress is crucial, Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) suggest that economic growth through modernization involves multiple factors, including capital stock growth and productivity increases. This perspective underscores that the process of catching up in economic development is influenced by technological opportunities and social changes, not just institutional frameworks. However, Komlos notes that despite economic growth, biological well-being can decline due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment, highlighting that economic growth does not automatically translate into improved living standards (Komlos 1998, p.8). Komlos argues that the relative price of nutrients increased during the Industrial Revolution, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, which in turn affected their nutritional intake (Komlos 1998, p.8). This suggests that while technological progress and capital accumulation are crucial for economic growth, they must be complemented by improvements in living standards and nutritional intake to ensure sustainable development. The implication is that while institutions may play a role in shaping long-run economic growth, they are not the sole determinant, and other factors such as technological progress and living standards must also be considered.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This technological innovation, by improving property rights enforcement, fostered a more stable and predictable environment for agricultural investment. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) adds nuance to this by highlighting that while barbed wire improved efficiency, it did so within a broader context of institutional dynamics. The enforcement of property rights through barbed wire fencing was not merely a technological solution but also an institutional one, as it required legal and social frameworks to support and enforce these new boundaries. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, their impact is contingent on the institutional environment in which they are implemented.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial for shaping long-run economic growth, as North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. North (1989, p.6) contends that institutions facilitate market exchanges by specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is not static but evolves with the growing complexity of the economy. They emphasize that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of market exchanges. A critical implication of this institutional framework is the development of capital markets, as evidenced by the success of English capital markets in the seventeenth century (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This success underscores the importance of credible commitments from the regime, which are necessary for economic growth. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that these institutions must be appropriately chosen to mitigate incentive problems that reputation alone cannot address (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This nuanced view of institutional development suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness depends on their design and enforcement mechanisms.

The institutional framework that underpins economic growth is not merely a static backdrop but a dynamic force that shapes the trajectory of technological progress and economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54; 2005, p.12) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both underscore the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements. Mokyr highlights that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains. Abramovitz further elaborates on how technological opportunity encourages rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn accelerates economic development. Both scholars agree that institutions play a critical role in facilitating the efficient use of innovations, ensuring that technological progress translates into sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10; 1973, p.4) adds that the mass application of technological innovations, which is a key feature of modern economic growth, is closely linked to the progress of science. This progress, in turn, is based on the efficient use of innovations, which requires robust institutional support. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5) argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs. This reduction in transaction costs is crucial for economic development, as it allows for increased specialization and division of labor. Ogilvie (2007, p.7; 2007, p.6) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30; 2010, p.4) provide historical evidence that supports the idea that institutions can enhance economic growth by protecting property rights and reducing externalities. Ogilvie shows that various pre-modern institutions, such as merchant guilds, were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. Hornbeck demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights. This historical evidence highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth by facilitating technological progress and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.5). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions should be viewed primarily as efficient solutions to transaction costs or as mechanisms for resolving distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the success of English capital markets in the seventeenth century, which shows that credible commitments from the regime are necessary for economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54).
