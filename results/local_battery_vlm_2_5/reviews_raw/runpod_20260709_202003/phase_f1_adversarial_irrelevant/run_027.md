The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of the debate lies a fundamental disagreement: do institutions drive economic growth, or are they merely a byproduct of it? One position posits that robust institutions, such as secure property rights and effective governance, are essential for fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. Conversely, another position suggests that institutions are shaped by economic factors, such as population growth and savings rates, and thus are not the primary drivers of long-run economic performance. The tension between these views underscores the need to examine the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Understanding this relationship is crucial for policymakers and economists seeking to promote sustainable economic development. The evidence from historical and contemporary studies provides a rich tapestry of insights into how institutions can shape economic growth, particularly through their impact on property rights and transaction costs. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties and fostering long-run economic growth. This institutional reliability is not merely about enforcing rules but also about ensuring that these rules can be reliably enforced and adapted. North (1989, p.2) elaborates that as economies grow more complex, the need for reliable institutions increases. These institutions must facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby enabling individuals to engage in transactions with strangers. This institutional framework supports economic growth by providing a stable environment where individuals can confidently participate in markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) caution that institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms. Institutions can enhance the efficacy of reputation by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This interplay between institutions and reputation mechanisms underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. They argue that European colonizers established institutions that maximized their rents, rather than promoting long-run growth. These institutions, designed to extract resources and maintain political power, hindered economic development in many colonies. This institutional framework persists, affecting current income levels. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions underscores their enduring impact on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) highlight that the institutional differences among countries colonized by Europeans are a key factor in their economic performance. The legacy of colonial institutions, therefore, continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies, influencing their long-term growth and development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These conflicts drove the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which were not merely efficient solutions to economic problems but were also shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups. The merchant guilds, for example, were not always the inefficient cartels they are often portrayed as, but rather evolved as responses to the need for security and contract enforcement in an environment where distributional conflicts were rife. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who emphasize that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The implication is that the institutions themselves were deeply influenced by the underlying power structures and distributional conflicts. However, Ogilvie also acknowledges the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the efficiency of any single institution cannot be understood in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe were part of a complex system where changes in one part of the system could have ripple effects throughout.

Clark and Jacks, along with Broadberry and Gupta, re-evaluate historical economic growth theories by examining specific factors like coal extraction costs and international price arbitrage, rather than focusing on institutions as a driver of long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the Industrial Revolution was not driven by coal extraction costs, but instead by other factors, and that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining were modest. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in labor productivity in the tradable goods sector. These findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth may have been overstated in some historical contexts. Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this perspective by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. This implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex and context-dependent than previously thought.

During the Industrial Revolution, economic growth did not uniformly translate into improved health outcomes for the population, a phenomenon Komlos (1998, p.13) attributes to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This decline in biological well-being, shared by van Zanden (2009, p.19), highlights that economic growth does not necessarily lead to improved health outcomes for the population. The skill premium, which is a predictor of long-term economic performance, was not sufficient to offset the negative effects of poor nutrition and inadequate income gains. This was particularly evident in urban areas, where rapid population growth and industrialization led to overcrowding and poor sanitation. The guilds, which were institutions that guaranteed the fair execution of the apprentice contract, played a role in this by monopolizing skills and restricting entry into their trade, which kept their relative pay above market-clearing levels. The beneficial effects of the guilds on the intergenerational transfer of skills may therefore be counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different institutions playing different roles at different times.

Hornbeck (2010, p.34) and Whatley (2018, p.2) both examine the impact of specific technologies on economic outcomes, with Hornbeck focusing on agricultural productivity and Whatley on slave exports. The introduction of barbed wire fencing and gunpowder technology had significant impacts on their respective sectors, enhancing productivity and facilitating economic activities. Hornbeck shows that barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, leading to increased agricultural productivity. This technological innovation allowed farmers to allocate more land to crops, thereby increasing overall productivity. Whatley argues that gunpowder imports played a significant role in increasing slave exports through intertemporal production links and social processes. This technology enhanced the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving, supporting the gun-slave cycle hypothesis. These findings illustrate how technological advancements can drive economic growth by improving productivity and facilitating economic activities. However, these studies do not engage with the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, leaving open the question of how institutions might interact with technological changes to influence economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic activities. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework provided a reliable environment for economic exchanges, which in turn facilitated market development and economic growth. North similarly argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the development of efficient property rights and their enforcement is essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.2). Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This highlights the importance of considering the specific historical context and the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which maximized rents to European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, indicating that institutional differences played a significant role in this divergence (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that technological factors alone are insufficient to explain economic growth without considering institutional frameworks (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). Austin argues that the exchange-value product of Africans' labor was lower in Africa than in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken, highlighting the role of institutions in determining labor productivity and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.11). These findings illustrate how institutions interact with technological changes and economic activities to influence long-run economic growth. The key point of agreement is that institutions are essential for economic growth. There is a remaining disagreement over the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists did not promote long-run growth, suggesting that the nature of institutions matters (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which maximized rents to European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, and the resulting reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.
