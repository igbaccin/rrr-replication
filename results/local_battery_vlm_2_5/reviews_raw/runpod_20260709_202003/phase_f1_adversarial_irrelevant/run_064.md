The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is an understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained growth, and what factors enable or impede this process. One position argues that institutions facilitating secure property rights and credible commitments are pivotal for economic growth. This perspective contends that well-functioning institutions reduce transaction costs, promote investment, and foster innovation. Another perspective posits that technological factors, such as total factor productivity, are the primary drivers of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. This view suggests that technological advancements can overcome institutional deficiencies, leading to economic growth even in the absence of robust institutions. The debate hinges on whether institutions or technology are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, with implications for policy and historical interpretation. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, or whether technology can overcome institutional deficiencies, is central to understanding the mechanisms behind economic development. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, a shared finding of Acemoglu et al. (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the critical role of political institutions in establishing and enforcing rules that support economic development. North and Weingast emphasize the importance of the regime's commitment to these rules, noting that institutions can enhance the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by constraining behavior in situations where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework provided a stable environment for economic activities, ensuring that property rights were secure and that contracts were enforceable. However, Acemoglu et al. also note that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent differences in income per capita, qualifying the role of institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact can be modulated by exogenous factors such as mortality rates, which influence the type of institutions that are established.

Clark and Jacks's work on coal mining productivity during the Industrial Revolution, together with van Zanden's analysis of economic development in Europe, highlights that various factors beyond institutions influenced economic growth and living standards during this period (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Both studies focus on productivity and economic development, with Clark and Jacks estimating modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining from 1710 to 1869 (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This suggests that while economic growth occurred, it did not necessarily translate into improved living standards for the average person. Komlos's analysis of biological well-being during the Industrial Revolution further qualifies this pattern, showing that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This implies that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, and that institutional factors alone cannot explain the complex interplay between economic growth and living standards.

The literature on distributional conflicts and economic development highlights how institutions can both constrain and enable economic growth, but it does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) examine how institutional arrangements can be shaped by distributional conflicts, rather than solely by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie argues that institutions like merchant guilds and the Mesta were not simply inefficient cartels but rather served specific functions within their historical contexts, such as providing security and contract enforcement. This perspective challenges the view that institutions are always efficient solutions to economic problems. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) support this by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. Their analysis of how factor endowments influenced the development of institutions in the New World underscores the importance of historical context in shaping institutional arrangements. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) further illustrates this point by demonstrating how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains. This technological innovation allowed farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, highlighting how institutional changes can drive economic development. However, Hornbeck (2010, p.34; 2010, p.36) also notes the complexities involved in analyzing productivity, suggesting that the benefits of institutional changes may not be uniform across different contexts. This indicates that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, their effects are contingent on specific historical and environmental factors.

Stern (2004, p.3) (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; van Zanden 2009, p.19) discuss economic growth and inequality. The environmental Kuznets curve is one of the most prominent topics. It posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between environmental degradation and economic growth, suggesting that as economies develop, they initially experience increased environmental damage before eventually improving their environmental performance. However, this relationship is not universally applicable and its statistical foundation is fragile. The skill premium, which reflects the relative pay of skilled versus unskilled labor, is another critical factor in economic performance. A low skill premium indicates efficient labor and capital markets, as well as effective institutions for human capital formation. This, in turn, fosters long-term economic growth by encouraging investment in human capital (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14; 1985, p.29) emphasize that technological imbalances across sectors can drive secular inequality trends, highlighting the complex interplay between technological change and income distribution. However, these trends are not inevitable and can be influenced by factors such as human capital accumulation and shifts in wealth distribution. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how different factors interact to shape economic growth and inequality.

Bryant shows that structural differences in agricultural productivity between Europe and China shaped their respective economic trajectories, with Europe's shift towards large-scale farming and livestock rearing enhancing productivity and releasing labor for industrial employment (Bryant 2006, p.29). Meanwhile, China's focus on subsistence farming and domestic handicrafts limited economic growth and capital accumulation (Bryant 2006, p.29; Nunn 2008, p.3). This factor, combined with the demographic pressures and limited technological adoption in China, suggests that the institutional frameworks governing labor and resource allocation played a crucial role in determining long-run economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis of African women's contributions to economic growth further illustrates this point, showing that women's roles in agriculture and resource allocation were critical to economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). However, the extraction and export of resources under colonial rule altered these dynamics, potentially limiting the growth-enhancing effects of women's economic activities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.18). This underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks, resource allocation, and long-run economic growth.

Austin's analysis of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Broadberry and Gupta's exploration of the great divergence between Europe and Asia (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) underscore the complex historical factors shaping economic divergence. These authors jointly argue that simplistic explanations for economic divergence miss the nuanced interplay of historical periods and paths. Austin shows that the commercial motivations behind the Atlantic slave trade led to a paradoxical scarcity of labor in Africa, despite the region's high population density (Austin 2008, p.11). This scarcity, combined with the specific production techniques and environmental factors, influenced economic outcomes in Africa. They demonstrate that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn influenced the price of non-tradable commodities. This historical divergence was not merely a product of later developments but was already well underway before 1800. Pamuk's examination of the Black Death's long-term structural changes further qualifies this historical divergence, showing that the wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 was initiated by severe labor shortages and the decline in interest rates (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This historical divergence was not merely a product of later developments but was already well underway before 1800. However, Pamuk notes that the linkages between urban and rural sectors were not always equally strong, which could lead to persistent urban-rural differences in some countries (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This historical perspective highlights the importance of considering the complex interplay of historical factors, including labor productivity, wage rates, and structural changes, in understanding long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay of historical factors. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice, particularly those that enforce secure property rights, have been crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging, thereby improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is supported by Austin, who shows that the reversal of fortune thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). Austin's work highlights that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1). This historical perspective underscores the importance of considering the complex interplay of institutions, labour productivity, wage rates, and structural changes in understanding long-run economic growth.

The impact of technological imbalance on economic growth is another critical factor. Lindert and Williamson show that technological imbalance can be a prime source of secular inequality trends (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). They argue that the early rise in unbalanced productivity growth and in labor-force growth might seem to imply an inevitable rise in inequality, but this is not necessarily the case (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.29). This finding aligns with the work of Clark and Jacks, who demonstrate that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, contrary to the views of Wrigley and Pomeranz (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). Clark and Jacks' analysis of total factor productivity in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution era supports the idea that technological changes alone do not drive economic growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). Instead, the interplay of technological changes and institutional frameworks is what shapes long-run economic growth.

The environmental dimension of economic growth is also significant. Stern argues that the environmental Kuznets curve has a very flimsy statistical foundation and its demise may be imminent due to new generation of decomposition and efficient frontier models (Stern 2004, p.3). This perspective is crucial for understanding how improvements in technology, including changes in both productivity and emissions specific changes in process, can result in lower emissions per unit of output (Stern 2004, p.15). This technological improvement in productivity can be seen as a complement to the institutional frameworks that foster economic growth. Van Zanden's work on Europe between 1500 and 1800 shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This finding suggests that the interplay of technological changes and institutional frameworks is what shapes long-run economic growth, rather than technological changes alone.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether technological changes alone can drive economic growth.
