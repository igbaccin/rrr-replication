Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, yet the precise mechanisms and extent of their influence remain subjects of intense debate. The debate centres on whether institutions primarily facilitate growth through efficiency improvements or whether they serve other functions, such as managing distributional conflicts. One stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of institutions in promoting technological progress and economic efficiency. Another stream argues that institutions are crucial for managing social and political conflicts, which in turn influence economic outcomes. A third stream focuses on the specific ways in which institutions can enhance property rights and reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the multifaceted nature of institutions and their impact on economic development. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for grasping how institutions can either support or hinder long-run economic growth. This understanding is particularly important because it challenges the idea that economic growth is solely driven by technological advancements or market forces. Institutions are not just passive frameworks but active agents that shape economic trajectories. Institutions' role in facilitating technological progress is evident in the interplay between economic growth and technological advancement. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by technological advances and social changes. Kuznets, on the other hand, emphasizes that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This suggests that while technological progress is a driving force, institutions are necessary to harness its potential for economic growth. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions are pivotal in economic growth, particularly in shaping secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. They posit that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering economic growth. This institutional development was marked by the creation of mechanisms that ensured the enforcement of property rights, which in turn reduced transaction costs and encouraged market exchanges. The emergence of these institutions led to a reduction in transaction costs, which is a significant factor in economic development. By establishing secure property rights, these institutions provided a stable framework for economic activity, fostering growth and development. The development of capital markets in England during this period serves as a testament to the success of these institutions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that these institutions complement other mechanisms, such as reputation-building and punishment strategies, to enhance their efficacy. This complementarity is crucial because it ensures that the institutions can effectively mitigate incentive problems that reputation alone cannot address. For instance, the Glorious Revolution in England marked a significant institutional change that improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering economic growth. This institutional framework not only reduced the risk of expropriation but also provided a stable environment for economic activity, which is essential for long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that institutions inherited from colonial powers significantly influence contemporary economic outcomes in former colonies. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers played a crucial role in shaping the institutions established in these colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term resource extraction. This institutional framework persists today, affecting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, who sought to maximize their rents. This extractive institutional legacy, born from high mortality rates, continues to hinder economic growth in many former colonies. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic development.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) both focus on how secure property rights influenced agricultural development and economic institutions in specific historical contexts (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This mechanism highlights the importance of technological innovations in securing property rights, which in turn fostered economic development in agricultural sectors. This perspective underscores the role of institutions in addressing information asymmetries and principal-agent problems, thereby facilitating economic transactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also notes that the efficiency approach can sometimes obscure the distributional conflicts that underpin these institutions, suggesting that the focus on efficiency may not capture the full complexity of institutional dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This qualification implies that while secure property rights and efficient institutions can drive economic development, the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts must also be considered.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) argue that the dynamics of technological progress and social change are pivotal in driving economic growth. The catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by the interplay between technological opportunities and the growth of the capital stock. Technological progress not only facilitates modernization but also reduces the relative price of capital goods, thereby accelerating the growth of the capital-labor ratio. This mechanism is further amplified by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which Abramovitz (1986, p.4) notes enhances productivity and aggregate output. The role of institutions in facilitating this technological progress and social change is less directly addressed. Temin (2002, p.4; 2002, p.3) highlights that the post-WWII Golden Age in Europe was characterized by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, suggesting that institutional factors indirectly influence growth through labor reallocation. This perspective underscores the importance of considering how institutions shape the broader economic landscape, even if they are not the primary drivers of growth. The interplay between technological progress and social change, mediated by institutional frameworks, is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions are less critical. The process of technological advancement involves the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process is not merely a linear accumulation of knowledge but also depends on the existing technological landscape and the inventors' ability to identify gaps and opportunities. Kuznets further emphasizes that the mass application of technological innovations, which constitutes much of modern economic growth, is closely connected with the further progress of science. This implies that the efficiency of technological progress depends on the ability of societies to harness scientific discoveries for practical use. However, Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, institutions play a supporting role in ensuring that the benefits of technological advancements are sustained over the long run. Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is essential for understanding long-run economic growth, even if technological progress is the primary driver.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements. Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This aligns with Abramovitz's observation that technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor, thereby encouraging rapid growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets further elaborates on this by arguing that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also underscored by North's argument that institutions matter, as they specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The historical evidence from the American Plains shows that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutions are necessary to sustain and amplify the benefits of technological advancements. This underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth, as they provide the framework within which technological progress can be effectively harnessed and sustained.

While there is a consensus that institutions are essential for long-run economic growth, the precise mechanisms by which they operate remain a subject of debate. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.5). Ogilvie provides a counterpoint by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations. This historical comparison settles the disagreement by showing that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their formation and evolution are influenced by a complex interplay of efficiency and distributional factors.
