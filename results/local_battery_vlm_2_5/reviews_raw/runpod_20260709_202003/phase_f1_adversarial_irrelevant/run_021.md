The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives economic development over extended periods. The live disagreement focuses on the extent to which institutions, along with other factors such as geography or culture, determine economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in fostering growth, arguing that institutional frameworks create the conditions necessary for sustained economic development. Another position acknowledges the role of institutions but contends that their impact is contingent on broader historical and contextual factors, including the specific paths taken by different societies. A third position focuses on the efficiency of institutions, suggesting that institutions evolve to address specific economic challenges and that their effectiveness is key to understanding economic growth. The interplay between these positions reveals the complexity of institutional dynamics and their role in economic development. The debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic growth over the long term. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, focusing on how institutions enforce rules and facilitate change. Institutions complement reputation-building strategies by improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism, especially in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve its ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. The institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution illustrate this logic, showing how political institutions can support economic growth by ensuring the security of property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.” Austin (2008, p.11) reports, “I argue elsewhere that the speciﬁc reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are most plausibly understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including speciﬁc aspects of the natural environment.”

North and Weingast, alongside North, argue that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties and fostering long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They describe how the Glorious Revolution led to institutional changes that improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6) complements this by explaining that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling transaction costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability is crucial because it allows individuals in complex interdependent situations to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they have no personal knowledge or reciprocal exchange relationships (North 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that these institutions complement reputation-building and associated punishment strategies by improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism (North and Weingast 1989, p.7).

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional framework established during colonial rule was often designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional design was influenced by the mortality rates faced by Europeans in these colonies. High mortality rates in regions like Latin America and Africa led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as Europeans were reluctant to settle and invest in these areas. Lower mortality rates in regions like North America and Australia encouraged European settlement and the development of institutions that promoted economic growth. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The correlation between mortality rates, European settlements, and the establishment of early institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on long-term economic performance. Acemoglu et al. show that these early institutions continue to shape current economic outcomes, demonstrating the persistent influence of historical institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The role of institutions in economic growth is complex. Acemoglu et al. note that while institutions are crucial, they do not operate in isolation. The efficacy of institutions is influenced by various factors, including the political power dynamics and the economic incentives of the ruling elites. These dynamics can either reinforce or undermine the positive effects of institutions on economic growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Austin 2008, p.11) examine historical economic divergences and the role of various institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in labor productivity and the economic structures of the two regions. In particular, they show that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector. This scarcity was not inconsistent with a higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population, which may be taken as evidence of higher real per capita income. Bryant (2006, p.29) qualifies this divergence by showing that the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labor for proto-industrial and factory employments, which was not paralleled in China. This structural differentiation between sectors in Europe fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. The demographic realities of mounting land scarcity and a surfeit of cheap labor in China decisively governed all economic considerations, even to the point of resisting the spread of labor-saving technologies and the coordinating concentration of employment in town workshops and factories. This suggests that while institutional factors played a significant role in historical economic divergences, the specific mechanisms and conditions under which they operated varied across regions.

These technological advancements, however, are not isolated from institutional influences, as they often require supportive policies and infrastructure. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) and Whatley (2018, p.2) (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Whatley 2018, p.2) both demonstrate that technological advancements can significantly enhance economic productivity and development. For example, Hornbeck (2010, p.34) shows that the adoption of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains allowed farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, thereby increasing agricultural productivity by 23.4% over a decade. This technological innovation enabled farmers to allocate a larger share of their land to crops, further boosting productivity. Similarly, Whatley (2018, p.2) argues that gunpowder imports increased slave exports by enhancing the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving. This technological influx supported the gun-slave cycle hypothesis, illustrating how external technological factors can drive economic changes. The specific mechanisms through which these technological advancements influence long-run economic growth vary. Whatley (2018, p.2) adds that the economic development within slave trading networks was complex, involving intermediate imports like iron and steel, which could have supported local blacksmiths. This highlights the interconnected nature of technological advancements and their broader economic implications. These studies collectively underscore the multifaceted role of technology in economic development, showing that while technological advancements can drive significant economic changes, their impact is mediated by local conditions and broader economic networks. The role of institutions in facilitating or hindering these technological advancements remains an open question.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.3) share that the literature on African economic development highlights diverse historical factors, including slavery, colonial policies, and gender roles. The role of institutions, as understood by Hopkins, is pivotal in shaping the incentives for investment, thereby fostering economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Nunn identifies the slave trades as a significant impediment to economic development, highlighting that the production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping had negative impacts on subsequent development (Nunn 2008, p.3). This historical trauma has long-lasting institutional effects. The institutional frameworks established during these periods often perpetuated economic disparities, as seen in the gendered impact of production processes and the conditions that facilitated production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.18).

These historical factors are interconnected, as the institutions that emerged from colonial policies and slavery often reinforced gender inequalities. Women's control over agricultural production and allocation was increasingly limited to specific tasks, while men pre-empted women's allocation rights over crops grown for cash value (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor had profound implications for economic development, as it limited women's economic agency and contribution to growth. The historical impact of slavery and colonial policies on institutions involves economic structures, social norms, and gender roles that continue to influence economic outcomes today.

Sokoloff and Engerman focus on how institutions in pre-industrial societies influenced inequality and specific economic structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). They argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework was deeply interdependent with other economic and social structures. This interdependency of institutions within a wider framework suggests that the efficiency of individual institutions cannot be evaluated in isolation. Ogilvie further emphasizes that the interdependency of institutions means that changes in one area can have cascading effects on the entire economic and social system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is crucial because it underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic development. The downstream implication is that the efficiency of institutions in pre-industrial societies involved economic outcomes and the social and political structures that supported them. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions influenced long-term growth remain less clear.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the evolution of political and economic frameworks that support secure property rights and enforce market rules. North and Weingast show that the political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial, as they govern the rules of economic exchange and the enforcement mechanisms that ensure these rules are followed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is reinforced by North, who argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are fundamental for economic development. The efficiency of institutions in pre-industrial societies thus extends beyond economic outcomes to encompass the broader social and political structures that support them. The institutional frameworks that North and Weingast and North highlight create a stable environment where economic actors can confidently engage in transactions. This stability is crucial for long-term economic growth, as it allows for the development of complex social frameworks that can reduce uncertainties and support increasing specialization and division of labor.

The interdependency of institutions means that changes in one area can have cascading effects on the entire economic and social system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency is evident in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe, where institutions like serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild played significant roles. Ogilvie argues that these institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Broadberry and Gupta further illustrate this interdependency by showing how the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in institutional frameworks and productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as Acemoglu et al. show, were pivotal in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These changes highlight the role of institutions in determining economic outcomes, as they can either foster growth or hinder it depending on their design and enforcement. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions influenced long-term growth involve the creation of stable political and economic environments that support secure property rights and enforce market rules. The historical evidence from colonial institutions and pre-industrial Europe shows that institutional frameworks that support economic growth are those that can reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. The precise measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic growth can be seen in the long-term economic trajectories of former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal.
