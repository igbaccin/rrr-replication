The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve economically over time. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily efficient responses to economic needs or whether they are shaped by broader social and political dynamics. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions evolve to reduce transaction costs and enhance economic efficiency, thereby facilitating growth. On the other hand, other scholars contend that distributional conflicts and political dynamics play a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. This debate underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions function within broader social and political contexts. Institutions do not operate in isolation; their impact on economic growth is intertwined with technological progress and the dynamics of social change. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Abramovitz (1986, p.4) reports, “It needs extension and qualification.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing property rights and public choice are critical for long-run economic growth. They assert that the evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining the state's ex post behavior and improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). By ensuring that property rights were secure and enforceable, these institutions created an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. North (1989, p.6) also notes that the neoclassical model, focusing on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is insufficient for explaining economic growth (North 1989, p.5). This suggests that while secure property rights are essential, they are not the sole determinant of economic growth. The broader institutional context, including the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement, plays a crucial role. This underscores the importance of a comprehensive institutional framework that supports not only property rights but also the overall economic environment.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts drove the formation of key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They observe that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over the distribution of resources. These institutions were tools used by dominant groups to maintain their power and control over resources. Domar (1970, p.6) adds that the emergence of slavery or serfdom was influenced by the land-to-labor ratio. When labor was scarce and land was abundant, these institutions provided a way to secure labor and maintain production. The efficiency of these institutions was not the primary factor in their establishment. Instead, they were a response to the need to control labor in a context of distributional conflict. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone drive long-run economic growth. The implications of this view are significant for understanding the role of institutions in economic development. It suggests that the distributional conflicts and power dynamics that shape institutions may be as important as their efficiency in promoting economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) argue that the dynamics of technological advance and labor allocation are pivotal drivers of economic growth. The process of technological catch-up involves a complex interplay of factors. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights that the opportunity for rapid technological progress encourages the growth of the capital stock. This growth is driven by the returns to modernization and the reduction in the price of capital goods relative to labor, which in turn accelerates the capital-labor ratio. This dynamic is further complicated by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress that accompanies productivity growth. The implication is that backwardness can present unique opportunities for modernization, both in embodied and disembodied technology. This perspective suggests that the disequilibrium in labor allocation can be a significant factor in economic growth, independent of institutional frameworks. However, Temin (2002, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the business cycle can also produce rapid growth paths over shorter time spans, indicating that while technological dynamics and labor allocation are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth. The interplay of these factors suggests that economic growth is a multifaceted process influenced by various dynamics beyond institutional efficiency.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) both show that technological advances in property protection mechanisms can drive agricultural development. This technological improvement allowed farmers to secure their land and invest in agriculture with greater confidence, leading to increased productivity and economic growth. The direct technological effects of barbed wire, however, were not the sole drivers of this development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) highlights that the primary impact of barbed wire was its role in protecting farmers' investments from external threats, such as livestock damage, rather than its direct technological improvements (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This finding is supported by Pamuk (2007, p.22), who emphasises that the substitution of land and capital for labour, driven by increased wages relative to rents, was a key factor in long-term structural changes in Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This substitution process, facilitated by technological innovations, led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This interplay suggests that while technological advancements in property protection can drive agricultural development, the broader institutional context plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) both argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr posits that technological progress depends on the knowledge of existing techniques, often involving "recombinant" invention where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel ones. This process is further facilitated by "analogical" thinking, where inventors consciously or subconsciously adapt known ideas to create something new. Kuznets echoes this view, noting that technological innovations are closely tied to the specific needs of the countries in which they originate. This localised innovation suggests that technological progress is deeply intertwined with local contexts, including institutional frameworks. However, Mokyr acknowledges that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains from technological advancements. This qualification underscores the importance of institutions in complementing technological progress, even if the primary driver of growth is technological. Kuznets further elaborates that the mass application of technological innovations provides a positive feedback loop, driving further progress in science and technology. This dynamic highlights the interconnected nature of technological progress and institutional frameworks, where each reinforces the other to sustain long-term economic growth. This interplay suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the broader institutional context plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This relationship is rooted in the differential impact of mortality rates on colonial settlement patterns and the resulting institutional frameworks. These extractive institutions persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on the economic performance of these colonies. The mechanism through which mortality rates influenced institutional outcomes is clear: where European settlers faced high mortality, they established institutions that prioritized short-term extraction over long-term development. This extractive institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and limited economic opportunities, hindered the economic progress of these colonies. The downstream implication of this dynamic is that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today. For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that institutional differences among countries colonized by Europeans are a key factor in explaining current income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This finding underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, highlighting the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks in assessing contemporary economic performance.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with historical legacies playing a crucial part in contemporary economic performance. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights enforcement, as shown by North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5). This institutional framework is essential for sustaining economic improvement, as Mokyr (2005, p.54; 2005, p.12) demonstrates. Without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the benefits of economic growth can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets (1973, p.10; 1973, p.4) further underscores this point by highlighting how technological innovations, particularly those driven by scientific progress, require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technology and institutions is critical for long-term economic development.

The catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) shows that the same technological opportunity that permits rapid progress by modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock. This process is further complicated by the need for institutional frameworks that support the efficient use of new technologies. Ogilvie (2007, p.7; 2007, p.6) adds that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the social and political context in which economic institutions operate.

The enduring influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the institutional legacies of colonialism. These legacies continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today. For instance, Hornbeck (2010, p.30; 2010, p.4) shows that technological changes, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This example illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of new technologies. The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The remaining disagreement is whether institutions primarily facilitate efficiency or are shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Historical evidence from the American Plains settles this disagreement, showing that property rights protection and the reduction of externalities from livestock damage were critical for agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
