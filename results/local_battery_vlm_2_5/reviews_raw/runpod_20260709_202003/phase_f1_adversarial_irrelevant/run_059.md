The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of different nations and the broader dynamics of development. At stake is the very nature of economic progress and the factors that sustain it. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules and norms that govern behavior, are widely recognized as crucial, but their specific mechanisms and effectiveness are subject to debate. One key position posits that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity. This perspective emphasizes the efficiency gains that result from well-functioning institutions. Another position, however, argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and political power dynamics, suggesting that their role in economic growth is more complex and often driven by the interests of powerful groups. The tension between these views highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions influence economic outcomes over the long run. This debate is critical for both historical analysis and contemporary policy, as it informs how societies can best structure their institutions to promote sustained economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.10; 1973, p.4) and North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.5) both address the role of institutions in economic growth, but their approaches diverge significantly in their emphasis on technological progress and transaction costs, respectively.

The first stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10; 1973, p.4) argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Institutions play a crucial role in adapting to and leveraging technological changes, ensuring that new technologies are effectively integrated into the economy. Together, these perspectives underscore the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to technological advancement and efficient economic activity. This stream of scholarship suggests that institutions are not merely passive frameworks but active agents that shape the trajectory of economic development by enabling technological progress and reducing barriers to economic activity. Mokyr (2005, p.54; 2005, p.12) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both address the role of institutions in economic growth, but their focus on technological progress and social change, respectively, complements the emphasis on transaction costs and institutional efficiency.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that political institutions play a crucial role in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights. They posit that the development of political institutions that provide efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is vital for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that as economies grow, the increasing specialization and division of labor lead to a higher volume of transactions, making the efficient functioning of these institutions even more critical (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further demonstrate that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of market exchanges by providing constraints where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is particularly evident in the evolution of capital markets, where secure property rights and effective enforcement have been pivotal in driving economic development. This underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of economic growth that integrates the role of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped pre-industrial economic structures like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie's assertion that distributional conflicts are more important than efficiency in shaping economic institutions is supported by Domar's analysis of slavery and serfdom. Domar shows that slavery emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, which aligns with Ogilvie's broader claim about the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. This implies that the efficiency of institutions, such as guilds and serfdom, is less important than the power dynamics between groups. However, Domar also notes that the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor is not straightforward, as it depends on various factors including technological progress and capital use (Domar 1970, p.6). This qualification suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the efficiency of institutions cannot be entirely disregarded. The efficiency of institutions may still play a role in determining their persistence or replacement over time.

The literature on complex factors in economic growth and catch-up processes highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, emphasizing technological advance, social change, and macroeconomic conditions (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4). This technological opportunity permits rapid progress by modernization, encouraging rapid growth of the capital stock partly because of the returns to modernization itself, and partly because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor. This mechanism is not merely about technological diffusion but also about the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) further notes that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied, as well as in embodied, technology. This implies that technological progress and social change are not just about catching up but also about leveraging backwardness as an opportunity for modernization. Temin (2002, p.4) qualifies this view by suggesting that unusually rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. The business cycle can produce a path like this with a time span of a year or two. This suggests that while technological advance and social change are crucial, macroeconomic conditions and labor allocation play a significant role in economic growth and catch-up processes. This highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between technological advance, social change, and macroeconomic conditions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) extend this analysis by showing that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism can have a profound impact on the long-run economic growth of former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these colonies. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their control and extract resources, often at the expense of local economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional framework imposed by colonial powers was a critical determinant of economic outcomes. These institutions, which were extractive by design, significantly hindered the economic growth of the colonies. By exploiting differences in European mortality rates, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.2) demonstrate that the type of institutions established in the colonies was heavily influenced by the settler mortality rates. Higher mortality rates led to the establishment of more extractive institutions, which in turn had lasting effects on the economic performance of these regions. These extractive institutions persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on the economic development of the colonies. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies on long-run economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that economic growth is primarily driven by advancing technology and useful knowledge, not by institutions. Together, they highlight that technological progress is essential for economic growth, and that institutions alone are insufficient to sustain economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.4) elaborates on this by explaining that mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with the further progress of science, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for additional technological advances. Mokyr (2005, p.12) explains that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use. This knowledge allows inventors to transform existing ideas into something novel, which can lead to significant technological advancements. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, institutions play a supportive role in ensuring the sustainability of these advancements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they create the framework within which technological progress can be sustained. Institutions facilitate the dissemination and application of knowledge, which is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.12; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This is evident in how technological progress often depends on the knowledge of existing techniques, allowing inventors to transform ideas into novel advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Institutions also play a crucial role in mitigating coordination failures and rectifying policies, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, the impact of institutions extends beyond mere sustainability; they are integral to the very process of economic growth. Institutions specify and enforce property rights, reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for the efficient use of innovations, as it provides the necessary conditions for technological progress to translate into economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Kuznets (1973, p.10) and North (1989, p.6) both show that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements would not lead to sustained economic improvement. Institutions matter because they shape the environment in which economic activities occur, influencing the efficiency of resource allocation and the incentives for innovation (North 1989, p.6). This is shown by the fact that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of existing techniques, allowing inventors to transform ideas into novel advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Institutions also play a crucial role in mitigating coordination failures and rectifying policies, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The evidence shows that institutions are not just supportive but fundamental to long-run economic growth. The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the fact that they are shaped by politically powerful groups, who may design them to maximize rents rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the case of former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions shows that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutions designed to maximize rents can hinder long-run economic growth, even in the presence of technological progress. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus both supportive and fundamental, creating the conditions necessary for technological progress to drive sustained economic improvement.
