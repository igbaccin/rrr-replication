The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in the field of economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies can transition from stagnation to sustained economic progress. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions create the necessary conditions for economic growth by establishing secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. Another position contends that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geography, cultural traits, and historical contingencies are equally, if not more, important. The interplay between these positions highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. The causal role of institutions in economic growth is evident in the historical context of Europe, where the evolution of political and economic institutions significantly impacted economic development. North and Weingast argue that the political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This period saw the development of institutions that ensured credible commitments, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize the significance of credible commitments, institutions complement reputation-building by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (Author, Year, p.N). This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange, thereby fostering economic growth. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.6) explains that as economies grow and specialization increases, the complexity of transactions necessitates reliable institutional frameworks to reduce uncertainties. This institutional reliability is essential for maintaining confidence in economic exchanges, even among strangers. These institutions do not merely substitute for reputation-building mechanisms; they complement them by providing constraints that prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange, thereby fostering economic growth. Furthermore, the establishment of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms enabled individuals to engage in complex interdependent economic activities with confidence in remote outcomes (North 1989, p.2). This institutional evolution was crucial for England's economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for market exchanges and facilitated the development of economic institutions.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Politically powerful groups shaped these institutions to maximize rents for European colonists, promoting long-run growth was not their goal. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions established in their colonies. When mortality rates were high, European colonizers tended to implement extractive institutions that facilitated the exploitation of local resources and labor, thereby hindering economic development. These institutions persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on the economic performance of the colonies. When mortality rates were low, European colonizers were more likely to establish institutions that protected property rights and encouraged economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that there is a high correlation between mortality rates faced by soldiers, bishops, and sailors in the colonies and European settlements; between European settlements and early measures of institutions; and between early institutions and institutions today. This pattern of institutional development had a profound influence on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with those that received growth-enhancing institutions experiencing higher levels of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also observe that the impact of institutions on economic growth varies across colonies. The specific mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth can vary depending on the historical, political, and social context of each colony. For example, in some colonies, the presence of extractive institutions may have been mitigated by other factors, such as the availability of natural resources or the presence of a strong civil society. These findings highlight the importance of considering the broader institutional environment when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are a critical driver for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, efficiency is not the primary concern. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argue that these institutions were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over resources and power. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is through the formation of guilds. Ogilvie details how merchant guilds in the early medieval period were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement, functioning as "club goods" that benefited their members. As these guilds evolved, they became more about maintaining the power and privileges of their elite members, efficiency became less of a focus. This shift illustrates how institutional changes can be driven by the desire to maintain or enhance the economic and political power of certain groups, efficiency concerns are not the primary driver. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this analysis to New World societies, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can lead to institutions that perpetuate economic disparities. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the broader social and political context when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13) both find that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improvements in the standard of living. Komlos (1998, p.8) further elaborates on this disconnect by arguing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined. This was due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment, which resulted in a divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups. This biological decline, despite economic growth, indicates that the benefits of economic expansion did not trickle down evenly across society. This suggests that the relationship between economic growth and living standards is complex and mediated by factors such as income distribution and nutritional status.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) share that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and related arguments about economic divergence do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth but instead focus on historical contexts and specific economic conditions. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that the economic fortunes of regions have shifted over time, often in ways that are counterintuitive given their initial conditions. Austin (2008, p.11) notes that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa has shown that the region was often characterized by a scarcity of labor relative to cultivable land and capital, which complicates the simple narrative of labor productivity driving economic outcomes. The implication is that economic institutions, while important, are not the sole drivers of long-run growth; instead, specific historical and economic contexts play a significant role. This is further qualified by Bryant (2006, p.29) who argues that the productivity revolution in European agriculture, which released labor for proto-industrial and factory employments, did not have a parallel in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming. This highlights that the institutional frameworks that supported economic growth in Europe were not universally applicable or necessary for growth in other regions. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is nuanced and dependent on the specific historical and economic contexts of different regions.

The literature on African economic development and institutions examines specific historical and social factors that influenced economic outcomes, it does not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). This emphasis on institutions is part of a broader shift in development policy that incorporates governance and allows for the adaptation of institutions to meet present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This historical context is crucial for understanding the specific institutional challenges faced by African economies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, showing that the role of gender in economic development is another critical factor that institutions must address. Nunn (2008, p.3) observes that the production of slaves, which occurred through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping, also had negative impacts on subsequent development, indicating that the institutional frameworks that supported these practices were detrimental to long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.16). This suggests that the institutional legacy of historical practices can have enduring effects on economic outcomes, even as institutions adapt to meet contemporary needs. The historical context of African economic development, shaped by factors such as the slave trades and the contributions of women, underscores the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic activity. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), as institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce contracts facilitate economic exchange and investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) show that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England, which secured property rights and constrained the state's expropriatory powers, was crucial for fostering economic growth. These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. The development of efficient product and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, enabled individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence in remote outcomes (North 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) argues that institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others even when personal knowledge is lacking.

The historical context of African economic development highlights the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.11; 2008, p.1) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Colonial regimes supported African economic enterprise, they had an interest in fostering growth, not simply extracting resources. The kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists, maximizing long-run growth was not their goal. The results provide support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies, and via this channel, account for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical context of African economic development, shaped by factors such as the slave trades and the contributions of women, underscores the complex interplay between institutions and long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the great divergence between Europe and Asia. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23) argue that the great divergence was already well underway before 1800, showing that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector. The price of the nontradable commodity depends negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector. This divergence highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth. The historical context of European economic development, shaped by factors such as the evolution of political institutions and the development of markets, underscores the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are always extractive and designed to maximize rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), or whether they can also foster growth by securing property rights and enforcing contracts (North 1989, p.2). The historical evidence of African economic development, shaped by factors such as the slave trades and the contributions of women, settles this disagreement. This evidence shows that institutions can have both extractive and growth-fostering effects, depending on the historical context and the specific institutional arrangements in place.
