The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of inquiry in economic history and development economics. At stake is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic prosperity or merely ancillary factors that support other causes. This debate centers on two main positions: one emphasizing the foundational role of institutions, and the other highlighting the significance of technological progress and efficiency-driven institutional changes. Some argue that institutions matter because they define property rights and enforce contracts, reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. Some scholars argue that technological progress and the efficient use of innovations are the primary engines of growth. The tension lies in whether institutions are the catalysts for growth or if they simply adapt to technological advancements. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if they merely support other causes is central to understanding the mechanisms of economic development. This debate is particularly relevant in the context of modern economic growth, where the interplay between institutions and technology has been pivotal. North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.6) illustrates this point through the English financial revolution, where the development of institutions to facilitate market exchanges led to a significant reduction in costs per transaction and a growing proportion of GNP from the transactions sector. This institutional framework, by encouraging specialization and division of labor, multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges and, consequently, economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this argument by emphasizing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, had a significant impact on the economic development of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further qualify this by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The implications of these findings suggest that the long-term economic trajectory of nations is deeply influenced by the institutional frameworks established during their formative periods.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share that secure property rights are crucial for fostering agricultural development and reducing externalities from livestock damage, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights. This technological change significantly enhanced agricultural development by reducing externalities from livestock damage. Farmers could now more effectively control their land, preventing livestock from encroaching on their property. This reduced the need for constant vigilance and allowed farmers to invest more in their land, leading to increased productivity. The ability to protect property rights also incentivized farmers to adopt more sustainable practices, further enhancing agricultural development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) notes that the effects of barbed wire are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle, with the latter being more significant (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). North and Weingast emphasize that secure property rights foster economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and innovation. This suggests that the long-term economic trajectory of nations is deeply influenced by the institutional frameworks that protect property rights, as these frameworks encourage investment and innovation by providing a stable environment. North and Weingast also point out that these institutions complement reputation-building and associated punishment strategies (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This implies that while secure property rights are essential, they must be supported by a robust institutional framework that includes mechanisms for enforcing these rights and maintaining the state's commitment to them.

The accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both assert, is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Mokyr highlights that technological progress is often the result of recombining existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process is not linear; it requires a continuous feedback loop where new knowledge builds upon existing innovations. Kuznets further elaborates that the mass application of technological innovations is closely linked to scientific progress, creating a positive feedback loop that drives economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological advancements and scientific discoveries underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where each new innovation builds upon a foundation of accumulated knowledge. This implies that while technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, they must be supported by a robust institutional framework that can adapt to and integrate these new technologies effectively.

Ogilvie and Domar both examine specific historical institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, but do not directly address how these institutions relate to long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She critiques the predominant view that institutions like merchant guilds and craft guilds were efficient solutions to economic problems, instead suggesting that these institutions often served to entrench the interests of powerful groups. This perspective is supported by Domar, who posits that serfdom or slavery emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, highlighting the role of economic conditions in shaping institutional arrangements. The focus on distributional conflicts and economic conditions reveals that institutional arrangements are deeply influenced by the power dynamics and economic contexts of the time. Domar also notes that technological progress and capital accumulation can alter the profitability of slave labor, suggesting that changes in technology and capital use can influence the persistence of institutions like serfdom or slavery. This implies that while distributional conflicts and economic conditions are crucial in shaping institutions, technological advancements can also play a significant role in their evolution. The interplay between distributional conflicts, economic conditions, and technological progress underscores the complex nature of institutional development in pre-industrial Europe.

Institutional evolution is a dynamic process driven by both economic and technological factors. Mokyr shows that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets agrees that economic growth is driven by advancing technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This relationship between technological progress and economic growth highlights the significance of institutions in facilitating and sustaining economic improvements. Mokyr argues that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which can be hindered by coordination failures (Mokyr 2005, p.12). These coordination failures can be rectified through institutional progress, which complements technological advancements and ensures the sustainability of economic improvements. Kuznets echoes this by noting that technological innovations, often based on recent scientific discoveries, provide positive feedback that drives further progress (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological progress and institutional development underscores the complex nature of institutional evolution in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the neoclassical model's focus on population and savings as principal determinants is misleading (North 1989, p.5). North further explains that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth. Ogilvie provides a complementary perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This suggests that institutions are shaped by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. The interplay between efficiency and distributional conflicts in shaping institutions highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of technological innovations on agricultural development. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This technological innovation had direct and indirect effects on agricultural productivity, demonstrating the importance of institutions in facilitating the adoption and impact of new technologies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The shared finding is that institutions are crucial for economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5; Kuznets 1973, p.4). The remaining disagreement is how institutions are shaped, with Ogilvie arguing that distributional conflicts are key (Ogilvie 2007, p.7) and Acemoglu et al. emphasizing the role of colonial institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their impact on economic outcomes in former European colonies provides a clear measurement of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.
