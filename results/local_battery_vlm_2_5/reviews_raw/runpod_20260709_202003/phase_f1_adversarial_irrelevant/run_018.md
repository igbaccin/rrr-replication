The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory, as it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity and development. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained growth, and the mechanisms that underpin this transformation. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as technological progress or resource availability, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity. Others contend that technological advancements, often spurred by exogenous factors, are the true engines of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary or supportive role. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote economic development, as it informs the design of policies aimed at fostering growth. One stream of scholarship underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, highlighting how they mitigate transaction costs and enhance economic efficiency. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.6) contends that institutions mitigate transaction costs and thus enhance economic efficiency. This is because political institutions that specify and enforce property rights should lower costs per transaction, thereby facilitating market exchanges. As a result, the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. This commitment to rules improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, for example, not to expropriate their wealth. The institutions of the Glorious Revolution are considered to have improved the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) clarify that these institutions complement reputation-building and associated punishment strategies. The efficacy of institutions in fostering economic growth is thus tied to their ability to enforce rules and complement reputation mechanisms.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that the institutions established by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced economic outcomes. These institutions were shaped by the initial conditions faced by the settlers, particularly mortality rates, which determined the type of institutions implemented. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.2) argue that these institutions were directly influenced by the settlers' experiences and the political and economic conditions they faced. The implications of this historical institutional framework are profound, as it suggests that the economic disparities observed today in former colonies can be traced back to the institutional legacies of colonialism, which continue to influence economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) both show that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and early America were shaped by the need for secure property rights and the resolution of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that merchant guilds in the tenth and eleventh centuries provided security and contract enforcement as "club goods," suggesting that these institutions were efficient responses to the challenges of early trade. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) similarly demonstrates how barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enhanced agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) qualifies this by noting that craft guilds, for instance, were not always efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. This suggests that while secure property rights are important, the efficiency of economic institutions can also be influenced by other factors, such as the specific economic and social conditions of the time. This highlights the complexity of the relationship between property rights and economic institutions, and how this relationship can vary across different historical contexts.

The dynamics of technological advance and social change, as discussed by Abramovitz (1986, p.4) (Temin 2002, p.4), drive economic growth. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by technological opportunity, which encourages rapid growth of the capital stock. This is partly due to the returns to modernization itself and partly because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor. Temin agrees that the business cycle can produce a path like this with a time span of a year or two, and new growth theory and conditional convergence can produce a history like this with convergence with about 35 years or so to half-way convergence. This rapid technological progress and labor allocation are significant drivers of economic growth, as they facilitate the modernization of both embodied and disembodied technology. Temin qualifies this by noting that the business cycle can produce a path like this with a time span of a year or two. This suggests that while technological progress and labor allocation are crucial for economic growth, the timing and scale of these effects can vary significantly. This highlights the importance of understanding the dynamics of technological advance and social change in shaping long-run economic growth.

These changes, in turn, have reshaped economic structures and influenced the distribution of resources, impacting long-term economic performance. Komlos (1998, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) show that these events triggered substantial economic shifts, including changes in wages and technological innovations. The Black Death, for instance, led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, driven by increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital. This shift spurred substitution of land and capital for labor, with urban guilds playing a crucial role in technological innovations. The relative price of nutrients increased at the onset of modern economic growth, partly due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry. The relative price of food rose in Britain by 66 percent between 1770 and 1795, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption bundles. This had a significant impact on biological well-being, as seen during the Industrial Revolution. Despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This highlights the complex interplay between economic growth and biological well-being, underscoring the need to consider both income gains and nutritional status when assessing the impacts of economic changes.

The growth of useful knowledge and technological advancement are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr and Kuznets both contend that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, with Mokyr emphasizing that technological progress is far less monotonic and irreversible than institutional progress. Mokyr points out that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, and that much invention takes the form of recombining existing techniques. Kuznets, meanwhile, notes that mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with the further progress of science, which in turn is the basis for additional advances in technology. This implies that technological progress is a driver of economic growth and a catalyst for further scientific advancements. In this way, technological progress creates a positive feedback loop, where advances in technology spur further scientific discoveries, which in turn enable additional technological innovations. Kuznets also suggests that the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of less developed countries may not be abundant, highlighting the need for technological progress to be tailored to the specific needs of different regions. This underscores the importance of technological progress in driving economic growth, as it enables the efficient use of innovations tailored to the specific needs of different regions. This suggests that technological progress is a driver of economic growth and a key factor in the efficient use of innovations.

Institutional frameworks play a crucial role in facilitating technological progress and economic growth. Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr describes, is essential for sustaining economic improvement, as technological progress alone is insufficient to ensure long-term growth. Kuznets agrees, emphasizing that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations. This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54)(Kuznets 1973, p.4) underscores the necessity of institutions in managing the complexities of technological progress and ensuring its benefits are fully realized. The role of institutions is not limited to facilitating technological progress; they also help in managing the economic and social changes that accompany it. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they define property rights and enforce contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework is essential for the efficient use of technological innovations and the growth of the economy (North 1989, p.6). The interplay between technological progress and institutions is complex, and the efficient use of innovations requires a supportive institutional environment. Institutions provide the necessary conditions for technological progress to drive economic growth, as they facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies.

The relationship between institutions and technological progress is not always straightforward. Abramovitz notes that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This suggests that institutions play a role in shaping the economic growth process, but they are not the sole determinant. The interaction between institutions and technological progress is dynamic, and the efficient use of innovations requires a supportive institutional environment that can adapt to changing circumstances. Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions are not always efficient, and their effectiveness can vary depending on the specific historical and social context. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise nature of this relationship remains a subject of debate.
