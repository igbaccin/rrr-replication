The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that technological progress and other factors are more influential. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that govern behavior and interactions within a society. The debate hinges on whether institutions create the conditions necessary for sustained economic growth or whether they merely facilitate the impact of other drivers, such as technological innovation and resource allocation. This question is crucial for policymakers and economists alike, as it informs strategies for fostering economic development and addressing disparities in global wealth. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with some scholars emphasizing the role of institutions in enabling technological progress and others highlighting how institutions themselves can be shaped by the dynamics of economic and social change. The question of whether institutions are the cause or the effect of economic growth remains unresolved. Institutions matter in economic growth, but their role is not straightforward (North 1989, p.6; Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They identify that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange, the institutions governing how these rules are enforced, and the institutions governing how these rules may be changed. The evolution of political institutions is central to the development of markets as they specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England demonstrate that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. These institutions complement reputation-building and associated punishment strategies. The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrate how appropriately chosen institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. Thus, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). The technology of barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to demarcate their land more effectively, thereby reducing the incidence of trespassing livestock and associated damages. This technological innovation was particularly impactful in regions where timber was scarce, as it provided a cost-effective solution for land demarcation. Furthermore, the increased protection of property rights facilitated by barbed wire encouraged farmers to invest more in their land, leading to enhanced agricultural productivity. The substitution of land and capital for labor, as evidenced by the increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital, highlights the broader structural changes that accompanied technological advancements. This dynamic is reflected in the historical context of Europe, where urban guilds played a crucial role in technological innovations, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic development. The introduction of new technologies, such as firearms, further illustrates how capital-intensive industries emerged during this period, driving economic growth and structural changes. This pattern of technological innovation and institutional adaptation is crucial for understanding how property rights and structural changes can shape long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. posit that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The nature of these institutions was fundamentally shaped by the settlers' expectations of mortality rates, which in turn determined the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to the implementation of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for European colonists. This institutional framework persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita in these regions. This dynamic is further illustrated by the historical context of European settlements, where the correlation between mortality rates faced by European settlers and the establishment of extractive institutions is evident. The persistent nature of these institutions highlights how initial conditions set by colonial powers can have enduring effects on economic development. This legacy is evident in the strong correlation between early colonial institutions and current economic performance, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth. The enduring effects of these institutions suggest that the path dependency of economic development is deeply rooted in the historical context of colonial rule.

The catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, shared by Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8). These authors argue that the convergence of economic growth is contingent on various factors. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) notes that technological opportunities can accelerate the growth of the capital stock, which in turn reduces the technological age relative to chronological age (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic is further complicated by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which can significantly impact productivity growth. Komlos (1998, p.8) adds that the relative price of nutrients increased during the Industrial Revolution, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns in response to higher nutrient prices (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift in consumption patterns highlights the intricate relationship between technological change and economic behavior. However, Komlos (1998, p.8) also points out that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, depending on factors such as technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply (Komlos 1998, p.12). This qualification underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where technological advances and social changes interact in complex ways. The interplay between technological progress and economic behavior suggests that the path to economic convergence is fraught with challenges that go beyond institutional frameworks.

Technological advancement and knowledge growth are fundamental drivers of economic growth, suggesting that institutions may not be as critical as other factors in the long run (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective is evident in Mokyr's (2005, p.54) work, which highlights that technological progress relies heavily on the accumulation and recombination of existing knowledge. For example, Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that much invention during the Industrial Revolution involved recombining existing techniques, a process that often depends on "analogical" thinking. This mechanism suggests that the growth of useful knowledge involves the continuous integration and adaptation of existing knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.10) further underscores this point, arguing that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs and that the mass application of these innovations is closely connected with the progress of science. This interplay between technological progress and economic needs implies that the sustainability of economic improvement is closely tied to the relentless pursuit of new knowledge and its efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective suggests that although technological advancement is a primary driver of economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own and requires institutional support to fully realize its potential.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary frameworks that support and sustain technological advancements. The foundational role of institutions is evident in the work of Kuznets and Mokyr, both of whom highlight the interplay between technological progress and institutional development. Kuznets stresses that technological innovations drive economic growth, but this process requires institutional adjustments to ensure efficient use of these innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr echoes this perspective, noting that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional progress to achieve sustainable economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Mokyr 2005, p.12). This shared finding indicates that institutions actively contribute to economic growth, facilitating the effective application of new knowledge and technologies.

The dynamic relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Abramovitz and North. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). North, meanwhile, contends that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This synthesis shows that institutions are critical in managing transaction costs and specifying property rights, which are essential for fostering economic development. The historical introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, as documented by Hornbeck, provides a clear example of how institutions can enhance economic growth by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This empirical evidence underscores the practical impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective highlights how institutions can be designed to maximize rents for specific groups. The empirical comparison of former colonies shows that institutions play a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization. The historical evidence of colonial institutions settles the disagreement between neoclassical theories and institutional perspectives, demonstrating that institutions actively contribute to economic growth.
