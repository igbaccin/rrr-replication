The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained growth, and what factors sustain that growth over centuries. One live disagreement centers on the primacy of technological progress versus institutional frameworks. Some scholars emphasize technological innovation as the driving force, while others highlight the crucial role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining economic growth. A related debate concerns the efficiency of pre-industrial institutions, with some arguing that these institutions were primarily driven by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. The interplay between technology and institutions, and the underlying mechanisms that drive economic growth, remain central to this discourse. Institutions, whether efficient or driven by conflict, play a significant role in the economic development of societies. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the development of political institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is pivotal for economic growth. The evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England, for instance, underpinned the growth of capital markets and economic development. This evolution was driven by the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange, which in turn fostered secure property rights. The role of institutions in reducing transaction costs is evident in North's discussion of transaction costs in a growing economy. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) also highlight that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies. This nuance suggests that while institutions are essential, they must work in conjunction with other mechanisms to ensure effective enforcement and economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers, significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional designs implemented by European colonizers were often extractive, tailored to maximize the rents for the colonizers rather than to foster long-run economic growth. These extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of the colonies. This institutional legacy is evident in the high correlation between historical mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies, the presence of European settlements, and the establishment of early institutions. These early institutions, in turn, have a strong correlation with contemporary institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional framework set during the colonial period has had a profound and lasting effect on the economic performance of these regions. However, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions influence economic growth are complex and multifaceted, involving not only direct economic policies but also the broader social and political structures they engender. This complexity is further underscored by the fact that institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonizers often do not align with policies that promote long-term economic development. This misalignment can lead to persistent economic underperformance in regions where extractive institutions were entrenched during the colonial era.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that these institutions were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.30). She contends that the efficiency approach to institutions has been overemphasized, leading to a misinterpretation of various premodern institutions as efficient solutions to transaction obstacles. This perspective is supported by Hornbeck, who demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This underscores how property rights and economic institutions can be shaped by technological advancements that directly impact economic outcomes. However, the efficiency of these institutions is not always the primary driver; instead, they often arise from the need to manage conflicts and distribute resources. Ogilvie's argument extends to the idea that distributional conflicts can lead to the creation of institutions that, while not necessarily efficient, serve to mitigate these conflicts and maintain social order. This dynamic is evident in the evolution of property rights and economic institutions, where the focus is on managing conflicts rather than maximizing efficiency. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize economic outcomes, highlighting the role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) propose that technological advance and social change are essential for long-run economic growth. They argue that technological progress is a driver that influences the growth of the capital stock and productivity. The technological opportunity that permits rapid progress through modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock. This growth is driven by the returns to modernization itself and the reduction in the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor. Technological progress thus reduces the technological age towards chronological age, leading to a higher rate of rise of the capital-labor ratio. This dynamic is crucial for understanding the complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth. Abramovitz emphasizes that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in both disembodied and embodied technology. This perspective highlights the multifaceted nature of technological advance and its role in shaping long-run economic growth. Moreover, Temin's analysis of Europe's post-WWII Golden Age shows that unusually rapid economic growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium, coupled with technological progress, underscores the importance of social change in economic growth. However, Temin notes that the business cycle can also produce rapid growth paths, albeit over shorter time spans. This qualification suggests that while technological advance and social change are crucial, other factors, such as economic cycles, can also influence growth dynamics. This interplay between technological progress, social change, and economic cycles highlights the complex nature of long-run economic growth.

Economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) both assert that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating this with major inventions from the Industrial Revolution. This underscores that technological progress is not linear but rather driven by the necessity to address particular challenges, which in turn fuels further innovation. Mokyr extends this idea by noting that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking. This implies that the accumulation of knowledge and the ability to integrate it efficiently are crucial for sustained economic growth. However, Mokyr also warns that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that ensure its efficient and equitable application. This interplay between technological progress and institutional support is critical for long-run economic growth. Kuznets's assertion that technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science, in turn the basis for additional advances in technology, implies a cycle of innovation and knowledge accumulation that drives economic growth. This cycle is further supported by Mokyr's observation that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, highlighting the importance of a cumulative and interconnected knowledge base.

Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). North echoes this by emphasizing that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, which are crucial for long-run economic growth. The efficiency of institutions in providing security and contract enforcement is also highlighted by Ogilvie, who notes that merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.7).

The introduction of new technologies, such as barbed wire fencing, significantly enhances agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. Hornbeck shows that barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle, demonstrating the role of institutions in enhancing technological progress (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This technological progress, in turn, drives long-run economic growth by increasing agricultural productivity and facilitating market exchanges. The cumulative knowledge base and institutional support are essential for long-run economic growth, as they enable the efficient use of innovations and facilitate market exchanges. The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutional progress is as monotonic and irreversible as technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of technological progress and institutional progress in the context of the Industrial Revolution, which shows that institutional progress is not as monotonic and irreversible as technological progress.
