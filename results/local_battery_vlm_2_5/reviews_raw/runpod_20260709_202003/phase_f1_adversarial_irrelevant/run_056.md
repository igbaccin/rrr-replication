The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. The stakes are high, as institutions are often seen as malleable and thus potential targets for policy intervention. One side of the debate emphasizes the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights enforcement. The opposing side, however, argues that the efficiency of institutions is often overstated, and that distributional conflicts and other factors play a more significant role in shaping economic outcomes. These differing perspectives highlight the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is often framed through the lens of efficiency. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North's argument is that institutions that specify and enforce property rights efficiently reduce the costs per transaction, thereby promoting economic growth. This perspective is shared by Kuznets, who emphasizes the importance of technological progress and the need for institutional adjustments to efficiently use innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets argues that the mass application of technological innovations, which is a key aspect of modern economic growth, is closely connected with the progress of science. This connection suggests that institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the adoption and use of new technologies, thereby driving economic growth. The efficiency perspective, however, is not without its critics. Ogilvie challenges the notion that pre-industrial European institutions were efficient, arguing instead that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of the period (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie's argument underscores the significance of the political and social context in which institutions function, along with the potential for these institutions to advance the interests of powerful groups and promote economic efficiency.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of these institutions was pivotal in creating a stable environment for economic growth. The interplay between institutions and reputation mechanisms emphasizes the complex nature of economic growth, illustrating how secure property rights and effective enforcement are legal frameworks and social constructs that require ongoing reinforcement. This historical context illustrates how the evolution of institutions can have profound and lasting effects on economic development.

In a similar vein, colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) assert that the institutions established by European colonists were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers and promote long-run growth. This extractive institutional framework was a direct result of the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which often led to the creation of institutions that perpetuated their control and extraction of resources. High mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as the colonizers had less incentive to invest in long-term development and more incentive to exploit resources quickly. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a profound impact on the economic performance of former colonies, shaping their current income per capita. The enduring effects of these colonial institutions highlight the critical role that institutional frameworks play in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

While these dynamics are crucial, Temin (2002, p.4) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4); (Temin 2002, p.4) examine the dynamics of economic growth through various lenses, including technological advance and social change, without explicitly linking these factors to institutional frameworks. Temin (2002, p.4) identifies a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry as a key driver of Europe's post-WWII Golden Age of rapid economic growth. This period was characterized by a rapid shift of labor from traditional, low-productivity sectors to more modern, high-productivity industries. This shift was driven by deeper structural changes in the economy and policy initiatives. The rapid growth of the capital stock, facilitated by technological progress and the consequent reduction in the relative price of capital goods, further accelerated this process. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) underscores this with the observation that technological opportunities permit rapid progress and encourage the growth of the capital stock, thereby reducing the technological age of economies. This dynamic highlights how technological advances and social changes can drive economic growth independently of institutional factors, suggesting that institutional frameworks may not always be the primary determinant of economic performance.

This perspective has qualifications. This complexity implies that while technological progress and labor reallocation are significant drivers of growth, they do not operate in isolation. The interplay between these factors and broader economic conditions can create a more nuanced picture of economic growth dynamics. For instance, the growth of productivity can lead to an increase in aggregate output, broadening the horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. This broader perspective suggests that while institutional frameworks may not be the sole determinant of economic growth, they can influence the broader economic conditions that facilitate or hinder the dynamics of technological advance and social change.

Expanding on this, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30); (Ogilvie 2007, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They present a view of institutions as the product of power struggles and bargaining among different social groups and as solutions to transaction costs. The merchant guilds, for example, were shaped by the distributional conflicts among merchants, artisans, and other interest groups and were inefficient cartels. These guilds provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' to their members, reflecting the power dynamics and bargaining outcomes among these groups. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) similarly shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. The technology of barbed wire fencing directly improved technological efficiency, but its primary impact was on property rights protection. This finding underscores the role of technological change in shaping economic institutions and the broader economic conditions that facilitate or hinder technological advance. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) qualifies this view by noting that the efficiency of institutions changes over time as conditions evolve. This dynamic nature of institutional efficiency suggests that institutions are shaped by the broader economic and social conditions in which they operate and are solutions to transaction costs. This highlights the importance of considering the broader economic conditions and the distributional conflicts that shape institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

This perspective is echoed by Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share the view that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, emphasizing the accumulation of useful knowledge as the main force behind this process. Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that technological advancement is driven by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process relies on the knowledge of existing techniques, which alerts inventors to gaps and opportunities in the current technological landscape and individual genius (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress is closely linked to the needs of the country in which it originates, illustrating how necessity drives invention. This highlights the importance of complementary institutional changes that support and sustain technological advancements, ensuring that the benefits of innovation are not frittered away by inefficiencies or misallocation of resources.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted. Institutions, such as property rights and contract enforcement mechanisms, facilitate economic transactions by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Institutions also support technological progress, the growth of useful knowledge, and the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This is because institutions provide the necessary framework for economic actors to benefit from technological advancements. For instance, technological progress often depends on the knowledge of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which in turn relies on institutional support to prevent rent-seeking behaviors that could erode economic improvements (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.12).

Institutions also play a crucial role in the catch-up process of economic growth. The catch-up process involves technological advancements and the dynamics of social change and institutional adjustments (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This process is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the internal dynamics of technological advance and social change. Institutions facilitate this process by providing the necessary framework for the efficient use of innovations and the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the fact that they provide the necessary framework for the efficient use of innovations and the growth of the capital stock. This is because institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations and the growth of the capital stock, which in turn drives economic growth (North 1989, p.5). For example, the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This shows that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of debate. One point of agreement is that institutions facilitate economic transactions by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Another point of agreement is that institutions support technological progress, the growth of useful knowledge, and the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). The remaining disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions drive economic growth.
