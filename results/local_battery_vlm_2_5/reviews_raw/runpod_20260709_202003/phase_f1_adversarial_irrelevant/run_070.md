The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the economics literature. At stake is the fundamental question of how societies generate and sustain economic development over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutions versus technological progress. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks are pivotal in facilitating technological advancements and ensuring their efficient use. Others contend that technological progress itself drives economic growth, with institutions playing a supporting role. Additionally, there is a rival explanation that focuses on the efficiency of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective suggests that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were designed to address specific economic challenges, rather than being mere responses to technological change. The debate ultimately hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely facilitators of technological progress. This review will explore these positions, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. The literature on technological and social factors in economic growth highlights the central role of dynamics of technological advance and social change in shaping economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This dynamic underscores the importance of knowledge accumulation and the continuous refinement of technological processes in sustaining long-run economic growth. However, Mokyr also points out that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be limited and ultimately eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological and social factors are crucial, they must be supported by robust institutional frameworks to ensure sustained economic improvement.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of innovations, and thus play a pivotal role in economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Kuznets (1973, p.10) notes that innovations are often tailored to the specific needs of the countries in which they originate, implying that the transfer of these innovations to other contexts requires institutional adaptations to ensure their effective use. Their analysis of seventeenth-century England illustrates how political institutions can enhance the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional framework is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also point out that these institutions complement reputation-building strategies. This nuance suggests that while institutional adjustments are critical, they must be integrated with existing social and economic mechanisms to fully realize their benefits.

Hornbeck shows how technological advancements, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, can significantly enhance agricultural development by bolstering property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This technological shift enabled farmers to better protect their land and reduce externalities from livestock damage, thereby fostering economic growth. The mechanism behind this enhancement is the reduction of transaction costs associated with property disputes and the prevention of livestock damage, which in turn encourages investment in agricultural activities. This is evident in the American Plains, where the advent of barbed wire allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity. Pamuk notes that the effectiveness of such technological improvements can be contingent on broader economic and social conditions. For instance, the increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital during the late medieval period led to a substitution of land and capital for labor, a dynamic that influenced technological adoption and economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This suggests that while technological advancements can drive economic growth by enhancing property rights, their impact is modulated by the prevailing economic and social environment, including labor market conditions and the cost of capital.

During the Industrial Revolution, economic growth did not uniformly enhance biological well-being, with insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment undermining health outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.8; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). While technological advancements and increased demand for coal spurred economic growth, the benefits did not trickle down to the broader population (Komlos 1998, p.8; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5). The relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift in consumption patterns, driven by economic growth and technological change, highlights a critical mechanism by which institutions shape long-run economic growth: the distribution of income gains and the accessibility of essential resources. However, the relationship between economic growth and well-being is not deterministic, as evidenced by the decline in the relative price of food in the twentieth century despite rapid population growth (Komlos 1998, p.12). This qualification underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that can mitigate the negative health impacts of economic growth, such as ensuring adequate childhood nourishment and equitable income distribution.

Distributional conflicts are posited as the primary drivers shaping key economic structures in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) shared this posture. They argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) explicitly criticises the efficiency approach to explaining these institutions, noting that various pre-modern institutions have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to transactional obstacles. However, Domar (1970, p.7) posits that the profitability of slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, providing a specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts drive institutional outcomes. This mechanism highlights how economic conditions, particularly the scarcity of labor, can lead to the adoption of institutions like serfdom. Nevertheless, Domar (1970, p.6) qualifies this by noting that the relative profitability of free versus slave labor is not straightforward, depending on various factors including technological progress and capital use. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional outcomes and the need for a nuanced understanding of the distributional conflicts that shape them.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. The mechanism through which this occurs involves the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show a high correlation between European mortality rates in colonies and the subsequent establishment of extractive institutions. High mortality rates led to fewer European settlers, which in turn resulted in the implementation of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over development. This pattern is evident in colonies where European settlers faced high disease risks, leading to the establishment of institutions that facilitated the exploitation of local resources by colonial powers. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these extractive institutions were not designed to encourage economic development but rather to serve the interests of the politically powerful groups, which often meant maximizing short-term gains at the expense of long-term growth. The downstream implication of this mechanism is that the persistence of these extractive institutions has significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by noting that while institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, the specific pathways through which these institutions influenced growth are complex and multifaceted. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with technological progress and institutional frameworks intertwining to drive economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both highlight the significance of technological progress in economic growth, though they differ in their emphasis. Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This technological progress requires institutional progress to complement it, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) extends this idea by noting that technological opportunity permits rapid progress by modernization and encourages the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.10) adds that technological innovations must be efficiently utilized through institutional and ideological adjustments. This shows that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutions must facilitate the efficient use of innovations to drive economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further complicated by the distributional conflicts that arise within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) both acknowledge the importance of institutions in managing these conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that pre-industrial institutions, such as merchant guilds, were often more about managing distributional conflicts rather than mere efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) argue that coal, a key resource, played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that other institutional factors were more critical (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). This indicates that the efficiency of institutions in managing resources and conflicts is a crucial determinant of long-run economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, as discussed by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological improvement, coupled with institutional changes, highlights how property rights and institutional frameworks can drive economic growth by reducing externalities and enhancing productivity.

Institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and managing distributional conflicts. Kuznets (1973, p.10) shows that technological innovations, which constitute much of the distinctive substance of modern economic growth, are closely connected with the further progress of science (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.54) adds that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, highlighting the interconnected nature of technological and institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The precise role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes remains a subject of debate. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) agree that technological progress and institutional frameworks are crucial for economic growth. The specific pathways through which these institutions influence growth are complex and multifaceted (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4). The historical evidence of the American Plains, as discussed by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), shows that technological improvements coupled with institutional changes can drive economic growth by reducing externalities and enhancing productivity. The empirical comparison of technological progress and institutional frameworks in different historical contexts provides the necessary evidence to understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
