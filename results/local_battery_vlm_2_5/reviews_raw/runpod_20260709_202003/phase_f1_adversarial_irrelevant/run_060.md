The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is our understanding of how societies evolve and why some prosper while others stagnate. One live disagreement centers around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective governance in fostering economic growth. Others focus on the impact of institutional changes introduced by colonialism and the role of political factors in underpinning economic development. The tension lies in whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors like geography or by endogenous political processes. The question of how institutions matter in long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the broader debate on the drivers of economic development and the historical paths that lead to prosperity. This question is particularly relevant in understanding the divergence in economic performance between regions and the factors that sustain long-term economic growth. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, along with North, highlight the pivotal role of institutional evolution in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights and facilitating market exchanges. These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints that prevented reneging on agreements, thereby improving the state's ability to maintain its commitments (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North and Weingast note that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights enabled sustained economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, enabling increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The reliability of these institutions was essential for maintaining confidence in remote and impersonal exchanges, which are characteristic of complex economies (North 1989, p.2). North also notes that the evolution of these complex social frameworks requires institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with interdependent situations. This institutional reliability is fundamental for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, as it allows individuals to engage in transactions with those they do not personally know (North 1989, p.2). Thus, the development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms is a critical antecedent for long-term economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in these colonies determined the type of institutions established (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The variation in institutional quality and its effects on economic outcomes highlights the complex interplay between colonial history and contemporary economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are more influential than efficiency considerations in shaping pre-industrial European economic institutions. The authors argue that these institutions are geared toward redistributing resources among elites. Ogilvie highlights the role of merchant guilds, noting that 'merchant guilds of the twelfth to ﬁfteenth centuries as inefﬁcient cartels' were part of a broader institutional framework that served to redistribute wealth and power. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that inequalities in New World societies were reinforced by institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie further emphasizes the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the efficiency of individual institutions is often overstated (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The downstream implications of this institutional framework are complex. On one hand, the distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions may have hindered long-run economic growth by prioritizing elite interests over broader economic development. Ogilvie's argument that institutions are interdependent suggests that the efficiency of individual institutions is often overstated, and that their impact on economic growth may be more nuanced than traditional views.

This complexity is further highlighted by the varied interpretations of historical events and their economic implications. Austin's argument that the Atlantic slave trade's commercial motivation led to the lower exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa, despite prevalent labour scarcity, highlights the need for a disaggregated view of factor endowments and natural environments (Austin 2008, p.11). Bryant's empirical analysis of Europe and China's agricultural productivity shows that the structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors in Europe, coupled with urban population growth, fostered economies of scale and capital accumulation necessary for industrial expansion (Bryant 2006, p.29). These findings suggest that the institutional frameworks supporting economic growth in Europe were historically contingent and not universally optimal. Bryant's analysis also reveals that Chinese peasants were increasingly driven into a crops-only pattern, leading to a reverse substitution of human for animal labour power (Bryant 2006, p.28). This qualification underscores that the institutional frameworks supporting economic growth in Europe were contingent on specific historical and environmental conditions. The complex interplay of economic institutions, factor endowments, and environmental contexts suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is nuanced and context-dependent. Understanding this complexity is crucial for appreciating the varied trajectories of economic development across different regions and periods.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) both focus on the long-term economic development in Europe, but do not explicitly address institutions as a cause. The shared focus on economic development is on the long-term changes in productivity and wages, which are key indicators of economic growth. Van Zanden highlights the new optimism about early modern economic growth, noting that some scholars have argued for significant productivity gains in agriculture and other sectors (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23)  provide a detailed analysis of wage and price dynamics, showing how productivity differences between tradable and non-tradable sectors influenced economic outcomes. The productivity gains in agriculture and the dynamics of wages and prices are mechanisms that can drive long-term economic growth. Van Zanden's work also underscores the complexities and controversies in measuring economic growth during this period, with debates over real wages and productivity estimates. This complexity suggests that while productivity gains and wage dynamics are important, they are part of a larger, more intricate picture of economic development that includes institutional factors. This picture extends to the broader question of the great divergence between Europe and Asia.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the evolution of property rights and political frameworks in seventeenth-century England. North and Weingast demonstrate that the development of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework was essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering economic stability and growth. The role of institutions is further underscored by North, who argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. This institutional reliability allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2).

The significance of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the broader context of the great divergence between Europe and Asia. Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence was already well underway before 1800, with Europe experiencing higher productivity in the tradable goods sector compared to Asia. This productivity differential was a key factor in the economic divergence between the two regions, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This finding is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Both van Zanden and Acemoglu et al. agree that institutions are critical to understanding long-term economic growth. Van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting a more complex picture of economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This complexity is further highlighted by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa is almost unanimous that, until well into the 20th century at least, most of the region most of the time was characterised by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land as well as to capital. This indicates that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are most plausibly understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the detailed reconstruction of the development of rents in the Bassin de Paris (and other parts of France), from which estimates of the growth of productivity can be inferred (van Zanden 1999, p.2).
