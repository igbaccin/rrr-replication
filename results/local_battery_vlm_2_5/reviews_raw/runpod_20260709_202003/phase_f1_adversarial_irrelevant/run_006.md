The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One live disagreement in the literature is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they are merely one of several factors among many. Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity. Others contend that institutions are secondary to technological progress or exogenous factors, such as geographic conditions. Still others propose that institutions reflect distributional conflicts and are shaped by politically powerful groups, thereby influencing economic growth in complex ways. This review will explore these competing perspectives on the role of institutions in economic growth. It will begin with the perspective that institutions are pivotal in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that political institutions play a critical role in economic growth by specifying property rights and ensuring their enforcement. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. They note that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, indicating that political factors underpin economic growth. This encompasses the rules governing economic exchange, the institutions that enforce these rules, and the processes that determine how they can be changed. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) observe that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient. They illustrate this with the example of the Glorious Revolution, where institutional changes improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering economic growth. This highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the factors driving economic development. The role of institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement is a crucial factor in economic growth, as evidenced by the development of capital markets and the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution.

The effectiveness of these institutional changes is further illustrated by the impact of barbed wire fencing on agricultural development, as Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) demonstrate. Barbed wire provided a cost-effective solution to fencing large tracts of land, which was previously impractical due to the scarcity of timber. This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more efficiently, thereby enhancing their ability to invest in and maintain their agricultural endeavors. The protection of property rights facilitated by barbed wire fencing also reduced the incidence of livestock damage, a significant externality that had previously hindered agricultural productivity. This protection encouraged farmers to invest more in their land, leading to increased agricultural output and economic growth. The direct technological effects of barbed wire did not primarily drive these results. Instead, the increased protection from others' cattle played a more significant role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This finding underscores the importance of institutional factors, such as property rights and their enforcement, in shaping long-run economic growth. The ability to secure and protect property rights can have a profound impact on economic development, as seen in the case of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains. This dynamic extends beyond agricultural development, having broader implications for economic growth in various sectors.

This institutional dynamic, which favors elites in unequal societies, is further explored by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.3). This dynamic is evident in the institutional structures that emerged in slave societies, where large plantations and slave populations dominated. The efficiency of these large plantations and the extreme wealth inequality they produced created a system where elites had significant control over resources and power. This control was maintained through government policies that restricted competition and favored the interests of the elite class. This institutional framework, which prioritized the interests of the elite, hindered economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader economic development and innovation. Nunn's analysis (Nunn 2008, p.19) illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets facilitated trade in the Kongo kingdom, highlighting the importance of institutional structures in enabling economic activity. This suggests that the presence of a centralized government could potentially mitigate some of the negative effects of inequality by fostering economic development. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their broader implications for economic growth are areas for further investigation.

Distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie and Domar both show that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were primarily driven by conflicts over the distribution of resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.7)(Domar 1970, p.7). For instance, serfdom emerged as a response to land abundance and labor scarcity. This dynamic is evident in the transition from slavery to serfdom in medieval England and Russia, where the scarcity of labor made serfdom more profitable (Domar 1970, p.6). In this context, efficiency was often a secondary consideration. Domar suggests that the profitability of slave labor can be influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation, which complicates the distributional conflict narrative (Domar 1970, p.6). The downstream implications of this are significant: if distributional conflicts are the primary driver, then institutional reforms aimed at efficiency may not address the underlying issues that shape economic institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) extend this perspective by examining how colonial institutions have influenced the economic trajectories of former European colonies. The institutions established by European colonizers were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for the colonizers. These extractive institutions persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies. The mechanism through which this occurred is that the initial institutional framework set by the colonizers influenced the subsequent development of institutions, which in turn affected economic performance. The downstream implication of this is that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had a lasting impact on the economic development of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) notes that the impact of institutions on economic performance is not straightforward, and that other factors, such as geographic endowments, may also play a role. The persistence of extractive institutions can thus be attributed to the initial conditions set by colonial powers.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both emphasize the centrality of technological progress in economic development, highlighting that the accumulation of useful knowledge and the application of technological innovations are key drivers of growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions. This process involves the broader social and economic context that supports and sustains technological advancement. For instance, technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, and the existence of a supportive institutional framework can amplify the benefits of technological innovations. This suggests that while institutions may not be the primary drivers of economic growth, they play a significant role in facilitating and sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. The interplay between technological progress and institutional support is thus essential for long-term economic growth, as institutions can either enhance or hinder the effective use of technological innovations.
