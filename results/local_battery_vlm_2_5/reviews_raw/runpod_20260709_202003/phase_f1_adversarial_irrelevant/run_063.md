The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. One position emphasises the primacy of technological progress and knowledge accumulation, arguing that institutions merely facilitate or hinder this progress. Another position, however, posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, with technology and knowledge playing a secondary role. A third position argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations, suggesting that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than to promote economic development. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic growth and for designing policies that can foster long-term development. The role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and knowledge accumulation is a key area of contention. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their shared claim is that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights facilitates market exchanges and economic growth. He contends that as economies grow, the transaction sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, necessitating institutions that can manage the increasing complexity of exchanges. This institutional evolution is pivotal for economic growth, as it creates an environment where property rights are secure and transactions are efficient. They emphasize that institutions governing public choice are critical for economic growth, as they ensure that the regime is committed to enforcing rules and maintaining the integrity of property rights. This commitment is crucial for fostering a stable economic environment where investments can be made with confidence. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to integrate with existing social and economic structures, ensuring that they enhance rather than disrupt established norms and practices. Institutions must be designed to complement and reinforce existing mechanisms for maintaining property rights and enforcing commitments.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The mechanism through which this occurred was the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth. This extractive institutional setup was a direct result of the challenges faced by Europeans in colonizing areas with high mortality rates, which led to the implementation of institutions that facilitated exploitation rather than development. These early institutions persisted and shaped the current institutional framework, which significantly impacted income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further substantiate this by showing that institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. However, the persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic development, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have long-lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, led to the emergence of certain institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) examines merchant guilds and other institutions, showing that they were not merely inefficient cartels but rather emerged from power struggles and provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Domar (1970, p.7) similarly highlights that slavery or serfdom can be understood as a response to a high land-to-labor ratio and scarce labor, rather than cultural or political influences (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective underscores that institutions often arise from the need to manage conflicts and distribute resources, rather than from a purely economic logic of efficiency. The implications of these institutions for long-run economic growth remain complex. Domar (1970, p.7) notes that in a traditional society without technological progress and capital accumulation, the end of slavery is more certain as the marginal product of labor descends to the subsistence level (Domar 1970, p.7). This suggests that while distributional conflicts shape institutions, the economic dynamics they create can have significant long-term effects on growth trajectories.

Specific technological changes and historical events have played a pivotal role in shaping economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) both examine how technological and historical shifts influenced economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological change was not merely about improving agricultural productivity but also about managing the conflict between farmers and cattle owners, which had long-term implications for economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.22) similarly shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The technological changes during the late medieval centuries, such as the increased effectiveness of soldiers with firearms, further highlight the role of technological advancements in shaping economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.23). These historical events and technological changes, while not directly addressing the role of institutions, illustrate how economic development can be influenced by factors that are often outside the purview of institutional frameworks.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) both argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr's work underscores the importance of "useful knowledge" in driving economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements often result from the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking. This process, which involves inventors transforming known ideas into novel innovations, highlights how technological progress can be sustained through the continuous accumulation of knowledge. Kuznets further elaborates on this idea, noting that the mass application of technological innovations, many of which are based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that drives further advancements in technology and science. This interplay between technological progress and scientific discovery underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where advancements in one area fuel progress in another. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This nuanced view suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the role of institutions in managing and sustaining these advancements cannot be overlooked. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is complex, with each influencing the other in ways that shape long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements. Mokyr highlights the necessity of institutional progress to complement technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This view is supported by Kuznets, who argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Both authors recognize that institutions are essential for managing and sustaining technological advancements, thereby preventing rent-seeking behaviors that could erode economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Institutions matter because they provide the framework for property rights and enforce contracts, which are fundamental for economic growth. North argues that institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.6). This is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is complex, with each influencing the other in ways that shape long-run economic growth.

Institutions are not just about efficiency; they also play a role in distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective is supported by Hornbeck, who shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). These examples illustrate how institutions can be designed to address specific economic challenges and conflicts, thereby shaping long-run economic growth. The historical evidence shows that the introduction of new institutions, such as barbed wire fencing, can significantly enhance economic development by addressing specific challenges and conflicts. The empirical comparison of different institutional frameworks shows that institutions designed to address specific economic challenges and conflicts can significantly enhance long-run economic growth.
