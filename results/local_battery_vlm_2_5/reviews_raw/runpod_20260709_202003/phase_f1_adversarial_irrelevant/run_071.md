The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms driving prosperity over extended periods. At stake is understanding whether and how the rules, norms, and organizations that structure societies influence economic performance. The live disagreement centers around the primacy of technological progress versus institutional frameworks. Some scholars argue that technological advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth, suggesting that institutions play a secondary role in facilitating these advances (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Others contend that institutions are crucial in their own right, shaping the efficiency of economic exchanges and the sustainability of growth by mitigating coordination failures and enforcing property rights (North 1989, p.6). This debate underscores the need to examine how institutions interact with technological progress and other economic factors to sustain long-term growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They assert that political institutions play a crucial role in facilitating efficient market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges, leading to a reduction in costs per transaction. The aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies as specialization and division of labor increase, thereby fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further support this view by examining the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. They demonstrate that secure property rights and economic growth were underpinned by political factors, particularly the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. These institutions also complement reputation-building strategies by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This endogenous role of political institutions is crucial for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate this with the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution, showing how restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improved its ability to maintain commitments. This highlights the importance of political institutions in creating a stable environment conducive to long-term economic growth, as they provide a framework that supports efficient market exchanges and secure property rights.

The growth of useful knowledge and technological advancements are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that technological progress is driven by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which allows inventors to transform known ideas into novel innovations. This process relies on a collective knowledge base that informs inventors about existing techniques and potential gaps. Kuznets (1973, p.10) adds that technological innovations often respond to specific needs, illustrating how necessity can drive invention. This interplay between technological progress and economic needs highlights that technological advancements are deeply embedded in the economic and social fabric of societies. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible than institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement requires complementary institutional progress to prevent rent-seeking behaviors that could erode gains. Therefore, while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by institutional frameworks that ensure their benefits are widely distributed and sustained over the long term. This interdependence suggests that while technology is a primary driver, institutions play a critical role in harnessing its potential for long-run economic growth.

This interplay is further complicated by the multifaceted nature of economic growth, as highlighted by Komlos (1998, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4). Both authors underscore the importance of technological progress in driving economic growth, noting that technological opportunities can accelerate the growth of the capital stock and enhance productivity. This dynamic is evident in Komlos' analysis of the Industrial Revolution, where technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture influenced relative nutrient prices and consumption patterns. Technological advancement can also reduce the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby fostering a higher rate of capital accumulation. This mechanism is crucial for understanding how technological progress can lead to rapid economic growth, as it not only reduces the technological age but also increases the capital-labor ratio. However, Komlos also acknowledges that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, depending on factors such as the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply. This qualification underscores the conditional nature of economic growth, where technological progress must be supported by complementary factors to ensure sustained benefits.

Domar notes that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high, driven by the scarcity of labor. This mechanism is evident in the increased profitability of slavery in medieval England, where the shift from crop cultivation to sheep breeding exacerbated labor scarcity. Pamuk elaborates on this dynamic by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes. The plague's demographic impact led to a sharp increase in wages relative to rents and the price of capital, prompting a substitution of land and capital for labor across Europe. Epstein's framework highlights that urban guilds were the primary producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe. The implication is that technological progress and capital accumulation are influenced by the interplay between demographic changes and institutional responses. However, Domar qualifies this by noting that in traditional societies without technological progress and capital accumulation, the end of slavery is more certain as population growth continues and the marginal product of labor descends to the subsistence level. This qualification underscores the complexity of economic growth, where historical events and institutional responses are intertwined.

Hornbeck argues that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated investment in agricultural improvements. The mechanism by which barbed wire enhanced development was multifaceted: it not only provided a physical barrier to protect crops from stray livestock but also reduced the transaction costs associated with negotiating property rights. By clearly delineating boundaries, barbed wire minimized disputes and encouraged long-term planning. However, Ogilvie qualifies this by noting that the efficiency of property rights institutions is often overstated, as distributional conflicts can also drive institutional changes (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This qualification underscores the importance of considering both efficiency and power dynamics when examining the role of property rights in economic growth.

The nature of these institutions was often determined by the settler mortality rates, which in turn were influenced by factors such as disease environments. High mortality rates in colonies with tropical climates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in regions where European settlers faced high mortality risks, leading to the implementation of institutions that prioritized the extraction of resources over sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This dynamic was not uniform across all colonies. In regions where European settlers could establish permanent settlements, institutions tended to be more inclusive and growth-oriented. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic performance, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies well beyond the colonial era. This institutional legacy continues to influence current economic disparities, underscoring the profound and lasting effects of colonial institutions on economic development.
