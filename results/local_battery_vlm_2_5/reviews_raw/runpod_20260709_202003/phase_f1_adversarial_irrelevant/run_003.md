The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically, and whether the path of growth is determined by the institutions that govern economic and political life. The debate centers on two main positions: one that emphasizes the centrality of institutions in driving economic growth, and another that attributes economic development primarily to other factors, such as geography or initial conditions. The former position argues that institutions create the framework within which economic activities occur, influencing the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior. The latter position, however, suggests that institutions are merely one among many factors, and that other variables, such as geography or initial endowments, play a more significant role. The live disagreement is thus whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. The answer to this question has profound implications for understanding the historical trajectories of different economies and for designing effective policies to promote economic development. One key point of contention is the extent to which institutions can be changed to foster growth. Austin and North offer different perspectives on how institutions influence economic growth. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

The colonial institutional framework, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27), is a critical determinant of long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of former European colonies. These scholars posit that the institutions established during colonialism were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic development. This institutional framework, shaped by the political power dynamics of the time, often resulted in extractive institutions that hindered economic growth. One key mechanism through which these institutions operate is by influencing the distribution of political power. Colonizers tended to establish institutions that maintained their control and extracted resources, leading to a persistent institutional framework that did not foster economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to benefit the politically powerful groups rather than to encourage economic development. This dynamic is further evidenced by the high correlation between European mortality rates in colonies and the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The impact of these institutions varies across colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions was influenced by European mortality rates, which varied significantly across different regions. This variation in mortality rates led to different institutional outcomes, highlighting the importance of specific historical conditions in shaping long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutional frameworks, even after colonial rule ended, underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic development.

Building on this, Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Hornbeck, argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework, which favored elites, was influenced by the specific factor endowments of these societies. The abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor created conditions for extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. These inequalities were exacerbated by institutions that protected elite interests, such as those that secured property rights and financial sector structures that favored the wealthy. The persistence of these institutional frameworks, even after colonial rule ended, underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic inequality. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, for instance, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This mechanism highlights how institutional changes can shape economic development by altering the incentives and constraints faced by different groups in society. The impact of these institutions on economic inequality varies across regions. For example, the differentials in paths of development in Latin America have long been of central concern to scholars, who have highlighted the variation across societies in conditions relevant to growth such as the security of property rights, prevalence of corruption, structures of the financial sector, investment in public infrastructure and social capital, and the inclination to work hard or be entrepreneurial (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

North and Weingast, along with North, assert that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significant (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They argue that institutions governing public choice and property rights are crucial for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that political institutions can enhance the efficacy of reputation mechanisms by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. North elaborates on this by highlighting that the evolution of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is vital for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability is essential for fostering confidence in economic dealings, even in complex interdependent situations where personal knowledge of trading partners is limited (North 1989, p.2). However, North also notes that the development of more complex social frameworks requires institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with growing specialization and division of labor. The establishment of a set of property rights that allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with strangers is a key institutional requirement for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange.

Institutions have played a critical role in shaping the historical economic divergence between regions, a key aspect of long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) argue that the economic divergence between regions cannot be simplified to a single causal narrative, such as the 'reversal of fortune' thesis or the 'great divergence'. One specific mechanism that institutions affect this divergence is through the impact on labor productivity. In pre-colonial and colonial Africa, for instance, institutions that favored economic growth in certain contexts were not necessarily optimal for others (Austin 2008, p.1). This is because the economic rent and economic growth were often joint products, and the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex and context-dependent, with different institutions having different impacts on economic growth in different historical contexts.

The literature on African economic development, such as Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.3), highlights that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Hopkins argues that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Nunn finds that the slave trades in Africa had an adverse effect on economic development, illustrating that institutions that promote violence and exploitation can have lasting negative impacts. Hopkins further notes that institutions can be long-standing but are not immutable, suggesting that policy interventions can adapt inappropriate institutions to meet present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This adaptability is crucial for economic growth, as it allows for the correction of historical institutional failures. However, Nunn's work qualifies this adaptability by showing that the legacy of past institutions, such as the slave trades, can have persistent effects on economic development, even after policy reforms (Nunn 2008, p.16). Therefore, while institutions can be adapted, the historical context in which they operate must also be considered.

This complexity is further illustrated by Komlos and van Zanden, who argue that the relationship between economic growth and living standards in Europe is complex and not straightforwardly positive (Komlos 1998, p.13; van Zanden 1999, p.2). The Industrial Revolution brought significant economic growth, but this did not translate into uniform improvements in biological well-being. Komlos posits that despite the economic expansion, inadequate income gains and poor childhood nourishment led to a decline in biological well-being (Komlos 1998, p.8). One mechanism for this divergence is the rising relative price of nutrients, which led to a substitution of industrial commodities for food products and a shift within the food budget towards carbohydrates over protein-rich foods. This substitution pattern highlights how economic growth can lead to a decline in nutritional quality, even as aggregate purchasing power increases. Pamuk qualifies this pattern by showing that structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This historical context suggests that long-term structural changes, such as those induced by the Black Death, can significantly influence the relationship between economic growth and living standards. This relationship is further complicated by the fact that linkages between urban and rural sectors can weaken, allowing large urban-rural differences to persist. (Pamuk 2007, p.8).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) show that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for economic growth, as these institutions enforced property rights and ensured that the state would not expropriate wealth. This institutional framework was essential for maintaining the state's credibility and encouraging economic activity. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) further elaborates on this by arguing that institutions reduce transaction costs and uncertainties, which are critical for economic growth in complex interdependent societies. The establishment of efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms allows individuals to engage in market exchanges with confidence, even with those they do not know personally.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the historical context of Europe between 1500 and 1800. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) highlights the debate between optimists and pessimists regarding economic development in early modern Europe. Optimists argue that there was significant economic growth during this period, while pessimists contend that real wages declined. This debate illustrates the complexities of economic growth and the standard of living, suggesting that institutional frameworks significantly influence economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23) reinforce this by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by differences in productivity and institutional frameworks. This divergence highlights the importance of institutions in determining long-run economic growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its legacy. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth in the colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes in these regions. Austin (2008, p.11; 2008, p.1) qualifies the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by noting that the economic motivations for the Atlantic slave trade and colonial enterprises were complex and varied. The types of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal in others, further emphasizing the contextual nature of institutional impact on economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a subject of ongoing scholarly debate. The role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs is a shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Disagreement persists over the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth, particularly in the context of colonialism and its legacy. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists were detrimental to long-run growth in the colonies. This perspective is complicated by Austin's (2008, p.11) (Austin 2008, p.1) observation that the types of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal in others. The historical evidence of the Atlantic slave trade and its economic motivations settles this disagreement by showing that the exchange-value product of Africans' labor in Africa was lower than it was in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken. This empirical comparison underscores the complex interplay between institutions, economic motivations, and long-run economic growth.
