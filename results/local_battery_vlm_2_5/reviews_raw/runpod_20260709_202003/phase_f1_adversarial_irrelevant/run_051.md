The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and historical analysis. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain economic prosperity over extended periods. A live disagreement exists between those who view institutions as crucial for economic growth and those who emphasize other factors, such as technological progress or exogenous shocks. Institutions, in this context, refer to the rules, norms, and organizations that structure social interactions and economic activities. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if they merely facilitate growth driven by other factors. The key disagreement is between those who see institutions as the fundamental cause of economic growth and those who view them as secondary to technological advancements or other exogenous factors. The role of institutions in economic growth encompasses efficiency and the broader social and political contexts that shape economic outcomes. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly through the specification and enforcement of property rights. This perspective highlights the importance of political institutions in creating a stable environment for economic activity. This stability is achieved through institutions that complement reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints that prevent reneging on agreements, thereby enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6) critiques the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, as insufficient for explaining economic growth. He argues that institutions are essential for specifying property rights and providing enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.5). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in creating a stable environment for economic activity, which is crucial for sustained economic development.

This institutional stability is deeply influenced by the distributional conflicts highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30). This perspective is crucial for understanding the role of institutions in economic development, as it highlights the importance of power dynamics and political considerations in institutional design. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) demonstrates this through a detailed analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where key economic institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts. These institutions, far from being merely efficient solutions to economic problems, were often the result of struggles between different social groups vying for control over resources and decision-making power. Merchant guilds, for instance, provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' for their members (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This dynamic is further supported by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), who shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological change allowed farmers to secure their land and resources, thereby altering the distributional landscape and empowering them in negotiations with other stakeholders. The direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that while technological changes can facilitate institutional shifts, the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts remain critical in shaping economic institutions.

These power dynamics and distributional conflicts are further complicated by the catch-up process in economic growth, as noted by Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that rapid technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, encouraging capital stock growth and modernization. However, Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies this view by noting that technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture can lag behind industrial sectors, leading to increased relative nutrient prices and altered consumption patterns. This implies that while technological advance can drive economic growth, the distribution of its benefits across sectors and social groups is not uniform. The implications of this uneven distribution are significant, as they can influence the overall well-being of the population. For instance, Komlos (1998, p.8) shows that during the Industrial Revolution, despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that the benefits of technological progress and economic growth do not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of society.

This reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27), underscores the profound impact of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The institutions established by European colonists were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers. This is evident in the extractive institutions that were implemented in colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates. These institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) argue that the differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these extractive institutions, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions meant that they were not conducive to economic development, as they were designed to benefit the colonizers rather than the local population. This extractive institutional framework had lasting effects on the economic performance of these colonies, as it hindered the development of productive institutions that could drive long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies, and via this channel, account for a significant fraction of current income differences.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.54) argue that economic growth is driven primarily by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective posits that institutions play a secondary role, acting as facilitators of long-run economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process is deeply intertwined with the broader economic and social context, including the institutional framework that supports or hinders innovation. This suggests that while institutions may influence the direction and efficiency of technological progress, the core engine of economic growth remains the advancement of technology and knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.10) also points out that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This qualification underscores that while technology is the primary driver, institutions still play a crucial role in harnessing technological progress for sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue, with scholars highlighting various mechanisms through which institutions influence technological progress and economic development. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues, is the mainspring of economic growth, but it is not an autonomous process. Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows that technological progress relies on the existing knowledge base and often involves the recombination of existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This knowledge accumulation is facilitated by institutions that support the dissemination and application of new ideas. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) builds on this by noting that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Institutions play a crucial role in this process by providing the necessary framework for technological progress to be harnessed effectively. For example, North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for the efficient use of technological innovations, as Kuznets (1973, p.10) points out, mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

Institutions also play a pivotal role in addressing coordination failures and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This is because institutions provide the necessary governance structures to manage the complexities of technological change and ensure that economic benefits are widely distributed. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provides a concrete example of this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This shows that institutions can facilitate technological progress by addressing specific challenges that arise from economic development. Furthermore, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This highlights the importance of understanding the social and political contexts in which institutions operate, as these contexts can significantly influence the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth.
