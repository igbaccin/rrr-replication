The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics. The stakes are high, as the answer determines how policymakers should approach economic development. Institutions are the game itself, and the rules can be changed. On one hand, institutions are seen as fundamental to economic growth, providing the stable framework necessary for investment and innovation. On the other hand, some scholars argue that institutions are merely a byproduct of deeper forces, such as technological progress or geographic factors. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the driving force behind economic growth or merely a reflection of other underlying factors. Institutions can be either a catalyst for growth or an obstacle to it, depending on their design and enforcement. This tension is evident in the debate over whether institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had enduring effects on economic development. Institutions are not static; they evolve over time, and their impact on economic growth can vary significantly depending on the historical and cultural context. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a pivotal moment in this evolution, as it established a political framework that constrained the Crown's power to expropriate property. This institutional change was fundamental in creating an environment where economic actors could engage in long-term investments with greater confidence. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further argue that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional framework was particularly important in mitigating the incentive problem of expropriation, a significant concern for economic actors in pre-modern societies. The Glorious Revolution thus provided a credible commitment by the state to uphold property rights, a condition that was essential for sustained economic growth. This institutional change facilitated economic growth and laid the groundwork for the development of modern capital markets, as evidenced by the subsequent growth of capital markets in England.

Distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) both argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by power struggles and distributional conflicts. For instance, the persistence of serfdom and slavery can be explained by the high land-to-labor ratio and the scarcity of labor, which made these institutions profitable despite their inefficiencies. Domar (1970, p.6) shows that the profitability of slave labor can be sustained even with technological progress and capital accumulation, as long as the land-to-labor ratio remains high. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are pivotal for long-run economic growth. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.6) notes that the efficiency of institutions can vary over time and under different conditions, suggesting that while distributional conflicts may have initially shaped these institutions, their efficiency could have been a factor in their persistence or transformation. This nuanced view highlights the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional efficiency in shaping economic institutions over the long run. The efficiency of institutions, however, can be a factor in their persistence or transformation.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) argues that this technological change led to increased protection from others' cattle, which in turn drove agricultural development. This protection allowed farmers to invest more in their lands, knowing that their property rights were better secured. Pamuk (2007, p.22) supports this view by highlighting how technological innovations, such as the use of firearm-equipped soldiers, led to increased capital intensity in various industries. The direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This qualification suggests that while technological changes are important, the institutional framework that supports and enforces property rights is crucial for long-term economic growth. The effectiveness of these institutions in protecting property rights can vary, as seen in the differing impacts of technological changes across regions. This variability underscores the importance of understanding the specific conditions under which institutions can enhance economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge drives economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress is a critical component of economic development, suggesting that without institutional progress to complement this technological advancement, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly posits that technological innovations are closely connected with the progress of science, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for further technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective underscores the role of knowledge accumulation in driving economic growth, emphasizing that technological progress is not merely a byproduct of economic development but a fundamental driver. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial, as Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which can alert inventors to gaps and opportunities in the existing set of technologies (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This suggests that while institutions play a role in supporting technological advancements, the primary driver of long-term economic growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge. However, Kuznets (1973, p.10) also acknowledges that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, implying that while knowledge accumulation is crucial, the institutional framework can influence its effectiveness.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) further highlight that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. They argue that the institutional framework established by European colonizers significantly influenced economic outcomes. Specifically, the mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies shaped the type of institutions implemented; high mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This extractive institutional framework persisted over time, affecting current income per capita. These institutions were designed to extract resources efficiently. This institutional legacy highlights how historical factors can shape long-term economic trajectories. The persistence of these institutions suggests that their impact on economic growth is a deep-seated influence that continues to affect economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also note that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across all colonies. This implies that while institutions play a crucial role, their effectiveness can vary based on local conditions and the specific historical context of each colony.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they create the framework within which economic activities operate. Institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is shared by Kuznets, who argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr highlights, is the mainspring of economic growth, and without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional development underscores the deep-seated influence of institutions on economic performance.

The effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is not uniform and can vary based on local conditions and specific historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the importance of institutions in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This finding is supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence from former European colonies and pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists or powerful groups do not necessarily promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, as highlighted by Hornbeck, demonstrates how technological innovations and better protection of property rights can enhance agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This empirical comparison reveals that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their ability to facilitate technological progress and protect property rights.
