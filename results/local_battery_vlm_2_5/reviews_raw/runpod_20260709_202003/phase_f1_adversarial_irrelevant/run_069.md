The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanation for the divergent economic trajectories of nations over centuries. This question is central to a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who posit alternative mechanisms, such as geography or cultural factors. The debate hinges on whether institutions can be credited with fostering economic development through mechanisms such as secure property rights and efficient markets, or whether these factors are merely coincidental to deeper, underlying causes. The disagreement is also about the nature of institutions themselves: are they exogenous constraints that shape economic outcomes, or are they endogenous, shaped by the very economic processes they govern? The key issue is the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, and whether this relationship is direct and robust or indirect and contingent. The question of whether institutions are a cause or an effect of growth is central to understanding the mechanisms that drive long-term economic development. This is because the answer will determine the potential for policy interventions to foster growth through institutional design. The institutional view of growth suggests that the right institutions can stimulate economic development, but the counter-argument is that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper economic forces. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, together with North, argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They contend that these institutions complemented reputation-building strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining the state's ex post behavior. North and Weingast further illustrate this through the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution, where restrictions on the state's behavior improved its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North argues that the evolution of more complex social frameworks requires institutional reliability to reduce uncertainties associated with interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with strangers. North also acknowledges that the establishment of such a set of property rights depends on the development of efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are pivotal, their effectiveness in fostering economic growth is contingent on broader economic and social conditions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) share a common claim: the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, particularly the establishment of extractive institutions, are critical in determining long-run economic growth. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. The mechanism through which these institutions affect growth involves the political power dynamics of the colonizers. Colonizers who had high mortality rates established extractive institutions that did not encourage long-term economic development. This extractive institutional framework, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, persists and significantly affects current income per capita. This persistence is a continuing influence on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) note that the differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these extractive institutions, which in turn affected the economic performance of the colonies. The high correlation between mortality rates and European settlements, as well as between early institutions and current institutions, underscores the enduring impact of these colonial legacies. This framework highlights that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth, as they directly influence the economic performance of the colonies.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind economic performance and inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) emphasizes that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, the community, and various guilds, were not merely efficient solutions to economic problems but were also shaped by the struggles between different social groups. These institutions were the outcome of power dynamics and distributional conflicts rather than purely efficient responses to economic challenges. The institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies were reflective of the power dynamics and distributional conflicts between different social groups. This interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development highlights how economic performance and inequality are deeply intertwined with the power dynamics within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) further underscores that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. This broader institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, significantly influences economic performance and inequality. This framework suggests that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth, as they directly influence the economic performance of the colonies.

While Austin (2008, p.11) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) explore economic growth and living standards in Europe, their analyses do not explicitly address the institutional factors that shape long-run economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He emphasizes that the specific reasons for low labor productivity in Africa are best understood by considering the choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment (Austin 2008, p.11). This perspective highlights the complexity of economic development, suggesting that institutions may play a nuanced role in shaping long-run economic growth. This view implies that institutional factors, while important, are not the sole determinants of economic performance. Komlos (1998, p.13) adds another layer to this discussion by arguing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be mediated by factors such as income distribution and public health.

The historical interpretations of economic divergence focus on structural changes initiated by events such as the Black Death, rather than addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Bryant (2006, p.29) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800. They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector. The productivity revolution in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, whereas the Chinese pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household produced no structural differentiation between the two sectors. This difference in structural changes led to a more synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing in Europe. This significantly improved diets, increased productivity through the substitution of horse and oxen for human labour, and enhanced soil fertility. The English population grew from roughly 4 to 8.5 million between 1600 and 1800, with the percentage of those engaged in agriculture falling by nearly one-half, from 70% of the total to 36%. This was accompanied by remarkable gains in per capita productivity, rising discretionary incomes, and a doubling of grain and livestock output from 1700 to 1800. Chinese peasants, in contrast, were increasingly driven into a crops-only pattern, as contracting plots ruled out the planting of fodder cereals or grasses, and led to the reverse substitution of human for animal labour power. The linkages between the urban and rural sectors are not always equally strong, and it is quite possible that the linkages between urban wages and productivity in rural areas, where a much larger share of the total population lived, may become weak and large urban-rural differences may persist at times or in some countries. Despite these qualifications, the link between wages and standards of living remains (Pamuk 2007, p.8).

The literature on African economic development highlights diverse historical factors, including slavery, colonial policies, and gender roles, without directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that the new economic history of Africa, with its focus on institutions, can inform institutional reforms. This approach is complementary to Nunn (2008, p.3), who finds that Africa's slave trades had adverse effects on economic development. Both authors point to the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that institutions are not immutable but can be adapted to meet present development needs. For instance, the legacy of slavery in Africa and the subsequent institutional frameworks that emerged from it have shaped economic trajectories. This is not just the case for Africa, but also in the New World, where slavery resulted in institutions that were not conducive to economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further expand on the institutional impacts by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their analysis shows that as selected crops became commoditized for export production, women's control over production and allocation continued for some food crops, while for others that obtained a cash value, women's control was increasingly limited to allocating labor tasks. This dynamic underscores the gendered impact of institutional changes on economic activities. The role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to gender dynamics. The institutional frameworks that secured the appropriation, extraction, and export of resources in support of European industrialization also shaped Africa's economic development. This points to the broader implications of institutional legacies on economic growth, where historical factors continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with historical legacies and political factors playing crucial roles. Institutions governing public choice, such as those that secured property rights, have been instrumental in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) argue that the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England was critical for economic development, as these institutions underpinned secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules, which were essential for economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) highlights that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional reliability is essential for economic growth, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations.

Institutional frameworks also shape economic activities and development paths. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23) show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with labour productivity and wages playing significant roles. This divergence underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, as different institutional frameworks led to different economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) further illustrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. This points to the broader implications of institutional legacies on economic growth, where historical factors continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to securing property rights or reducing transaction costs; it also involves shaping economic activities and development paths. This complexity is evident in the historical evidence, where different institutional frameworks have led to different economic outcomes.
