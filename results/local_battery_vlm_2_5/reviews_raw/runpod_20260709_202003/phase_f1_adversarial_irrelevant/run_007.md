The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic development. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or resource endowments, drive this transition. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in facilitating economic growth by providing stable property rights and reducing transaction costs. Another position argues that institutions alone cannot explain long-run economic growth, and that other factors such as resource endowments and geography play a significant role. Yet another position suggests that the impact of institutions varies across different historical and geographical contexts. The debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic growth over the long run, and how these interactions may have evolved over time. Institutions can be understood as the rules of the game in a society, and the question is how these rules shape economic outcomes. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that the success of economic growth hinges on political factors underpinning market development, including institutions that enforce and adapt rules governing economic exchange. Institutions are critical in complementing reputation-building mechanisms, acting as constraints that prevent reneging and improve the efficacy of reputation-based strategies. This institutional framework is essential for maintaining the state's commitment to bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. Specifically, the Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can enhance the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains, thereby fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). As specialization and division of labor increase, the volume of exchanges multiplies, making efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms indispensable. This institutional reliability allows individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence, even in complex interdependent situations. North (1989, p.6) also emphasizes that the establishment of efficient property rights and a reliable medium of exchange is necessary for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. The institutional framework ensures that individuals can have confidence in their dealings with others, even in the absence of personal knowledge or reciprocal exchange relationships. This institutional reliability is essential for economic growth, as it reduces uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations and allows individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence.

This institutional legacy, however, is not limited to former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate. The institutional framework introduced by colonial powers was often designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-term economic growth. This extractive institutional design was a direct response to the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, who prioritized immediate gains over sustainable development. These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often did not encourage economic development, and the persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these colonies. The mechanisms by which these institutions were established varied, but the underlying pattern was that they were tailored to serve the interests of the colonizers, often at the expense of the indigenous population. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) highlight that the impact of these institutions on economic performance can be traced back to the differences in European mortality rates, which influenced the type of institutions established in the colonies. This historical context underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, showing that the institutional framework set during colonial times continues to shape economic development in former colonies today.

This institutional framework, however, was not uniform across all societies, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Hornbeck 2010, p.34) illustrate. This institutional framework, shaped by the initial factor endowments and the resulting power dynamics, led to policies that favored the elite, such as land grants and protective tariffs, which in turn hindered economic growth by limiting the development of competitive markets. The persistence of inequality over time is evident in the historical records of Latin American economies, where the institutional structures were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the ruling elites. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) notes that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This development underscores the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the ability to enforce property rights directly influenced agricultural productivity. However, the implications of these institutions extend beyond agricultural development. The unequal distribution of wealth and power, reinforced by institutional policies, created barriers to entry for new competitors, stifling innovation and economic growth. This dynamic highlights the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic development, showing that the policies and structures established in response to initial conditions can have lasting effects on economic performance.

This thesis, however, does not fully account for the institutional factors that Austin (2008, p.11); (Bryant 2006, p.29) emphasize. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, particularly in the context of African economic development. The thesis posits that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa is almost unanimous that, until well into the 20th century at least, most of the region most of the time was characterised by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land as well as to capital (Austin 2008, p.11). This scarcity is not inconsistent with the contention that the region had a higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population, which may be taken as evidence of higher real per capita income. The thesis that the economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, highlights the contextual nature of institutions favouring economic growth (Austin 2008, p.1). The kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. Bryant (2006, p.29) supports this idea, arguing that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This suggests a more nuanced view of economic growth, where the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments. This dynamic contrasts with the Chinese pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household, which produced no structural differentiation between the two sectors (Bryant 2006, p.29). This structural differentiation is a key mechanism by which institutions shape long-run economic growth, as it allows for the release of labour into more productive sectors. However, the Chinese economic pattern, tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts, restricted the scope for economies of scale and limited both the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production and the capital accumulation necessary for its expansion.

This economic pattern, however, contrasts sharply with the role of coal in the Industrial Revolution, as Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14), alongside Pamuk (2007, p.8), detail. They argue that these factors, while significant, do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. One specific mechanism by which these economic factors influence growth is through changes in productivity. Clark and Jacks estimate modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining over the Industrial Revolution era (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31), suggesting that while coal played a role, it was not the primary driver of economic growth. This aligns with Pamuk's finding that the Black Death led to long-term structural changes, including a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.8). These structural changes, in turn, influenced the distribution of labor and capital, which are crucial for economic growth. However, Pamuk notes that the linkages between urban and rural sectors can vary, which qualifies the extent to which these structural changes uniformly impact economic growth (Pamuk 2007, p.8). This variability in sectoral linkages highlights the complex interplay between economic factors and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

This decoupling, however, is not universally applicable, as van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) argue. They show that although the economy grew during the Industrial Revolution, this growth did not uniformly translate into better living standards. This decoupling is evident in the stagnation of real wages and the decline in biological well-being, despite overall economic expansion. The mechanism at work here is the divergence between economic growth and the distribution of its benefits. van Zanden (1999, p.2) points out that this divergence is further complicated by the misalignment between economic growth and real wage increases, with real wages often stagnating or even declining. This misalignment suggests that economic growth does not automatically improve living standards, and the benefits of growth may be contingent on broader institutional and distributional factors. The implication is that while economic growth is necessary for improving living standards, it is not sufficient on its own.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and ensuring their enforcement. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework, by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, reduced transaction costs and facilitated market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Austin's work on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are most plausibly understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment (Austin 2008, p.11). This approach underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes in different historical and geographical contexts.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is complemented by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce uncertainties and facilitate complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). The institutional requirements necessary for realizing productivity gains entail the development of efficient product and factor markets and a reliable medium of exchange (North 1989, p.2).

However, the relationship between economic growth and living standards is not straightforward, as shown by van Zanden's work, which argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective is supported by Clark and Jacks, who show that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining over the Industrial Revolution era were only modest (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). The implication is that while economic growth is necessary for improving living standards, it is not sufficient on its own. The benefits of growth are contingent on broader institutional and distributional factors, as well as relative price effects and changes in consumption patterns.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement between the roles of institutions and economic growth lies in the analysis of total factor productivity gains. The modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining, as shown by Clark and Jacks, provide a clear measurement of the limited impact of economic growth on living standards in the absence of supportive institutions (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth and improving living standards.
