The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development over centuries. There is a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutional frameworks in fostering growth and those who attribute economic development primarily to other factors, such as technological advancements or resource endowments. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that structure economic interactions and govern the enforcement of property rights. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the critical determinants of long-run economic growth or merely one among many factors. Understanding this relationship is crucial for policating effective economic policies and for comprehending the historical trajectories of different economies. The discussion begins with the institutions-centric view, which posits that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and economic exchange is pivotal for economic growth.

The institutions-centric view emphasizes the role of credible commitments and secure property rights in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed, with a critical factor being the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by North, who highlights that the evolution of more complex social frameworks, including efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, is essential for reducing uncertainties and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). These institutional structures are not merely complementary to reputation-building strategies but actively improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is further supported by the work of Clark and Jacks, who argue that the extraction costs of coal rose only modestly with cumulative output, indicating that technological barriers and coal rents played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). The institutional framework that supported secure property rights and efficient market exchanges was the primary driver of economic growth during this period.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) contend that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions that enforced rules and constrained the state's ex post behavior was crucial for maintaining commitments to constituents and preventing expropriation. This institutional framework was essential for reducing uncertainties and facilitating market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth. This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with strangers. This highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional development, where secure property rights are just one component of a broader institutional framework that supports economic growth. The institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution exemplify how political institutions can improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic growth.

These findings suggest that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, where the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate. The extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining their political power. This dynamic highlights the influence of colonial institutions on economic development, where the design of institutions by colonizers was driven by the desire to extract maximum benefit from their colonies, often at the expense of long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.2) establish that the determinants of European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, played a crucial role in shaping the institutions of these colonies. The high correlation between mortality rates and European settlements, as well as the persistence of these institutions over time, underscores the lasting impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic performance. This pattern is further qualified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) who find that institutions significantly contributed to the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, thereby accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences.

This complexity is further highlighted by critiques of institutional theories on economic development, which challenge the simplistic views of institutional theories by highlighting the complexities and dynamics involved in economic development, suggesting that other factors beyond institutions play significant roles (Austin 2008, p.11; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Austin contends that the Atlantic slave trade was driven by commercial motivations, leading to a lower exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa compared to their destinations, and argues that the scarcity of labour in Sub-Saharan Africa does not necessarily contradict the higher population density and urban population in the region (Austin 2008, p.11). This perspective underscores the need to consider specific aspects of the natural environment and the choice of production techniques in understanding labour productivity. This highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which institutions operate. Austin further notes that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the institutions that favoured growth in certain contexts may not be optimal in others (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view challenges the idea that efficient institutions alone drive economic growth, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic development is more complex and context-dependent. Ogilvie's framework of interdependent institutions within a wider system further qualifies this notion, emphasizing that institutions cannot be studied in isolation but must be understood as part of an entire institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by a web of interdependent factors, each influencing the others in complex ways.

This complexity is further emphasized by van Zanden (1999, p.2); (Komlos 1998, p.13) who argue that the relationship between economic development and living standards in historical Europe is more complex than often portrayed, as it does not follow a linear path of improvement. For instance, during the early modern period, economic growth did not necessarily translate into better living standards. This perspective is supported by Komlos (1998, p.8), who showed that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that economic growth alone is not sufficient to improve living standards, and other factors, such as income distribution and nutrition, play a crucial role. Pamuk (2007, p.8) further qualifies this relationship by demonstrating that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This implies that exogenous shocks, such as pandemics, can have lasting effects on economic development and living standards. Pamuk (2007, p.15) also notes that the skill premium began to decline under severe labor shortages, which could have encouraged human capital formation. This suggests that labor market conditions can influence the distribution of skills and wages, further complicating the relationship between economic development and living standards. The intricate interplay of economic growth, living standards, and exogenous shocks highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of these factors in historical Europe.

Stern (2004, p.3) and Kuznets (Stern 2004, p.3; Kuznets 1973, p.12) both acknowledge that technological progress is a key driver of economic growth and environmental change. Stern (2004, p.3) argues that technological improvements can lead to lower emissions per unit of output, even if this is not the intended consequence (Stern 2004, p.3). This aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.12), who emphasizes that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.12) highlights the need for a wider supply of tested data, which means essentially data that have been scrutinized in the process of use for economic analysis (Kuznets 1973, p.12). This suggests that the relationship between technological progress and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving not only technological changes but also institutional and ideological adjustments. However, Stern (2004, p.3) cautions that the EKC has a very flimsy statistical foundation and its demise may be imminent due to new generation of decomposition and efficient frontier models (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that while technological progress is crucial for economic growth, its environmental impact is not straightforward and requires careful analysis.

This re-evaluation of historical economic drivers and divergences emphasizes the significant roles of coal extraction costs and pre-1800 economic conditions in shaping economic outcomes, as Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) re-evaluate. This suggests that the industrial revolution was not driven by a single factor but was the result of a complex interplay of technological, economic, and institutional factors. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, highlighting that pre-industrial economic conditions, including labor productivity and wage levels, were critical in setting the stage for later economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This implies that long-run economic growth is not solely determined by industrialization but is deeply rooted in pre-existing economic structures and conditions. However, these factors do not directly address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, suggesting that while technological and economic conditions are crucial, the institutional framework within which these conditions operate is equally important.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is critical, as they establish the framework within which economic activities operate. North and Weingast demonstrate that secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitments from political institutions, are essential for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These commitments reduce uncertainties and enhance the reliability of institutions, which is vital for complex interdependent economic systems (North 1989, p.2). The historical context of seventeenth-century England illustrates how the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Moreover, Austin highlights that institutions supporting economic enterprise were key in certain historical contexts, even if these institutions varied across different periods and regions (Austin 2008, p.1). The significance of institutions in shaping economic growth is further underscored by Stern, who argues that improvements in technology and productivity are closely linked to institutional changes (Stern 2004, p.3). This is because technological advancements and increases in total factor productivity are often driven by institutional developments that support efficient use of resources and reduce emissions per unit of output (Stern 2004, p.15).

The interaction between technological progress and institutional frameworks is pivotal for understanding long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks show that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were modest, indicating that technological improvements alone were insufficient for sustained growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This underscores the importance of institutional support in leveraging technological advancements for economic growth. The role of institutions in driving economic growth is further illustrated by van Zanden, who argues that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that institutional factors played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The historical evidence from Europe shows that long-term growth in total factor productivity was possible even in traditional agricultural settings, highlighting the importance of institutional support for economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The historical case of England during the Glorious Revolution exemplifies how institutional changes can enhance the state's ability to maintain commitments and support economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This evidence shows that the institutional framework is essential for leveraging technological advancements and driving long-run economic growth.

The precise historical evidence and empirical comparisons that settle the disagreements between scholars are the case studies of England during the Glorious Revolution and the long-term growth in total factor productivity in traditional agricultural settings in Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These cases demonstrate that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for leveraging technological advancements and supporting economic enterprise.
