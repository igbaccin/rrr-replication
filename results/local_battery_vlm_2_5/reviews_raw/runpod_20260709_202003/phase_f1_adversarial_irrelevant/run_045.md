The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages with the fundamental drivers of economic development. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. One live disagreement centres on the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as technological change or demographic shifts. Some scholars argue that institutions play a crucial role in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activities, while others contend that technological advancements and demographic changes are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the notion that different historical contexts may require different institutional arrangements to achieve optimal economic outcomes. Austin highlights the complexity of economic growth by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, with different institutional arrangements favouring growth in different historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.1). This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward and may vary depending on the specific historical and economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that institutions established during colonialism were crucial in shaping long-run economic growth, with these specific institutional changes being more critical than general institutions. By leveraging differences in European mortality rates, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.2) identify that high mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions. This pattern of extractive institutions was designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies. These institutions influenced economic growth by extracting resources, not fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) stress that these institutions were designed to extract rents, which contradicts the idea that institutions encouraging private property rights would naturally develop. Extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonizers, can have a lasting negative impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate this by showing that institutions played an important role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, and via this channel, account for a significant fraction of current income differences. This finding suggests that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a profound and lasting effect on economic development, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies in a way that general institutions did not.

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the enforcement and change of rules are governed by institutions, which are critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They highlight that secure property rights, which are enforced through effective political institutions, are essential for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast present the Glorious Revolution in seventeenth-century England as a pivotal moment where institutional changes secured property rights and enabled economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional evolution was crucial for reducing transaction costs and increasing the reliability of economic exchanges, which in turn facilitated market development and economic growth (North 1989, p.2). North argues that the development of efficient property rights and enforcement mechanisms is vital for economic growth, as it allows for confidence in remote transactions and complex interdependencies, even in the absence of personal knowledge or reciprocal relationships. These institutions complement reputation-building strategies by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. North also emphasises that the institutional requirements for realizing productivity gains in a growing economy include the development of efficient product and factor markets, as well as a reliable medium of exchange. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions in economic growth, as restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve its ability to maintain bargains with constituents. However, North cautions that the evolution of more complex social frameworks will not occur if institutional structures cannot reduce the uncertainties associated with such situations, underscoring the importance of institutional reliability in economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both focus on how specific institutions influenced economic development and inequality. Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, are the key to understanding core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, while in the New World, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies. The distributional impact of institutions is evident in how they shaped economic outcomes and power structures. Ogilvie highlights that the efficiency approach has been broadly applied to reinterpret various premodern institutions, suggesting that these institutions were efficient solutions to specific obstacles. For instance, the Mesta in pre-modern Spain was seen as an efficient solution to property rights and resource access issues. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective underscores how institutions can create and maintain economic disparities. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated varied significantly across different contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may be insufficient. This interdependency is crucial for understanding how institutions shape economic development and inequality. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency highlights the complexity of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic outcomes.

The revisionist view of economic growth during the Industrial Revolution challenges conventional wisdom by offering alternative explanations for why growth occurred. Austin (2008, p.11) and Bryant (2006, p.29) share the posture that historical economic growth theories are oversimplified and that the conventional views fail to capture the complexity of historical economic development (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis has been used to explain why some regions experienced economic growth while others did not, but Austin (2008, p.11) contends that it fails to account for the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa. Bryant (2006, p.29) elaborates on this by highlighting how the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, whereas the Chinese pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household produced no structural differentiation between the two sectors (Bryant 2006, p.29). This suggests that the institutional frameworks and agricultural practices in different regions played a crucial role in shaping economic development. Komlos (1998, p.13) notes that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This implies that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed and that the institutional frameworks that supported growth may have also contributed to inequality.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that the Industrial Revolution and the economic divergence between Europe and Asia were driven by factors other than coal extraction. They assert that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution and that the great divergence was already underway before 1800. Clark and Jacks establish that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining were only modest during the Industrial Revolution era. This finding is supported by van Zanden, who contends that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. The modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining suggest that the Industrial Revolution was not solely driven by technological advancements in coal extraction. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) show that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that economic divergence was influenced by broader factors beyond coal extraction. This implies that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth was complex and multifaceted, involving technological factors and broader economic conditions.

Lindert and Williamson, together with Temin, examine various factors influencing economic growth, including policy dynamics and labor allocation (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; Temin 2002, p.2). They identify that growth and inequality trends are shaped by the interplay between total factor productivity and labor-force growth across sectors. This interplay is influenced by the elasticity of product demands and technological imbalance, which can drive secular inequality trends (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). Temin's work on Europe's post-WWII Golden Age further illustrates this dynamic. Temin shows that unusually rapid economic growth during this period was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.3). In other words, the shifts in labor allocation and productivity growth across sectors played a critical role in shaping economic growth patterns. This perspective suggests that while policy dynamics and labor allocation are crucial, the broader economic conditions, such as technological progress and demographic changes, also significantly influence long-run economic growth. Lindert and Williamson note that the magnitudes of these forces matter, and in some cases, they may not be strong enough to outweigh other factors such as human capital accumulation or the effects of Engel's Law on rural landed wealth (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.29). This qualification underscores the complexity of the factors influencing economic growth and the need for a nuanced understanding of how these dynamics interact over time.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study that highlights the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights. North and Weingast show that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in fostering economic growth by ensuring secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Similarly, North argues that the evolution of institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). He further emphasizes that institutional reliability allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, which is crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2). These insights underscore the foundational role of institutions in economic development. Institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing uncertainties and enabling individuals to engage in transactions with remote parties (North 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of technological and demographic changes. This qualification underscores the complexity of the factors influencing economic growth and the need for a nuanced understanding of how these dynamics interact over time. This finding suggests that the economic impact of technological changes depends on the institutional context in which they occur (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). For example, the growth of total factor productivity in coal mining was modest, indicating that institutional factors were more critical in driving economic growth during this period (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31).
