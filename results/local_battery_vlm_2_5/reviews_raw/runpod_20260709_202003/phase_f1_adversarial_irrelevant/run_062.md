The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is whether institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic and political interactions, are merely a reflection of economic conditions or active drivers of economic change. Two positions stand out in the debate. One position, rooted in neoclassical theories, emphasizes factors such as population growth and savings as primary determinants of economic growth. The opposing view, championed by institutional economists, posits that institutions themselves are crucial for fostering economic development. This debate is significant because it shapes our understanding of how economies evolve over time and what policies might be effective in promoting growth. A central theme in this debate is the extent to which institutions can be designed to support long-term economic growth, or whether they are merely a byproduct of broader economic and social changes. This question is particularly relevant for understanding the historical experiences of different regions and for informing contemporary development strategies. Institutions play a crucial role in the evolution of economic systems and are closely linked to the development of markets and property rights.

The institutional approach to economic growth, as advocated by North and Weingast, highlights the importance of secure property rights and credible commitments in fostering economic development. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). This view underscores the idea that institutions can shape economic outcomes by influencing the behavior of economic agents and the efficiency of markets. The institutional approach provides a framework for understanding how political and economic institutions interact to promote or hinder economic growth. This framework is essential for analysing the historical development of economies and for designing policies that support long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. establish that colonial institutions significantly shaped economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)(Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were often extractive and did not foster long-run growth. This institutional framework was influenced by European settlement patterns, which were in turn shaped by mortality rates faced by Europeans in these colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as Europeans were less likely to settle in areas where they faced significant health risks. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on economic performance, with extractive institutions persisting and significantly impacting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional differences among colonies, rooted in these historical factors, are a key determinant of current economic disparities. However, the persistence of these institutions suggests that while initial conditions were crucial, the path dependence of institutions also plays a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

This institutional framework is further examined by Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Hornbeck, who show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and supported elite interests, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). The institutions that evolved in these societies were shaped by initial factor endowments, such as the abundance of land relative to labor, which led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. These institutions, in turn, reinforced inequality through policies that favored elites, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. The mechanism through which these institutions operated was the restriction of competition, which was achieved through various government policies that advantaged the elite. This institutional framework not only maintained but also exacerbated inequality over time. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, for instance, enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This example illustrates how institutional changes can significantly impact economic development, albeit in a context where the initial conditions were more favorable for growth. However, Hornbeck also notes that the productivity gains from such institutional changes were not uniform across all regions, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and geographical factors in shaping economic outcomes (Hornbeck 2010, p.36).

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Whatley (2018, p.2) argue that factors such as coal and gunpowder imports played significant roles in economic development. Whatley's analysis of gunpowder imports supports the gun-slave cycle hypothesis, showing that imported gunpowder technology increased the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving (Whatley 2018, p.2). This suggests that technological imports can drive economic development by enhancing productivity in specific sectors. However, Whatley also notes that the economic development within slave trading networks was complex, involving various intermediate imports and social processes (Whatley 2018, p.15). This complexity highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, where specific factors interact with social and technological elements to shape long-run economic growth.
