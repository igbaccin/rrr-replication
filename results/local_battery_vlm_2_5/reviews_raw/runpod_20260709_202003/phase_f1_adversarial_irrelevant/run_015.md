The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can illuminate why some economies prosper while others stagnate. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they merely facilitate other underlying factors. One position argues that institutions, through their ability to reduce transaction costs and enforce property rights, are essential for economic growth. This view posits that efficient institutions create an environment conducive to investment, innovation, and economic activity. Another position suggests that institutions are secondary to technological progress and that economic growth is primarily driven by advances in technology and knowledge. This perspective holds that institutions play a supporting role, enabling the efficient use of technological innovations. A third position, less directly engaged with the topic, focuses on distributional conflicts as the primary determinant of institutional structures, suggesting that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and social conflicts rather than by economic efficiency. A fourth position, which also engages the topic only obliquely, highlights the role of specific technological innovations in enhancing property rights and reducing externalities, thereby indirectly influencing economic growth. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions is pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, with the political institutions of seventeenth-century England serving as a key case study. The development of political institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges significantly reduces transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth. North (1989, p.6) highlights that as economies grow, the emergence of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement becomes increasingly important. This institutional framework not only lowers costs per transaction but also expands the aggregate volume of exchanges, driving economic growth. The focus on political institutions governing public choice underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic development. This institutional framework is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This endogenous role of political institutions ensures that the state can maintain its commitments, such as not expropriating wealth, which is essential for sustained economic growth. The institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution in England illustrate how political institutions can improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were not designed to maximize long-run growth but rather to maximize the rents to European colonists. This extractive approach to institution-building was influenced by the mortality rates experienced by European settlers. Colonies with high mortality rates were less likely to attract European settlers, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions that prioritized the extraction of resources over long-term economic development. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring influence of colonial-era policies on contemporary economic conditions.

Economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved social welfare, as both Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) demonstrate. The decline in biological well-being during the Industrial Revolution, despite economic growth, underscores the intricate relationship between economic indicators and social welfare. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) highlights that technological progress and the growth of the capital stock can accelerate economic development, but these gains do not automatically improve the biological well-being of the population (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during this period illustrates that economic growth alone does not ensure equitable distribution of benefits across society. The relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). These findings suggest that the benefits of economic growth are not uniformly distributed and that social policies play a crucial role in ensuring that economic gains translate into improved well-being for all segments of society.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) contends that the efficiency approach has led to a misinterpretation of pre-modern institutions, such as merchant guilds and the Mesta, as efficient solutions to transactional obstacles. Instead, these institutions can be understood as the outcome of power struggles and distributional conflicts. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) adds that technological changes, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This suggests that while technological advancements can drive institutional changes, these changes are not necessarily efficient in the traditional sense but rather a response to underlying distributional conflicts. However, Hornbeck (2010, p.4) qualifies this by noting that the direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results, implying that the protection of property rights is more crucial. This underscores the importance of considering the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, as they can lead to institutional changes that may not be efficient but are nonetheless driven by the need to protect property rights and manage externalities. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) demonstrates that even craft guilds, traditionally seen as obstacles to technological change, can be understood as institutions that emerged from distributional conflicts and power struggles.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions are less critical. They posit that economic growth is fundamentally fueled by technological advancements, which in turn depend on the accumulation and recombination of existing knowledge. Mokyr highlights that technological progress often relies on "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process is not merely about individual genius but is deeply intertwined with the existing body of knowledge and the ability to identify gaps and opportunities within it. Kuznets (1973, p.10) complements this view by noting that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating how necessity can drive invention. This interdependence between technological progress and the existing knowledge base underscores the importance of a dynamic and adaptive environment for sustained economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.12) also recognizes that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress is necessary to complement and sustain economic improvements. Without institutional adjustments to manage coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded. This highlights a crucial downstream implication: while technological progress is essential, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that ensure its sustainable application.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both underscore that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements, arguing that without such progress, economic improvements can be eroded by coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly notes that the mass application of technological innovations, a key driver of modern economic growth, requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). These perspectives align with North (1989, p.6), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify and enforce efficient property rights and facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The shared finding is that institutional frameworks are indispensable for leveraging technological progress to drive long-run economic growth.

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. While North (1989, p.6) emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and the craft guild were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective is supported by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), who demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The historical evidence from Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that technological progress in agriculture, such as the introduction of barbed wire, required institutional adjustments to fully realize its economic benefits. This empirical comparison settles the debate by illustrating that while institutions may be shaped by distributional conflicts, they are also essential for leveraging technological progress to drive long-run economic growth.
