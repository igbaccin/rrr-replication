The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic expansion. One live disagreement centers around the efficacy of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving long-term economic performance, while others highlight the importance of technological innovations and natural resource endowments. Additionally, there is debate over the timing and nature of institutional changes that catalyze economic growth, with some arguing for gradual evolution and others for abrupt, revolutionary shifts. Furthermore, the interplay between institutions and other factors, such as population dynamics and savings rates, adds complexity to the discussion. Institutions matter because they provide the rules and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic behavior and outcomes. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely complementary factors remains unresolved. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not merely academic; it has practical implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can inform efforts to promote sustainable development and reduce poverty. In this context, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of inquiry, as it sheds light on the fundamental processes that underpin economic development. This review will explore how institutions have been understood to influence economic growth, with a focus on the causal mechanisms proposed by various scholars. North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) argue that the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They posit that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 was a critical juncture in this evolution, as it established a political framework that constrained the sovereign's ability to expropriate wealth. This institutional change enhanced the credibility of commitments to property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. North further elaborates on this mechanism, asserting that as economies grow and become more complex, the need for reliable institutions that can enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges increases (North 1989, p.2). The establishment of such institutions is a complex process. North and Weingast note that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, highlighting the importance of endogenous political institutions in maintaining the state's credibility (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This interplay between political institutions and economic growth suggests that the development of credible commitments is a dynamic process, shaped by both political and economic factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the colonial institutions introduced by European powers have had a lasting impact on the economic development of former colonies, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists. For example, colonies with high mortality rates for Europeans, such as those in Africa and Latin America, were more likely to develop extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable economic growth. These institutions, designed to maximize short-term gains for colonial powers, persist today and continue to hinder economic development. The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of historical context in understanding current economic disparities. The role of these institutions in shaping long-run economic growth varies across former colonies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that in some colonies, such as those in North America and Australia, the establishment of inclusive institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment led to significant economic growth. This variation highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development, suggesting that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth depends on the specific historical and contextual factors at play. These institutions, shaped by the political and economic interests of colonial powers, have had a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectories of former colonies, illustrating the enduring influence of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Hornbeck, show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the initial factor endowments of these societies, characterized by an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was further entrenched by institutions that favored elites, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. Hornbeck's study on the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains illustrates a specific mechanism through which property rights and externalities from livestock damage influenced agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This mechanism highlights how secure property rights can enhance productivity and economic growth, but it also underscores that the benefits of such institutions are not universally distributed. In societies with high inequality, the advantages of secure property rights may be concentrated among the elite, exacerbating existing disparities.

Van Zanden and Komlos both challenge the idea that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 necessarily improved the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Van Zanden counters the "optimistic" narrative about early modern economic growth by arguing that it did not translate into significant improvements in living standards. He critiques the traditional view that agricultural productivity growth in France between 1450 and 1800 was a sign of economic progress. Komlos extends this critique to the Industrial Revolution, showing that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to inadequate income gains and poor childhood nourishment. His analysis reveals that the relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food, and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods, a pattern that undermines the conventional view that aggregate purchasing power captures living standards. The implication of these findings is that economic growth alone does not guarantee improvements in the standard of living. Indeed, the biological well-being of the population can decline even as economic growth proceeds.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This claim is shared by Austin, who critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation (Austin 2008, p.11). Broadberry and Gupta's argument hinges on the productivity gap between Europe and Asia, which was already significant prior to 1800. This gap was driven by differences in labour productivity in the tradable goods sector, with Europe exhibiting higher productivity. This productivity gap was further exacerbated by the structural differences in agricultural practices. In Europe, the productivity revolution in agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering growth-enhancing articulations between town and country (Bryant 2006, p.29). This structural differentiation was absent in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts, limiting the scope for economies of scale and capital accumulation (Bryant 2006, p.29). These differences in agricultural practices and labour productivity had profound implications for economic development, with Europe's structural changes leading to increased urbanisation and industrialisation. Bryant qualifies this narrative by highlighting the demographic realities that governed economic considerations in both regions, particularly the role of land scarcity and labour surplus in China (Bryant 2006, p.28). These factors constrained China's ability to adopt labour-saving technologies and concentrate employment in town workshops and factories, despite the potential benefits of such structural changes. These findings suggest that the great divergence was a result of institutional differences and deep-seated structural and demographic factors that shaped economic trajectories.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Pamuk (2007, p.8) argue that economic factors such as resource availability and technological constraints played crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth, often independent of institutional frameworks. Clark and Jacks, for example, demonstrate that coal extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output during the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining were modest at best. This finding challenges the notion that coal was a primary driver of industrial growth. Pamuk (2007, p.8) similarly highlights the long-term economic impacts of the Black Death, showing that the pandemic initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These economic shifts were driven by demographic and labor market dynamics. Pamuk (2007, p.15) further elaborates that the decline in the skill premium under severe labor shortages encouraged human capital formation, which had lasting effects on economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.15) also notes that this trend varied across Europe, as the skill premium in Italy rebounded with population recovery. This variability underscores the complex interplay between economic factors and long-run growth, which can be both regionally specific and temporally contingent. Therefore, the role of economic factors in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, influenced by both immediate resource constraints and long-term structural changes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is critical, as they provide the framework within which economic activities occur. The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is central to fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North and Weingast argue that the Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby enhancing economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These changes are part of a broader process where institutions evolve to reduce uncertainties and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). North shows that as economies grow more complex, institutional reliability becomes essential for maintaining confidence in remote transactions (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability allows for the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, which is crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.2).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different regions and periods.
