The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development theory. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules of the game in a society, influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives and constraints. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as technological advancements or geographic endowments, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they determine the efficiency of resource allocation and the protection of property rights, while others posit that technological progress and social changes are the key drivers. The debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth can inform strategies for fostering sustainable economic development. Kuznets and Abramovitz share a focus on the interplay between technological progress and economic growth, highlighting the importance of innovation and institutional adaptation. Kuznets emphasises that technological innovations are closely tied to the specific needs of the countries where they originate, suggesting that institutional adjustments are necessary for the efficient use of these innovations. Abramovitz extends this idea by noting that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth and the need for a holistic approach that considers both factors. Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.” Abramovitz (1986, p.4) reports, “It needs extension and qualification.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate that these institutions complement reputation-building strategies and act as constraints to prevent reneging on commitments, thereby enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also highlight the role of institutions in maintaining the state's ability to uphold bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating wealth. This institutional framework is crucial for economic growth, as it ensures that the rules governing economic exchange are both enforced and adaptable. However, North (1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it overlooks the critical role of institutions in economic output. This suggests that while institutions are essential for economic growth, their impact is not merely about enforcing rules but also about creating an environment where economic exchanges can thrive. The development of such institutions is therefore a key factor in understanding long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. One specific mechanism is that the type of colonial institutions established was heavily influenced by the mortality rates of European settlers. High mortality rates in areas like Africa and Latin America led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This extractive institutional framework persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. This pattern suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting effects on the institutional environment, which in turn influenced economic performance. The impact of these institutions is evident in the significant fraction of current income differences that can be attributed to the institutional legacies of colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The enduring effect of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding the historical context in which they were formed and how they continue to shape economic outcomes today.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped pre-industrial economic structures like serfdom and guilds, challenging institutional explanations for these outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie's critique of efficiency-based explanations is supported by Domar's analysis, which highlights that the profitability of slavery or serfdom is influenced by the land-to-labor ratio and the scarcity of labor. Domar argues that in societies with a high land-to-labor ratio and scarce labor, slavery or serfdom emerges as a means to secure labor for agricultural production. This perspective aligns with Ogilvie's contention that the efficiency of institutions is not the primary driver of their formation. Instead, the distribution of resources and power among different social groups plays a crucial role. Ogilvie's argument is further supported by Domar's observation that in traditional societies without technological progress and capital accumulation, the end of slavery is more certain as the marginal product of labor descends to the subsistence level. This dynamic underscores the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. However, Domar also notes that technological progress and greater use of capital do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared with free labor. This qualification suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, other economic conditions can also influence the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds. This interplay between distributional conflicts and economic conditions highlights the complexity of institutional formation and persistence in pre-industrial economies.

Historical events and technological changes have shaped long-run economic development through complex pathways that extend beyond direct institutional influences. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) both show that specific technological changes and historical events influenced economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) finds that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological change directly improved agricultural productivity by allowing for more efficient land use and better protection of crops. Pamuk (2007, p.22) argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This historical event triggered a substitution of land and capital for labor across the continent, driving technological innovations in urban guilds. Pamuk (2007, p.22) emphasizes that the urban guilds, rather than rural industries, were the producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe. This finding highlights the role of historical events in shaping long-term economic structures, even if they do not directly target institutions. While these papers do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, they illustrate how technological changes and historical events can have profound and lasting effects on economic development. These effects can, in turn, influence the institutional landscape over time, as economic structures adapt to new technologies and historical shocks.

Technological progress, not institutions, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets argues that technological innovations, often developed to meet specific national needs, are pivotal in driving economic growth. This is particularly true for countries undergoing modernization, where technological progress is closely linked to scientific advancements. Abramovitz extends this idea by noting that the process of catching up in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advancement and social change. This catch-up process is complex and involves not just technological adoption but also broader social and economic transformations. For instance, Abramovitz shows that technological opportunities encourage rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor. This dynamic highlights how technological progress can lead to increased productivity and aggregate output, driving economic growth. Abramovitz also points out that the backwardness of a country can present an opportunity for modernization. This modernization can occur through both embodied and disembodied technology, suggesting that the catch-up process is multifaceted and not solely dependent on technological progress. This implies that while technological progress is a significant driver of economic growth, the institutional landscape and social changes play a crucial role in facilitating this progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that economic growth is driven by technological progress, which is closely connected with the further progress of science and the application of technological innovations. This view aligns with Abramovitz's (1986, p.4) observation that the modernization process, which includes both embodied and disembodied technology, is crucial for economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) extends this idea by noting that the same technological opportunity that permits rapid progress through modernization also encourages the growth of the capital stock, which in turn accelerates economic growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by North (1989, p.5), who argues that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading. North (1989, p.6) contends that institutions matter because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who show that institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups, rather than to maximize long-run growth. This indicates that the institutional landscape can significantly influence the direction and pace of economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) provides a counterpoint by arguing that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) notes that various pre-modern institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to specific obstacles, but this efficiency perspective may overlook the underlying power dynamics. This view is complemented by Hornbeck (2010, p.4), who shows that technological innovations like barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) further illustrates that direct technological effects are not the sole drivers of economic growth, as institutional changes also play a significant role. The historical evidence of barbed wire fencing's impact on agricultural development shows that institutional changes can enhance economic growth by reducing transaction costs and protecting property rights. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement between Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts and Hornbeck's emphasis on technological and institutional changes, demonstrating that both factors are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth.
