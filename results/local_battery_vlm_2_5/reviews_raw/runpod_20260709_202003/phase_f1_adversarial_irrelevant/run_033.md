The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve from stagnation to sustained economic progress. A live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights is crucial for fostering economic growth. Another position posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. A third position highlights that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, suggesting that institutional factors were at play much earlier than often assumed. The interplay between these positions underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth. The centrality of institutions in economic growth is evident in the historical context of seventeenth-century England, where North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework underpinned the development of markets and the enforcement of rules, which in turn facilitated economic exchanges and growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the remarkable evidence from capital markets, where secure property rights encouraged investment and economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Furthermore, the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency is highlighted by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties and enabling complex interdependent situations, which are necessary for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange.

The specific institutional changes that North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) highlight in seventeenth-century England are particularly illustrative of how institutional reliability can foster secure property rights. This institutional framework was crucial for specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a significant turning point, as it established political institutions that constrained the state's ex post behavior, thereby enhancing its credibility and commitment to maintaining property rights. This credibility was essential for economic growth, as it encouraged investment and economic activity by reducing uncertainties and enabling complex interdependent situations. North (1989, p.2) further elaborates on this point, emphasizing that institutional reliability is vital for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) clarify that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism. This institutional framework, by reducing uncertainties and enhancing market efficiency, laid the foundation for long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) extend this idea by examining how colonial institutions, which were often imposed from outside, could similarly shape long-run economic outcomes. These scholars demonstrate that the design of colonial institutions was primarily driven by the need to maximize rents for European colonists. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who show that the mortality rates faced by European settlers in the colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to the creation of extractive institutions, which were designed to exploit the colonies' resources. This institutional framework, characterized by extractive policies, persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these regions. The extractive nature of these institutions, which were not conducive to long-term economic development, highlights the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) further emphasize that the differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks, which in turn affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by showing that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic development is complex and multifaceted. This suggests that while extractive institutions may have hindered long-run economic growth in some colonies, they also played a role in the economic development of others.

This variability in outcomes underscores that the impact of extractive institutions on economic growth is not uniform, as van Zanden (1999, p.2) notes for Europe between 1500 and 1800. This is evident in the stagnation of real wages and the decline in biological well-being despite economic growth. The optimists' view of economic growth as a driver of higher living standards is challenged by evidence showing that real wages declined in the pre-industrial period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Komlos (1998, p.13) adds that during the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to poor childhood nourishment and insufficient income gains (Komlos 1998, p.13). This suggests that the relationship between economic growth and living standards is complex and multifaceted.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (2008, p.11) argue that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia began well before 1800, driven by differences in productivity and wages. They contend that the lower silver wages in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector. This divergence stemmed from variations in economic structures and factor endowments, as well as institutional differences. Austin (2008, p.11) elaborates on this by highlighting that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa shows that most of the region was characterized by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land and capital. This labour scarcity, coupled with specific aspects of the natural environment, influenced the choice of production techniques and, consequently, labour productivity. This dynamic implies that economic growth trajectories were shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including natural endowments and institutional contexts. Austin (2008, p.1) further notes that economic rent and economic growth were often joint products, underscoring the importance of historical context in understanding economic development. This suggests that the institutions favoring economic growth in certain historical contexts may not have been optimal in others, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the institutional factors at play. The complexity of these interactions underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth and the importance of considering a wide range of factors when examining long-run economic trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Hornbeck, further illustrate this complexity by demonstrating how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). These institutions often took the form of policies that protected elite interests, such as those that secured property rights for large landowners while limiting the ability of smaller farmers to compete effectively. This institutional framework maintained and exacerbated economic disparities. The persistence of these inequality-enhancing institutions can be understood through the lens of elite capture, where powerful groups shape policies to maintain their privileged status. This dynamic is evident in the historical development of many New World societies, where elites used their political influence to secure favorable policies that reinforced their economic dominance. Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains illustrates how property rights and agricultural development are intertwined. While barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural productivity by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage, it also had the effect of consolidating landholdings in the hands of larger farmers, further entrenching inequality (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This mechanism underscores how institutional changes can have both direct and indirect effects on economic development, with indirect effects often reinforcing existing power structures. The implications of these institutional dynamics are profound, as they suggest that the path to inclusive economic growth is fraught with challenges rooted in historical inequalities and the persistence of elite-dominated institutions.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating the conditions necessary for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The institutional framework, by specifying and enforcing property rights, allows for the development of complex social frameworks that support economic growth. The reliability of these institutions is crucial, as it enables individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence, even in highly interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the interplay between institutional changes and economic development. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting the significance of institutional differences in shaping economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This divergence is underpinned by differences in labour productivity and institutional structures that facilitated or hindered economic growth. Acemoglu et al. build on this by arguing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies, showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not prioritize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional framework supports economic growth and shapes the distribution of its benefits.

The historical evidence shows that institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth. Van Zanden argues that there was no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that institutional changes did not uniformly lead to improved living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This points to the complexity of institutional impacts, where economic growth does not necessarily translate into widespread prosperity. Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic growth varies across contexts and time periods (Austin 2008, p.11). The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Austin lies in the measurement of labour productivity and the distribution of economic rents, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies.
