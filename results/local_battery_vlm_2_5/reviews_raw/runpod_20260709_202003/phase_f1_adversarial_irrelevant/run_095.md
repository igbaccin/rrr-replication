The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. The explanation for why some economies thrive while others stagnate, and the extent to which institutions drive this divergence, is at stake. The live disagreement centers around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or natural resources, in fostering economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for economic development, while others contend that institutions are merely a side effect of economic growth or that they do not play a central role. A third perspective suggests that institutions are important but that their role is often overshadowed by other factors. This debate has significant implications for policymaking and our understanding of economic development.

The significance of institutions in economic growth is highlighted by the work of North and Weingast, who emphasize the role of political institutions in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They point out that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve its ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. North and Weingast's analysis underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic growth by highlighting the institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution as a key factor in England's economic development. This perspective is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as he contends that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the evolution of more complex social frameworks will not occur if such institutional structures cannot reduce the uncertainties associated with such situations. Therefore, institutional reliability is essential for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. This theme of institutional reliability and its impact on economic growth is a recurring one. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This perspective highlights the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes, as the institutions introduced by colonialism had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. The significance of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the work of Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. Their analysis of the role of labour productivity in the tradable goods sector in Asia and Europe highlights the importance of institutional factors in determining economic outcomes. They point out that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn is influenced by institutional factors. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as the divergence between Europe and Asia can be traced back to institutional differences that affected labour productivity and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework was not merely about establishing rules for economic exchange, but also about creating mechanisms for enforcing these rules and ensuring they could be reliably maintained. North and Weingast highlight that the Glorious Revolution served as a critical turning point, where political institutions were restructured to enhance the state's ability to uphold its commitments to constituents, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability is crucial as it allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, even when dealing with strangers. The establishment of such property rights enables individuals to participate in impersonal exchange, which is fundamental for economic growth (North 1989, p.2). North also points out that the evolution of these institutional frameworks is contingent on their ability to reduce uncertainties associated with complex social interactions. This highlights that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to the increasing complexity of economic and social systems. The development of reliable political institutions is thus a prerequisite for sustained economic growth, as it provides the necessary stability and predictability for economic actors to operate effectively.

The institutional frameworks established during colonialism had a profound impact on the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27). These institutions were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, particularly their desire to maximize rents. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates is a key mechanism through which colonialism influenced economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the formation of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, showing that the initial conditions set by colonial powers had lasting effects on the institutional frameworks of these regions. This perspective does not fully account for the potential adaptive capacity of institutions over time. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) acknowledge that while institutions play a crucial role in economic growth, the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is context-dependent, influenced by the interplay of historical factors and contemporary economic conditions.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Komlos (1998, p.13) critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for its oversimplification of historical causation and period compression. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the exchange-value product of Africans' labour in Africa was lower than in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken, and that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are most plausibly understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment. Komlos (1998, p.13) argues that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment, contra conventional views based on aggregate purchasing power. This suggests that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards, especially when income gains are insufficient to offset the negative effects of industrialization. This pattern is further illustrated by Pamuk (2007, p.8), who shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These structural changes did not uniformly benefit all regions, as the linkages between urban and rural sectors were not always equally strong. Pamuk (2007, p.8) shows that the skill premium began to decline under the conditions of severe labour shortages. This decline continued with the drop in interest rates across Europe in the aftermath of the Black Death, which should be expected to encourage human capital formation.

This perspective highlights the importance of energy sources and productivity gains in shaping economic development. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining were modest during the Industrial Revolution, challenging the notion that coal was a primary driver of economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) extend this perspective by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, suggesting that productivity differences were crucial in driving economic divergence. This implies that early economic divergences were influenced more by productivity gains and energy sources than by institutional frameworks. Broadberry and Gupta's focus on productivity and wages does not fully account for the role of technological advancements and industrial innovations in driving economic growth. The mechanism through which productivity gains and energy sources shape economic development remains an open question.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature. Institutions underpin economic development by fostering secure property rights and supporting economic growth via political factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Furthermore, institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In the context of the development experience of the former colonies, this implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This includes the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, which should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The institutional requirements that are necessary in order to be able to realize the productivity gains associated with the model of impersonal exchange entail both the development of efficient products and factor markets and of a medium of exchange with reliable features (North 1989, p.2).
