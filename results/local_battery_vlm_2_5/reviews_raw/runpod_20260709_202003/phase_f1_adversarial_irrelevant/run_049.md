The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, shaping economic incentives and constraints. This question is intellectually significant because it challenges the primacy of other factors, such as geography or culture, in economic development. At the heart of the debate lie two key positions. One position argues that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This position emphasizes the role of political factors in underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. The other position highlights the role of extractive institutions in maximizing rents to politically powerful groups, promoting long-run growth. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are designed to maximize economic development or to serve the interests of specific groups. Institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how they are structured and enforced. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.”

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights are a necessary condition for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). In the case of seventeenth-century England, the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The political factors underpinning economic growth encompass the institutions governing how these rules are enforced, the rules governing economic exchange. North and Weingast highlight that appropriately chosen institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. The Glorious Revolution of 1688, for example, marked a significant shift in institutional constraints on the state's ability to expropriate wealth, thereby enhancing the credibility of commitments to property rights. This institutional evolution was critical for economic growth, as it reduced uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others. North notes that the institutional requirements necessary for realizing productivity gains entail both the development of efficient product and factor markets and a reliable medium of exchange. These institutional changes facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, contributing to economic growth by enabling increasing specialization and division of labor. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is thus a key factor in economic development.

Acemoglu et al. propose that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly shaped current income per capita, suggesting that these specific institutional changes are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, had a lasting impact on the economic development of former colonies. This is because the institutions established during colonialism were shaped by politically powerful groups that sought to maintain their control and extract resources, leading to the creation of extractive institutions that hindered economic growth. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, where Europeans were less likely to settle, is a key mechanism through which colonialism affected long-run economic growth. These institutions persisted over time and significantly influenced current income per capita. The correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the establishment of European settlements, early measures of institutions, and institutions today underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The role of European settlements in shaping institutions is complex. The presence of European settlers could also lead to the development of institutions that protect property rights and encourage economic growth, depending on the specific historical and political context. This qualification highlights the complexity of the relationship between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth, as the impact of these institutions can vary across different settings.

This institutional bias toward elites, which is also shared by Hornbeck, was influenced by the factor endowments of these societies. The abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, combined with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power, led to the development of institutions that protected the interests of the elite. These institutions, in turn, shaped the economic trajectory of these societies by limiting opportunities for broader economic participation and innovation. This dynamic is evident in the agricultural sector, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This development highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth, even within the constraints imposed by pre-existing inequalities. The persistence of these inequalities suggests that the benefits of such institutional changes may not be evenly distributed, potentially leading to long-term economic disparities. This underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, as the impact of these institutions can vary across different settings and historical contexts.

This complexity is further illustrated by the relationship between economic development and the standard of living in Europe from 1500 to 1800, which is not straightforwardly positive. van Zanden argues that the traditional view of stagnation in Europe’s early modern economic growth is being challenged by recent optimists, who contend that there was significant economic growth during this period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This optimism is supported by Komlos, who notes the rapid urbanization and industrialization during the Industrial Revolution, with cities like London and Paris seeing substantial population growth (Komlos 1998, p.13). Komlos also points out that despite these economic developments, biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). This suggests that while economic growth was occurring, it did not translate into improved living standards for the general population. The implications of this finding are significant, as it indicates that economic growth alone is not sufficient to improve the standard of living; other factors, such as income distribution and nutrition, play crucial roles. This tension between economic growth and the standard of living highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which economic development impacts societal well-being.

The Great Divergence between Europe and Asia began well before 1800, driven by structural changes in wages and productivity, and the Black Death's impact on European labor markets (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Pamuk 2007, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that arbitrage led to a single international price for tradable goods, measured in silver, which reflected productivity differences between Europe and Asia. This period saw severe labor shortages, which drove down the skill premium and encouraged human capital formation. Pamuk (2007, p.8) notes that these changes were not uniform across Europe; for instance, Italy experienced a resurgence in the skill premium in the sixteenth century as its population recovered. This variability suggests that while the Black Death and subsequent labor market changes were significant drivers of the Great Divergence, regional differences in response to these changes also played a crucial role. These findings imply that the Great Divergence was a complex interplay of factors, including productivity differences, labor market changes, and regional responses to these changes.

The roles of coal and distributional conflicts in economic development are crucial to understanding the complex interplay of factors driving economic change. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) both highlight that specific factors, such as the role of coal and the nature of distributional conflicts, can significantly influence economic outcomes. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution. They demonstrate this by examining extraction costs and total factor productivity in coal mining, finding only modest gains in productivity over the Industrial Revolution era. This perspective challenges the notion that coal was a primary driver of industrial growth, suggesting instead that other factors, such as technological advancements and institutional changes, were more critical. Ogilvie presents a complementary view, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She critiques the efficiency approach, which often interprets institutions as solutions to transactional obstacles, and instead emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts in shaping these institutions. This framework suggests that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts within society. Ogilvie's analysis of the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework further qualifies the implications of Clark and Jacks's findings. By recognizing the interdependency of institutions, Ogilvie underscores that the impact of coal on economic development cannot be isolated from the broader institutional context. This interdependency means that even if coal's role was modest, its effects were mediated through the complex web of institutions and power dynamics that characterized pre-industrial Europe. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about how they are shaped by and respond to broader economic and social conflicts.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and ensuring their enforcement. North and Weingast argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England, facilitated by political institutions, was crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with Europe's institutional framework supporting higher productivity and economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This institutional advantage is further underscored by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The role of institutions in economic growth is about creating a stable and predictable environment that encourages investment and innovation.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks. Clark and Jacks's findings on the modest role of coal in the Industrial Revolution highlight that technological progress alone is insufficient to drive economic growth without a supportive institutional context (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). The institutional framework can either facilitate or hinder the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, thereby influencing long-run economic growth. Van Zanden's analysis of economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 shows that changes in the standard of living were shaped by broader institutional and economic factors (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This interdependency between technology and institutions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where institutions act as both enablers and constraints.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about how they are shaped by and respond to broader economic and social conflicts. Institutions that provide credible commitments and secure property rights foster economic growth by reducing uncertainty and encouraging investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The evolution of institutions is influenced by power dynamics and social conflicts. For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can stifle economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is about the power dynamics that shape their evolution. The historical evidence of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England and the divergence between Europe and Asia provides a clear measurement of the role of institutions in economic growth. The empirical comparison of productivity gains and the development of secure property rights shows that institutions are a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth.
