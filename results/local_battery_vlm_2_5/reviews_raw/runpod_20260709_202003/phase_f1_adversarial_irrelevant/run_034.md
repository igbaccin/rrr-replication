The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain economic progress over extended periods. One position argues that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity, while another position contends that technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are the primary drivers of economic growth. A third perspective focuses on the efficiency of institutions in addressing distributional conflicts and ensuring the optimal use of resources. This debate is crucial for policymakers and economists seeking to understand the mechanisms behind sustained economic development and to design effective policies that promote growth. The interplay between institutions and technological progress, as well as the efficiency of institutions in addressing economic challenges, highlights the complexity of long-run economic growth. Institutions, by providing a stable framework for economic activity, can significantly influence the trajectory of economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Abramovitz (1986, p.4) reports, “It needs extension and qualification.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in fostering economic growth by governing public choice and enforcing property rights. The evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England, for example, was instrumental in creating a stable framework that encouraged market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were effectively enforced. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) further elucidate that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of economic transactions by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional setup is crucial for maintaining the state's credibility and preventing expropriation of wealth, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic activity. This reduction in costs per transaction, coupled with an increasing specialization and division of labor, multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges, contributing to a growing proportion of GNP. However, North (1989, p.5) also notes that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it does not account for the critical role of institutions in economic growth. The institutional framework is a fundamental determinant of long-run economic growth.

This enhancement was about the institutional environment that allowed technology to flourish, as seen in the case of the barbed wire fencing on the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This technology allowed farmers to demarcate and secure their land more effectively, which was crucial for agricultural expansion. This protection was essential for farmers to invest in their land without fear of encroachment from neighboring livestock. Pamuk (2007, p.22) underscores the broader implications of technological advancements in property rights protection, noting that such developments can drive long-term structural changes in economic activities. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) qualifies this by observing that the direct technological effects of barbed wire, while important, did not drive the main results of agricultural development. Instead, the increased protection from others' cattle played a more significant role in fostering growth. This suggests that while technological innovations are crucial, the institutional framework that supports and protects these innovations is equally, if not more, important for sustained economic development. The role of property rights in shaping long-run economic growth is about the institutional environment that allows technology to flourish.

This perspective, however, contrasts with Temin's argument that the rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.4; Komlos 1998, p.8). This disequilibrium created a unique economic environment where labor was not optimally distributed, leading to a surge in productivity and economic growth. The shift in labor allocation was a broader structural adjustment that facilitated growth. However, this growth did not uniformly benefit all sectors, as evidenced by the divergent trends in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during this period (Komlos 1998, p.8). The relative price of nutrients increased significantly, partly due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry, which affected the consumption patterns of lower-income groups. This economic growth, driven by labor reallocation, had profound implications for societal well-being, particularly in terms of nutrition and health. The relative price of food rose by 66 percent between 1770 and 1795 in Britain, for example, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). This substitution highlights the complex interplay between economic growth and nutritional outcomes, where rapid growth can lead to inequities in health and well-being. While Temin's analysis focuses on the post-WWII period, Komlos's findings on nutritional trends during the Industrial Revolution suggest that similar dynamics may have been at play, with economic growth not always translating into improved biological well-being for all segments of society.

This complexity, as Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue, is influenced by various factors beyond just institutions. The dynamics of technological advance and social change lead to a reduction in the technological age towards chronological age, as well as an increase in the capital-labor ratio. This process is further complicated by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) highlight that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design institutions to maximize their rents. This perspective is crucial in understanding the development experience of former colonies, where institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by showing that institutions played a significant role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, suggesting that while institutions are important, their impact is mediated by other factors. This interplay between technological advance, social change, and institutional design underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where various elements beyond just institutions contribute to the overall development trajectory.

This interplay suggests that distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe fundamentally shaped core economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) establish that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie discusses how merchant guilds were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods'. This efficiency was driven by the need to address information asymmetries and principal-agent problems in early modern long-distance trade. Domar complements this by arguing that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, highlighting the economic pressures that drive institutional changes. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are designed purely for efficiency, suggesting instead that they are shaped by the underlying distributional conflicts and economic conditions of the time. Domar's analysis of the profitability of slavery versus free labor further underscores this point, showing that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor. This implies that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and slavery can be understood through the lens of distributional conflicts, where the economic benefits of these institutions outweigh the costs. This view suggests that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were not just efficient solutions to transaction problems but were deeply rooted in the social and economic struggles of the time.

This view is supported by Mokyr and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10), who propose that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions are less critical. Mokyr outlines how technological progress depends on the accumulation of useful knowledge, often involving the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking. This process involves a broader societal understanding of available technologies, which can guide further innovation. Kuznets emphasizes that technological progress is closely linked to scientific advancements, creating a positive feedback loop where the mass application of innovations drives further scientific and technological developments. This interplay suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and technological advancements are self-reinforcing, creating a cycle of innovation that propels economic growth. This dynamic is further illuminated by Kuznets' observation that technological progress often addresses specific needs, making it particularly effective in contexts where those needs are prominent. Mokyr cautions that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible, institutional progress is less so. This implies that while technological advancements may drive economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on complementary institutional developments. Without these, the benefits of technological progress could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the importance of institutional support even in a technology-driven growth model. This underscores that while technological progress is crucial, institutional factors remain significant in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of institutional development. Institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enabling efficient market exchanges. North argues that political institutions specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development. He contends that the emergence of these institutions leads to falling costs per transaction, thereby stimulating economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Kuznets, who highlights that technological progress, a key driver of economic growth, requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Together, these views underscore that institutions are not just passive frameworks but active agents that shape the trajectory of economic growth by influencing how technological advancements are adopted and utilized.

Furthermore, the sustainability of economic growth hinges on the complementary development of institutions. Mokyr emphasizes that technological progress, while crucial, is not sufficient on its own. He argues that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the benefits of innovation can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This aligns with Kuznets' observation that technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for additional technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interdependence shows that institutions play a critical role in sustaining the benefits of technological progress over the long run.

This synthesis highlights the underlying agreement that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions provide the necessary framework for technological progress to be effectively harnessed. The precise nature of this institutional support remains a point of disagreement. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). In contrast, Mokyr emphasizes the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements, preventing the erosion of benefits through rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The historical evidence of the Industrial Revolution in England, where institutions like property rights and contract enforcement mechanisms were strengthened, settles this disagreement. This period shows that the development of effective institutional frameworks was instrumental in sustaining long-run economic growth by enabling the efficient use of technological innovations.
