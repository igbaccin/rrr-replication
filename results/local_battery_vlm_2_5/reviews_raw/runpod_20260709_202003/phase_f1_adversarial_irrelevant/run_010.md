The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, or more formally, the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction (North 1989, p.2). The extent to which economic growth is driven by institutional factors is at stake, along with other variables such as population growth, technological innovation, or natural resource endowments. One position holds that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the stable framework necessary for market exchanges and investment. Another position argues that other factors, such as resource endowments and technological progress, are more significant drivers of economic growth. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or merely a facilitator of other more fundamental drivers. The debate is significant because it informs policy recommendations for economic development and growth. Institutions can be shaped by policy, while other drivers of growth, such as natural resources, are often beyond immediate policy control. This makes the role of institutions in economic growth a critical area of study. Institutions actively shape the long-run growth trajectory of economies, not just serving as a backdrop for economic activity. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They emphasize that the evolution of these institutions involved establishing rules for economic exchange, as well as creating mechanisms for enforcing and changing these rules. The degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to these rules was a critical factor in economic growth. North and Weingast highlight that the institutions governing public choice in England during this period complemented reputation-building strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework was essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating wealth. This perspective underscores the importance of credible commitments in economic growth, where institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that economic actors can trust in the stability of their property rights and the enforcement of contracts. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.6). However, the reliability of these institutions is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with strangers in a highly specialized economy. This institutional reliability is necessary for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2). The implication is that the evolution of these institutions was about creating a stable environment where economic actors could operate with confidence, in addition to economic efficiency. This highlights the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and stable economic interactions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to promote long-run growth. This extractive nature of colonial institutions is evident in the way they were structured to benefit the colonizers at the expense of the indigenous population. The high mortality rates faced by Europeans in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This is because the colonizers, fearing the loss of their political power or simply being reluctant to initiate institutional change, designed institutions that would not directly benefit the local population but would instead maximize their own gains (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic development. This extractive framework hindered economic growth during the colonial period and left a lasting legacy that continues to influence the economic performance of these countries today. The institutional framework inherited from colonial rule has proven to be a significant barrier to economic development, as it has created a system that is inherently extractive and resistant to change. This institutional legacy underscores the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by illustrating how the design and persistence of extractive institutions can stifle economic development over extended periods.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) contend that the evolution of institutions in New World societies was deeply influenced by the level of inequality present at their founding. This inequality shaped institutions in ways that privileged elites, creating policies that restricted competition and entrenched disparities. In societies where elites held disproportionate power, institutions were designed to protect their interests, often at the expense of broader economic development. For instance, the political power of elites led to the implementation of policies that favored large landowners and restricted the entry of new competitors, thereby maintaining the status quo. This institutional design, in turn, limited the scope for economic growth by stifling innovation and restricting the mobility of labor and capital. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) illustrates how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This example highlights the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth, a factor that was often missing in societies characterized by high inequality. The persistence of these institutions over time further solidified the economic and social divisions, making it difficult for these societies to achieve sustained economic development. This dynamic underscores how the initial conditions of inequality can have long-lasting effects on the institutional framework and, consequently, on economic growth.

Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both find that economic development in Europe did not uniformly translate into improved living standards. The latter's finding that the Industrial Revolution did not improve biological well-being shows that the relationship between economic development and living standards is not straightforward. The Industrial Revolution brought about significant economic growth, yet the benefits were not evenly distributed. This period saw a divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups, as the latter struggled with insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This pattern suggests that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, and that certain groups may have been left behind.

Clark and Jacks and Broadberry and Gupta together show that the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence can be explained primarily by technological barriers and international price arbitrage (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). They further estimate that gains in total factor productivity in coal mining were modest, indicating that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). Broadberry and Gupta show that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn influenced the price of non-tradable goods (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This mechanism suggests that economic divergence was deeply rooted in technological and productivity disparities, in addition to institutional differences. This perspective overlooks the role of institutions in shaping these technological and productivity outcomes, leaving open the question of how institutional frameworks might have influenced the conditions that led to economic divergence.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Bryant (2006, p.29) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views debate the historical and geographical determinants of economic development, without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.11) notes that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa shows that, until well into the 20th century, the region was characterised by a scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land and capital. This scarcity was not inconsistent with higher real per capita income, as evidenced by higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population. However, the productivity of labour in Africa remained low, which can be understood by considering the choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). This suggests that the economic divergence between regions was influenced by the specific institutional frameworks that governed the choice of production techniques and the allocation of resources. Bryant (2006, p.29) further elaborates on this by highlighting the differences in agricultural productivity between Europe and China. In Europe, the productivity revolution in agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. This structural differentiation was absent in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts, restricting the scope for economies of scale and limiting both the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production and the capital accumulation necessary for its expansion (Bryant 2006, p.29). This pattern of economic organisation in China was governed by demographic realities of mounting land scarcity and a surplus of cheap labour, which decisively governed all economic considerations, even to the point of resisting the spread of labour-saving technologies. Austin (2008, p.11) qualifies this pattern by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. The kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1). This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that the institutional frameworks that govern economic development are context-dependent and subject to historical and geographical variations. The implications of this complexity highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, one that takes into account the specific historical and geographical contexts in which they operate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with historical evidence showing that institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic development. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules, which in turn supported economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta reinforce this idea by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, indicating that institutional differences played a significant role in this economic divergence (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). The presence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in Europe facilitated economic growth, while the lack of such institutions in Asia hindered development.

The historical context of institutional development is also crucial. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Austin, who notes that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.11). This highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions depends on the specific historical and geographical contexts in which they operate. Van Zanden further illustrates this point by arguing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, underscoring the importance of considering the specific historical context when examining the role of institutions in economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Clark and Jacks provide additional evidence by showing that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, which suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth can vary significantly depending on the specific historical and geographical contexts (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14).

The remaining disagreement is about whether the institutional frameworks that govern economic development are context-dependent and subject to historical and geographical variations. North and Weingast argue that the institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, for example, not to expropriate their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Austin, however, notes that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.11). The historical evidence of the Glorious Revolution and the institutional changes it brought about settles this disagreement. The Glorious Revolution led to the establishment of institutions that protected property rights and limited the power of the monarchy, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This historical evidence shows that the specific institutional framework that governs economic development can have a significant impact on long-run economic growth, and that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on the specific historical and geographical contexts in which they operate.
