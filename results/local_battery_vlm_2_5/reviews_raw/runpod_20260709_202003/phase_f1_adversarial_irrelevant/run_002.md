The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central theme in economic scholarship. The question at stake is whether and how institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, influence the trajectory of economic development over the long term. This debate pits scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions against those who prioritize other factors, such as technological progress or geographical determinants. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they merely facilitate other underlying causes. Some scholars argue that institutions create the necessary framework for economic activities, while others contend that technological advancements and social changes are the true engines of growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. The tension lies in understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions might influence economic outcomes, and whether these mechanisms are sufficient to explain long-run growth. The debate is crucial for understanding the pathways to economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers, then policy efforts should focus on institutional reform. If technological progress or other factors are more critical, different policy approaches may be more effective. The key to resolving this debate lies in uncovering the specific ways in which institutions interact with other factors to shape economic growth. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement. North (1989, p.6) highlights that political institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms. These insights suggest that institutional reforms aimed at enhancing property rights and political stability can have profound effects on long-run economic growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). The technology provided a cost-effective way to demarcate land and prevent cattle from encroaching on neighboring farms, thereby securing property rights. This security allowed farmers to invest more confidently in agricultural improvements, knowing that their investments would not be compromised by external factors. Domar (1970, p.7) also points out that the relative profitability of free versus slave labor can be complex and dependent on various factors, including technological progress and capital use. This complexity highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how property rights and technological advancements interact to shape long-run economic growth. The interplay between secure property rights and technological innovations, as exemplified by the introduction of barbed wire, illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic development by enhancing the security and efficiency of agricultural practices.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) highlight the multifaceted nature of economic growth dynamics, emphasizing factors like technological progress and labor allocation. They argue that economic growth is influenced by the interplay between technological opportunity and the dynamics of social change, which encourages rapid growth of the capital stock and increases in aggregate output. This growth is driven by a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which includes both embodied and disembodied technologies. The same technological opportunity that permits rapid progress by modernization also encourages rapid growth of the capital stock, partly because of the returns to modernization itself and partly because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor. The rate of rise of the capital-labor ratio tends to be higher in these circumstances. This is further complicated by the fact that growth of productivity also makes for an increase in aggregate output. This disequilibrium suggests that the dynamics of labor allocation can significantly impact economic growth, even in the presence of technological progress. Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation and application of useful knowledge and technological advances, Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue. This perspective posits that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role. Technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel applications. This process involves coordination failures that can be rectified through institutional progress. Kuznets (1973, p.10) highlights that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention. Mokyr (2005, p.54) cautions that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors that erode gains. The implication is that while technology is the primary driver, institutions are crucial for sustaining and directing technological progress towards economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that the mass application of technological innovations provides a positive feedback loop, enhancing scientific progress and further technological advancements. This interplay suggests that while institutions are not the primary drivers, they are essential for the efficient use and sustainability of technological innovations, ensuring that economic growth is a sustained process. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the long-run economic growth that technological progress initiates.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) establish that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. The extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had lasting effects on economic development. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritized their own interests over broader economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The establishment of these extractive institutions was influenced by European mortality rates in colonies, with high mortality leading to fewer European settlers and more exploitative institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers, particularly the type of institutions they implemented, have had persistent effects on economic outcomes. The correlation between early institutions and current economic performance highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era policies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) note that while institutions played a crucial role, the surge in industrialization among formerly poor colonies also contributed significantly to current income differences, indicating that institutional factors are part of a broader dynamic influencing long-run economic growth.

Institutions, as argued by North and Mokyr, play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54; North 1989, p.6). Mokyr highlights the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress, noting that without it, economic improvements could be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is echoed by North, who emphasizes that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is essential for reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Kuznets and Abramovitz further elaborate on this idea by showing that technological progress is about innovation and the institutional and ideological adjustments that allow societies to efficiently use these innovations (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Abramovitz argues that the growth of productivity and the capital stock are intricately linked to technological progress, which in turn requires institutional frameworks that support modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of colonial institutions, where the type of institutions implemented by colonial powers has had persistent effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring impact of these early institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Institutions matter because they shape the economic environment in which technological progress and modernization can thrive, ultimately driving long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, as described by Hornbeck. This technological innovation significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This example underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the effective use of technological advancements, as Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire led to increased agricultural productivity and economic growth in the region (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The historical evidence from the American Plains and the broader context of colonial institutions both demonstrate that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment that supports technological progress and economic development. The specific historical evidence of the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and the empirical comparison of agricultural development on the American Plains settle the disagreement on the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that institutions are essential for long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
