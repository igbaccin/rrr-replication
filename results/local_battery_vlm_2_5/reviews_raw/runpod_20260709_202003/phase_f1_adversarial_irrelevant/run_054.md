The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of prosperity and stagnation. At stake is the identification of the key drivers behind sustained economic performance, which can inform policy and theoretical frameworks. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize technological progress and those who prioritize institutional frameworks as the primary catalysts for economic growth. Technological progress advocates argue that advancements in knowledge and innovation are the mainsprings of economic growth, while institutionalists contend that the effectiveness of institutions in facilitating transactions and enforcing property rights is crucial for sustained growth. Technological progress is often seen as a necessary but not sufficient condition for economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role in ensuring that technological advancements are effectively utilized. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, contra simple convergence hypotheses. Kuznets underscores that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is essential for understanding long-run economic growth, with institutions often serving as the conduit through which technological advancements translate into economic prosperity. This institutional framework reduced transaction costs, fostering economic growth. This mechanism is not limited to Europe. Acemoglu et al. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism, particularly the extraction of wealth by European colonists, was a critical factor in shaping economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) explain that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) posit that distributional conflicts underpin the core economic structures of pre-industrial Europe. They jointly argue that these conflicts provide a better explanation for institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild than theories focused on efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) highlights the role of merchant guilds in providing security and contract enforcement as 'club goods'. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were merely responses to transaction costs or information asymmetries. Domar (1970, p.7) further elaborates on this by suggesting that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce, which aligns with the idea that these institutions are shaped by underlying power dynamics rather than purely economic efficiencies. Domar (1970, p.7) also notes that the profitability of slave versus free labor depends on various factors including technological progress and capital use (Domar 1970, p.6). This qualifies the downstream implication that technological progress and greater use of capital necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor.

Hornbeck's analysis of the American Plains demonstrates that specific technological changes can significantly influence economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). The introduction of barbed wire fencing, for instance, enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, which in turn enhanced agricultural development. This technological shift is a story of institutional change, as farmers adapted their practices to exploit the new technology. Hornbeck's findings suggest that the economic impact of technological change is contingent on the institutional context in which it occurs. This is echoed in Pamuk's examination of the Black Death's economic impact, which highlights how structural changes initiated by the plague led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk attributes this divergence to the substitution of land and capital for labor, a process driven by the relative increase in wages. This substitution was an institutional adaptation, as new technologies and labor practices emerged in response to the demographic shock. Pamuk also notes that the role of guilds in technological innovations provides important insights into the process. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between technological change, historical events, and institutional adaptation in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on technological and social dynamics of economic growth emphasizes factors beyond institutions, such as the dynamics of technological advance and social change, which are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) both highlight that technological progress and social change are pivotal in shaping economic growth trajectories. This process is driven by the interplay between technological progress and the relative price dynamics of capital and labor. The reduction in the price of capital goods relative to labor intensifies the growth of the capital stock, thereby accelerating economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this dynamic by demonstrating that differences in mortality rates among European colonizers led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn affected current income per capita. This historical context underscores how exogenous factors, such as mortality rates, can influence institutional frameworks and, consequently, economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the impact of institutions on economic performance is not uniform, and that exogenous variation in institutions can significantly affect economic outcomes. This qualification suggests that while technological and social dynamics are crucial, the institutional framework can mediate these effects, highlighting the complex interplay between these factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share the view that economic growth is primarily driven by advancing technology and useful knowledge, suggesting that institutions are not the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.10)(Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.54) elaborates on this perspective by emphasizing that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of technological advancement involves a complex interplay of existing knowledge and inventive leaps. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further supports this view by noting that technological innovations are closely tied to specific needs and contexts, highlighting that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may be limited (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This context-specific nature of technological progress suggests that while institutions may facilitate or hinder technological advancement, they are not the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. The implication is that while institutions can support economic growth, their role is secondary to the fundamental drivers of technological advancement and useful knowledge. This perspective underscores the importance of technological progress as the mainspring of economic growth, with institutions playing a supporting rather than primary role.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted. Institutions facilitate economic growth by addressing coordination failures and reducing rent-seeking behaviors. This perspective aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.10), who highlights that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Institutions play a crucial role in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) argues that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework supports the growth of the capital stock and the rise of the capital-labor ratio, as technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Kuznets (1973, p.10) also notes that the mass application of technological innovations, which constitutes much of modern economic growth, is closely connected with the further progress of science, providing a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Institutions matter in economic growth because they create an environment where technological advancements can be effectively utilized. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in enabling technological progress and economic growth.
