The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious yet pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of the mechanisms that drive sustained economic performance over extended periods. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic activity, are critical determinants of economic growth. One position emphasizes that institutions are fundamental in facilitating technological progress and efficient resource allocation. Another position, however, argues that technological advancements and exogenous factors are the primary drivers of growth, with institutions playing a secondary or even negligible role. The central issue is whether institutions are indispensable for long-term economic growth or if they are merely incidental to other more decisive factors. Institutions may influence economic growth by ensuring that technological progress is sustainable and that economic reasonableness is maintained over the long term (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Mokyr 2005, p.12; Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

Technological advancement is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This is because technological progress is driven by the accumulation of knowledge and the recombination of existing techniques, which in turn stimulates further innovation. Kuznets (1973, p.4) notes that the mass application of technological innovations, often based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that further drives economic growth. This process is not merely a linear sequence of events but an interrelated system where one advancement induces another. Kuznets (1973, p.4) suggests that technological progress often requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This implies that while technological advancement is the primary driver, institutions play a supporting role in facilitating and sustaining this growth.

Institutions are crucial in determining the distribution of skills and their economic value. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Sokoloff and Engerman (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that the skill premium, influenced by institutional frameworks, is a key factor in determining economic growth. In this view, institutions that facilitate the efficient transfer of skills and maintain competitive labor markets are essential for long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that in Western Europe, guilds played a significant role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite criticisms of their monopolistic tendencies (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This institutional support for skill development is crucial for economic growth, as it ensures that the workforce is adequately trained and productive. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that in societies with high levels of inequality, such as those in the New World, institutions often advantaged elites, restricting competition and hindering economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This suggests that while institutions can facilitate skill development and economic growth, their effectiveness depends on the broader social and economic context in which they operate.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions which ensure secure property rights are essential for economic growth. In particular, the development of political institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges reduces transaction costs and fosters economic growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by explaining that as economies grow, the number of transactions increases, making efficient property rights and their enforcement crucial for maintaining low transaction costs. This commitment underpins the credibility of property rights, which in turn encourages investment and economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) caution that while institutions are necessary, they complement reputation-building and punishment strategies. They act as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic growth depends on their presence and their ability to complement and enhance existing social mechanisms. This interplay between institutions and social mechanisms is crucial for understanding the broader context in which economic growth occurs.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers, significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, were often extractive and did not encourage long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the institutions introduced by colonialism were designed to benefit the colonizers, not to maximize long-run growth. This extractive nature of institutions was a result of politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, who designed these institutions to suit their interests. The persistence of these institutions, even after the end of colonial rule, has had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) further demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of these extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. This variation in mortality rates acted as a source of exogenous variation in institutions, highlighting the role of exogenous factors in shaping institutional frameworks. This suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have a lasting impact on the institutional frameworks of former colonies, which in turn affect their economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide empirical support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies. The impact of these institutions on economic performance is not uniform across all colonies. The varying degrees of institutional development and the persistence of extractive institutions in some colonies highlight the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, even after the end of colonial rule, has had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these countries, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have a lasting impact on the institutional frameworks of former colonies.

Indeed, the relationship between economic growth and health outcomes is complex and multifaceted. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) both highlight that economic growth is not always accompanied by improvements in living standards. The growth of productivity and capital stock can lead to an increase in aggregate output (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Komlos (1998, p.8) shows that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in trends underscores that the benefits of economic growth are not uniformly distributed across society. The relationship between population growth and food prices is complex and depends on factors such as technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply (Komlos 1998, p.12). These insights suggest that while economic growth can drive technological progress and capital accumulation, the social dynamics of growth, including income distribution and nutritional outcomes, play a critical role in determining the overall well-being of a population.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving both technological progress and the development of economic reasonableness. Mokyr shows that technological progress, while crucial, requires complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Without institutional progress, economic improvements could be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This perspective aligns with North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (Mokyr 2005, p.54; North 1989, p.6). The development of economic institutions that support market exchanges is essential for long-run economic growth, as it enables increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. However, Acemoglu et al. highlight that institutions can also be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not always positive and can vary depending on the design and enforcement of these institutions.

Institutional frameworks also influence the distribution of skills and the formation of human capital, which are critical for long-term economic performance. Van Zanden argues that a low skill premium reflects efficient labor and capital markets and well-functioning institutions for human capital formation, which contribute to long-term economic growth. This is supported by Kuznets, who notes that technological innovations, when mass-applied, provide positive feedback for further scientific and technological advancements (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). The skill premium and the efficient transfer of skills are closely linked to the economic performance of a society, as they affect the relative price of capital goods and the overall investment in human capital. Institutions that guarantee the fair execution of contracts, such as guilds, can also have a negative impact by restricting entry and keeping relative pay above market-clearing levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.27). This highlights the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth, where institutions can both facilitate and hinder long-term economic performance.

The consensus is that institutions play a critical role in long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). However, the precise impact of institutions on economic growth remains a subject of debate, with some arguing that institutions can be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This debate is settled by the historical evidence of the Industrial Revolution, which shows that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The measurement of technological progress and institutional development during this period provides a clear comparison of the relative impacts of these factors on economic growth.
