The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is whether institutions, such as property rights and governance structures, are crucial drivers of economic development or merely secondary factors. The live disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture, and whether these institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. One camp argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while another camp posits that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper economic and social forces. Understanding this debate is essential for designing effective policies to promote economic development, as it directly influences how policymakers view the potential for institutional reform to drive growth. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus central to both theoretical and practical economic considerations. Acemoglu et al. have shown that colonial institutions significantly influenced current income per capita, suggesting that these institutions are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These scholars argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and affected current income per capita. A high correlation exists between mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies, European settlements, early measures of institutions, and institutions today. The persistence of these institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who note that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This perspective underscores the importance of the institutional legacies of colonialism in shaping economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. also observe that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted. They emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and that the benefits of institutional change may not be evenly distributed. This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions have had a significant impact on economic development, the effects are not uniform and depend on the specific context and power dynamics at play.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) note that institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to greater inequality and advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies often restricted competition, which contributed to the persistence of inequality. The specific focus on how the European colonies' different environments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality highlights that these differences could persist over time and affect development through their impact on institutions. For instance, the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor in the New World economies meant that many were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This inequality was reinforced by institutions that favored the elite, such as those that restricted competition and protected property rights. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) illustrates this dynamic with the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This mechanism of property rights protection is a key example of how institutional changes can have downstream effects on economic development. The benefits of such institutional changes may not be evenly distributed. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) emphasize that the qualitative thrust of this approach may not be entirely novel, as economists traditionally emphasize the pervasive influence of factor endowment. What is new, however, is the specific focus on how the extremely different environments in which the Europeans established their colonies may have led to societies with very different degrees of inequality, and on how these differences might have persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on the institutions that evolved.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and Pamuk (2007, p.8) both show that a growing economy may not translate into a higher standard of living. The Industrial Revolution saw rapid urbanization and productivity changes, yet these shifts did not uniformly improve living standards. Komlos (1998, p.13) highlights that despite rapid urban growth and industrialization during the Industrial Revolution, total factor productivity even declined in the 1840s (Komlos 1998, p.13). This decline suggests that economic growth did not necessarily lead to improved living standards. Pamuk (2007, p.8) echoes this, noting that the linkages between urban wages and rural productivity can weaken, leading to persistent urban-rural differences in standards of living (Pamuk 2007, p.8). These findings underscore that economic growth does not always translate into improved living standards, especially when considering the distribution of income and the relative prices of goods. Furthermore, the skill premium began to decline under severe labor shortages, which should be expected to encourage human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). However, the persistence of large urban-rural differences suggests that the benefits of economic growth may not be evenly distributed, and that the institutional framework governing economic activity plays a crucial role in determining who benefits from growth and how those benefits are distributed.

North and Weingast, along with North, establish that institutions are critical in shaping long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The institutional framework governing economic activity is not merely a set of rules but also includes the mechanisms for enforcing and changing these rules. This framework is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast show that the Glorious Revolution in England resulted in institutional changes that enhanced the state's credibility and its ability to maintain commitments to constituents, thereby promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These political institutions complement reputation-building strategies by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. North further elaborates that the development of efficient property rights and their enforcement is essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability allows for confidence in economic dealings, even in complex interdependent situations. However, North also notes that the evolution of more complex social frameworks requires institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with such situations (North 1989, p.2). This underscores the importance of institutional reliability in enabling individuals to engage in economic activities with confidence, even when dealing with strangers. The implication is that the benefits of economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework governing economic activity.

Institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the economic divergence between regions, as shown by Austin (2008, p.11); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the economic divergence between Africa and the Americas by not accounting for the different historical paths of development, including the role of institutions. The economic divergence between regions was influenced by the economic motivations of colonial regimes, which could either support or extract from local economic enterprise. This is supported by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in labour productivity and economic institutions. The divergence in economic growth between regions can be traced back to differences in labour productivity in the tradable goods sector, with Asia having relatively low productivity. This suggests that the institutional frameworks governing economic activity in different regions had a significant impact on long-run economic growth. Austin notes that the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. This underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional context when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
