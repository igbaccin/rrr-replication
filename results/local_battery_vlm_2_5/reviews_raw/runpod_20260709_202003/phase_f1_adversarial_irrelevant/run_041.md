The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is whether institutions should be seen as a primary driver of economic growth or as a secondary factor that complements other determinants. One live disagreement is whether institutions matter in economic growth, or whether population and savings are the principal determinants. Another debate is whether institutions are a primary driver of economic growth or a secondary factor that complements other determinants. Institutions are widely believed to shape economic growth by influencing the incentives for productive activities. The precise mechanisms through which institutions operate are a subject of intense debate. This disagreement is particularly salient in the context of historical economic growth, where the role of institutions has been both championed and challenged. The debate hinges on the extent to which institutions can be credited with fostering secure property rights and economic growth, or whether other factors, such as technology or geography, play a more significant role. The relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while others contend that technology and geography are more significant factors. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.”

This secure framework of property rights was deeply intertwined with the broader institutional environment that shaped economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the development of institutions that effectively enforce property rights and limit expropriation by the state is crucial for long-term economic growth. The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can secure property rights and constrain the state's ability to expropriate wealth, thereby fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) show that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the state's credibility in maintaining its commitments to constituents. This dynamic highlights the importance of institutions that balance power and encourage investment, as extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect long-term economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent institutional differences that continue to influence current income per capita. The implications of these findings suggest that the initial conditions and institutional frameworks set during colonial periods can have enduring effects on economic development. Understanding these dynamics is essential for grasping how institutional changes can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic growth.

Institutional structures play a pivotal role in reducing uncertainties that facilitate economic growth, though they do not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The establishment of such property rights allows individuals to confidently participate in impersonal exchanges, which is essential for realizing productivity gains (North 1989, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) notes that the efficiency approach has been applied to various pre-modern institutions, such as merchant guilds and the Mesta, interpreting them as efficient solutions to specific economic challenges. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also argues that the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework can complicate the study of economic institutions. Institutions like the European family system and community-based welfare systems in pre-industrial England were interdependent with various markets and social structures, potentially contributing to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying institutions in isolation may overlook their complex interactions and cumulative effects on economic development. Therefore, understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a comprehensive analysis of entire institutional frameworks.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). These policies created barriers to entry for non-elites, limiting their access to resources and opportunities, and thereby maintaining a stratified social structure. This institutional framework, which prioritized the interests of the elite, had significant implications for economic development. This is particularly evident in the context of agricultural development, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced productivity by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This suggests that the institutional framework, which favored the elite, limited the broader economic benefits that could have been achieved through technological advancements and improved agricultural practices. The persistence of inequality hindered economic growth and shaped the distribution of its benefits, further entrenching the elite's advantage.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) re-examine the historical factors that influenced economic growth by questioning the significance of coal in the Industrial Revolution and the timing of the great divergence between Europe and Asia. This perspective challenges the conventional view that coal was a primary driver of industrial growth. This shared finding highlights that the role of specific factors, such as coal, in economic growth has been overstated. Austin (2008, p.11) qualifies this perspective by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This implies that while coal and other factors may have contributed to economic growth, their impact varied across different contexts and time periods. The re-evaluation of historical factors that influenced economic growth underscores the complexity of economic development, suggesting that no single factor can fully explain long-run economic growth.

The distinct agricultural productivity patterns and demographic changes in Europe and Asia, therefore, offer a more nuanced understanding of historical economic divergence. Pamuk (2007, p.8) and Bryant (2006, p.29) both argue that the agricultural productivity revolution in Europe and the Black Death's impact on wages and labour markets were critical factors in economic divergence. The productivity revolution in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employment, fostering economic growth and structural differentiation between urban and rural sectors. This dynamic was notably absent in China, where agricultural practices remained tied to subsistence farming and domestic handicrafts, limiting economies of scale and capital accumulation (Bryant 2006, p.29). The demographic realities of land scarcity and abundant cheap labour in China further constrained economic considerations, hindering the adoption of labour-saving technologies and the concentration of employment in workshops and factories. This pattern contrasts with England, where larger agricultural units enabled a synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing, significantly improving diets, increasing productivity, and enhancing soil fertility (Bryant 2006, p.28). The skill premium began to decline under severe labour shortages, encouraging human capital formation and influencing the economic trajectory of Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.15). These factors collectively shaped the economic development paths of Europe and China, highlighting the importance of agricultural productivity and demographic changes in historical economic divergence.

Van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) examine the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, highlighting that economic growth did not necessarily translate into improved living standards. During this period, economic growth was not uniform across Europe. This growth did not uniformly improve the standard of living, as evidenced by the long-term decline in real wages. The divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower-income groups further illustrates this disparity. This trend was exacerbated by the relative price effect, where the price of nutrients increased due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry (Komlos 1998, p.8). This period saw significant urbanization, with cities like Paris and London experiencing substantial population growth, but this did not translate into improved living standards for all. This disconnect between economic growth and living standards underscores the complex interplay between economic development and social welfare.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by the evolution of property rights and public choice mechanisms. North and Weingast emphasize that the political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial, particularly the institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in seventeenth-century England, where the Glorious Revolution led to secure property rights, fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North similarly argues that institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework ensures that even as interdependence grows, individuals can have confidence in their economic dealings, regardless of personal knowledge of their trading partners (North 1989, p.2). This confidence is vital for sustaining economic growth over the long run.

The relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and not always straightforward. van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective challenges the optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, which often overlook the long-run decline of real wages. The disconnect between economic growth and living standards is further illustrated by Broadberry and Gupta, who show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence highlights how institutional differences can lead to varying economic outcomes, even in the absence of significant technological changes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further supported by Clark and Jacks, who demonstrate that the gains in total factor productivity in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution were modest (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). Economic growth is driven by technological advancements and by institutional factors that facilitate efficient resource use.
