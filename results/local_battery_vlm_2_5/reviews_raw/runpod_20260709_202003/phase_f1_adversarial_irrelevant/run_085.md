The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve from stagnation to sustained prosperity. One live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms that drive this transformation. On one hand, some scholars argue that technological progress is the primary engine of economic growth. Others contend that institutional frameworks are crucial for harnessing technological advancements and ensuring their sustainable impact. Yet another perspective posits that the growth of useful knowledge itself is the mainspring of economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role in mitigating coordination failures. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is thus central to unraveling the dynamics of long-run economic growth. This interplay is evident in the historical context of the Industrial Revolution, where technological innovations were accompanied by institutional changes that facilitated their widespread adoption and sustained impact. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

This influence is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.23) who argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, played a crucial role in determining the economic trajectories of colonized regions. The mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that hindered economic growth. A key mechanism here is the correlation between European settlement patterns and the institutions established in the colonies. These early institutions, in turn, had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these regions, with extractive institutions persisting and limiting growth. Austin (2008, p.23) extends this by noting that the economic rents from colonialism and slavery were associated with the profits from Atlantic trade, which enabled the commercial bourgeoisie to demand institutional changes that protected their property rights. This highlights how the economic benefits from colonial ventures influenced institutional development in Europe. The institutions' effects on economic growth varied. Nunn (2008, p.3) provides a nuanced perspective by showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping had negative impacts on subsequent development. This suggests that while colonial institutions shaped by mortality rates were significant, the specific economic activities and social structures within these colonies also played a critical role in determining long-run economic outcomes. The complexity of these interactions underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on economic growth.

North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) contend that political institutions that establish and enforce property rights are pivotal for economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further argues that as economies grow, the transaction sector expands, comprising a larger proportion of GDP. This expansion is driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional design, influenced by politically powerful groups, can lead to extractive institutions that hinder growth. These institutions, shaped by the interests of colonial powers, can significantly impact economic outcomes. The role of political institutions in shaping economic growth is further illuminated by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights. They argue that institutions governing public choice, such as those that evolved in seventeenth-century England, were crucial for economic growth. This credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that the state adheres to its part of bargains with constituents, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging.

The growth of useful knowledge is essential for economic development, and technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This process is not linear; it requires the integration of new knowledge with existing practices, and it depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use. For example, technological progress often relies on "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process is further facilitated by the awareness of existing techniques, which helps inventors identify gaps and opportunities in the current technological landscape, thereby preventing the misspending of resources on redundant innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The expansion of useful knowledge drives economic growth and facilitates efficient use of technological innovations. This positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific progress underscores the interconnected nature of knowledge-driven growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interconnectedness indicates that useful knowledge growth drives economic growth and is essential for maintaining long-run economic development.

A group of papers by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), Komlos (1998, p.8), and Temin (2002, p.4) examines specific historical factors like technological change and agricultural development, and the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. These papers do not directly address how institutions might influence economic outcomes over the long run. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient use of land and resources, directly contributing to economic growth. Komlos (1998, p.8), on the other hand, highlights the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This finding underscores the complex interplay between economic growth and social welfare, indicating that technological advancements alone are not sufficient to ensure broad-based improvements in living standards. Temin (2002, p.4) further complicates this picture by arguing that the unusually rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. The rapid growth in the twentieth century contrasts with the earlier period, indicating that the relative price of food declined despite rapid population growth. This points to the role of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply in shaping economic outcomes.

Economic inequality and structural changes influence long-term economic performance by shaping the skill premium and the formation of human capital. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a finding that the skill premium is a key indicator of economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that a low skill premium indicates efficient labor and capital markets, as well as effective institutions for human capital formation, which are crucial for long-term economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This mechanism is supported by Pamuk (2007, p.22), who shows that the Black Death led to structural changes that increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital, fostering technological innovations and capital-intensive industries (Pamuk 2007, p.22). These structural changes also created a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, indicating that technological advancements can drive economic growth and intensify economic inequality (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This pattern suggests that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, they can also exacerbate economic inequality. The persistence of inequality is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5).

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Bryant (2006, p.11) both argue that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) notes that the same technological opportunity that permits rapid progress by modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock. This is because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor, fostering a higher rate of rise of the capital-labor ratio. Bryant (2006, p.11) supports this by noting that the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labor for proto-industrial and factory employments, creating a dynamic that was absent in other societies. This dynamic fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country, increasing demand for industrialized commodity production and capital accumulation. Bryant (2006, p.11) also notes that demographic realities, such as land scarcity and abundant cheap labor in China, significantly influenced economic considerations, even leading to resistance against labor-saving technologies. This highlights that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, the societal and demographic context in which these advancements occur plays a crucial role in determining their impact. The implications of these findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not merely about adopting new technologies but also about creating an environment where these technologies can be effectively utilized and integrated into the broader economic system.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by their ability to facilitate the effective utilization of technological advancements. Mokyr and Abramovitz both highlight that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; institutional progress is equally crucial. Mokyr argues that without institutional advancements to complement technological progress, economic improvements may not be sustainable and could be eroded by rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Similarly, Abramovitz emphasizes that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This suggests that institutions play a critical role in creating an environment where technological innovations can be effectively integrated into the broader economic system.

Institutions also facilitate the efficient use of innovations by addressing coordination failures and ensuring the fair execution of contracts. van Zanden and North both underscore the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enforcing property rights. van Zanden notes that institutions like guilds in western Europe played a significant role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite criticisms of their monopolistic tendencies (van Zanden 2009, p.19). North argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for reducing costs per transaction and increasing the volume of exchanges, which in turn drives economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This indicates that institutions are essential for creating a stable and predictable environment that encourages investment and innovation.

The historical evidence shows that the interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is pivotal for long-run economic growth. Kuznets and Bryant both provide insights into how technological progress and institutional adjustments have shaped economic growth. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Bryant highlights that the lack of structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors in China hindered economic growth, despite technological advancements (Bryant 2006, p.29). This historical comparison illustrates that the effectiveness of institutions in facilitating technological progress and economic growth is contingent on the specific historical and cultural context. The skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance; a low skill premium reflects efficient labor and capital markets and effective institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The historical evidence from Europe and China shows that institutions that support the efficient use of technological innovations are crucial for long-run economic growth.
