The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At the heart of the debate lies the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one among several factors. One camp contends that institutions, such as property rights, political stability, and the rule of law, are essential for fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity. Another camp posits that geographic factors, such as climate and natural resources, or cultural factors, such as social norms and values, are more influential in determining economic outcomes. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making, as understanding the drivers of economic growth can inform strategies for development and economic reform. Institutions, broadly defined, create the rules of the game that shape economic behavior and outcomes. For instance, credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that economic actors can rely on the stability of their investments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This stability, in turn, encourages long-term planning and investment, which are crucial for economic growth. The evolution of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs and increasing the efficiency of economic activity.

The Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a turning point in English political history, establishing a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the sovereign and ensured that property rights would be secure. This institutional change was crucial for economic growth because it provided a stable political environment where economic actors could rely on the enforcement of property rights. The political institutions that emerged after the Glorious Revolution improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, ensuring that property rights would not be expropriated. This stability encouraged long-term investment and planning, which are essential for economic growth. The institutional changes also complemented reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement would facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework allowed individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange (North 1989, p.2). These institutional changes were not just about economic rules but also about the political framework that enforced them, highlighting the critical role of institutional evolution in long-run economic development. This secure institutional environment fostered economic growth by providing the stability and predictability needed for long-term investment and planning.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped long-run economic growth. This is because the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers, not to promote long-term economic development. These institutions, tailored to the interests of the colonizers, persisted over time and continue to influence current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European mortality rates in colonies were a key determinant of whether extractive institutions were established, with high mortality leading to more extractive systems. This pattern of institutional development was driven by the specific historical context of colonial rule, not just by geographic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further substantiate this by showing that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization, accounting for significant differences in current income levels among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) caution that while institutions are critical, they are not the sole determinant of economic performance. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional impact on economic growth, highlighting that while colonial institutions set a foundational path, other factors also influence long-term economic trajectories.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.3) discuss how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality contributed to the persistence of inequality. These institutions favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and reinforcing inequality. This dynamic is evident in the factor endowments of New World societies, where an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to high living standards on average. Other aspects of their factor endowments varied, resulting in extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This inequality was further exacerbated by the evolution of institutions that were not conducive to economic growth, as seen in the case of slavery in the New World. The production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping had negative impacts on subsequent development, highlighting the detrimental effects of institutional inequality on economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.3). Nunn's work on Africa's slave trades shows that the adverse effects of slavery on economic development are a broader phenomenon, not limited to the New World. This underscores the importance of understanding the institutional mechanisms that perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth. It is clear that the persistence of inequality in societies with initial high levels of inequality is a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth, and that the institutions that evolve in such societies play a significant role in this process.

Moreover, the interaction between technological advancements and institutional frameworks can either amplify or mitigate their effects on economic development. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains boosted agricultural development by enabling farmers to protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This technological innovation allowed for better management of farmland, leading to increased crop productivity and a shift in land allocation towards more productive uses. Whatley, in a related vein, highlights the role of gunpowder imports in increasing slave exports through improved productivity in capturing slaves (Whatley 2018, p.2). This intertemporal production link underscores how technological advancements can drive economic activities, albeit in different contexts. The specific institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder these technological impacts remain under-explored. The implications of these technological changes on long-run economic growth are complex, as they interact with existing institutions and social structures. For instance, while barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development, it also had downstream effects on land use and property rights, which could have broader economic implications beyond immediate productivity gains. The role of institutions in mediating these technological impacts on economic growth is a critical area for further research.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that despite significant economic progress in Europe during the Industrial Revolution, the biological well-being of the population declined. The economic gains from the Industrial Revolution were insufficient to offset the increased costs of living and the poor nutritional status of children. Van Zanden (1999, p.2; 2009, p.19) supports this view, arguing that the relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was complex and not straightforwardly positive. Van Zanden (2009, p.19; 2009, p.27) further elaborates that the skill premium, which reflects the efficiency of labor and capital markets, is a critical predictor of long-term economic performance. Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies that while economic growth is necessary for improving living standards, it is insufficient on its own. The distribution of income and the quality of childhood nourishment are also crucial factors. The decline in biological well-being during the Industrial Revolution highlights the importance of considering not just economic growth but also the broader social and nutritional context in which this growth occurs.

The historical paths leading to economic divergence between regions are complex and multifaceted. Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) both argue that the economic divergence between regions was driven by factors such as colonial policies and economic dynamics, not solely by institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.11) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms underlying economic divergence by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the motivation for the Atlantic slave trade was commercial, and that the exchange-value product of Africans' labor in Africa was lower than in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken. This suggests that labor productivity in Africa was low due to specific aspects of the natural environment and the choice of production techniques. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) support this view by demonstrating that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that economic dynamics were the primary drivers of economic divergence. Austin (2008, p.11) notes that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, and that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on specific historical and contextual factors. The interplay between economic dynamics and institutional frameworks highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors driving economic divergence.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the security of property rights and the enforcement of economic rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) demonstrate that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This is because institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs and enhance the reliability of economic exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23) further illustrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, highlighting the long-term impact of institutional frameworks on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by their ability to complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, thereby enhancing the efficacy of market mechanisms. Institutions can improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability is essential for economic growth, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). Austin (2008, p.11; 2008, p.1) shows that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. This indicates that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others, underscoring the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth.

The specific historical and contextual factors that influence the impact of institutions on economic growth are also highlighted by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a significant role in the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This thesis underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions operate and their long-term effects on economic growth. The complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the fact that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the long-term growth of total factor productivity in France between 1450 and 1800, which shows that traditional agriculture was able to generate sustained economic growth despite the absence of modern economic institutions (van Zanden 1999, p.2).
