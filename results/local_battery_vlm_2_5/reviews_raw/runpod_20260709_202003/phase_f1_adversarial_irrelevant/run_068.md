The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained economic progress and the extent to which institutions, as formal and informal rules governing behavior, influence this process. The live disagreement centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as technological progress or geographic endowments. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that technological advancements and social changes play a more significant role. Still, others emphasize the importance of geographic factors and exogenous shocks in shaping economic trajectories. This debate is crucial for policymakers and economists seeking to design effective strategies for promoting economic development.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and ensuring efficient property rights. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, because neoclassical theories that focus solely on population and savings as principal determinants fail to account for the complexity of economic systems (North 1989, p.5). North contends that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development, as they lower costs per transaction and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional framework established by colonial powers significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of institutions on economic growth. The emphasis on institutions as a driver of economic growth underscores the need for effective governance and property rights enforcement to sustain long-term development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets and the commitment of the regime to enforce these rules. The institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, providing constraints where reputation alone was insufficient. This is illustrated in the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution, where restrictions on the state's ex post behavior enhanced its credibility in maintaining bargains with constituents. By ensuring that property rights were secure and enforceable, these institutions facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This growth in transactions is a direct result of increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates during colonization shaped the institutions established in colonies, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The paper's central claim is that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted and had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in mortality rates faced by Europeans as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance. This variation is highly correlated with European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutions, underscoring the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by initial colonial conditions, continues to influence economic performance today. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to benefit European colonists, has had long-lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, illustrating the profound impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) contend that institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe are better understood through the lens of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) challenges the prevalent view of merchant guilds as inefficient cartels, arguing instead that they provided crucial security and contract enforcement. Domar (1970, p.7) identifies slavery and serfdom as responses to high land-to-labor ratios and scarce labor. Both Ogilvie and Domar highlight that these institutions were adaptive responses to specific economic conditions. A significant downstream implication of this perspective is that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to their ability to manage distributional conflicts and secure economic benefits for their members, even if they are not the most efficient solutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) acknowledges the complexity of these institutions, noting that craft guilds and merchant guilds had varying impacts on technological change and economic efficiency. This nuanced view challenges the simplistic notion that these institutions were uniformly detrimental to economic growth. Instead, it suggests that their impact depended on the specific historical and economic context in which they operated.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Komlos (1998, p.8) focus on specific technological changes and their impacts on agricultural development and biological well-being. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological change allowed farmers to better control their land and resources, leading to increased agricultural productivity. This suggests that technological advances, while beneficial for agricultural development, did not necessarily translate into improved health outcomes for the population. Komlos (1998, p.12) clarifies that population growth does not always lead to a rise in food prices, depending on factors such as the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply. This nuanced view highlights that the impact of technological changes on economic development is complex and multifaceted.

Mokyr highlights that technological advance is a key driver of economic growth, independent of institutional frameworks. This is supported by Abramovitz, who notes that the dynamics of technological advance and social change are central to economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Mokyr identifies coordination failures as a critical mechanism through which technological progress influences economic growth. He argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be unsustainable. Abramovitz further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, which in turn accelerates the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic underscores the importance of technological advance in driving economic growth, as it directly influences the capital-labor ratio and aggregate output. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that technological progress can be influenced by factors such as rent-seeking behavior, which can erode economic gains over time (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the complex interplay between technological advance and social change in shaping economic growth, and the need for supportive institutions to ensure that the benefits of technological progress are sustained.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North and Acemoglu et al.
