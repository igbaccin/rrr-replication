The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the explanatory power of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, or technological change. One live disagreement centres on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions are endogenous, shaped by economic forces and in turn shaping economic outcomes. On the other hand, others posit that institutions are exogenous, pre-determined by historical or cultural factors and influencing economic growth independently. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms driving economic development and for designing effective policies to foster growth. The institutional perspective posits that institutions such as property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability are critical for economic growth. Institutions provide the rules of the game that shape economic interactions and incentivize productive activities. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11).

These institutions complement reputation-building strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, for example, not to expropriate their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North elaborates that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should manifest in the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The institutional reliability is essential, because it means that even as the network of interdependence caused by the growth of specialization widens we can have confidence in outcomes that are necessarily increasingly remote from our personal knowledge (North 1989, p.2). This institutional evolution in England demonstrates that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about creating a framework that ensures these rules are enforced and adapted over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) maintain that the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth. They argue that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were more likely to be established in colonies with high mortality rates. This institutional design, which did not encourage economic development, was shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining their political power and feared losing it with institutional development. The persistence of these extractive institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further support this view by showing that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. This claim is qualified by the fact that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were not uniform across all colonies. The specific institutional changes that were introduced depended on the historical and geographical context of each colony. This variability in institutional changes means that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth was not uniform across all colonies.

Distributional conflicts, not institutions themselves, are argued to shape core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie argues that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts, challenging the prevailing view that these institutions evolved to maximize economic benefits. This perspective is shared by Sokoloff and Engerman, who highlight that institutions in societies with greater inequality often served to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework was interdependent with other economic systems, such as labor and credit markets, as Ogilvie notes. This interdependency means that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions was part of a broader system. Ogilvie cautions that studying one institution in isolation can lead to an incomplete understanding of its role in economic development. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their interplay with distributional conflicts, suggesting that a holistic approach is necessary to fully grasp the dynamics of long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) both argue that the historical divergence in economic performance between regions cannot be attributed to a single, simple cause, but rather to a complex interplay of factors including institutions, labour productivity, and economic rent, suggesting a multifaceted approach to understanding economic growth. Austin (2008, p.11) points out that the economic motivation for the Atlantic slave trade was commercial, driven by the higher exchange-value product of Africans' labour in destinations compared to Africa. This dynamic underscores the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the labour scarcity in Africa was a function of population density and of the specific institutional and environmental contexts. They show that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that institutional factors influencing productivity played a crucial role in economic divergence. The implication is that institutions shaping labour productivity and economic rent are pivotal in understanding long-run economic growth. This suggests that the institutional framework must be examined in conjunction with natural and environmental factors to fully grasp its impact on economic growth.

Hopkins and Nunn both highlight that historical factors such as slave trades and colonialism have significantly impacted African economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). These historical factors have shaped the institutional landscape in Africa, influencing long-run economic growth. Hopkins argues that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The legacy of slave trades, as Nunn shows, has had adverse effects on economic development by influencing the evolution of institutions that are not conducive to growth. Nunn's analysis demonstrates that the use of slaves was detrimental to society, and the production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping also had negative impacts on subsequent development. This suggests that the institutional framework in Africa has been profoundly shaped by these historical events, creating a context where institutions may not provide the necessary incentives for investment and economic growth. Hopkins further qualifies this by noting that institutions can be long-standing without being immutable, and the task of policy is to adapt inappropriate institutions to present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). This implies that while historical factors have shaped institutions in Africa, there is potential for institutional reforms to address the challenges posed by these historical legacies and promote economic growth. The adaptation of institutions to current development needs is crucial for overcoming the adverse effects of historical factors on long-run economic growth in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in their ability to provide credible commitments and reduce transaction costs. North and Weingast demonstrate that the political factors underpinning economic growth involve institutions that enforce rules and constrain the state's behavior, preventing expropriation and maintaining bargains (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is crucial for fostering secure property rights and facilitating market exchanges, which are essential for economic development. Broadberry and Gupta show how the divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, highlighting that institutions governing trade and labor productivity played a significant role in this economic divergence (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). These findings underscore the importance of institutional reliability in enabling complex economic interactions and ensuring long-term growth.

The institutional framework also plays a critical role in addressing historical legacies and promoting economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by Ogilvie, who contends that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This view underscores the importance of understanding the broader institutional framework and its interdependencies in shaping economic outcomes.

A shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for reducing transaction costs and ensuring credible commitments (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions can be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, and not promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin, by contrast, highlights that economic rent and growth can be joint products, and that institutions favoring growth in one historical context may not be optimal in another (Austin 2008, p.11). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence on colonial institutions in Africa. Austin's analysis shows that the commercial motivations behind the Atlantic slave trade and the specific reasons for low labor productivity in Africa can be understood by considering the choice of production techniques and the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). This historical context provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions shaped by colonialism influenced long-run economic growth in Africa.
