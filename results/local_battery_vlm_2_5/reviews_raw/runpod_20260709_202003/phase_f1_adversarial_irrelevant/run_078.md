The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of development. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of technological progress and those who stress the foundational role of institutional frameworks. Technological progress advocates, such as Mokyr, argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutional progress is necessary to sustain technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). In contrast, institutionalists, like North, contend that institutions matter profoundly in economic growth, as they shape the framework within which economic activities occur and influence the efficiency of transactions (North 1989, p.5). The interplay between technological innovation and institutional development is thus crucial for understanding how economies grow over the long term. This interplay is evident in the catch-up process in economic growth, where technological advances and social changes are intertwined with the dynamics of institutional evolution (Abramovitz 1986, p.4).

The growth of useful knowledge and technological progress are central to understanding long-run economic growth. Mokyr highlights that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions. Kuznets complements this view by noting that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, and their effective use requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This interplay is evident in the Industrial Revolution, where major inventions were tailored to the specific needs of the country of origin (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The effective use of technological innovations thus depends on the institutional framework that supports their application. Institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, ensuring that economic growth is sustained over the long term. This dynamic highlights the importance of institutions in shaping the long-term trajectory of economic growth.

Colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to maximize rents for the colonizers rather than promote long-run economic growth. These institutions were designed to extract resources efficiently under harsh conditions, leading to persistent economic underdevelopment. This pattern is evident in regions like Latin America and Africa, where high mortality rates correlated with the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, hindered the development of property rights and inclusive economic policies, which are crucial for sustained growth. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these regions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the institutional framework established during colonial times continues to influence current economic performance. The legacy of extractive institutions is evident in the underdeveloped property rights and weak governance structures that characterize many former colonies today. Furthermore, the institutional frameworks that were established during colonial times have had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these regions, as they continue to influence current economic outcomes. This dynamic highlights the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic development, underscoring the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic trajectories.

North (1989, p.6) (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) focus on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth. North argues that institutions are crucial for specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. This institutional framework is pivotal for fostering economic growth, as it ensures that the costs per transaction decrease while the volume of exchanges increases. This commitment is essential for securing property rights and ensuring that the state maintains its part of bargains with constituents, thereby preventing expropriation of wealth. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.7) also point out that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, rather than substituting for them. This nuanced view underscores the importance of political institutions in improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism, especially in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. One downstream implication of this institutional framework is that it can mitigate incentive problems that are insufficiently policed by reputation, thereby fostering a more stable and predictable economic environment. This institutional stability is crucial for long-run economic growth, as it provides a solid foundation for sustained economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of technological advancement is not merely about the accumulation of new knowledge but also about the effective use and recombination of existing knowledge. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) complement this view by showing that the expansion of coal output during the Industrial Revolution was driven by increased demand rather than technological innovations in mining (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). This suggests that the economic growth spurts driven by technology are often fueled by the effective utilization of existing resources and knowledge, rather than merely by new discoveries. This implies that while knowledge and technology are crucial drivers of economic growth, they require a supportive institutional framework to ensure long-term sustainability.

Abramovitz elaborates on the catch-up process in economic growth, highlighting how technological opportunities can accelerate the growth of the capital stock while Kuznets emphasizes the role of technological innovations in modern economic growth. Both authors agree that the dynamics of technological progress are crucial for understanding economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) notes how technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby enhancing the capital-labor ratio. This mechanism underscores how technological advances can drive economic growth by making capital more accessible and affordable, which in turn stimulates investment and productivity. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that the accumulation of material and social technology is essential for economic growth, especially in less developed countries where the stock of suitable innovations may be limited. This implies that the efficient use of technological innovations requires not just their availability but also the capacity to adapt and implement them effectively. Kuznets (1973, p.4) adds that the mass application of technological innovations provides a positive feedback loop, driving further advancements in science and technology. This feedback loop suggests that technological progress is self-reinforcing, creating a virtuous cycle of innovation and growth. This qualification highlights that while technological progress is a key driver, it is not the sole determinant of economic growth.
