The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure economic and political interactions, drive or hinder economic development. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who point to other factors, such as geographic endowments or technological change. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they shape the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior, thereby fostering or impeding growth. Others contend that institutions are merely a byproduct of deeper structural factors, such as geography or culture, and thus play a secondary role in economic development. Still others propose that institutions are endogenous to economic growth, meaning that they evolve in response to economic changes rather than driving them. This disagreement highlights the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. The debate centres on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or whether they are merely one of many factors that contribute to it. If institutions are indeed the primary cause, then policymakers should focus on institutional reform as a means of promoting economic development. If, however, institutions are secondary factors, then other policies, such as infrastructure development or technological innovation, may be more effective. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast's seminal work highlights that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 marked a critical juncture in this evolution. The success of the revolution is evident in the subsequent development of capital markets, which thrived under the new institutional framework. The institutional changes post-Glorious Revolution improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This institutional reliability was crucial for reducing uncertainties in increasingly complex economic interactions. North further elaborates that the development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is essential for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the overall reliability of economic interactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North and Weingast's analysis underscores that the institutional framework established during this period was critical for long-run economic development, as it provided the necessary conditions for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were extractive and did not foster long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were shaped by the political power of colonists, who were reluctant to initiate changes that would not directly benefit them. This extractive institutional framework was established early on and persisted over time, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanism through which these institutions affected economic outcomes is clear: they did not encourage economic development, and were designed to extract resources rather than promote growth. Acemoglu et al. identify that the mortality rates faced by European colonists in the colonies were a key determinant in whether extractive institutions were established. Acemoglu et al. also argue that the persistence of these institutions over time is a crucial factor in understanding current income differences, highlighting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development is not a transient effect but a long-lasting one (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The implication is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes today, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions were rooted in the struggles between various social groups over the control of resources and economic opportunities. This perspective challenges the traditional view that institutions are primarily designed to maximize efficiency. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that the merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries were often inefficient cartels, but the elder merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods. The efficiency of these institutions was a byproduct of the distributional conflicts between different social groups. The institutions that emerged from these conflicts were shaped by the power dynamics and interests of the dominant groups. The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions often led to the creation of institutions that favored the interests of the dominant groups, which in turn could hinder economic growth by limiting competition and innovation. Van Zanden critiques the 'stagnationist' interpretation of early modern economic growth, noting that while some studies suggest long-term growth in total factor productivity, these optimists often overlook the decline in real wages and the poor conditions of childhood nourishment (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Komlos elaborates on this by showing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in the life expectancy of upper and lower income groups underscores that economic growth did not uniformly benefit all segments of society. The relative price effect further complicates this picture, as the price of nutrients increased due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry. This led consumers to rearrange their consumption bundles, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods, which had significant implications for nutritional intake and overall health (Komlos 1998, p.8). The persistence of economic growth alongside declining biological well-being suggests that the benefits of economic development were not evenly distributed, and that institutions may have played a role in shaping these outcomes.

The papers by Hopkins and Nunn (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3) share a focus on the role of specific historical factors in shaping Africa's economic development. Hopkins highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with Nunn, who demonstrates that the slave trades in Africa had adverse effects on economic development by disrupting social structures and reducing human capital. The impact of these historical factors was profound, as they shaped the institutional landscape in ways that were not conducive to economic development. Nunn further underscores the role of geography in long-term economic development, noting that factors such as climate and disease prevalence influenced agricultural productivity and overall economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.16). Hopkins acknowledges that institutions can be long-standing but not immutable, suggesting that policy interventions can adapt inappropriate institutions to meet present development needs (Hopkins 2009, p.10). Thus, while historical factors such as slave trades and geographical constraints have significantly influenced Africa's economic trajectory, the potential for institutional reform offers a pathway to overcoming these challenges.
