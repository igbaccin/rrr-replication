The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of development and prosperity. At the heart of the debate lies a live disagreement: while some scholars emphasize the primacy of technological progress and innovation as the driving forces behind economic growth, others argue that institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal constraints like laws and informal constraints like norms and conventions, play a crucial role in facilitating or hindering growth. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world consequences for policy-making and economic development strategies. Institutions, by shaping the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior, can either foster an environment conducive to growth or create barriers that stifle it. For instance, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they determine the efficiency of property rights and the effectiveness of enforcement mechanisms, which in turn influence the costs of transactions and the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective challenges neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as the principal determinants of growth (North 1989, p.5). In turn, Kuznets' work underscores the interconnectedness of technological innovations and institutional adjustments, suggesting that the efficient use of innovations requires complementary changes in institutions and ideologies (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is a recurring theme that will be explored in more detail in the following sections.

North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. The English financial revolution, for instance, illustrates how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can drive economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the context of former European colonies, where institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) point out that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not just geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This suggests that the long-term impact of institutions on economic growth is not merely a function of their initial design but also of their evolution over time.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Domar (1970, p.7) both demonstrate that property rights are integral to economic development. For instance, the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their lands more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity and economic growth. However, Domar (1970, p.7) acknowledges that the relative profitability of free versus slave labor depends on various factors, including technological progress and capital use, which can complicate the direct impact of property rights on economic growth (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while property rights are crucial, their effects on economic development are contingent on broader economic and technological conditions.

Technological and institutional adjustments are pivotal for economic growth, as both Kuznets and North and Weingast argue that efficient use of innovations requires complementary institutional changes (Kuznets 1973, p.10; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Kuznets (1973, p.10) posits that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may be limited, given that technological progress often responds to the specific needs of the country of origin (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This suggests that technological innovations may not readily transfer across different economic contexts, necessitating local adaptations. They argue that institutions governing public choice can complement reputation-building mechanisms, thereby improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This indicates that the successful implementation of technological innovations depends on the presence of robust institutional frameworks that can enforce rules and maintain commitments over time. This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary for economic growth, the process is dynamic and self-reinforcing, with technological progress continually fueling further advancements.

These changes are exemplified by historical events such as the Black Death and technological advancements during the Industrial Revolution, which significantly influenced long-term economic structures, initiating a series of changes that reshaped economic landscapes across Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift was not merely a short-term adjustment but a long-term structural change that altered the economic dynamics of the continent, as soldiers became more expensive and those with firearms fought more effectively (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Komlos (1998, p.8) argues that the relative price of nutrients increased during this period, partly due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry. This increase in relative nutrient prices led to a substitution of industrial commodities for food products, further altering consumption patterns and economic structures. The extent to which these structural changes directly impacted long-term economic growth remains a subject of debate. Temin (2002, p.4) notes that economic growth during the post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, suggesting that historical events and technological advancements can have lasting effects on economic growth even in the absence of direct institutional changes. This implies that while historical events and technological advancements play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic structures, their impact on economic growth is mediated by a complex interplay of factors, including labor allocation and relative prices.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.24) share the view that economic growth and social change are driven by a complex interplay of factors, including technological progress and demographic shifts. Abramovitz notes that technological opportunities encourage rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn reduces the relative price of capital goods. This mechanism is crucial for understanding how technological advancements can drive economic growth. Lindert and Williamson add that technological progress and demographic forces can influence income inequality, but these effects are not inevitable and depend on the specific historical context. The interaction between technological progress and demographic changes can lead to significant economic growth, but the outcomes are contingent on various factors, including the pace of technological change and the distribution of labor. Bryant (2006, p.11) qualifies this by highlighting the importance of structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors. European agricultural productivity released labor for industrial employment, fostering growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. This structural differentiation was absent in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming and domestic handicrafts, limiting the scope for economies of scale and capital accumulation. The implications of these findings suggest that the institutional framework, including property rights and labor market structures, plays a critical role in mediating the impact of technological progress and demographic changes on economic growth. The structural changes in the economy, such as the release of labor from agriculture, can significantly influence the trajectory of economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1) argue that technological innovations are the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel applications (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of knowledge accumulation and innovation is not merely a linear progression but involves complex interactions and dependencies. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, its long-term impact is contingent on supportive institutional frameworks that prevent rent-seeking behaviors and promote economic reasonableness. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) further emphasize that technological constraints, such as the vast unexploited coal reserves in the northeast, played a significant role in shaping the economic landscape (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5). The interplay between technological progress and institutional support is evident in the historical context of economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations. Kuznets shows that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Together, these sources show that institutions are essential for translating technological progress into sustained economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54), but its sustainability is contingent on complementary institutional progress. Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional support is evident in the historical context of economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz highlights that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This suggests that institutions are not just passive recipients of technological progress but active agents that shape the economic landscape. Institutions that promote economic reasonableness and prevent rent-seeking behaviors are essential for sustained economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Lindert and Williamson argue that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, highlighting the importance of institutions in balancing these goals (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24). This shared finding indicates that institutions can foster both growth and equality, which is crucial for long-run economic growth (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24; Mokyr 2005, p.54). The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the fact that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This interdependence between technology and institutions underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
