The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study that addresses how societal structures and rules influence economic development over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement over the mechanisms by which institutions exert their influence. One perspective emphasizes the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights in fostering economic growth. Another perspective focuses on the efficiency of institutions in resolving coordination failures and facilitating technological progress. A third perspective highlights the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. Understanding these dynamics is essential for comprehending the historical trajectories of economic development and for informing policy decisions aimed at promoting sustainable growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, as seen in the development of capital markets, underscores the importance of credible commitments in economic growth. Credible commitments, such as those that emerged in seventeenth-century England, were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective highlights the endogenous role of political institutions in mitigating incentive problems and maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents. The influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes extends beyond the immediate effects of European settlers, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue. Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established by European colonizers were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for the colonists and promote long-run growth. This extractive nature was a result of the political power dynamics at play, where colonizers implemented institutions that secured their interests and maintained their control (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. also show that differences in European mortality rates in colonies led to varied settlement patterns, which in turn shaped the institutions established. High mortality rates deterred settlement, leading to the implementation of extractive institutions that persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. note that while these institutions were extractive, they also played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This suggests that even extractive institutions can have complex and multifaceted effects on economic development, influencing outcomes in ways that go beyond simple exploitation.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7); (Domar 1970, p.7) focus on specific economic institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, examining their efficiency and distributional impacts. They argue that these institutions can be understood as efficient solutions to specific economic problems, such as contract enforcement and property rights. For example, merchant guilds in the tenth and eleventh centuries provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the specific economic contexts in which institutions operate. Domar (1970, p.7) qualifies the efficiency of these institutions by noting that their profitability can be influenced by factors such as technological progress and capital accumulation (Domar 1970, p.6). This suggests that while institutions may be efficient in the short term, their long-term impact on economic growth can be complex and multifaceted.

The technological advancement that propels economic growth necessitates complementary institutional adjustments, as Kuznets (1973, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.54) argue. Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that the accumulation of material and social technology is crucial for economic growth, noting that inventions often respond to specific needs and can be the mother of invention. This technological progress is closely linked to scientific advancements, which in turn drive further technological innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.54) builds on this by highlighting that technological progress depends on the knowledge of existing techniques and often involves recombining known methods to create novel solutions. This process of technological advancement involves coordination failures that require institutional progress to rectify. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that the mass application of technological innovations, many of which are based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that facilitate the efficient use of technological innovations, ensuring that economic growth is sustained over the long term. Technological progress and institutional adjustments are thus intricately linked, with each reinforcing the other to drive economic growth.

Institutions also play a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the efficiency of market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is essential for reducing costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and supports economic growth by lowering barriers to trade and investment. Additionally, North and Weingast emphasize that credible commitments by political institutions are vital for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This involves the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions that enforce these rules and govern how they may be changed. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how political factors underpin economic growth and the development of markets.

Moreover, institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the distribution of power and resources. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the importance of institutional design in determining economic outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective underscores how institutions reflect the interests of dominant groups and shape economic structures accordingly.
