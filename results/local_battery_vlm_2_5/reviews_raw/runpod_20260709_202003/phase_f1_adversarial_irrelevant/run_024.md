The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship, with debates centering on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely complementary factors. Institutions are understood as the formal and informal rules that govern behavior in a society. Some scholars argue that institutions are critical for economic growth, facilitating technological progress and efficient resource allocation. Others contend that technological advancements themselves drive economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. A third position posits that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, with efficiency considerations being secondary. The intellectual stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions in economic growth has implications for policy design and economic development strategies. The debate hinges on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and the extent to which these mechanisms are independent of other factors such as technological progress. Institutions can foster economic growth by reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.6). This framework is essential for understanding how economic growth can be sustained over the long run. The extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they are shaped by other factors, remains a key question. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was instrumental in lowering costs per transaction, thereby enabling a growing proportion of GNP to be allocated to the transactions sector. This development was essential for fostering economic growth. They highlighted that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional change improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) provide a historical perspective that supports the role of institutions in economic growth. They noted that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not necessarily encourage economic development, but their design often maximizes the rents to politically powerful groups, such as European colonists. This implies that the institutional framework can significantly influence economic outcomes, even if it does not directly maximize long-run growth.

Colonial institutions established by European settlers significantly influenced economic development in colonies, with the nature of these institutions often determined by mortality rates faced by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.3). These institutions were characterized by coercion and the extraction of rents, which stifled economic development by restricting entry to and exit from markets (Austin 2008, p.23). The establishment of extractive institutions was deeply rooted in the economic and social structures of the colonies themselves, as well as being a product of colonial policies. This historical legacy of extractive institutions continues to affect current economic outcomes, as seen in the persistent low income per capita in many former colonies. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic development.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions are less critical (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective posits that the growth of useful knowledge propels technological advancement, which in turn fuels economic growth. This mechanism is supported by Kuznets, who notes that technological innovations are closely connected to the progress of science, which provides a positive feedback loop for further technological advances (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological progress and scientific advancement underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where new knowledge continuously drives innovation. This qualification suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, its impact can be uneven, depending on the specific needs and contexts of different economies. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of economic growth, where technological progress plays a pivotal role but must be understood in conjunction with other factors, including the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for efficient use of innovations.

The social and technological dynamics of economic growth are marked by a complex interplay between technological progress and social change, as Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) jointly show. Abramovitz argues that technological opportunities encourage rapid growth of the capital stock, both due to the returns from modernization and the relative price reduction of capital goods. This dynamic is further complicated by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which Abramovitz (1986, p.4) elaborates on. The acceleration in the growth of the capital stock, driven by technological progress, tends to reduce the technological age relative to chronological age, thereby enhancing productivity growth. This focus on distributional conflicts suggests that the efficiency of institutions is not the sole determinant of their emergence and persistence. This dynamic relationship implies that institutions evolve in response to changes in technological and social conditions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Historical factors like technological changes and demographic shifts influenced economic growth, but they do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22; Komlos 1998, p.8). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) examines the impact of barbed wire fencing on agricultural development, noting that the technology enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). Pamuk (2007, p.22) discusses how the Black Death led to long-term structural changes, including a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, driven by increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital (Pamuk 2007, p.22). These technological and demographic shifts highlight the importance of historical context in economic growth, but they do not delve into how institutions mediated these effects. This uneven distribution implies that the institutional context, such as policies on income redistribution and public health, played a crucial role in determining the outcomes of economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Temin 2002, p.4) share a focus on the relationship between economic growth and social inequality, but they do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. They argue that economic growth can be influenced by various factors, including the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that in New World societies with greater inequality, elites were advantaged through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective is supported by Temin (2002, p.4), who emphasizes that economic growth can be driven by disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. The institutional context, such as policies on income redistribution and public health, played a crucial role in determining the outcomes of economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.3) further illustrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This suggests that the institutional frameworks that supported or hindered these contributions were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. The role of institutions in mediating the effects of economic growth on social inequality is a critical area for further research, as it can provide insights into how policies and structures can be designed to promote more equitable outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and ensuring its sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both emphasize that technological advancements are crucial drivers of economic growth, but they also highlight the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting and sustaining these advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) discusses how institutional progress is essential for addressing coordination failures and preventing rent-seeking behaviors that could undermine economic improvements. Similarly, Kuznets (1973, p.10) argues that economic growth is intrinsically linked to technological progress, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These arguments underscore the necessity of robust institutions in harnessing the full potential of technological advancements for long-term economic growth.

The dynamics of technological progress and institutional support are further elucidated by Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and North (1989, p.6). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) shows that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, emphasizing the role of institutions in facilitating modernization and technological progress. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how institutions that specify and enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges are crucial for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. This perspective is supported by Hornbeck (2010, p.4), who demonstrates that technological innovations, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities. These findings collectively illustrate that institutions are essential for translating technological innovations into sustained economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between technological progress and social change. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and North (1989, p.5) both acknowledge that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and social dynamics, as well as being efficient solutions to transaction costs. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that many pre-industrial institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were driven by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. North (1989, p.5) contends that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs and technological knowledge, overlook the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes. These insights highlight that institutions are not just passive facilitators of economic growth but active agents shaped by and shaping social and economic dynamics. The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; North 1989, p.5). The disagreement is whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence of pre-industrial institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, settles this disagreement by showing that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers of institutional development.
