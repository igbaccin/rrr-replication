The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, encompassing the rules and norms that govern social and economic interactions, are crucial in fostering or hindering economic progress. A central debate focuses on how institutions, along with other factors like geography or culture, influence economic growth. Some scholars emphasize that institutions are the primary determinants of economic performance, arguing that well-designed institutions can facilitate economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and efficient markets. Other scholars, however, contend that institutions are but one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that their impact can be overshadowed by other variables, such as resource endowments or historical contingencies.

A key point of contention lies in the interpretation of historical evidence. For instance, some scholars argue that the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets were crucial, focusing on institutions that govern how rules are enforced and changed. These institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the state's ex post behavior improve its ability to maintain bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement. This institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties and allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations. As a result, the development of efficient product and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, becomes crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in fostering long-run economic growth. This role is primarily through the establishment of secure property rights and the development of efficient markets, which facilitate impersonal exchange. This institutional framework provided a reliable basis for economic interactions, even among strangers, thereby enabling the realization of productivity gains from specialization and division of labor. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is crucial for reducing transaction costs, which are integral to economic development. These institutions create a stable environment where individuals can confidently engage in complex interdependent situations, fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.2) adds that creating such intricate social structures demands institutional stability to reduce uncertainties. This reliability ensures that individuals can have confidence in their dealings, even with those they do not know personally. As a result, the establishment of reliable institutions is essential for sustaining long-run economic growth, as they provide the necessary foundation for efficient markets and secure property rights.

Despite these institutional advancements, improvements in aggregate purchasing power did not necessarily translate into better biological well-being or higher life expectancy for all segments of society during the early modern period (Komlos 1998, p.13; van Zanden 1999, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.8). This suggests that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed across society. The relative price of nutrients increased, partly due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry. This led consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). Pamuk (2007, p.8) further qualifies this by noting that urban-rural differences in wages and productivity could persist, affecting the standards of living. This implies that while economic growth may increase aggregate purchasing power, it does not guarantee improvements in biological well-being for all segments of society. This is especially true when growth is not accompanied by improvements in nutrition and living conditions.

This dynamic is particularly evident in societies with pronounced inequality, where institutions can reinforce elite advantages, as highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). They focus on the New World, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power was established early on. This inequality was perpetuated through institutions that restricted competition and favored elites, creating a persistent barrier to broad-based development. This mechanism is particularly relevant in contexts where inequality is high, as it shows how institutional changes can directly impact productivity and resource allocation. The enduring nature of inequality indicates that institutional changes alone might be insufficient to address longstanding disparities. The role of institutions in mitigating or exacerbating inequality remains a critical factor in long-run economic growth, as it directly influences the distribution of resources and opportunities.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, with the persistence of these institutions significantly affecting current income per capita. These institutions, shaped by European colonists to maximize their own rents, often did not foster long-run growth. For example, in colonies where European mortality rates were high, extractive institutions were established, which persisted and had a lasting impact on economic performance. This mechanism is evident in the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn influenced current income levels. The connection between these institutions and economic growth is intricate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) find that institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. Nevertheless, the impact of these institutions is not uniform across all colonies, suggesting that other factors, such as the specific historical context and the nature of colonial rule, may also play a role in shaping long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions, even after the end of colonial rule, highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies on economic development.

Austin (2008, p.11) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26) examines the historical economic divergence and the role of institutions, but it does not directly engage with the question of whether institutions are irrelevant to long-run economic growth. This perspective is grounded in a disaggregated view of factor endowments, including specific aspects of the natural environment, which influenced labor productivity. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that prevalent labor scarcity in Sub-Saharan Africa is not inconsistent with higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population, which may indicate higher real per capita income. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) support this view by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.23) explain that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, highlighting the role of productivity differences in shaping economic divergence. Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal in others. The implications of this are profound, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and contingent on specific historical and environmental factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with historical evidence and theoretical frameworks highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This is echoed by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The establishment of efficient property rights and reliable enforcement mechanisms allows for confidence in economic dealings, even in highly interdependent situations. This institutional reliability is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, underscoring the critical role of institutions in economic development.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the work of Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This divergence is linked to differences in productivity and wage structures, with Europe's higher productivity in the tradable goods sector contributing to its economic advancement. Austin qualifies this by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal in others (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on specific historical and environmental factors, adding nuance to the understanding of institutional effectiveness.

Acemoglu et al. provide a complementary perspective by arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They show that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists did not necessarily encourage long-run economic development. This highlights the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes, with extractive institutions often hindering growth. The shared finding is that institutions play a pivotal role in economic growth, but the precise mechanisms and outcomes vary across different historical and geographical contexts (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the role of productivity differences in shaping economic divergence, as shown by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23).
