The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, encompass formal rules such as constitutions and laws, as well as informal constraints like norms and conventions. Understanding their role is crucial because it helps explain why some economies thrive while others stagnate. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as technology or geographic conditions, play a more significant role. Institutions may facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources, the incentives for innovation, and the protection of property rights. Another live debate involves the efficiency of institutions: are they designed to maximize economic growth, or do they serve other purposes, such as maintaining the power of certain groups? The resolution of these debates has profound implications for policy-making and economic theory. Institutions can be seen as both a cause and a consequence of economic growth, creating a complex interplay that requires careful analysis. The efficiency of institutions in promoting economic growth is a contentious issue, with some scholars arguing that institutions are designed to maximize growth, while others contend that they often serve the interests of powerful groups. This tension is evident in the divergent views on the role of institutions in economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

Acemoglu et al. and others (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) present a compelling argument that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The mortality rates faced by European settlers influenced the type of institutions established in these colonies, with higher mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonists rather than promote long-term economic growth. This institutional framework was not conducive to economic development, as it was shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their control and extract resources. The implications of these institutions persist today, as they continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that the institutions established during colonial times played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. However, the persistence of these institutions is not merely a historical artifact; it is a dynamic process. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) note that the impact of institutions on economic performance can be estimated by exploiting differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development. This dynamic interplay between historical institutions and current economic outcomes underscores the importance of understanding the long-term effects of institutional legacies.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). However, the direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results, suggesting that the institutional framework supporting property rights is crucial for agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Domar (1970, p.7) reinforces this idea by arguing that the profitability of slave labor, a different form of property rights, is influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation. This indicates that the effectiveness of property rights institutions depends on the broader economic context, including technological advancements and capital availability (Domar 1970, p.6). The interplay between technological change and property rights institutions suggests that economic development is not solely driven by technological progress but also by the institutional framework that supports and protects property rights. This dynamic relationship underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts played a crucial role in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the prevailing notion that these institutions were primarily driven by economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by the need to manage conflicts over resource distribution. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument, showing that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions were structured to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies often restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. These distributional conflicts highlight how institutions are not just economic tools but also mechanisms for managing social and political power. The persistence of these institutions, despite changes in economic conditions, indicates that their primary function was to maintain the status quo rather than to adapt to new economic realities. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which institutions operate, as these contexts can significantly influence their development and longevity.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. Their work highlights that institutional frameworks, which define property rights and enforce market rules, play a crucial role in facilitating economic transactions and reducing transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) explains that as economies grow, the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights become increasingly important. This institutional framework supports market exchanges, leading to a reduction in costs per transaction and an increase in the aggregate volume of exchanges. This commitment is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, thereby preventing wealth expropriation. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) clarify that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining behavior in situations where reputation alone is insufficient. This institutional framework is necessary to mitigate incentive problems that reputation strategies alone cannot address. However, North (1989, p.5) cautions that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, and capital, oversimplify economic growth. He argues that these models fail to account for the significant role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of economic growth that includes the institutional context in which economic activities occur.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share the view that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress is not merely about the invention of new tools but also involves the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel solutions. This process depends on a preexisting knowledge base and the ability to identify gaps and opportunities in current technologies. However, Kuznets (1973, p.10) also notes that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This qualification suggests that while technological progress is crucial, its benefits are maximized when complemented by supportive institutions. Thus, while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its sustainability and effectiveness are enhanced by a supportive institutional framework.

Technological progress is a key driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4). It is not merely the accumulation of knowledge, but the effective application of this knowledge that propels economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) both highlight that technological progress is closely connected with the advancement of science and the ability to apply innovations efficiently. Kuznets (1973, p.10) shows that mass application of technological innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provide a positive feedback loop that enhances economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which further underscores the importance of a knowledge base that can be effectively utilized. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial; without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore pivotal.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is underscored by North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, highlighting that the neoclassical model, which describes the output of an economy as a function of inputs, is misleading without considering the role of institutions. North (1989, p.6) shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further support this view by demonstrating that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth, which highlights the importance of institutional design in economic outcomes (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This underscores that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that institutions can significantly enhance economic development by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provides a clear example of this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains. This technological innovation enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, demonstrating the practical benefits of institutional support for technological progress (Hornbeck 2010, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also supports this view by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) shows that many pre-modern institutions, traditionally condemned for blocking economic improvements, have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to specific obstacles, highlighting the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). The precise historical evidence of the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains and the reinterpretation of pre-modern institutions in Europe settles the disagreement by demonstrating the practical benefits of institutional support for technological progress.
