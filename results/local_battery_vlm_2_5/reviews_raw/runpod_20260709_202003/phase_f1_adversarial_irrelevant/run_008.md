The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the identification of the fundamental drivers behind sustained economic expansion, as well as the potential for policy intervention to foster growth. The live disagreement centres around whether institutions are a primary cause of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geography or cultural attitudes, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions create the necessary conditions for economic growth by establishing property rights, enforcing contracts, and reducing transaction costs, while others contend that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper economic or social forces. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for understanding historical economic development and for crafting effective policies to promote growth in developing economies. Institutions matter because they set the rules of the game, which can either facilitate or hinder economic activity. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, together with North, highlight the pivotal role of institutional evolution in shaping long-run economic growth. Their shared finding is that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast explain that the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution, which included restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state, significantly improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This institutional reliability is essential because it reduces uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing for confidence in dealings with individuals with whom one has no personal knowledge (North and Weingast 1989, p.7; North 1989, p.2). North elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should manifest in the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges. This institutional framework reduces transaction costs, enabling a growing proportion of economic activity to occur through market exchanges. As specialization and division of labor increase, the aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies, further driving economic growth. However, North also notes that institutional reliability is not automatic; it requires the development of efficient product and factor markets, as well as a reliable medium of exchange. This underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its critical role in sustaining long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. identify that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth by establishing extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This extractive institutional framework was not designed to encourage economic development but to maximize the rents to European colonists. The mechanism through which these institutions affected economic growth is that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. This extractive institutional framework was not designed to encourage economic development but to maximize the rents to European colonists. This institutional legacy persists and significantly affects current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. The results support the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies, and via this channel, account for a significant fraction of current income differences. This suggests that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) along with Hornbeck (2010, p.34) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality have historically advantaged elites through policies that restrict competition, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional bias is evident in the New World societies, where the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset.

This institutional bias is further exemplified by the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) shows that this technological innovation significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. However, the benefits of such technological advancements were unevenly distributed, often favoring those with existing economic and political power. This pattern suggests that while technological progress can spur economic growth, the institutional context in which it occurs determines who ultimately benefits from these advancements. This dynamic underscores the enduring role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) jointly highlight that economic development did not always translate into improved standards of living in Europe. This is evident in the divergence between economic growth and biological well-being during the Industrial Revolution. Komlos shows that despite overall economic expansion, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. These findings challenge conventional views that equate economic growth with universal improvements in living standards. Van Zanden's critique of optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth in Europe underscores this point, showing that the relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and not always positive. However, Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies this by noting that relative price effects and changes in consumption patterns also played a significant role in shaping living standards during this period. This suggests that the impact of economic growth on living standards is mediated by various factors, including the distribution of income and changes in relative prices. In this context, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth becomes crucial, as they influence how the benefits of economic development are distributed and how resources are allocated.

Pamuk (2007, p.8) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) both argue that historical factors, such as the Black Death and pre-1800 economic conditions, played a significant role in shaping the economic trajectories of different regions. Pamuk (2007, p.8) highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This divergence can be attributed to differences in labour productivity in the tradable goods sector, with Asia experiencing relatively low productivity compared to Europe. The mechanisms of arbitrage and silver wages played a crucial role in this divergence. However, the linkages between urban and rural sectors were not always equally strong, which could lead to significant urban-rural differences in wages and living standards. This pattern suggests that the impact of historical events on economic development is complex and multifaceted, involving both immediate and long-term effects. Pamuk (2007, p.8) qualifies this view by noting that the linkages between urban wages and productivity in rural areas could weaken, leading to persistent differences between urban and rural sectors. This qualification underscores the importance of considering the diverse impacts of historical events on different sectors of the economy.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views debate the historical and geographical determinants of economic development, without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). This thesis does not account for the complexity of labour productivity in Africa, which can be understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). Bryant (2006, p.29) further elaborates on this by noting that the economic realities in different regions, such as Europe and China, were governed by distinct demographic and economic considerations. In Europe, the productivity revolution in agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering growth-enhancing articulations between town and country (Bryant 2006, p.29). This structural differentiation was absent in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts, limiting both the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production and the capital accumulation necessary for its expansion. Austin (2008, p.11) qualifies this view by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products in certain historical contexts, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa (Austin 2008, p.1). The kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is context-dependent.
