The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic progress over centuries. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors such as technology, population growth, or resource availability play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions, by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, create an environment conducive to economic activity and innovation. Others posit that technological advancements and scientific discoveries are the true engines of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. The crux of the disagreement lies in whether institutions are the necessary foundations upon which economic growth is built, or merely facilitators of a process driven by other factors. Institutions' role in economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with some scholars emphasizing their role in mitigating transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, while others view them as responses to technological changes and distributional conflicts. The debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth. Economic growth demands both technological progress and institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

North (1989, p.6) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) argue that economic growth is driven by institutions that facilitate market exchanges and protect property rights. These findings underscore the role of institutions in mitigating coordination failures and externalities, thereby promoting economic growth. Moreover, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also observe that these institutions complement reputation-building, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their ability to complement and enhance existing social mechanisms.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both focus on how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, addressing the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth. Distributional conflicts, driven by the struggle over resources and power, played a crucial role in the formation and evolution of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft and merchant guilds. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic challenges but were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts among different social groups. Kuznets (1973, p.10) notes that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This suggests that the institutions shaped by distributional conflicts were mechanisms for adapting to technological changes and ensuring their efficient use. However, Kuznets (1973, p.4) further qualifies this by highlighting that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries might not be abundant, implying that the institutional adaptations driven by distributional conflicts might not always lead to efficient use of innovations. This points to a complex interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations, where the latter are influenced by the power dynamics and distributional conflicts of the time.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Domar (1970, p.7) reveal how inequality and institutional policies shaped development paths, particularly in New World societies. They show that institutions in societies with greater inequality, such as those in the Americas, were structured to advantage elites through various government policies. This institutional framework restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in these societies was a direct result of the large plantations and the predominance of slave populations. The efficiency of these plantations and the demographic makeup of the populations made inequality a structural feature of these economies. Domar (1970, p.7; 1970, p.6) adds that the profitability of slavery was influenced by the land-to-labor ratio and the availability of labor. This means that technological progress and greater use of capital did not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared with free labor. The persistence of inequality in these societies was thus a result of institutional policies and a function of economic conditions that favored the continuation of slavery. These findings suggest that the institutional adaptations driven by distributional conflicts in these societies had a profound impact on their economic development, shaping their paths in ways that reinforced existing inequalities.

The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr and Abramovitz have highlighted, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This perspective emphasizes the role of technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge in fostering long-run economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological progress depends on the existing knowledge base, often involving the recombination of known techniques. This process can lead to significant innovations, such as Cort's puddling and rolling process during the Industrial Revolution. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) complements this view by noting that technological opportunities encourage the growth of the capital stock, as technological progress reduces the relative price of capital goods. This dynamic creates a feedback loop where technological advancements spur further economic growth, as the productivity gains from new technologies drive increases in aggregate output. Mokyr (2005, p.12) cautions that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustainable economic improvement. Institutional progress is also necessary to complement technological advancements and prevent the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where the accumulation of knowledge is a critical but not isolated factor. The sustainability of economic growth therefore requires technological innovation and the development of institutions that can support and sustain these innovations.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is profound, as institutions introduced by colonial powers significantly shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This mechanism is evident in the design of institutions that maximized rents for European colonists rather than fostering sustainable growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time has had lasting effects on economic performance, as early institutional structures laid the groundwork for current institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate that the role of institutions in economic growth extends to the surge of industrialization and the significant income differences observed today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) qualify this by noting that the impact of institutions on economic performance is moderated by the exogenous variation in institutional structures, highlighting the complex interplay between historical factors and institutional design (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are formed and how they evolve over time.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally intertwined with technological progress and the efficient use of innovations. Institutions, by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, play a crucial role in enabling economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the historical context of the Industrial Revolution, where technological progress was complemented by institutional progress to sustain economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains. Kuznets (1973, p.4) further illustrates this by noting that the mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with the progress of science, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for technological advancement. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is crucial for long-run economic growth.

The dynamics of technological advance and social change also influence the catch-up process in economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that the same technological opportunity that permits rapid progress by modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock. This is because technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to the price of labor, thereby accelerating the rate of rise of the capital-labor ratio. Kuznets (1973, p.10) supports this by noting that the stock of innovations suitable to the needs of less developed countries is not abundant, and that necessity is often the mother of invention. This underscores the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments for the efficient use of innovations, highlighting the complex interplay between technological progress and institutional design. The historical evidence of the Industrial Revolution in England, where major inventions were responses to specific needs, illustrates this dynamic.

The enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are formed and how they evolve over time. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the craft guild. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were efficient solutions to transaction costs. North (1989, p.5) supports this by arguing that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The historical context of pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions evolved to address specific needs and conflicts, shaping long-run economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of the American Plains, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development. Hornbeck (2010, p.4) shows that barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle. This historical evidence illustrates how institutions, by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities, can facilitate economic growth. The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that growth of productivity makes for an increase in aggregate output, broadening the horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. This shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving both technological progress and institutional development.
