The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained growth. One position is that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enabling efficient resource allocation. Another position is that institutions are shaped by powerful groups to maximize rents, often at the expense of long-run growth. A third position identifies technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth, with institutions playing a supporting role. The live disagreement centres on whether institutions are the cause or the consequence of economic growth, and whether they primarily serve to maximize efficiency or to extract rents. The debate extends to whether technological progress is the main driver of economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary or secondary role. This literature review will explore these positions, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth.

The first stream of scholarship underscores the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating economic growth. Institutions, by specifying and enforcing property rights, can lower the costs per transaction and increase the volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs is crucial for economic growth, as it allows for greater specialization and division of labor. Institutions also play a role in coordinating technological progress. Without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may not be sustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This highlights the interdependence of technological and institutional progress in driving economic growth. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The growth of economic reasonableness, or the development of institutions that support economic growth, is not always linear but is essential for sustaining long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions are a necessary condition for the sustainability of economic growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the role of political institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). He explains that as economies grow, the complexity of transactions increases, necessitating institutions that can efficiently manage and enforce property rights. They highlight that the institutions governing public choice and the enforcement of rules were crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn stimulated economic growth. The Glorious Revolution is presented as a pivotal moment where institutional changes improved the state's ability to maintain commitments to constituents, thereby enhancing economic stability. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) observe that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms. This qualification underscores the importance of a multi-faceted approach to understanding institutional determinants of economic growth, where political institutions play a complementary role in enhancing the efficacy of reputation mechanisms. This perspective highlights that economic growth is a function of technological progress and a supportive institutional environment.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This technological innovation allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, which in turn fostered a more productive agricultural sector. By securing their property, farmers could invest more confidently in improvements, knowing that their efforts would not be undermined by unauthorized grazing. This security encouraged the adoption of more advanced agricultural techniques and increased capital investment, which Domar attributes to the profitability of slave labor in certain contexts (Domar 1970, p.7). The direct technological effects of barbed wire were not the only drivers of agricultural development. Hornbeck notes that the primary benefits came from the enhanced protection of property rights, which reduced the risk of damage from stray livestock and enabled farmers to better manage their resources (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This protection mechanism is crucial as it aligns with broader institutional frameworks that support economic growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and enforceable. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that institutions in societies with greater inequality often advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional framework, where property rights are well-defined and enforced, is essential for sustained economic development, as it encourages investment and innovation.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions have been pivotal in shaping long-term economic trajectories. The extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates were designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, which prioritized the interests of the colonizers, led to persistent economic underdevelopment. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had a lasting effect on income per capita, highlighting the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in explaining current economic disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic development, even in the absence of direct colonial rule.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the analysis of distributional conflicts and urban development illuminates the institutional foundations of pre-industrial Europe. These scholars contend that the institutional structures in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, communities, and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) specifically challenges the efficiency-based explanations for the origins of merchant guilds and other economic institutions, arguing that these institutions were often the result of distributional conflicts. They note that while urbanization was crucial for economic development, it was not uniformly distributed across Europe, with central and eastern Europe lagging behind. This regional disparity in urban development highlights how institutional factors, influenced by distributional conflicts, can lead to divergent economic paths. However, the role of urbanization in economic growth is complex, as it involves not just the concentration of industries but also the broader infrastructure that supports diverse economic activities. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) further complicates this picture by suggesting that the efficiency of these institutions varied over time, with changes in conditions leading to the replacement of old institutions with new ones that were more efficient under the new circumstances. This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional efficiency underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.22) and Komlos (1998, p.8) focus on long-term structural changes and their economic impacts, but do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This dynamic shift in economic structures is echoed in Komlos (1998, p.8), who argues that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This indicates that structural changes, such as those in wages and technology, can have profound impacts on economic development. Komlos (1998, p.12) further qualifies this by noting that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, depending on factors such as technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply. This highlights the complex interplay between demographic changes and economic structures, suggesting that while institutions may influence these factors, they are not the sole determinants of long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in fostering technological and knowledge-driven advancements is significant. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr (2005, p.54) specifically notes that technological progress is often driven by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This recombination process underscores the importance of a knowledge base that allows for the efficient application of new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that technological innovations are closely tied to the specific needs of the countries in which they originate, highlighting the contextual nature of technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This context dependency suggests that the effectiveness of technological advancements in driving economic growth is contingent on the institutional and ideological frameworks that support their application. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) qualifies this by noting that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The implication is that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its impact is mediated by the broader institutional environment, which can either facilitate or hinder the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. This underscores the intricate relationship between technological advancements and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions facilitate the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, thus driving long-run economic growth. This is illustrated by Mokyr's observation that technological progress depends on the growth of useful knowledge, which in turn requires institutional progress to complement it (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets similarly argues that advancing technology requires institutional adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This shows that institutions are crucial for translating technological progress into economic growth.

Institutions also shape economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This suggests that institutions are not just efficient solutions to economic problems, but are also shaped by the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement, which facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This shows that institutions play a critical role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic efficiency.

This demonstrates that institutional changes can have a significant impact on economic growth by facilitating the adoption of new technologies and reducing transaction costs.
