The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the extent to which institutions can influence economic outcomes over extended periods. The live disagreement hinges on whether institutions are a primary driver of economic growth or if other factors, such as technological progress or exogenous shocks, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions provide the necessary framework for economic activities to flourish, while others contend that technological advancements and social changes are the primary engines of growth. The debate is further complicated by the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, such as the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This interplay suggests that institutions may operate in conjunction with other variables. The central question is how institutions shape long-run economic growth and whether their influence is direct or mediated through other factors. Institutions may facilitate or hinder economic growth by influencing the adoption and diffusion of technologies, affecting the allocation of resources, and shaping incentives for economic actors. The role of institutions in economic growth is thus a multifaceted issue that requires a nuanced understanding of the interactions between institutions and other economic variables. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. They show that high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional framework, shaped by the initial conditions faced by colonizers, persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic development. This historical institutional path dependence underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, highlights the deep-rooted nature of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping long-term economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) further emphasize that the impact of institutions on economic performance can be estimated by exploiting differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation. This approach underscores the importance of historical context in understanding the role of institutions in economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn fostered investment in agricultural improvements. Barbed wire's impact was not merely technological; it also provided a critical institutional framework that reinforced property rights and mitigated the risks associated with livestock damage. This institutional support was crucial for agricultural development, as it created a stable environment where farmers could invest in their land with greater confidence. For example, in societies with greater inequality, elites often use their political influence to shape institutions in their favor, which can restrict competition and limit opportunities for broader economic development. This dynamic highlights a downstream implication: while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, the distributional effects of these institutions can be complex. The persistence of inequality in societies with strong property rights institutions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how these institutions interact with broader social and economic factors, as the benefits of secure property rights can be unevenly distributed.

Distributional conflicts were a primary driver in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.7) (Domar 1970, p.7) demonstrate. These conflicts were fundamental forces that influenced the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds. For example, Ogilvie contends that merchant guilds, which have often been portrayed as inefficient cartels, played a crucial role in providing security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' in the early medieval period. This perspective challenges the view that these institutions were merely responses to efficiency concerns. Domar (1970, p.6) complements this by arguing that the emergence of slavery or serfdom was closely tied to the land-to-labor ratio, suggesting that institutional structures were deeply influenced by the economic conditions of the time. This highlights that the distributional conflicts were about adapting to economic realities. However, Domar (1970, p.7) also notes that the profitability of slave versus free labor is complex and depends on various factors, including technological progress and capital use. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how economic institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and how these institutions, in turn, influence long-run economic growth.

The literature on technological advance and social change in economic growth, such as Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4), does not explicitly engage with the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. This mechanism suggests that technological advances can foster economic growth by making capital more accessible and productive. However, Temin (2002, p.4) notes that the business cycle can influence economic growth over short periods, indicating that technological progress and social change are not the sole drivers of economic growth (Temin 2002, p.3). This qualification underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including institutional frameworks, when examining long-run economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) also emphasizes that backwardness can present opportunities for modernization in both embodied and disembodied technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4), which can further stimulate economic growth. The implication is that institutions play a critical role in shaping the environment in which technological advances and social changes occur, thereby influencing long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share the posture that the institutions introduced by colonialism are important but do not directly address long-run economic growth. They focus on how institutional changes, such as the development of property rights and their enforcement, facilitate market exchanges and economic growth. North (1989, p.6) highlights that as economies grow, political institutions evolve to specify and enforce efficient property rights. This reduces transaction costs and increases the volume of market exchanges, thereby contributing to economic growth. They argue that institutions governing public choice complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of maintaining bargains and preventing expropriation. This institutional framework supports long-term economic stability. The implication of this pattern is that the evolution of institutions governing public choice can significantly influence long-run economic growth by ensuring the security of property rights and the stability of market exchanges.

The relationship between institutional changes and economic growth is not straightforward. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.7) show that the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution in England were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. They illustrate how specific institutional features can mitigate incentive problems that reputation alone cannot address. This endogenous role of political institutions underscores the importance of credible commitments in maintaining economic stability. North (1989, p.5) suggests that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, and capital, are misleading because they do not account for the critical role of institutions in economic growth. Institutions matter because they shape the environment in which economic activities occur, influencing long-term economic outcomes. The implication is that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by other factors, including the credibility of commitments and the effectiveness of enforcement mechanisms.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both argue that technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of economic growth, suggesting that institutions may be less critical than other factors (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress depends on the knowledge of existing techniques and often involves "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process of recombination and analogical thinking is crucial for sustained economic growth, as it allows for the continuous development of new technologies. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further supports this idea by noting that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This mechanism of technological advance, driven by the recombination of existing knowledge and the solving of practical problems, fuels economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10) qualifies this by noting that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). In this way, while technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, institutions still play a role in facilitating its effective implementation.

Moreover, the role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond facilitating technological progress. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.5). He posits that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Hornbeck (2010, p.30), who demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This historical evidence illustrates how institutions can directly influence economic outcomes by addressing specific challenges faced by societies. The case of barbed wire fencing shows that institutional changes can lead to tangible economic benefits, supporting the broader argument that institutions are vital for long-run economic growth.

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons provided by North and Hornbeck settle the remaining disagreement on the role of institutions in economic growth. This shows that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by addressing specific challenges and facilitating the effective implementation of technological innovations.
