The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectory of modern economies. At stake is the fundamental mechanism by which societies transition from stagnation to sustained growth. One live disagreement revolves around the causal primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as technological progress and innovation. Institutions are argued to facilitate economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity, while technological progress is seen as the driving force behind economic development, with institutions adapting to maximize its benefits. Another debate centers on the efficiency of pre-modern institutions, with some scholars viewing them as efficient solutions to specific economic problems, while others see them as products of distributional conflicts. The debate on the role of institutions in economic growth is thus multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of institutional impact and efficiency. North and Kuznets share a view that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth, but their arguments diverge on the specifics of this role. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. This institutional framework complements reputation-building mechanisms and associated punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This stability is particularly evident in the success of capital markets, which thrived under the regime committed to these rules. North (1989, p.6) argues that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it overlooks the role of institutions in economic output. This institutional framework, by providing secure property rights and effective enforcement, creates an environment conducive to long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is supported by Domar (1970, p.7), who shows that slavery or serfdom emerged when the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor was scarce. The merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries, for example, were often seen as inefficient cartels. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) suggests that 'elder' merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods'. This highlights that the efficiency of institutions can vary over time and that distributional conflicts can lead to the persistence of seemingly inefficient institutions. Domar (1970, p.7) qualifies this by noting that technological progress and greater use of capital do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared with free labor. This implies that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and influenced by a variety of factors, including distributional conflicts and technological changes.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism is supported by the high correlation between European settlements, early institutions, and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, even after decolonization, highlights their enduring impact on economic performance. This indicates that the type of institutions established was a function of mortality and the economic incentives faced by colonizers.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) focus on specific factors influencing economic development, such as technological changes and resource utilization. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) highlights how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed for increased agricultural productivity and more efficient use of land, illustrating how specific technological changes can drive economic development. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.1) argue that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, emphasizing that the expansion of coal output was primarily due to increased demand rather than technological innovations in mining. This suggests that the economic impact of resource utilization depends on broader market dynamics. The downstream implication of this is that the role of specific factors in economic development is contingent on the broader economic context.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that technological progress, fueled by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, is a key driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of knowledge accumulation in driving technological advancements, which in turn propel economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly highlights that technological innovations are closely linked to scientific progress, creating a positive feedback loop that enhances economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interdependence suggests that the efficient use of technological innovations requires not just institutional adjustments, but also a broader cultural and ideological shift that supports the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements may not be sustainable, as they can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification underscores the nuanced role of institutions in supporting technological progress, even if they are not the primary drivers of economic growth.

Institutions facilitate economic growth by addressing coordination failures and reducing transaction costs. Kuznets shows that technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science, which forms the basis for additional advances in technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Kuznets also points out that the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of less developed countries is not abundant (Kuznets 1973, p.10). North similarly argues that institutions matter by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges and reduces costs per transaction, thereby enhancing economic growth.

The role of institutions in supporting technological progress is nuanced. Mokyr argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who highlights that mass application of technological innovations is closely connected with the further progress of science (Kuznets 1973, p.4). North further elaborates on this by stating that the neoclassical model is largely misleading since it does not account for the role of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.5). The growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they require complementary institutional progress to be sustainable.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by distributional conflicts and the efficiency of pre-industrial institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the community (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). The efficiency of these institutions is often debated. For instance, Ogilvie points out that pre-modern institutions have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to various obstacles to possible transactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This suggests that the efficiency of institutions is a dynamic concept that evolves with changing conditions. Kuznets shows that the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of less developed countries is not abundant (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the adoption and diffusion of new technologies.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This is supported by Hornbeck, who argues that barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth includes the protection of property rights and the reduction of externalities. North shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is multifaceted and includes both technological and non-technological factors.

The centrality of institutions in economic growth is evident in the complementary roles they play in technological progress and property rights enforcement. The shared finding is that institutions facilitate economic growth by addressing coordination failures and reducing transaction costs (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement is whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
