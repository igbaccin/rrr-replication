Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms by which they exert this influence are hotly debated. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions primarily facilitate growth by enhancing efficiency or by redistributing resources. One position posits that institutions foster growth by creating an environment conducive to economic efficiency, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. Another perspective suggests that institutions can also drive growth by redistributing resources in ways that benefit certain groups, even if this does not always align with overall efficiency. This debate is central to understanding the broader dynamics of economic development and the factors that contribute to sustained growth over extended periods. To illuminate this issue, it is essential to examine how institutions shape economic outcomes through both efficiency and redistribution mechanisms. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution were instrumental in creating a credible commitment to property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This is because the new institutions improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thereby reducing the risk of expropriation (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North elaborates on this by noting that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional reliability is essential for economic growth because it allows individuals to engage in impersonal exchange with confidence, even in complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). The evolution of these institutions follows a complex process. North highlights that the development of more complex social frameworks requires institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with growing specialization (North 1989, p.2). This points to the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic factors to drive long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not institutional efficiency, are the core drivers behind the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. The institutional frameworks in these societies were shaped by the interests of elites, who used their power to create and maintain institutions that preserved their wealth and status. For instance, Ogilvie highlights that merchant guilds, traditionally viewed as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement for elites in the early medieval period. This efficiency served as a tool for maintaining elite control. Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate this point by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern indicates that these institutions primarily maintained the existing social order. This perspective does not diminish the importance of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie acknowledges the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework, noting that the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interlinked with various markets and contributed to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency indicates that while distributional conflicts may have been the primary driver, the specific institutional arrangements that resulted from these conflicts could still influence economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former European colonies, with extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these institutions, established during colonial rule, is a critical factor in understanding current income disparities. The mechanism through which this occurs is rooted in the political power dynamics of the time. European colonists, driven by the desire to maximize their own gains, implemented institutions that served their interests. This dynamic is evident in the high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the establishment of European settlements, which in turn influenced the early institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to extract resources, persisted over time and continue to affect economic performance today. The impact of these institutions varies across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that while institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of some formerly poor colonies, the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is complex, with some colonies experiencing significant economic gains while others remain stagnant. The variation in outcomes highlights the importance of understanding the specific institutional arrangements introduced during colonial rule and their long-term effects on economic development. This variation in economic outcomes underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped economic trajectories in different regions.

Hornbeck's analysis (Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Whatley 2018, p.2) of technological factors highlights how specific innovations can drive historical economic developments, such as agricultural productivity and slave trade dynamics. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This finding is echoed in Whatley's examination of the gun-slave cycle, which demonstrates how the importation of gunpowder technology increased slave exports by enhancing the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving. The relationship between technology and economic growth is complex. Whatley's analysis suggests that the impact of gunpowder technology on slave exports was mediated by social processes and intertemporal production links. This nuanced understanding of technological factors in economic development underscores the importance of considering the broader institutional context in which technological innovations are introduced.

Economic growth does not necessarily improve biological well-being or life expectancy across all income groups. Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in health outcomes between income groups was exacerbated by changes in relative prices, which influenced consumption patterns. Komlos (1998, p.8) argues that the relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This shift in consumption patterns, driven by economic growth and technological change, had profound implications for biological well-being. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) further illustrates this point by noting the long-run decline of real wages, which compounded the challenges faced by lower-income groups. The impact of economic growth on living standards varies across regions or time periods. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) observes that while formal institutions like guilds played a role in skill development, their monopolistic tendencies could also restrict entry and maintain higher pay levels, potentially offsetting the benefits of economic growth. This institutional context highlights the complex interplay between economic growth, institutional factors, and living standards.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) jointly argue that historical paths shaped by colonial policies and economic dynamics drove economic divergence between regions. This divergence was not merely a function of institutional frameworks. The reversal of fortune in Africa, for instance, was rooted in the commercial motivations behind the Atlantic slave trade, where the exchange value of African labor was higher in destinations than in its source regions. The scarcity of labor in relation to cultivable land and capital in Sub-Saharan Africa underscores the complexity of factor endowments and production techniques (Austin 2008, p.11). This historical path of labor exploitation and economic dynamics was not uniform across regions. The relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, highlighting the role of economic factors beyond institutional frameworks. The arbitrage of international prices and the equalization of wages between sectors within each economy further illustrate the economic dynamics at play (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). Austin (2008, p.11) also notes that economic rent and economic growth often coexisted, suggesting that the institutions favoring growth in one context might not be optimal in another (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view of economic divergence underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including historical paths and economic dynamics, when examining long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that govern economic exchange and enforce property rights. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that these institutions complement reputation-building by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is echoed by North, who underscores that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North's argument is that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). Together, these views highlight that the reliability of institutions is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby fostering economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the interplay between economic rent and growth. Austin shows that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective is significant because it underscores that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view is supported by Broadberry and Gupta, who illustrate the economic dynamics at play through the arbitrage of international prices and the equalization of wages between sectors within each economy (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23) underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including historical paths and economic dynamics, when examining long-run economic growth.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further clarified by the broader debate on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective is supported by van Zanden, who argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Van Zanden's argument is that the optimists have either ignored the work of the price historians who build up a strong case for the long run decline of real wages or claimed that there is something wrong with the way in which these were calculated for the pre-industrial period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; van Zanden 1999, p.2) underscores the importance of considering the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the development of efficient products and factor markets and of a medium of exchange with reliable features (North 1989, p.2). This development allows individuals in highly complex interdependent situations to have confidence in their dealings with individuals of whom they have no personal knowledge and with whom they have no reciprocal and ongoing exchange relationships (North 1989, p.2).
