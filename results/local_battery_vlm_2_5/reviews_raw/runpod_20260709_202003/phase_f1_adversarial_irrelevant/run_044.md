The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic development. Key positions in this debate include the view that institutions are paramount in fostering growth, and the rival view that other factors, such as natural resources or technological advancements, play a more significant role. Additionally, there is a complementary perspective that highlights antecedent factors, such as the evolution of property rights and political stability, which can trigger institutional development. Understanding the interplay between these factors is crucial for comprehending the mechanisms behind economic growth and for informing policy decisions aimed at fostering development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the colonial institutions established by European powers played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. The mortality rates experienced by European settlers in these colonies influenced the type of institutions that were established. These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritized short-term gains over sustainable development. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these regions. The extractive nature of these institutions means that they do not encourage economic development, and instead, they are designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This institutional legacy continues to affect current income differences among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.2) argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This finding is supported by evidence showing that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). These institutions supplement reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is essential because it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations. The establishment of a set of property rights allows for impersonal exchange, which is necessary for realizing productivity gains associated with increased specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.2). The development of these complex social frameworks requires institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with such situations. This institutional evolution results from deliberate political and economic processes. The institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution in England exemplify how restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state can improve its ability to maintain bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England thus played a crucial role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the critical role of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Hornbeck 2010, p.34) share a central claim that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition. This inequality was a persistent feature that shaped institutional frameworks. The persistence of inequality and the evolution of institutional frameworks are inextricably linked; the institutions that emerged in these societies reflected and reinforced the power dynamics of the elites. The initial factor endowments, such as land and natural resources, contributed to the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This inequality was maintained through institutions that restricted competition, thereby perpetuating the unequal distribution of resources and power. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains exemplifies how institutional changes can enhance property rights and reduce externalities, thereby fostering economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.34; 2010, p.36) qualifies that the benefits of such institutional changes depend on factors such as woodland coverage and crop types, highlighting the contextual nature of institutional impacts.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14); (Pamuk 2007, p.8) explore specific historical events and resources that influenced economic changes, addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Clark and Jacks investigate the role of coal in the Industrial Revolution, arguing that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution. They find that extraction costs for coal rose only modestly with cumulative output, leading to modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining over the Industrial Revolution era. Pamuk similarly focuses on a specific historical event, the Black Death, and its long-term structural changes. He argues that the Black Death initiated changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk shows that the skill premium began to decline under conditions of severe labor shortages, which should be expected to encourage human capital formation. However, this decline was not uniform across Europe; in the sixteenth century, the skill premium in Italy went back up along with the recovery of the population. This pattern suggests that the impacts of historical events like the Black Death on economic changes are contingent on local conditions and may not be uniform across different regions. These studies highlight the importance of considering specific historical events and resources in understanding economic changes, while also acknowledging the contextual nature of these impacts. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of economic development, recognizing that institutional factors are just one piece of a complex puzzle.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Bryant (2006, p.29) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This divergence was driven by differences in economic conditions and productivity, with Europe experiencing a productivity revolution in agriculture that released labour for industrial employment. This shift was facilitated by the structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors, which fostered growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. In Europe, the increase in urban population, particularly in small and mid-sized manufacturing towns and cities, created a dynamic environment that supported industrialized commodity production and capital accumulation. This demographic and economic shift was accompanied by remarkable gains in per capita productivity, rising discretionary incomes, and a doubling of grain and livestock output from 1700 to 1800 (Bryant 2006, p.28). China's economy remained tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts, limiting the scope for economies of scale and restricting the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production. This pattern highlights the importance of specific historical paths and economic conditions in shaping regional development trajectories, even before the advent of modern industrialization. The differing trajectories of Europe and Asia underscore the complex interplay between economic conditions and development outcomes, suggesting that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on broader historical and economic contexts.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 is complex, with some scholars arguing for a lack of clear correlation (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Van Zanden (1999, p.2) criticizes optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, highlighting that some studies have shown long-term growth in total factor productivity in France between 1450 and 1800, while other research points to a long-run decline in real wages. This discrepancy suggests that economic growth during this period did not uniformly translate into improved living standards. The relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This pattern indicates that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, and that the standard of living did not improve uniformly across different segments of society. Moreover, the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during this period underscores the complex interplay between economic development and living standards. This suggests that while economic growth may have occurred, it did not necessarily lead to a broad improvement in the standard of living for all segments of society.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different studies emphasizing various aspects of institutional development and their impact on economic outcomes. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is complemented by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Together, these studies underscore that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic development. This institutional framework allows for the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, enabling individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence (North 1989, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 further supports this view, showing that differences in productivity and wages were already evident in the tradable goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). These findings collectively highlight that institutional reliability is a key factor in economic growth, as it allows for the development of efficient markets and a reliable medium of exchange (North 1989, p.2).

The complex interplay between economic development and living standards is further illuminated by van Zanden's analysis of Europe between 1500 and 1800, which challenges optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Van Zanden points out that the new optimism about early modern economic growth often overlooks the long-run decline in real wages and the complexities of calculating these for the pre-industrial period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective is supported by Clark and Jacks' detailed examination of coal mining productivity during the Industrial Revolution, which shows that gains in total factor productivity were modest (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). These studies collectively indicate that economic growth does not necessarily lead to a broad improvement in living standards for all segments of society, as benefits can be unevenly distributed. This divergence is evident in the differing life expectancy trends between upper and lower income groups, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how economic growth translates into improvements in living standards.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is shown to be complex and multifaceted. All sources agree that institutions matter, as North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.2) show. There is disagreement on whether the benefits of economic growth are uniformly distributed. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) argues that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, as evidenced by the long-run decline in real wages. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) show that gains in total factor productivity were modest, indicating that economic growth did not necessarily lead to a broad improvement in living standards. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups, as this provides a clear measurement of how economic growth translates into improvements in living standards.
