The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory, with debates centering on whether institutions are primary drivers or secondary factors. On one side, institutional economists argue that the efficiency and effectiveness of institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. On the other side, technological determinists posit that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role in adapting to and implementing technological changes. This debate is intellectually significant because it shapes our understanding of how societies can achieve sustained economic development. Institutions, by setting the rules for economic interactions, can either foster or hinder the accumulation of capital and technological advancements. A nuanced understanding of this relationship is essential for crafting effective economic policies. The debate hinges on the interplay between institutions and technology, with some scholars emphasizing the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth through efficient property rights and market exchanges. Some view technological progress as the main driver, with institutions adapting to these changes. The question of whether institutions shape long-run economic growth or merely respond to it remains a pivotal point of contention. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates was a key mechanism through which this impact was realized. European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates, such as those in North America and Australia, established institutions that protected private property and fostered economic growth. In contrast, colonies with high mortality rates, such as those in Africa and Latin America, were characterized by extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. These institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. note that the impact of institutions on economic performance is complex and depends on various factors, including the initial conditions of the colonies and the specific institutional arrangements that were established. This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects were not uniform across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. conclude that the impact of institutions on economic performance is a critical factor in understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, highlighting how specific institutional changes can drive economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This institutional change reduced the cost of fencing, making it feasible for farmers to enclose larger areas of land. The increased protection of property rights enabled by barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to invest more confidently in their land, leading to increased productivity and economic growth in the region. Instead, the enhanced protection of property rights played a crucial role in fostering economic development. This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment where investments in land and agriculture can be made with greater security.

The efficiency of institutions is not the primary explanation for their formation and persistence. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that distributional conflicts and economic structures are more fundamental determinants of institutional arrangements. Ogilvie notes that merchant guilds and other pre-modern institutions have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to various economic obstacles, but this efficiency-focused view overlooks the role of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. For instance, the Mesta in pre-modern Spain, which was traditionally seen as a barrier to agricultural improvements, has been reinterpreted as an efficient solution to high fencing costs and resource access issues. This efficiency perspective overlooks the underlying power struggles and distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions. Domar, on the other hand, demonstrates that the profitability of slavery or serfdom is linked to the land-to-labor ratio and labor scarcity, rather than cultural or political factors. This insight highlights that institutions like serfdom are shaped by economic conditions and the distribution of power, not just by efficiency considerations. The efficiency approach to institutions, while useful, can oversimplify the complex interplay of economic structures and distributional conflicts. Domar's analysis suggests that technological progress and capital accumulation do not necessarily reduce the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor, underscoring the enduring influence of economic structures and power dynamics. The persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds is thus deeply rooted in the economic conditions and power dynamics of the time, which shape these institutions in ways that go beyond mere efficiency considerations.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) both emphasize that technological progress and labor reallocation are pivotal drivers of economic growth. Abramovitz explains that technological advances reduce the price of capital goods relative to labor, accelerating capital stock growth and increasing productivity. This dynamic is particularly pronounced in economies undergoing modernization, where the adoption of new technologies can rapidly enhance the capital-labor ratio. Temin further elaborates on this by highlighting the role of labor reallocation. Temin argues that the rapid economic growth observed in post-WWII Europe was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This shift allowed for significant productivity gains as labor moved from less efficient sectors to more productive ones. The disequilibrium in labor allocation thus facilitated a more efficient use of resources, contributing to the overall economic growth. Temin also notes that this process can be complex and influenced by various factors, including the business cycle and conditional convergence. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the growth dynamics, as simple convergence hypotheses may not fully capture the intricacies of economic development. The interplay between technological progress and labor reallocation highlights the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where both factors contribute to productivity gains and overall economic development.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights was instrumental in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. This process is evident in the increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges and contributed to the growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework was critical in providing a secure environment for economic activities, as it constrained the state's ex post behavior and improved its ability to maintain commitments to constituents. The institutions of public choice in England during the seventeenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the economic landscape by fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, thereby contributing to long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The role of institutions in economic growth is not limited to providing secure property rights. This complementarity is crucial as it ensures that economic activities are not solely reliant on individual reputations, which can be insufficient to prevent reneging in certain circumstances. Thus, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was a multifaceted process that involved not only the specification and enforcement of property rights but also the development of mechanisms that complemented reputation-building strategies, thereby fostering a stable and secure environment for economic growth.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, not institutions, a view shared by Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is what propels economic growth. Mokyr argues that technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial drivers of economic growth. Mokyr also points out that technological progress is often dependent on the existing knowledge of techniques and can involve "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel ones. This process of technological advancement is not merely a linear accumulation of knowledge but involves a dynamic interplay of existing techniques and innovative thinking. Kuznets reinforces this perspective by highlighting that technological innovations are closely linked to the specific needs of the countries in which they originate. This suggests that the advancement of technology is not just a matter of scientific discovery but also of addressing practical needs, which in turn drives economic growth. However, Mokyr cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements could be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors might erode the gains made through technological advancements. This implies that while technology is a primary driver, institutions play a supporting role in ensuring the long-term benefits of technological progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements and addressing coordination failures. Institutions provide the necessary framework for technological progress to be sustained and translated into economic improvements. Mokyr's work shows that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements can be unsustainable due to rent-seeking behaviors that erode gains made through technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). By ensuring that technological progress is complemented by institutional progress, economies can avoid the erosion of gains and sustain long-term economic improvements.

Moreover, institutions are crucial for addressing the practical needs that drive technological advancements. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations are often a response to specific needs, and their efficient use requires institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This is supported by Abramovitz's observation that technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby encouraging the growth of the capital stock and accelerating economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Together, these insights underscore the importance of institutions in ensuring that technological progress is not only achieved but also effectively utilized to drive long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the need for complementary institutional progress to address distributional conflicts and ensure efficient use of innovations. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions like merchant guilds and craft guilds were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement, highlighting the importance of institutions in facilitating economic activities (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This is consistent with Hornbeck's finding that technological advancements, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). These examples illustrate how institutions can support technological progress and drive economic growth.
