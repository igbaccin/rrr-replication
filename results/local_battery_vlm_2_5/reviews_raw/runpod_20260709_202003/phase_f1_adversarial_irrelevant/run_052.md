The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules such as laws and informal constraints such as norms, drive economic performance over extended periods. One position in the literature argues that institutions that provide secure property rights and enforceable contracts are crucial for economic growth. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), as institutions that ensure reliable enforcement of rules can mitigate uncertainties and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.2). However, another position contends that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex, with other factors such as technological change and resource endowments playing significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they interact with other variables in nuanced ways. This review synthesises this scholarship to elucidate the multifaceted role of institutions in long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). Their analysis highlights how institutional frameworks can mitigate uncertainties and facilitate market exchanges. This commitment underpins the reliability of institutions, which in turn supports economic growth by reducing uncertainties and enhancing market efficiency. These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby supporting a growing economy. However, the efficacy of these institutions is not absolute. North and Weingast clarify that institutions complement rather than substitute for reputation-building and punishment strategies (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This means that while institutions are vital, they work best when they enhance the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This nuanced view underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development, showing that their role is not just about enforcing rules but also about creating an environment where economic actors can have confidence in their dealings.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. The colonial institutions established by Europeans were often designed to maximize rents for the colonizers rather than to promote long-term economic growth in the colonies. This extractive institutional framework was a direct result of the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which prioritized immediate gains over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that these extractive institutions were not merely a byproduct of geographic or climatic factors, but were intentionally crafted to serve the interests of the colonizers. This institutional design had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of the colonies, shaping their development paths for centuries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) highlight that the differences in European mortality rates in the colonies led to the establishment of these extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. This mechanism underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance, demonstrating that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies today.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) jointly examine the relationship between economic growth and living standards during the early modern period, focusing on specific historical periods without explicitly addressing how institutions influence long-term economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.13; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This decline in well-being was exacerbated by the relative price effect, where the price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns. The divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups further underscores the unequal distribution of economic gains (Komlos 1998, p.8). Pamuk (2007, p.8) extends this analysis by highlighting the long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap persisted due to the linkages between urban wages and productivity in rural areas, where a much larger share of the total population lived (Pamuk 2007, p.8). The skill premium began to decline under severe labour shortages, and the drop in interest rates across Europe encouraged human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, therefore, had a complex relationship with living standards, influenced by both economic and demographic factors.

The interplay between social structures and economic outcomes highlights how historical factors such as slavery and colonialism have shaped economic development in different regions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) argue that societies with greater inequality, like those in Latin America, developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This process occurred through various government policies that advantaged the elite class, such as those that restricted access to education and political power. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This suggests that the institutions that evolved in these societies were not conducive to economic growth due to their focus on maintaining elite power structures. This has implications for understanding the role of institutions in economic development in Africa, where the legacy of colonialism and slavery has left a lasting impact on institutional structures. Nunn (2008, p.3) finds that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, complementing the research of Engerman and Sokoloff (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) which shows that slavery in the New World resulted in the evolution of institutions that were not conducive to economic growth. The production of slaves, which occurred through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping, also had negative impacts on subsequent development. This indicates that the historical factors that shaped the development of institutions in Africa and the New World have had a profound impact on their economic trajectories.

Clark and Jacks, along with Whatley, focus on specific factors such as coal mining costs and gunpowder imports, which are not directly related to the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Whatley 2018, p.2). Clark and Jacks's analysis of coal mining costs during the Industrial Revolution reveals that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output, leading to very modest gains in total factor productivity (TFP) in the industry. This finding suggests that the technological barriers and coal rents did not significantly drive economic growth during this period, as the gains in productivity were limited (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). Whatley's examination of gunpowder imports supports the gun-slave cycle hypothesis, demonstrating that imported gunpowder technology increased the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving. However, Whatley's analysis also points to the complexity of these relationships, as the direction of causality can be bidirectional, with increased slave exports potentially attracting European gunpowder technology to Africa. This bidirectional relationship complicates the straightforward narrative of technological determinants of economic growth, suggesting that the interplay between external factors and local economic activities is nuanced and multifaceted (Whatley 2018, p.15). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus not isolated from these specific factors, but rather, these factors can interact with institutions in complex ways that influence economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are discussed in terms of commercial motivations, colonial regimes, and economic rent, rather than focusing on how institutions influence long-run economic growth. They show that the economic outcomes in these regions were driven by factors such as labour scarcity, productivity differences, and the dynamics of economic rent and growth. For instance, Austin (2008, p.11) highlights that the Atlantic slave trade was motivated by the commercial exchange-value product of Africans' labour. This indicates that economic outcomes were influenced by the interplay between commercial motivations and labour productivity. This suggests that the economic divergence was not solely a product of institutional differences but was also influenced by underlying economic factors such as labour productivity and commercial motivations. This implies that while economic factors played a significant role, the institutional context in which these factors operated also mattered.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) argue that secure property rights and credible commitment are essential for economic growth. They show that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England, which ensured secure property rights, was critical for fostering economic growth. The Glorious Revolution, for example, established institutions that constrained the state's ex post behavior, thereby improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) also emphasizes the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. He argues that the development of efficient property rights and a reliable medium of exchange are essential for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. The establishment of such institutions allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in highly complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). This institutional framework is necessary for the growth of specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

Economic growth is further influenced by the specific institutional contexts in which economic factors operate. Austin (2008, p.11; 2008, p.1) argues that the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. He shows that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. This suggests that the institutional context in which economic factors operate also matters. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23) demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. They argue that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector. This implies that the economic divergence was not solely a product of institutional differences but was also influenced by underlying economic factors such as labour productivity and commercial motivations. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14; 2007, p.31) show that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output in the coal mining industry, which suggests that there were only very modest gains in total factor productivity in coal mining over the Industrial Revolution era. The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output. This indicates that the economic divergence was not solely a product of institutional differences but was also influenced by underlying economic factors such as labour productivity and commercial motivations.

However, there is disagreement about the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth.
