The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic development. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are crucial drivers of economic growth or merely ancillary factors. Some scholars emphasize the primacy of technological progress and institutional adjustments, while others argue that institutions themselves are the key determinants. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks is central to this debate. Technological progress can drive economic growth, but its sustainability and efficiency depend on the institutional context. Institutions can either facilitate or hinder the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. This interplay is evident in the historical record, where periods of rapid technological change often coincide with institutional innovations that support economic growth. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policating effective strategies for economic development. To explore this question, we can begin by examining the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress and economic growth. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 was a pivotal moment in this process. It established a credible commitment to property rights and constrained the arbitrary power of the monarch. This shift in political institutions reduced the risk of expropriation, thereby encouraging investment and economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The impact of these institutions on economic growth varies across societies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design institutions to maximize their rents rather than to foster long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the positive effects of secure property rights on economic growth depend on the specific configuration of political institutions and the interests of the powerful groups within a society.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.23) show that European mortality rates in colonies shaped the development of extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. These mortality rates influenced the likelihood of European settlement, which then determined the type of institutions established in the colonies. High mortality rates in colonies like those in Africa led to the establishment of extractive institutions, where the primary goal was to extract resources. Lower mortality rates in colonies like those in North America enabled the establishment of institutions that protected property rights and encouraged investment. The impact of these institutions is both historical and persistent, as early institutions have a lasting effect on current economic performance. This pattern underscores the importance of initial conditions in shaping institutional outcomes. The role of economic rents in this process is complex. Austin (2008, p.23) argues that in capitalist and industrialized economies, economic rents can drive innovation and investment, contributing to economic growth. In pre-colonial African economies, the extraction of certain rents was part of economic growth, indicating that the relationship between rents and growth is context-dependent. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by the economic environment in which they operate.

The economic environment is an active participant in the institutional mediation of technological change. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share the finding that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). Technological advancement is a dynamic interplay of existing knowledge and innovative recombination. Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that technological progress often relies on "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform existing ideas into novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process is interconnected with the broader economic and institutional environment. Kuznets (1973, p.10) notes that the mass application of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This implies that while technological advancement is crucial, the institutional framework plays a supportive role in harnessing the full potential of innovations. This highlights the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to the application and sustainability of technological innovations.

This interplay between technology and institutions is vividly illustrated in the context of agricultural practices and societal structures, as explored in Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Domar (1970, p.7). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) highlights how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity. Domar (1970, p.7) similarly argues that the profitability of slavery was influenced by the land-to-labor ratio and technological progress, suggesting that agricultural techniques and capital use played a crucial role in shaping economic structures. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) qualify this by noting that the efficiency of large plantations and the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in societies like those in the West Indies and South America were also driven by institutional factors. These findings indicate that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, the institutional framework that governs property rights and resource allocation is equally important. The efficiency gains from technological innovations are contingent on the broader institutional context, which can either amplify or mitigate their effects.

Pamuk (2007, p.22) and Komlos (1998, p.8) share a view that specific historical events, such as the Black Death and the Industrial Revolution, initiated long-term economic changes (Pamuk 2007, p.22; Komlos 1998, p.8). The Black Death triggered a shift in the labor market, increasing wages and altering the cost of capital relative to labor. This shift was a structural change that influenced technological innovations and economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.22) highlights that urban guilds were the primary drivers of technological advancement during this period (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This technological shift was further influenced by the increasing cost of soldiers and the effectiveness of firearms, which spurred the development of more capital-intensive industries (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Temin (2002, p.4) shows that economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, which suggests that labor market dynamics played a crucial role in economic growth during this period (Temin 2002, p.4). This implies that while historical events can drive economic changes, the institutional framework governing these changes can either amplify or mitigate their effects, shaping long-run economic growth in profound ways.

This interplay of factors highlights the complexity of the institutional framework governing economic growth, as found by Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7). This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie's (2007, p.7) exploration of pre-industrial European institutions, which illustrates how distributional conflicts can shape economic institutions in ways that go beyond efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's (2007, p.7) examination of merchant guilds and other pre-modern institutions reveals that these entities were often designed to address specific obstacles to transactions, such as information asymmetries and principal-agent problems. These institutions, while not always efficient in the traditional sense, played crucial roles in securing property rights and contract enforcement. This suggests that the efficiency of institutions is not the sole determinant of their longevity or impact. Abramovitz's (1986, p.4) emphasis on the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress further underscores the importance of considering multiple factors in understanding economic growth. The downstream implications of these findings are qualified by the recognition that institutional changes are often driven by shifts in underlying conditions, which can render previously efficient institutions obsolete. This dynamic nature of institutions highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how various factors interact to shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with various scholars highlighting different aspects of this relationship. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations by providing a stable framework for economic activities. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective is complemented by Abramovitz, who emphasizes the complex nature of the catch-up process in economic growth, influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Together, these views underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in harnessing technological progress for sustained economic growth.

Moreover, the interaction between technological progress and institutional development is crucial for economic sustainability. Mokyr highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is thus essential for long-term economic growth.

The dynamic nature of institutions further complicates their role in economic growth. Ogilvie notes that pre-modern institutions, often viewed as inefficient, were actually efficient solutions to specific obstacles in their historical contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This historical perspective is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). These examples illustrate how institutions evolve in response to changing conditions, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics in economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating technological progress and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. For instance, Mokyr emphasizes the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological advancements to ensure economic sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is supported by Hornbeck's empirical evidence on the impact of barbed wire fencing, which shows that institutions can enhance economic development by addressing specific obstacles in their historical contexts (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
