The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high, as the answer can inform policy decisions and theoretical frameworks. A live disagreement exists between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasize the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights, while others highlight the role of technological change and total factor productivity. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they are necessary and sufficient for sustained growth. This question is central to understanding the historical trajectories of different economies and the potential paths for future development. The answer to this question can illuminate the mechanisms through which economic growth occurs and the policies that can foster it. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional change was complemented by the development of capital markets, which further supported economic growth. This indicates that the specific design of institutions matters significantly. The institutional framework established in seventeenth-century England, with its constraints on state behavior, provided a foundation for secure property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) observe that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates highlights the importance of initial conditions in shaping institutional outcomes. This suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, the path dependence of institutions means that initial conditions play a critical role in determining long-term economic trajectories.

Institutional structures, which are specific types of institutions, play a crucial role in reducing uncertainties and shaping economic outcomes by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This framework is essential for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with strangers. The institutional requirements for realizing these gains include the development of efficient products and factor markets and of a medium of exchange with reliable features. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) elaborates on this by noting that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial. This means that studying one institution in isolation may not be sufficient, as entire institutional frameworks play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The European family system, for instance, was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions. This interlinked institutional system contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. The implications of this interdependency are significant, as it underscores the need for a holistic approach to understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes. This is particularly relevant in the context of New World societies, where Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This highlights how the efficiency of institutional structures can be shaped by broader social and political contexts, which in turn influence economic growth.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both discuss economic development and changes in the standard of living without explicitly addressing institutions as a causal factor (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). van Zanden highlights the debate surrounding economic growth in Europe between 1500 and 1800, noting the emergence of optimistic interpretations that challenge traditional stagnationist views (van Zanden 1999, p.2). These interpretations suggest that early modern economies, particularly in regions like France and the Netherlands, experienced significant productivity growth and even "modern economic growth" during this period. This perspective is supported by detailed reconstructions of agricultural productivity and GDP per capita estimates. Komlos, on the other hand, focuses on the Industrial Revolution, arguing that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). This decline is attributed to the rising relative price of nutrients, which led to a shift in consumption patterns away from protein-rich foods. The divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups further underscores the complex relationship between economic development and living standards. These findings suggest that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards, particularly for lower-income groups. van Zanden's optimism about early modern economic growth is tempered by Komlos's nuanced view of the Industrial Revolution, which highlights the importance of considering the distribution of income and the impact of relative price changes on consumption patterns. This interplay between economic development and living standards is crucial for understanding the broader implications of economic growth.

Lindert and Williamson, alongside Kuznets, focus on technological advancements and institutional adjustments as key drivers of economic growth (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; Kuznets 1973, p.12). They argue that these factors, while essential, do not directly explain long-run economic growth through institutions. Instead, they highlight the importance of sectoral productivity imbalances and the role of technological change in shaping economic outcomes. For instance, Lindert and Williamson note that "technological imbalance can be a prime source of secular inequality trends" (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). This imbalance can lead to significant shifts in income distribution, as different sectors experience varying rates of productivity growth. Bryant shows that the absence of structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors can limit economic dynamism. In China, the concentration of handicrafts within peasant households prevented the growth-enhancing articulations between town and country that were seen in Western Europe (Bryant 2006, p.29). This structural difference highlights how institutional arrangements can either facilitate or hinder the impact of technological advancements on economic growth. The implications of these findings suggest that while technological progress is crucial, the institutional framework governing its application plays a pivotal role in determining economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not directly addressed by studies focusing on structural changes and their impacts on economic growth. Temin (2002, p.2) and Williamson (1996, p.25) (Temin 2002, p.2; Williamson 1996, p.25) both examine the economic dynamics that drive growth, but neither directly engages with the institutional framework that underpins these dynamics. Temin argues that the Golden Age of post-WWII growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, a structural change that propelled economic growth (Temin 2002, p.2). This shift highlights how economic growth can be influenced by structural changes in labor markets, but it does not address how institutional arrangements facilitate or hinder this process. The implication is that while structural changes are crucial drivers of economic growth, the institutional context within which these changes occur is often overlooked. Pamuk (2007, p.8) illustrates this point by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This example underscores the importance of structural changes in economic growth, but it also highlights the need to consider the institutional factors that shape these changes. The structural changes in labor markets and wage structures can drive economic growth, but the institutional framework that governs these changes is equally important.

Stern (2004, p.3) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) (Stern 2004, p.3; Hornbeck 2010, p.34; Whatley 2018, p.2) highlight the importance of external influences like technology improvements and resource management in economic growth. Technology improvements can enhance productivity and reduce emissions per unit of output, even if this is not the intended consequence. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) demonstrates that technological advancements, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, can significantly enhance agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. Whatley (2018, p.2) argues that gunpowder imports increased slave exports by enhancing the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving (Whatley 2018, p.2). This technological factor, imported from Europe, illustrates how external technological improvements can drive economic growth by increasing productivity and facilitating economic development. The impact of these technological factors varies and can depend on the adoption and implementation of best practice technologies. Stern (2004, p.3) notes that while some countries may adopt best practice technologies, others lag behind, using less efficient technologies (Stern 2004, p.15). This variation in technology adoption underscores the complex interplay between technological factors and economic growth, where the efficiency of technology use can significantly influence economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.3) share a finding that historical factors such as slavery and colonial policies have played a significant role in shaping African economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores that the institutional framework in Africa has been influenced by historical factors, which in turn affects long-run economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.3) further elucidates that the slave trades had adverse effects on economic development, showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping also had negative impacts on subsequent development (Nunn 2008, p.3). This historical context is important because it illustrates how past institutional frameworks and practices have had lasting effects on the continent's economic trajectory. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, indicating that the role of gender in economic development is complex and multifaceted (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This highlights that while historical factors have shaped economic development, the contributions of different social groups, such as women, have also been influential.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) examine various historical factors contributing to economic divergence and industrial development. They argue that economic growth was driven by productivity gains in key sectors like coal mining and tradable goods, as well as by institutional changes. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) show that coal mining in the UK saw only modest gains in total factor productivity during the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that other factors, such as technological innovation and market expansion, were more crucial (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) similarly demonstrate that pre-1800 productivity differences in tradable goods sectors between Europe and Asia were significant, indicating that the great divergence was already underway before the Industrial Revolution (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). These findings suggest that economic growth in the long run was influenced by a complex interplay of technological, market, and productivity factors, not just institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.11) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation, highlighting the importance of considering different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.11). This implies that while productivity gains and technological innovations were crucial, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth cannot be dismissed entirely, as they interacted with these factors in nuanced ways.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of England's Glorious Revolution, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that these institutions complemented reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is crucial for understanding how political factors underpin economic growth and the development of markets.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is further illustrated by Lindert and Williamson's analysis of the Kuznets Curve, which highlights the possible causal role of imbalance in the rates of total factor productivity advance across sectors (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). They note that if product demands are elastic, technological imbalance can be a prime source of secular inequality trends. This perspective aligns with Stern's argument that improvements in the state of technology involve changes in both productivity and emissions-specific changes in process, resulting in lower emissions per unit of input (Stern 2004, p.3). These technological advancements, when combined with effective institutional frameworks, can drive long-run economic growth.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living is not always straightforward. Van Zanden's analysis of European economic development between 1500 and 1800 shows that there is no clear relationship between economic growth and improvements in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This observation underscores the complexity of the factors influencing long-run economic growth, including the role of institutions. Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths further supports this complexity (Austin 2008, p.1). He highlights that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). These changes show that appropriately chosen institutions can improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in precisely those circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This historical case demonstrates the critical role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, settling the debate over the relative importance of institutions versus other factors in shaping long-run economic growth.
