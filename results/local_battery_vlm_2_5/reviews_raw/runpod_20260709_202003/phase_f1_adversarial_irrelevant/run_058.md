The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or mere facilitators of technological progress. Some scholars argue that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the role of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. Others maintain that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role in sustaining economic improvement. This debate is crucial for understanding the dynamics of economic development and the conditions under which sustained growth can be achieved. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are necessary for economic growth or if technological progress alone can drive long-term economic development. The stakes are high: if institutions are crucial, then policy makers must focus on creating and maintaining effective institutions to foster economic growth. If technological progress is the primary driver, then efforts should be directed towards investing in research and development and fostering innovation. The outcome of this debate has significant implications for economic policy and development strategies. This recognition of coordination failures and the need for policy rectification sets the stage for understanding how institutions governing public choice can foster secure property rights, as North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. The emergence of these institutions was crucial for economic growth, as they provided a stable framework that encouraged investment and economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The institutions did not merely govern economic exchange but also ensured that the rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner, which was essential for maintaining economic stability and growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation system by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework was particularly important in mitigating incentive problems that could not be adequately policed by reputation alone, thereby enhancing the state's ability to maintain its commitments to constituents. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it overlooks the critical role of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.5). The development of effective institutions that specify and enforce property rights is a necessary condition for long-run economic growth, as it provides the foundation for a stable and predictable economic environment. This institutional framework not only reduces transaction costs but also encourages investment and economic activity, thereby driving long-run economic growth.

The influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes extends beyond Europe, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate. These institutions, often extractive in nature, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. The logic of this mechanism is that the institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who aimed to maintain their control and extract resources, rather than foster economic development. This institutional framework, rooted in colonial history, persists today and continues to affect economic performance. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the strong correlation between early colonial institutions and current institutional structures, which in turn influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that European mortality rates, which determined the type of institutions established in colonies, are closely linked to current economic conditions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which hindered economic growth and development. These institutions, designed to extract resources, have had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The impact of these institutions is evident in the significant fraction of current income differences that can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) reinforce this finding by showing that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies. The institutional legacy of colonialism continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutions on long-run economic growth.

This enduring influence of historical institutions is further illustrated by Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7), who show that property rights and economic institutions can significantly impact agricultural development. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains is a notable example of how technological innovations can enhance property rights protection and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological change allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity. The direct technological effects of barbed wire, such as improved fencing, were not the primary drivers of this enhancement. Instead, the key benefit lay in the increased protection from others' cattle, which reduced the externalities and enabled more effective property rights enforcement. This finding aligns with Ogilvie's (2007) argument that pre-modern institutions, such as merchant guilds, were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also notes that the efficiency approach has its limitations, as it can sometimes overlook the distributional conflicts that underlie many institutional arrangements. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need for a nuanced understanding of how property rights and economic institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The literature on technological advance and social change in growth highlights how these factors influence economic development independently of institutional frameworks (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Komlos 1998, p.8). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argues that modernization and technological progress can accelerate the growth of the capital stock, reducing the technological age of economies and enhancing productivity. This process is driven by the returns to modernization and the relative price reductions of capital goods, which stimulate investment and economic growth. Technological progress also broadens the horizon of scale-dependent technological improvements, further propelling economic development. Komlos (1998, p.8) provides a complementary perspective, noting that during the Industrial Revolution, despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups underscores the complex interplay between economic growth, technological change, and social well-being. The relative price of nutrients increased during this period, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This substitution pattern highlights how technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture lagged behind industrial advancements, influencing consumption patterns and biological well-being. This qualification underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where technological advancements and social changes can have both positive and negative effects on long-run economic growth and well-being.

This complex interplay is further explored by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.22), who argue that the great divergence in economic growth between Europe and Asia was already significant by the early modern period. Urban development and productivity differences played a pivotal role in this divergence. They emphasize that the spatial concentration of diverse industries fostered external economies of scale, which were not realized in proto-industrial settings. This urban infrastructure provided benefits such as specialized information flows, skilled labor, and thick markets for inputs and outputs (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to wage gaps between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. He notes that as wages increased relative to rents and the price of capital, substitution of land and capital for labor began to occur across the continent (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This substitution process was driven by technological innovations, particularly in urban guilds, which were the producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22). However, Pamuk (2007, p.22) notes that the formation of 'industrial districts' to reap economies of localization did not necessarily imply large-scale urbanization, and may be quite consistent with proto-industrialization (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between urban development, technological innovation, and economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, not institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows that technological progress is a function of institutional progress and of the accumulation of knowledge. He notes that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel ones. This process depends on the knowledge base that inventors can draw upon. Kuznets (1973, p.10) similarly highlights that technological innovations are closely connected to the progress of science, which in turn provides the basis for additional technological advances. This suggests that the accumulation of useful knowledge creates a positive feedback loop, driving further technological progress and economic growth. Institutions are thus necessary for the efficient use of innovations, even if they are not the primary drivers of growth. This underscores the complex interplay between knowledge accumulation, technological progress, and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions facilitate the efficient use of innovations and technological progress, which is essential for long-run economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both underscore the necessity of institutional progress for sustaining economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress alone is insufficient for long-term economic growth; institutional progress is required to complement and sustain technological advancements. This is because the accumulation of useful knowledge, which drives technological progress, must be matched by institutional frameworks that prevent rent-seeking and ensure efficient use of resources. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that economic growth is closely tied to technological progress, which in turn relies on institutional and ideological adjustments. This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4) shows that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth by ensuring that technological innovations are effectively utilized.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North (1989, p.6) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4), who both emphasize that institutions matter beyond the neoclassical focus on population and savings. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions specifying and enforcing property rights are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework supports the growth of the transactions sector, which is essential for economic development. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) complements this by showing that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by technological advance and social change, which are themselves shaped by institutional dynamics. Both authors highlight that institutions are not merely facilitators but active drivers of economic growth, supporting the idea that institutions shape long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to technological progress and efficient resource allocation.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provide empirical evidence that institutions are essential for economic development by examining specific historical cases. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that pre-industrial institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective highlights that institutions are shaped by the social and political context in which they operate. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities. This case illustrates how institutions can facilitate technological progress and economic growth by addressing specific challenges faced by economic actors. Together, these studies (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.4) demonstrate that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by adapting to and addressing the specific needs of economic actors.
