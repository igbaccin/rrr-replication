The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory. Institutions, as the formal and informal rules that structure social interactions, are often seen as key drivers of economic performance. The degree to which institutions directly cause economic growth or merely facilitate it is still debated. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary determinants of economic growth, emphasizing their role in reducing transaction costs and providing a stable framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.6). Others posit that technological progress and social change are the main drivers of growth, with institutions playing a secondary role (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This disagreement hinges on whether institutions are seen as causal agents or as complementary factors that support other drivers of economic growth. Institutions can be viewed as the foundation upon which technological advancements and social changes build, or as mere facilitators that respond to underlying economic forces. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or merely supportive actors is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. Institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework reduces costs per transaction and multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges, thereby increasing the size of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP. This mechanism is pivotal because it underpins the development of economic institutions that support market exchanges. North and Weingast highlight that the development of capital markets in England, for example, was a direct result of political institutions that ensured secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. The success of these institutions is evident in the robust development of capital markets, which in turn supported economic growth. The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond enforcing property rights; it also involves broader governance structures that ensure the stability and predictability of economic interactions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) further elucidate that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional framework ensures that the state remains committed to its part of the bargain, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic growth.

Ogilvie and others (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These scholars challenge the prevailing notion that institutions are central to long-run economic growth by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) critiques the efficiency approach, which interprets various pre-modern institutions as solutions to transaction obstacles. Ogilvie contends that this approach overlooks the significant role of distributional conflicts in the formation and persistence of these institutions. For instance, the merchant guilds of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries are often seen as inefficient cartels, but the 'elder' merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries are viewed as efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. This perspective suggests that the efficiency of institutions is a byproduct of underlying distributional conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further this argument by examining the New World societies, where institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. Their analysis highlights that in societies with greater inequality, institutions were designed to maintain the status quo, thereby perpetuating economic disparities. This insight underscores the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions that hinder long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) notes that the efficiency of institutions can change over time as conditions evolve, leading to the replacement of old institutions with new ones that are more efficient under the new conditions. This dynamic suggests that while distributional conflicts play a crucial role in shaping institutions, the efficiency of these institutions can adapt to changing circumstances, potentially influencing long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers determined the type of institutions implemented in the colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists. These institutions persisted over time, creating a path-dependent process that continues to affect economic performance today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these 'equilibrium institutions' were designed to maximize the rents for the colonizers. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a profound effect on the economic trajectory of former colonies, shaping their current income levels and development paths. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide empirical support for the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that the differences in institutions introduced by colonialism account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that the impact of these institutions is not uniform across all colonies, as local conditions and adaptations can modify their effects over time.

Historical events, such as the introduction of new technologies and major demographic shifts, have played a pivotal role in shaping economic development trajectories. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) both demonstrate how specific technological changes and historical events influenced economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological innovation allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity, illustrating how technological changes can drive economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.22) similarly highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk (2007, p.22) argues that the demographic shock of the Black Death led to a shift in the balance of power between labor and capital, driving technological innovations and economic restructuring (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift in the labor market dynamics illustrates how historical events can catalyze economic changes that persist over the long run. The specific mechanisms through which these technological changes and historical events influence economic development are not always straightforward. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) acknowledges that while barbed wire had direct technological effects, its primary impact came from increased property protection and reduced externalities, suggesting that the institutional framework plays a crucial role in mediating the effects of technological change (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This qualification underscores the importance of considering the broader institutional context when examining the role of technological changes and historical events in economic development.

Komlos (1998, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both highlight the multifaceted nature of economic growth, emphasizing the complex interplay between technological advance, social change, and economic outcomes. Komlos (1998, p.8) illustrates how the Industrial Revolution, while fostering economic growth, also led to a decline in biological well-being due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This pattern underscores the importance of income distribution and nutritional standards in mediating the effects of economic growth on social well-being. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) extends this perspective by noting that technological progress can accelerate capital stock growth, which in turn influences productivity and aggregate output. This dynamic suggests that technological change, by reducing the price of capital goods relative to labor, can drive economic growth. This mechanism is significant because it shows how technological progress can create a virtuous cycle of increased capital investment and productivity gains. Komlos (1998, p.8) qualifies this optimistic view by pointing out that the relative price of nutrients can increase during periods of rapid technological change, leading to a substitution effect where consumers reduce their intake of protein-rich foods. This substitution can have detrimental effects on biological well-being, particularly for lower-income groups who may not be able to afford a balanced diet. This qualification highlights the need for policies that ensure equitable access to nutritious food, especially during periods of rapid economic and technological change.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) propose that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, not institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological progress, which depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, is the primary driver of economic growth. This highlights the complex interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may not be abundant, indicating that the benefits of technological progress are not universally distributed. This qualification suggests that while technological progress is a powerful driver of economic growth, its impact can be uneven, potentially exacerbating economic disparities.
