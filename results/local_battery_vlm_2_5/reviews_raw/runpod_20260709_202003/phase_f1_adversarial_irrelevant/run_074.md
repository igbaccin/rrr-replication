The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Understanding this role is crucial for discerning the underlying mechanisms that drive sustained economic progress. The live disagreement in the literature centres on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as technological progress and geographic determinants. One camp argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while another posits that technological advancements and social changes are more critical. A third perspective highlights the importance of institutional adjustments in facilitating the efficient use of innovations. The interplay between these factors is complex, and the debate hinges on whether institutions operate as catalysts or as mere facilitators of growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. They emphasize that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. This institutional framework underpins economic growth by creating an environment where the costs per transaction decrease, despite the growing volume of exchanges due to increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets. The institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed are as important as the rules themselves, as they ensure that the regime is committed to these rules. This interplay between political and economic institutions is crucial for understanding how institutional frameworks can drive long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.5) also acknowledges that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading if it does not account for the role of institutions in economic growth. The institutions that North and Weingast describe ensure that the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents is improved, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions established by European settlers were pivotal in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions were often designed to extract resources and foster development, reflecting the interests of the colonizing powers. European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita. The mortality rates faced by Europeans in these colonies influenced the type of institutions that were implemented, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic growth. This institutional design was not conducive to economic development, as it was tailored to maximize rents for European colonists and promote growth. In this context, the institutions that Acemoglu et al. describe were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which often conflicted with the long-term economic interests of the colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the enduring impact of colonial legacies on economic outcomes. The institutions established during colonial rule continue to influence economic performance today, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical roots of economic development.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both argue that economic growth dynamics are influenced by factors beyond simple convergence hypotheses. These factors include technological advance, social change, and labor allocation. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) posits that technological opportunity encourages rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor. This mechanism highlights how technological progress can drive economic growth by altering the capital-labor ratio. Temin (2002, p.3) further elaborates on this by suggesting that the business cycle can produce rapid economic growth over short periods, aligning with the idea that technological and social changes can create disequilibria that drive growth. The extent to which these factors contribute to long-run economic growth is qualified by the broader context of institutional frameworks, which can facilitate or hinder the realization of growth potentials.

Secure property rights are crucial for agricultural development, as evidenced by the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which enhanced farmers' ability to protect their land and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7). This development is about technological advancement and the institutional framework that supports and protects property rights. The economic incentives that drive institutional changes include technological improvements and the protection of property rights. Domar (1970, p.6) qualifies this by noting that the relative profitability of free and slave labor depends on various factors, including technological progress and capital accumulation. This suggests that while secure property rights are crucial for agricultural development, the broader economic context also plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. This interplay between property rights and economic development underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering growth.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions are less critical (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This process of knowledge accumulation and technological advancement involves complex interactions and dependencies. This interdependence between technological progress and contextual needs underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth. Without institutional support, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting long-term economic improvement. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks highlights the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where technological advancements are necessary but not sufficient on their own.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, highlighting that the neoclassical model's focus on inputs like land, labor, and capital is misleading (North 1989, p.5). He posits that efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) build on this by showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their analysis of former European colonies demonstrates that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly impacted relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic development.
