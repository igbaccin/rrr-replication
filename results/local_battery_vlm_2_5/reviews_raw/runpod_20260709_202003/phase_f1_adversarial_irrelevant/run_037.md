The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because the answer determines how societies can foster sustained economic prosperity. The live disagreement centers on the role of institutions versus exogenous factors like geography or culture in driving economic growth. One side emphasizes the primacy of institutional frameworks in creating the conditions for economic development, while the other acknowledges institutions as important but also stresses the influence of other factors. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers or merely facilitators of economic growth, with implications for policy and our understanding of historical economic trajectories. This tension is evident in the scholarly literature, where different streams of research offer varying perspectives on the causal mechanisms linking institutions and growth.

The impact of political institutions on economic growth is a central theme in the literature. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that political factors, such as the degree to which the regime is committed to enforcing rules, underpin economic growth and market development. This commitment is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold bargains with constituents, thereby preventing expropriation of wealth. These institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by constraining behavior in situations where reputation alone is insufficient. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the state's ex post behavior enhance its ability to maintain agreements with constituents. North also emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. The evolution of more complex social frameworks, including efficient product and factor markets, is essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. This institutional reliability allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in highly complex interdependent situations. The establishment of a reliable medium of exchange and a set of property rights enables individuals to engage in transactions with those they do not personally know, fostering economic growth. The development of efficient products and factor markets, along with a reliable medium of exchange, is integral to the institutional framework that supports economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.6) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of these institutions was not limited to establishing rules for economic exchange but also involved creating mechanisms to enforce and change these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor. This commitment was essential for maintaining the state's credibility and ensuring that it did not expropriate wealth from its constituents. North (1989, p.2) elaborates on this by emphasizing the importance of institutional reliability in reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks. This reliability is crucial for facilitating market exchanges and ensuring that individuals can confidently engage in transactions with those they do not personally know. This institutional framework supports economic growth by enabling specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This means that while institutions provide a necessary framework, they work best when paired with social mechanisms that enforce compliance. The interplay between institutional frameworks and social mechanisms creates a robust environment for economic growth, where secure property rights and reliable institutions foster long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. They argue that the institutions established during colonial periods were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This extractive institutional framework was a direct result of the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which prioritized short-term gains over sustainable development. The establishment of these institutions was influenced by factors such as mortality rates faced by European settlers, which in turn affected the type of institutions implemented. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, as Europeans were less likely to settle in areas where they faced significant health risks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic performance of the colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence current economic outcomes, as the extractive nature of these institutions has hindered long-run economic growth in many former colonies. The institutional framework established during colonial periods has had a profound and enduring impact on the economic development of these regions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) observe that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These societies were marked by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from their inception, which shaped the institutions that evolved over time. The institutions in these societies often favored elites through various government policies, which in turn restricted competition and hindered economic growth. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) notes that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This shows that when property rights are secure and competition is encouraged, economic development can flourish. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth can be contingent on geographical and environmental factors.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) argue that economic development in Europe between the 16th and 19th centuries did not translate into immediate improvements in living standards. This is because economic growth during the Industrial Revolution was not uniform and did not necessarily lead to widespread improvements in biological well-being. Komlos (1998, p.8) shows that despite economic growth, childhood nourishment was poor, leading to declines in biological well-being. This suggests that the relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and mediated by factors such as income distribution and nutritional status. This complexity is further illustrated by Pamuk (2007, p.8), who argues that long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap highlights how historical events can shape economic outcomes over the long run, independent of institutional frameworks. Pamuk (2007, p.15) also notes that the skill premium declined under severe labor shortages, suggesting that labor market dynamics can influence economic outcomes in ways that are not always predictable. This indicates that while institutions may play a role in shaping economic growth, other factors such as demographic changes and labor market conditions are also crucial.

These factors, while significant, are not isolated but are deeply embedded within the broader economic and social contexts. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Whatley (2018, p.2) argue that these factors played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes during pivotal periods. Clark and Jacks contend that coal mining productivity gains were modest during the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that the impact of coal on economic growth was less significant than previously thought. This perspective is supported by Whatley's examination of gunpowder imports, which highlights how external technological factors could influence economic activities such as slave exports. Whatley (2018, p.2) points out that the gunpowder technology increased the productivity of resources devoted to slaving, thereby driving economic changes through intertemporal production links and social processes. Whatley (2018, p.15) also notes that the economic development within slave trading networks was complex, involving various intermediate imports such as iron and steel. This complexity suggests that while specific factors like gunpowder imports could drive economic changes, the overall impact was mediated by broader economic and social dynamics. This indicates that although specific factors can influence economic growth, their effects are often intertwined with larger economic and social structures.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the economic trajectories of regions like Africa and Europe diverged due to colonial institutions, is challenged by Austin and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). They argue that this thesis oversimplifies historical causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin notes that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa indicates that until well into the 20th century, most of the region was characterised by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land as well as to capital. This labour scarcity is not inconsistent with higher real per capita income, as evidenced by a higher average population density and a relatively larger urban population compared to regions like North America and Australasia. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) support this view by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They propose that the relatively low silver wage in Asia must reflect low productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn is influenced by labour productivity in both the tradable and non-tradable sectors. This implies that the economic divergence between regions was driven by complex interactions between labour productivity, wages, and the price of non-tradable goods. Austin (2008, p.11) qualifies this by noting that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products in different historical contexts, and the institutions that favoured economic growth in certain contexts were not necessarily optimal in others. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by broader economic and social dynamics, and that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by providing the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic exchange and mitigate transaction costs. The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution, which show how restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, for example, not to expropriate their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7).
