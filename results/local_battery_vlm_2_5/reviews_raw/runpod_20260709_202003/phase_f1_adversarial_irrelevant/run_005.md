The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or resource endowments, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, particularly those governing property rights and political stability, are crucial for fostering economic growth. This perspective posits that secure property rights and effective governance create an environment conducive to investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term economic development. Another position contends that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth. Proponents of this view suggest that geographical factors, such as climate and natural resources, as well as historical contingencies, can significantly influence economic outcomes. The disagreement hinges on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors in shaping economic growth trajectories. Understanding this dynamic is essential for designing policies that promote sustainable economic development. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing various aspects of this relationship. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, together with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The political institutions that emerged during this period played a crucial role in enforcing rules and commitments, thereby reducing the risks associated with economic exchange. North and Weingast emphasize that these institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone was insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework was essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. These institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which in turn encourages specialization and division of labor, thereby multiplying the aggregate volume of exchanges. Moreover, North underscores the importance of institutional reliability in reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they have no personal knowledge (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is crucial for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, as it enables individuals to engage in economic activities with confidence in the outcomes. Overall, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the critical role of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutions established by colonial powers played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of their former colonies. The specific mechanisms by which European policies influenced colonial institutions are evident in the differential mortality rates experienced by European settlers. These mortality rates were a key determinant of whether European settlers established extractive or inclusive institutions in their colonies. When mortality rates were high, European settlers were less likely to establish permanent settlements and more likely to implement extractive institutions that maximized short-term resource extraction rather than long-term economic development. This pattern is supported by the high correlation between mortality rates and the establishment of European settlements, which in turn influenced the development of early institutions. These early institutions had a lasting impact on the institutional frameworks of the colonies, shaping their economic performance over the long run. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.2) exploit these differences in mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance, reinforcing the idea that the institutional legacies of colonialism are a critical factor in understanding current economic disparities. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies on economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Hornbeck, argue that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). One mechanism through which this occurred was the concentration of political power, which enabled elites to shape institutions in their favor. This power dynamic was reinforced by the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital, which allowed elites to maintain their dominant position (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these institutions over time had significant implications for economic development, as they restricted competition and hindered the emergence of a more inclusive economic system. Hornbeck's analysis of agricultural development on the American Plains illustrates how the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced productivity by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This technological innovation highlights how institutional changes, even those driven by technological progress, can shape economic outcomes by altering the incentives and constraints faced by economic actors. These findings suggest that the institutional legacies of colonial policies continue to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by collapsing distinct historical periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.11; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Austin's critique is that the thesis does not account for the specific contexts in which institutions evolve, and that these contexts can significantly alter the economic outcomes of institutional changes. This is a shared finding of Austin and Ogilvie, who both highlight that institutions are not uniformly efficient across different historical contexts. Austin underscores that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are best understood by considering the choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). Ogilvie extends this by arguing that the efficiency of institutions is contingent on the broader institutional framework in which they operate (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that institutions cannot be studied in isolation. Ogilvie provides a historical example of how the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). However, Ogilvie qualifies this by noting that the interdependency of institutions means that changes in one area can have unpredictable effects on others, complicating the straightforward application of efficiency-based theories. Thus, the institutional legacies of colonial policies continue to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve.

Komlos and Pamuk share a focus on how historical events drive long-term structural changes that shape economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.13; Pamuk 2007, p.8). This perspective is evident in Komlos's analysis of the Industrial Revolution, which shows that despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This finding challenges conventional views based on aggregate purchasing power, highlighting that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. In particular, Komlos notes that the relative price of nutrients increased during the Industrial Revolution, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This shift in consumption patterns reflects the complex interplay between economic growth and nutritional outcomes, underscoring the importance of considering the distribution of income and the relative prices of essential goods. Pamuk's examination of the Black Death's impact on wage gaps between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 adds a historical dimension to this analysis. Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that influenced wage dynamics, suggesting that severe labor shortages encouraged human capital formation. However, the skill premium in Italy saw a resurgence in the sixteenth century alongside population recovery, indicating that the dynamics of wage gaps are influenced by a multitude of factors, including demographic changes and labor market conditions. This nuanced understanding of historical events and their long-term economic impacts underscores the need for a comprehensive analysis of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping economic outcomes over time.

Clark and Jacks, alongside Broadberry and Gupta, re-evaluate the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence by examining specific factors like coal extraction costs and pre-1800 economic conditions in Europe and Asia (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). They argue that the Industrial Revolution was not driven by coal extraction, and that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. Clark and Jacks demonstrate that total factor productivity in coal mining increased only modestly during the Industrial Revolution era, suggesting that coal played a negligible role in driving economic growth during this period. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis, which shows that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that economic divergence was already significant before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta further elaborate that wages were equalized between sectors within each economy, and that the price of non-tradable commodities depended negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector. However, Clark and Jacks' assumption that deeper mining was required to reach the same quality reserves over time, and their estimation of an upper-bound gain in productivity, qualifies the extent to which coal extraction costs influenced long-run economic growth. This suggests that while coal extraction costs were not a primary driver of the Industrial Revolution, other factors such as labour productivity and economic conditions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reliable economic transactions. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth, as these institutions underpinned credible commitments that supported secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules that encouraged economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North further elaborates on this point, arguing that institutions matter in economic growth by reducing transaction costs and ensuring reliable property rights, which are fundamental for impersonal exchange and economic specialization (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is crucial because it allows individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, even when dealing with strangers.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth in the colonies. This highlights how institutions can have a profound impact on economic outcomes by determining the distribution of wealth and the incentives for economic development.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the role of labour productivity and economic conditions. Broadberry and Gupta show that the price of non-tradable goods depends negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This indicates that institutions that enhance labour productivity in both sectors are crucial for economic growth. Austin underscores the importance of considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11; Austin 2008, p.1). These factors collectively shape the economic conditions that influence long-run growth.

Institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reliable economic transactions. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, a remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups to maximize rents rather than to promote long-run growth. This is evident in the context of colonialism, where institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement involves measuring the distribution of wealth and the incentives for economic development in former European colonies. This measurement type shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists had a profound impact on economic outcomes, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
