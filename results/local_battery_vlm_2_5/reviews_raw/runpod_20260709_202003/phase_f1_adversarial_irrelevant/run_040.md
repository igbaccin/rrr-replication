The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, encompassing formal rules and informal norms that govern economic interactions, are central to understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate. The debate centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing efficiency, or if they also play a role in shaping the distribution of economic benefits. Some argue that institutions matter because they provide the stability and predictability necessary for economic actors to engage in long-term investments and innovations. Others posit that institutions are crucial because they determine how the gains from economic growth are distributed, influencing social cohesion and political stability. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy design and implementation, as well as for our understanding of historical economic trajectories. Institutions matter because they are the fundamental mechanisms through which economic growth is sustained and distributed. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutions governing public choice are pivotal for secure property rights and economic growth. Their work highlights that the political institutions of seventeenth-century England facilitated the development of economic institutions that lowered transaction costs and fostered market exchanges. This institutional framework was crucial for economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for market transactions to thrive. This mechanism is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who demonstrate that the Glorious Revolution in England led to institutional changes that constrained the state's ex post behavior, thereby enhancing its credibility in maintaining agreements with constituents. This credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and that the state does not expropriate wealth, which is a critical factor in fostering long-term economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further illustrate that these institutions complement reputation-building strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This institutional framework promotes economic growth and maintains the benefits of growth over the long term.

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the European settlers' mortality rates in colonies shaped the institutions they established. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to foster long-run economic growth. These institutions were extractive, designed to benefit the colonizers at the expense of the local population, and therefore did not encourage economic development. The mortality rates of European settlers influenced the type of institutions established in the colonies, which in turn shaped the long-run economic outcomes. This suggests that the institutions established during colonial times have persisted and continue to affect economic performance today. The persistence of these institutions underscores their enduring impact on economic outcomes, illustrating how historical institutional choices can have long-lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory.

The growth of economic reasonableness is essential to technological progress, as both technological and institutional adjustments are necessary for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, and that much invention takes the form of recombining existing techniques. This process of "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform existing ideas into something novel, highlights the interconnected nature of technological and institutional progress. The knowledge of existing techniques not only alerts inventors to gaps and opportunities but also prevents potential inventors from misspending their resources. This process underscores the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress, as without it, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets (1973, p.10) also points out that the characteristics of modern economic growth are interrelated, with one inducing another in a cause and effect sequence. This interrelation highlights the importance of both technological and institutional adjustments in driving long-run economic growth. The positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific progress further underscores the need for institutional adjustments to support the efficient use of innovations. This feedback loop suggests that technological progress alone is not sufficient for long-run economic growth, and that institutional progress is necessary to complement it.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) share the finding that the effectiveness of institutions in protecting property rights is crucial for economic development. Hornbeck demonstrates this through the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which allowed farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological advancement enabled farmers to secure their land and investments, fostering agricultural development. Ogilvie adds that the efficiency of institutions in enforcing property rights is a key determinant of economic outcomes. The introduction of barbed wire fencing not only enhanced agricultural productivity but also reduced the transaction costs associated with protecting property, thereby facilitating economic growth. Ogilvie qualifies this by noting that not all pre-modern institutions can be viewed solely through the lens of efficiency. The Mesta, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners, was traditionally seen as inefficient but has been reinterpreted as an effective solution to the high cost of building fences to define property rights and to the problem of 'hold-up' threats by towns denying herds access to common resources. The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) share a focus on the dynamics of technological advance and social change in economic growth. These authors argue that rapid economic growth can be driven by a broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress and disequilibrium in labor allocation between sectors. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) elaborates that technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor. This reduction can accelerate the growth of the capital stock and increase aggregate output. Temin (2002, p.3) argues that the business cycle can produce rapid economic growth over a short time span of a year or two. This qualification suggests that the dynamics of technological advance and social change in economic growth are not limited to the growth of the capital stock but also include the modernization of technology.

The papers in this cluster (Komlos 1998, p.8; Pamuk 2007, p.22) examine structural changes and their effects on societal outcomes. Komlos (1998, p.8) argues that during the Industrial Revolution, the relative price of nutrients increased, leading to a decline in biological well-being due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This mechanism is traced to the slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture compared to industry, as well as diminishing returns to labor in food production. Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap was driven by sharp increases in wages relative to rents and the price of capital, which substituted land and capital for labor. These structural changes also led to the emergence of more capital-intensive industries, such as those producing firearms. However, Komlos (1998, p.12) qualifies this by noting that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, as it depends on factors such as the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations. Kuznets argues that the interrelated nature of modern economic growth necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments to harness technological progress effectively. This view aligns with North's emphasis on the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing market exchanges (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North 1989, p.6). North shows that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth. This institutional framework supports the growth of the transactions sector, which in turn drives overall economic output. Kuznets and North share a core agreement that institutions are critical for economic growth, as they enable the efficient use of technological innovations and reduce transaction costs. They disagree on the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Kuznets focuses on the role of institutions in facilitating technological progress, while North emphasizes their role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing market exchanges. This disagreement is settled by the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains. Hornbeck shows that barbed wire significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This historical evidence demonstrates the importance of institutions in facilitating technological progress and reducing transaction costs, thereby supporting long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
