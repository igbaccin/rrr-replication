The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. At stake is the understanding of how societal structures and rules influence the trajectory of economies over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions primarily facilitate growth through efficiency gains or whether they are shaped by distributional conflicts and power dynamics. Some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic efficiency, arguing that well-defined property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for sustained growth. Others contend that institutions are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, reflecting underlying power struggles and distributional conflicts rather than purely efficiency-driven motives. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to understand the root causes of economic stagnation or growth in different regions. Institutions matter in economic growth, as they are instrumental in shaping the economic environment. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that the development of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement is essential for economic growth. This institutional framework facilitates market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering a growing proportion of the economy's transactions sector. The efficacy of these institutions is further enhanced by their ability to complement reputation-building mechanisms, acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. North and Weingast's analysis underscores the importance of political institutions in maintaining the state's commitment to rules, which is critical for economic development. This perspective is supported by North's broader argument that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it overlooks the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (North 1989, p.6). The development of secure property rights, as highlighted by North and Weingast, is a key mechanism through which institutions influence economic growth. However, North acknowledges that while institutions are crucial, the specific institutional arrangements that emerge can vary widely across different historical and cultural contexts. This variability suggests that the path to economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by a range of factors beyond just institutional design.

Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. share a finding that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies determined the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance. This approach highlights that the institutional legacies of colonialism are a crucial factor in understanding current income disparities among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which in the colonial context were the European settlers. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, underscores the deep-rooted impact of colonial institutional frameworks on long-run economic outcomes. These results indicate that the path to economic growth is influenced by both institutional design and the historical and political contexts in which these institutions are established.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) maintain that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) highlights that many pre-modern institutions, such as merchant guilds and the Mesta, have been re-interpreted as efficient solutions to various economic obstacles. However, she contends that these institutions often emerged from distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Domar (1970, p.7) suggests that in a traditional society without technological progress and capital accumulation, the end of slavery is more certain as population increases and the society becomes Malthusian (Domar 1970, p.6). This implies that the profitability of slave labor decreases as the marginal product of labor descends to the subsistence level, qualifying the efficiency-based explanations of institutional persistence. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay between economic conditions, distributional conflicts, and institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. They contend that societies with highly unequal distributions of wealth and human capital, such as those dominated by large slave plantations, developed institutions that favored elite interests at the expense of broader economic growth. This dynamic is evident in regions like the West Indies and South America, where the efficiency of large plantations and the predominance of slave labor led to extreme wealth disparities. These unequal distributions reinforced institutional structures that protected elite interests, limiting economic competition and stifling growth. For example, the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological change allowed for more efficient agricultural practices, but it also highlights the importance of property rights in shaping economic outcomes. However, the direct technological effects of such innovations do not solely drive economic growth; the broader institutional context plays a crucial role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that while property rights and technological advancements are important, they must be situated within a broader institutional framework that supports equitable economic development.

Mokyr's analysis complements this by showing that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which in turn depends on a broad knowledge base (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This interplay between technological innovation and institutional support is essential for economic development. Mokyr also notes that without complementary institutional progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets underscores this by noting that the mass application of technological innovations, which forms a significant part of modern economic growth, is closely linked to advances in science. This positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific progress is crucial for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The implication is that while technological advancements are pivotal, they must be supported by institutional frameworks that facilitate their efficient use and prevent rent-seeking behaviors. This ensures that the benefits of technological progress are not eroded over time, thus maintaining long-term economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Mokyr argues that technological progress is crucial for economic growth, but it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without institutional advancements, the benefits of technological innovations can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting the long-term impact of economic improvements. Kuznets similarly highlights the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments in efficiently utilizing technological innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific progress is crucial for sustained economic growth, but this requires supportive institutions that facilitate the efficient use of innovations and prevent rent-seeking behaviors (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

The neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of economic growth, is challenged by North's argument that institutions play a critical role in economic development (North 1989, p.5). North contends that transaction costs and the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges are essential for economic growth. Institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are key to reducing costs per transaction and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective aligns with Kuznets' view that economic growth requires institutional frameworks that support the efficient use of technological innovations.

The efficiency approach to understanding core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe is critiqued by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Ogilvie's critique underscores the importance of considering the political and social context in which institutions operate, rather than focusing solely on their efficiency. This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and may not always be designed to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the role of institutions in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The historical evidence from the American Plains, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development, supports the idea that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). Hornbeck shows that barbed wire enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, demonstrating the practical impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. This evidence settles the disagreement between those who emphasize the efficiency of institutions and those who focus on distributional conflicts, showing that institutions matter in economic growth.
