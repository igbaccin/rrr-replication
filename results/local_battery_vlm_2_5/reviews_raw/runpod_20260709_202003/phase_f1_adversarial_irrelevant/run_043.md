The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because the answer determines whether institutions are merely reflections of economic conditions or active drivers of economic development. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions exert influence. One position, articulated by North and Weingast, posits that institutions governing public choice and property rights are crucial for economic growth. Another position, exemplified by Austin, suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms by compressing different historical periods and paths. Additionally, there are rival causes that do not operate through institutions, such as the role of coal in driving the Industrial Revolution, as argued by Clark and Jacks. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy and economic development strategies. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work highlights that the Glorious Revolution led to institutional changes that ensured the state's commitment to maintaining property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth. This political shift was pivotal because it established a credible commitment to property rights, which in turn encouraged investments and economic activities. North (1989, p.6) and Kuznets (1973, p.12) both agree that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic growth, but they differ in how they see this role. North (1989, p.6) elucidates that as economies grow, the complexity of social frameworks increases, necessitating institutional structures that can mitigate uncertainties and support impersonal exchanges. This institutional reliability is pivotal because it allows individuals to engage confidently in transactions with strangers, fostering the division of labor and specialization (North 1989, p.2). Kuznets (1973, p.12) further explains that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are crucial for harnessing the productivity gains associated with technological progress. This dynamic nature of institutions suggests that while they are essential for economic growth, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to changing technological and social landscapes.

van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) share the view that the relationship between economic development and improvements in living standards in Europe is not as straightforward as often assumed. Both authors challenge the notion that economic growth inevitably leads to better living conditions, particularly in the context of early modern Europe. Van Zanden highlights the debate surrounding the 'stagnationist' interpretation of economic growth, noting that while some argue for significant productivity gains in agriculture, others contend that real wages declined over the long run. This debate underscores the complexity of measuring economic development and its impact on living standards, suggesting that economic growth does not uniformly translate into improved well-being. Komlos further illustrates this point by examining the biological well-being of populations during the Industrial Revolution, showing that despite economic growth, biological standards of living declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This finding indicates that economic growth alone is insufficient to improve living standards, especially when income gains are not evenly distributed or when essential needs like nutrition are not adequately met. The divergence in life expectancy between income groups during this period highlights the importance of considering the distributional effects of economic growth. Moreover, the relative price effect, where the price of nutrients increased due to slower technological change in agriculture compared to industry, further complicates the relationship between economic growth and living standards. This effect led consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This dynamic shows that economic growth can have unintended consequences on living standards, particularly when relative prices and consumption patterns are disrupted. This research implies that policies targeting improved living standards should address both economic growth and the distribution of income and the relative prices of essential goods.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (2008, p.11) argue that the Great Divergence and Reversal of Fortune theses focus on historical economic transformations but do not directly address the causal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. These theses highlight the differences in economic performance between regions over time, but they do not offer a specific mechanism by which institutions influence economic outcomes. The low silver wage in Asia reflects low productivity in the tradable goods sector, indicating that productivity differences played a significant role in the economic divergence between Europe and Asia. This mechanism is supported by Austin (2008, p.11), who argues that labor scarcity and low productivity in Africa were influenced by the choice of production techniques and the natural environment. This qualification suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and context-dependent, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutional frameworks influence economic outcomes over the long run.

The literature on economic development in Africa and the role of women focuses on historical factors, gender contributions, and policy implications (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was often overlooked, as men largely pre-empted women's allocation rights over crops grown for a cash value. Women continued to manage all aspects of production and allocation for some food crops, preserving their economic influence. Policies and institutions were established to secure the appropriation, extraction, and export of resources, both human and natural, in support of the expansion of the manufacturing base and production in Europe. This dynamic highlights the gendered impact of production processes and the conditions that facilitated production. The implications of these policies and institutions are profound, as they shaped the economic landscape of Africa in ways that continue to resonate today.

Clark and Jacks, together with Hornbeck, examine specific factors that influenced economic development, distinct from institutional frameworks (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). Clark and Jacks contend that coal played a negligible role in driving the Industrial Revolution, as extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output. This finding suggests that technological barriers and coal rents did not significantly hinder productivity gains in coal mining during the Industrial Revolution era. The upper-bound estimate of total factor productivity gains in coal mining from 1710 to 1869 was only modest, indicating that coal extraction was not a primary driver of economic growth. The role of coal in economic development was thus more limited than often assumed. Hornbeck's analysis of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains reveals that agricultural development was significantly enhanced by enabling farmers to protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, highlighting the importance of technological innovation in agricultural productivity. However, Whatley's examination of gunpowder imports in Africa complicates this narrative, as it shows that the introduction of gunpowder technology increased the productivity of African resources devoted to slaving, supporting the gun-slave cycle hypothesis (Whatley 2018, p.2). This implies that technological advancements in one sector can have unintended consequences in others, influencing economic development in complex ways. The economic impact of technological innovations, therefore, extends beyond direct productivity gains, shaping broader economic dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they create the framework within which economic activities unfold. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitments underpin growth, as they foster secure property rights and economic growth. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.2), who contends that institutions reduce uncertainties and enhance confidence in economic transactions, thereby facilitating growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) further elaborate that institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. The institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution in England illustrate this dynamic, showing how restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve its ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth by ensuring that property rights are secure and that economic transactions are reliable. Institutions thus play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and reliable economic transactions.
