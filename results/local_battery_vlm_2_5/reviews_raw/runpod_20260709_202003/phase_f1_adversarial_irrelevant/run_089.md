The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve from underdevelopment to prosperity. The live disagreement centres on the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game, influence economic performance. One stream of thought emphasizes the primacy of institutions in driving growth, while another posits that technological progress, not institutional frameworks, is the key determinant. A third view posits that distributional conflicts shape core economic institutions, with efficiency taking a secondary role. This debate is crucial for policy, as it informs whether interventions should focus on institutional reform or technological advancement. The implications are significant, as they guide how resources are allocated to foster economic growth. Institutions that facilitate market exchanges and enforce property rights are pivotal for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement underpins economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets of the period, where the political commitment to these rules fostered economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, acting as constraints where reputation alone is insufficient. They improve the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by mitigating incentive problems that are insufficiently policed by reputation alone. This indicates that the institutional framework supports economic growth and enhances the efficiency of market exchanges. North (1989, p.5) warns that neoclassical models, focusing on inputs like land, labor, and capital, can be misleading. These models overlook the critical role of institutions in economic growth, suggesting that without effective institutions, societies would not achieve significant economic development. The implications of this institutional framework are profound, as they show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-run economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological advancement also had a direct impact on the agricultural landscape, transforming how farmers managed their land and livestock. Pamuk (2007, p.22) similarly notes that technological innovations, particularly in capital-intensive industries, played a crucial role in shaping economic development in pre-industrial Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.22). The effects of barbed wire fencing are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle, with some indications that direct technological effects do not drive the main results (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This suggests that the institutional framework, which includes secure property rights, is more critical than the technological change itself. This finding is further supported by Pamuk, who highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This implies that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-run economic growth, as they allow for the development of new technologies and the protection of investments.

Colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established during colonial periods were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, with long-run economic growth being a secondary consideration. This is because the politically powerful groups shaping these institutions were more concerned with maintaining their control and extracting resources than with fostering development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic performance. The extent to which these institutions can be modified to promote growth remains a critical question. Acemoglu et al. show that the results provide broad support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Ogilvie and Domar both argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shaped pre-industrial economic structures like serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie shows that various pre-modern institutions, from merchant guilds to vigilante justice, have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to transaction obstacles. This focus on efficiency overlooks the role of distributional conflicts. For instance, merchant guilds in the tenth and eleventh centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions also served to concentrate power and resources among elites, perpetuating existing social hierarchies. Domar's argument that slavery or serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce further supports this view (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective suggests that pre-industrial economic structures were deeply intertwined with power dynamics and distributional conflicts, not merely responses to efficiency needs. Domar's analysis qualifies this by highlighting that technological progress and capital accumulation can reduce the profitability of slave labor, thereby influencing the persistence of serfdom and slavery (Domar 1970, p.6). This indicates that while distributional conflicts are crucial, economic factors also play a significant role in shaping pre-industrial economic structures.

Economic growth dynamics are driven by complex interactions between technological advance, social change, and labor allocation, as Abramovitz's analysis of the catch-up process in economic growth and Temin's focus on labor allocation dynamics illustrate (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4). Abramovitz's analysis highlights that technological progress and capital accumulation can accelerate economic growth, reducing the age of the capital stock and increasing the capital-labor ratio. This mechanism is particularly salient in economies undergoing modernization, where the adoption of new technologies and the expansion of the capital stock can drive rapid economic growth. Temin's work on post-WWII Europe's Golden Age further elucidates this dynamic by showing that rapid economic growth can be driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This implies that the reallocation of labor from less productive sectors to more productive ones can significantly boost economic growth. Temin's analysis also suggests that the business cycle can produce rapid growth over short periods, qualifying the long-term implications of labor reallocation. This points to the broader significance of economic fluctuations in shaping growth dynamics, highlighting that while technological progress and labor reallocation are crucial, the broader economic context also plays a vital role in determining long-run economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) both argue that technological advancement is the key to sustained economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that technological progress is fueled by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process involves a cumulative and interdependent expansion of knowledge, not merely isolated inventions. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations are often tailored to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention. This interplay between knowledge accumulation and technological innovation suggests that economic growth is deeply rooted in the continuous generation and application of new ideas. Kuznets also points out that the efficient use of these innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This qualification underscores that while technological progress is crucial, the broader economic and social context plays a significant role in determining how these advancements translate into long-run economic growth.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Kuznets' work highlights that the efficient use of technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This is because technological progress alone is not sufficient for sustained economic improvement. Mokyr, echoing this perspective, argues that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the benefits of economic growth can be limited and ultimately eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This shared finding underscores that institutions are active shapers of how technological changes translate into economic growth, not merely passive recipients.

Institutional frameworks both facilitate and constrain economic development. North argues that institutions matter because they define and enforce property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The historical evidence from the American Plains shows that technological innovations, when paired with effective institutional frameworks, can drive substantial economic growth. This illustrates how institutions can create an environment where technological progress is both sustained and effectively utilized.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz contends that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This complexity is evident in the historical record, where technological progress often depends on the knowledge of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which in turn relies on institutional support for the dissemination and application of knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The historical evidence from the Industrial Revolution in England shows that necessity often drives invention, and this interplay between knowledge accumulation and technological innovation suggests that economic growth is deeply rooted in the continuous generation and application of new ideas (Kuznets 1973, p.10). The efficiency of these innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This suggests that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of efficiency, distributional conflicts, and social dynamics, all of which influence long-run economic growth.

The specific historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains shows that technological innovations, when paired with effective institutional frameworks, can drive substantial economic growth. This historical evidence settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in economic growth by demonstrating that institutions are active shapers of how technological changes translate into economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.4; North 1989, p.6).
