The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense debate. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic prosperity over extended periods. Scholars disagree on the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic and political interactions, are pivotal in fostering sustained economic development. One side posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others argue that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geography, culture, or technological change play more significant roles. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development in both developed and developing nations. Understanding the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth can provide insights into how to design effective policies that promote sustained prosperity. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one of many factors is a live disagreement, with scholars on both sides presenting compelling evidence to support their positions. North and Weingast assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the critical role of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. They emphasize that the degree to which a regime is committed to these rules is a critical political factor. This commitment is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. North and Weingast further illustrate that institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state enhance its credibility. North elaborates on this by highlighting that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial for facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. As economies grow and become more specialized, the size of the transactions sector increases, making institutional reliability essential for maintaining confidence in remote and impersonal exchanges. North's argument underscores the necessity of institutional structures that can reduce uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, thereby enabling individuals to engage in transactions with those they do not personally know. This institutional reliability is fundamental for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. North warns that the evolution of more complex social frameworks will not occur if such institutional structures cannot reduce the uncertainties associated with such situations. This implies that the institutional environment must be sufficiently robust to support the increasing complexity of economic interactions as societies develop.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes by influencing the design and persistence of institutional frameworks in former colonies, a finding shared by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27). The institutional changes introduced by colonial powers often prioritized the extraction of rents for European colonists. This extractive focus was driven by the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which designed institutions to maximize their benefits. This mechanism highlights how the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are crucial in determining long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time is a function of their initial design and the ongoing political and economic dynamics within the colonies. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) point out that the impact of institutions on economic performance can be estimated by exploiting differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation. This suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers, combined with the enduring effects of these institutions, play a pivotal role in shaping economic trajectories. The enduring influence of colonial institutions underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which current economic outcomes are embedded.

In New World societies with greater inequality, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). This process was driven by the initial factor endowments of the colonies, which led to extreme wealth disparities from the outset. These disparities were exacerbated by institutions that favored elites, such as those governing property rights and political power. The resulting inequality was a fundamental driver of institutional evolution. This institutional framework, which favored elites, restricted competition and hindered economic dynamism. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. The broader implications of property rights institutions extend beyond agricultural productivity. The security of property rights, a crucial factor in economic growth, was often undermined by institutions that favored elites, leading to persistent inequality and stunted development. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutions, inequality, and economic growth in the New World.

The economic growth in Europe did not uniformly translate into improved living standards, as argued by van Zanden and Komlos. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) critiques optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, highlighting that while there was economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800, it did not correspond to a clear improvement in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The optimists, such as Hoffmann, suggest that traditional agriculture in France generated long-term growth in total factor productivity between 1450 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). However, Komlos (1998, p.13) demonstrates that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.13). This divergence in the trends of economic growth and living standards is further complicated by the relative price effect. Komlos (1998, p.13) shows that during the onset of modern economic growth, the relative price of nutrients increased, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). This substitution pattern indicates that while economic growth was occurring, it did not necessarily benefit all segments of society equally, particularly those dependent on agricultural products. This pattern of economic growth not translating into improved living standards suggests that the benefits of economic development were not evenly distributed, leaving many segments of society vulnerable to declines in biological well-being.

Historical events and structural changes played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic divergences. Broadberry and Gupta, together with Pamuk, (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Pamuk 2007, p.8) show that significant economic disparities, such as those between Europe and Asia before 1800 and the wage gaps within Europe by 1450, were driven by fundamental shifts in productivity and labor dynamics. Broadberry and Gupta demonstrate that the great divergence was already underway before 1800, with Asia exhibiting lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn led to lower silver wages. This productivity gap was a critical factor in the economic divergence between Europe and Asia. Pamuk adds that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These changes included severe labor shortages and a decline in the skill premium, which encouraged human capital formation. Pamuk also notes that the linkages between urban and rural sectors were not always equally strong, which could lead to persistent urban-rural differences in wages and standards of living. The impact of these structural changes on long-run economic growth highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between productivity, labor dynamics, and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29) debate the historical and geographical determinants of economic development, without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. In particular, he argues that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa has consistently characterized the region as having a scarcity of labor relative to cultivable land and capital. This scarcity is paradoxically coupled with a higher average population density and relatively larger urban population, which Austin suggests may indicate higher real per capita income. This view contrasts with Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's contention that these attributes are evidence of higher real per capita income. Bryant (2006, p.29) extends this critique by arguing that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. He posits that the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labor for proto-industrial and factory employments, fostering a structural differentiation between agricultural and industrial sectors. This dynamic was absent in China, where the economy remained tied to subsistence farming and supplemental domestic handicrafts. The demographic realities of mounting land scarcity and a surfeit of cheap labor in China decisively governed all economic considerations, even to the point of resisting the spread of labor-saving technologies. This structural difference highlights how institutional frameworks and economic conditions can shape long-run economic growth trajectories. Bryant (2006, p.29) also notes that larger agricultural units in England permitted a synergistic mix of grain production and livestock rearing, significantly improving diets and increasing productivity through the substitution of animal for human labor. This pattern of agricultural development in England differs from the crops-only pattern in China, where contracting plots ruled out the planting of fodder cereals or grasses. This institutional and demographic context in Europe facilitated remarkable gains in per capita productivity, rising discretionary incomes, and a doubling of grain and livestock output from 1700 to 1800. These findings underscore how the interplay between institutions, labor dynamics, and economic conditions can profoundly influence long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the efficiency and reliability of economic interactions. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice, such as those that emerged in seventeenth-century England, were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because institutions can complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). For example, Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with Europe's institutional framework facilitating economic growth through efficient property rights and effective enforcement (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). North's work underscores that institutions are essential for reducing uncertainties associated with complex social frameworks, allowing for the realization of productivity gains in highly interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2).

These findings highlight that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North's argument is that the evolution of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists were prevalent in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further illustrated by van Zanden, who argues that the traditional agriculture of France was able to generate long-term growth of total factor productivity between 1450 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the development of capital markets in seventeenth-century England. This development shows that institutions can foster secure property rights and economic growth by providing a reliable framework for economic interactions (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence, combined with the empirical comparison of total factor productivity growth in France and the Dutch economy, highlights the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2).
