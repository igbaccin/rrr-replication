The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as technological innovation, natural resources, or geographical advantages, play a more decisive role. Some scholars argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights is crucial for fostering economic growth. Others contend that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living is complex and not solely determined by institutional factors. Additionally, there are rival causes that propose different mechanisms for economic growth, such as the role of coal in the Industrial Revolution or the impact of the Atlantic slave trade. The debate also involves identifying antecedents that trigger institutional changes, such as the development of rents in France or the impact of colonial regimes on African economic enterprise. Understanding these dynamics is essential for comprehending the long-term economic trajectories of nations.

The role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth is a central theme in the literature. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). They emphasize that these institutions complement reputation-building by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. North similarly argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). This perspective highlights the importance of institutional reliability in reducing uncertainties associated with complex interdependent situations, allowing for confidence in outcomes that are necessarily increasingly remote from personal knowledge.

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped current income per capita, suggesting that these specific institutional changes are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanism behind this is that colonies with high mortality rates for Europeans led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. These extractive institutions persisted over time, influencing current economic performance. This argument is supported by the high correlation between historical mortality rates, European settlements, and the establishment of early institutions, which in turn shaped contemporary institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). Acemoglu et al. exploit these differences in mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance. This perspective underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions, which were not initially designed to promote economic development but rather to extract resources. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of historical institutional changes in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This is particularly relevant in understanding how the legacy of colonialism continues to influence economic performance in former colonies.

This institutional change reduced uncertainty and encouraged investment, as individuals could have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they had no personal knowledge (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.2). This institutional framework allowed for increasing specialization and division of labor, which in turn multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). However, North (1989, p.6) also notes that the evolution of more complex social frameworks requires institutional reliability to reduce uncertainties associated with growing specialization.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts and inequality were the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial societies. These distributional conflicts shaped institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and merchant associations, which were not merely efficient responses to economic challenges but were also mechanisms for maintaining elite power and controlling resources. The Mesta in Spain, for example, was not just an efficient solution to property rights and resource access issues, but also a means for shepherds and sheep-owners to secure their interests against competing land uses. The persistence of these institutions was closely tied to the maintenance of social hierarchies and the prevention of economic mobility, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth by limiting the scope for innovation and competition. The interdependency of these institutions within broader frameworks complicates a straightforward efficiency narrative. For instance, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with various markets and family systems, suggesting that the institutional framework as a whole could have influenced economic growth. This complexity underscores the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation.

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) revisit the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence by examining specific factors like coal extraction costs and the timing of economic divergence between Europe and Asia. These studies underscore the importance of understanding the economic context during the Industrial Revolution. Clark and Jacks estimate that total factor productivity in coal mining increased only modestly during the Industrial Revolution era. This finding suggests that the role of coal in driving the Industrial Revolution may have been overstated. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This timing aligns with the modest gains in coal productivity estimated by Clark and Jacks. However, Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the divergence, which hinges on lower productivity in Asia's tradable goods sector, implies that other factors beyond coal extraction costs were at play. Broadberry and Gupta suggest that differences in labour productivity between tradable and non-tradable sectors influenced the price of non-tradable commodities, which in turn affected wages and overall economic performance. Thus, while coal extraction costs may not have been a primary driver of the Industrial Revolution, the broader economic context, including labour productivity and market dynamics, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Reevaluating historical economic growth and development involves challenging conventional views and revisionist positions, as Austin (2008, p.11) and Komlos (1998, p.13) argue that historical causation is more complex than often portrayed. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the economic historiography of Sub-Saharan Africa is almost unanimous that, until well into the 20th century at least, most of the region most of the time was characterised by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land as well as to capital. Austin's (2008, p.11) contention that the region had a higher average population density, and a relatively larger urban population, than North America and Australasia. These attributes, if they can be confirmed empirically, may be taken as evidence of higher real per capita income. Komlos (1998, p.8) argues that the relative price of nutrients increased, partly because technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture were slower than in industry but also because of diminishing returns to labor in food production. This had a direct effect on the biological well-being of the population, as the declining productivity in agriculture led to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This divergence in the life expectancy of the upper and lower income groups during the period under consideration. This implies that the economic benefits of the Industrial Revolution were not uniformly distributed across the population. Bryant (2006, p.29; 2006, p.28) qualifies this implication by noting that the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labour for proto-industrial and factory employments. Where the Chinese pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household produced no structural differentiation between the two sectors. This structural differentiation between the two sectors was a key factor in the economic growth of Europe.

Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa, along with the work of Nunn, has brought to the forefront the importance of institutions in shaping economic development, particularly in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). This literature highlights that the legacy of slavery, along with the broader institutional frameworks, has had a profound impact on the continent's economic trajectory. Akyeampong and Fofack build on this by showing that the role of women in African economic growth was significant both before and during the colonial period. They argue that women's control over various aspects of production and resource allocation was crucial, even as men increasingly pre-empted women's allocation rights over crops grown for cash value (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). However, the institutional frameworks established during colonial times often prioritized the extraction of resources to support European manufacturing bases, thereby altering the dynamics of production and resource allocation. This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on Africa's economic development, shaping both the agricultural and industrial sectors. The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that institutions can be long-standing but not immutable, suggests that policy adaptations are necessary to address these historical legacies and foster economic growth in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

Historical events and technological innovations have significantly influenced economic development by catalyzing structural changes. Temin (2002, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.8) jointly establish that both events such as the Black Death and technological advancements have profoundly influenced economic development. Temin (2002, p.2) identifies that economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, a phenomenon that can be observed over varying time spans. This dynamic is echoed in Pamuk (2007, p.8), who explores how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to significant wage gaps between regions by 1450. These historical events disrupted traditional economic patterns, creating conditions that fostered structural changes and economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.15) notes that the skill premium began to decline under severe labor shortages, suggesting that while historical events can catalyze economic shifts, the specific conditions and timing of these events are crucial in determining their long-term economic impact. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) illustrates this by showing that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation highlights how structural changes, driven by historical events or technological advancements, can lead to increased productivity and economic growth. While these studies do not directly address the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, they underscore the importance of understanding the interplay between historical events, structural changes, and economic development.

Van Zanden and others (van Zanden 1999, p.2; van Zanden 2009, p.19) argue that agricultural productivity and wage developments were critical drivers of economic development in Europe. The latter argues that the skill premium, which reflects the efficiency of labor and capital markets and the effectiveness of institutions for human capital formation, is a predictor of long-term economic performance. This suggests that efficient markets and strong institutions for skill development were crucial for sustained economic growth. This complexity is underscored by the varied interpretations of economic growth during this period, with some scholars arguing for significant productivity gains in agriculture and others highlighting the decline in real wages. This indicates that while agricultural productivity and wage developments were important, their impact on economic growth was mediated by broader economic and institutional factors. The guilds, for instance, played a dual role in economic development by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills but also by potentially restricting market entry and maintaining above-market pay levels. This suggests that the institutional framework governing skill development and labor markets was a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth in Europe.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the complementary effects of secure property rights and credible commitments. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.7) argue that the evolution of political institutions in England during the seventeenth century was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Similarly, North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.2) emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by reducing uncertainties and facilitating market exchanges. These findings are further supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26; 2006, p.23), who show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of guilds on skill development and labor markets, which mediated the effects of agricultural productivity and wage developments (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This institutional framework, which governed skill development and labor markets, was a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth in Europe. The historical evidence from capital markets shows that the success of these institutions was remarkable (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), underpinning the broader economic and institutional factors that mediated the impact of agricultural productivity and wage developments on economic growth.
