The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the extent to which formal and informal rules, along with their enforcement mechanisms, drive or hinder sustained economic expansion over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions that foster growth. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitating market efficiency, such as secure property rights and effective contract enforcement, are crucial for long-run economic growth. Some scholars suggest that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can also influence economic outcomes. This debate underscores the need to understand how different institutional frameworks impact economic performance over time. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is pivotal in this context. Institutions may either complement or hinder the adoption and diffusion of new technologies, thereby shaping the trajectory of economic growth. The challenge lies in identifying the specific institutional arrangements that promote sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers were more likely to establish extractive institutions in areas where mortality rates were high, as these settlers were primarily interested in maximizing short-term extraction rather than long-term growth. This institutional framework was designed to extract resources efficiently with minimal investment in local development. This mechanism was driven by the settlers' primary goal of maximizing their own gains. The high mortality rates led to the establishment of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic growth, thereby setting a trajectory for persistent underdevelopment in these regions. This persistence of extractive institutions has had a lasting effect on income per capita, demonstrating the profound and enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The legacy of these institutions continues to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the critical role that historical institutional frameworks play in shaping long-run economic growth.

Building on this, Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) demonstrate that targeted institutional reforms can catalyze economic development by modifying the incentives and capabilities of economic actors. Their shared finding is that technological and institutional innovations can reshape economic landscapes by enhancing property rights and reducing externalities. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity. Pamuk (2007, p.22) similarly highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital drove substitution of land and capital for labor, fostering technological innovations. This pattern suggests that institutional changes, such as those driven by technological advancements or demographic shifts, can have lasting effects on economic development by altering the incentives and capabilities of economic agents. The specific mechanisms through which these changes occur can vary widely. For instance, while Hornbeck (2010, p.30) focuses on the direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle, Pamuk (2007, p.22) emphasizes the role of urban guilds and rural industries in technological innovations. This variation underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional context in which economic development occurs.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) jointly argue that specific historical institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts and economic conditions. The role of distributional conflicts is evident in the formation of institutions like the Mesta, which, while traditionally vilified, were reinterpreted as efficient solutions to specific problems. The Mesta's role in securing property rights and contract enforcement in impersonal, long-distance trade highlights how institutions can emerge from complex interactions between economic agents and their environment. Domar (1970, p.7) complements this by showing that slavery or serfdom can emerge when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce. This economic condition, combined with the distributional conflicts, shapes the institutional landscape. However, the transition from slavery to free labor is influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation, which can reduce the profitability of slave labor (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). This reinterpretation suggests that institutions, once seen as inefficient cartels, can be understood as efficient providers of security and contract enforcement under specific conditions (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This highlights the complexity of institutional arrangements and their role in shaping economic development.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share a focus on institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which, while not directly addressing long-run economic growth as an outcome, provide insights into the role of institutions in shaping economic development. The former paper argues that institutions play a critical role in economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. This process involves the emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights, leading to a growing proportion of transactions in the economy (North 1989, p.6). The latter paper extends this argument by highlighting the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political institutions can complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, showing that restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state can improve its ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). However, North (1989, p.5) cautions that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, are misleading because they fail to account for the institutional factors that drive economic growth.

Mokyr highlights that technological progress is driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This knowledge is not created in isolation but depends on existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process involves recombination and adaptation of existing knowledge. Kuznets elaborates that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention. This interdependence between technological progress and economic growth suggests that advances in technology create new economic opportunities. Mokyr cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements may be limited. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be supported by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets further notes that the mass application of technological innovations provides a positive feedback loop, enhancing further progress in science and technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interdependence underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where technological advances and institutional adjustments reinforce each other.

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in fostering economic growth, as they provide the necessary structure for technological advancements to flourish. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.5). This reduction in transaction costs is crucial for economic efficiency and growth, as it allows for more transactions to occur, thus increasing the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6). Kuznets shows that technological progress is essential for economic growth, but it must be accompanied by institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interdependence between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where one reinforces the other. Kuznets further argues that the mass application of technological innovations, which constitutes much of the distinctive substance of modern economic growth, is closely connected with the further progress of science, in its turn the basis for additional advance in technology (Kuznets 1973, p.10). Mokyr also shows that technological progress often depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use, and much invention took the form of the recombination of existing techniques (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This highlights the importance of institutional frameworks that support the diffusion of knowledge and technological progress.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by the impact of property rights and their enforcement. North shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This demonstrates how institutional changes that enhance property rights can have a significant impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This shows that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth, can have a significant impact on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise role of institutions in shaping economic growth is evident in the historical evidence of technological progress and institutional adjustments. The historical evidence shows that technological progress and institutional adjustments reinforce each other, and that institutional changes that enhance property rights can have a significant impact on economic growth. The empirical comparison of former European colonies shows that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth, can have a significant impact on economic growth.
