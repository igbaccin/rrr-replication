The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, as it addresses the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies can transition from stagnation to prosperity and maintain that trajectory over extended periods. A live disagreement exists between those who emphasize technological progress as the primary driver of economic growth and those who highlight the pivotal role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining that growth. Institutions, in this context, encompass the rules, norms, and organizations that govern economic interactions. The debate hinges on whether technological advancements alone can drive growth or if the effectiveness of institutions in managing these advancements is equally, if not more, crucial. Institutions may shape growth by influencing the allocation of resources, the protection of property rights, and the enforcement of contracts. This review explores how institutions interact with technological progress to influence long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) focus on the role of political institutions in enforcing rules and maintaining credibility, highlighting that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by constraining the state's behavior and improving its ability to uphold bargains. This institutional framework, which includes the enforcement of property rights and the creation of a stable political environment, is essential for fostering economic growth. Specifically, North argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges leads to a reduction in transaction costs, which in turn stimulates economic growth. This is achieved through the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. As a result, the costs per transaction decrease, while the size of the transactions sector grows as a proportion of GNP, driven by increased specialization and division of labor. However, North acknowledges that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward. North contends that neoclassical models, which focus on inputs like land, labor, and capital, are misleading because they do not account for the role of institutions in economic growth. The neoclassical model assumes that economic growth is primarily a function of these inputs and technological knowledge, but North argues that this formulation is incomplete. The role of institutions, particularly those that govern public choice and enforce property rights, is crucial for fostering economic growth. Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions persisted over time and had a profound impact on the economic development of these regions. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that these institutions, in turn, shaped current income per capita in former colonies, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). However, the specific pathways through which these institutions affect economic outcomes can vary, and Acemoglu et al. note that the impact of institutions on economic performance is contingent on the broader political and economic context in which they operate (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). For example, the persistence of extractive institutions can be reinforced by political elites who benefit from the status quo, further entrenching economic underdevelopment.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) share the finding that institutions in pre-industrial Europe emerged from distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) and Domar (1970, p.6) together show that the guild system, including craft and merchant guilds, and serfdom were shaped by power struggles among different social groups. These institutions were not just efficient solutions to economic problems, but also tools for maintaining social control and extracting rents. For instance, guilds often restricted entry and competition, benefiting existing members at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Similarly, serfdom emerged in contexts where land was abundant and labor scarce, allowing landowners to maintain control over labor. This institutional setup, driven by distributional conflicts, had significant economic implications. The persistence of these institutions did not necessarily enhance long-run economic growth. Domar (1970, p.7) argues that the end of slavery or serfdom is paradoxically more certain in traditional societies without technological progress and capital accumulation, as the marginal product of labor eventually descends to the subsistence level. This suggests that while distributional conflicts shaped institutions, the economic outcomes of these institutions were complex and not always conducive to sustained growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). The technological change brought about by barbed wire fencing exemplifies how institutional improvements can drive economic growth. This technological innovation not only improved farming efficiency but also facilitated the settlement and development of the American West. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) similarly highlight that technological advancements and institutional changes, such as those driven by barbed wire, can significantly impact economic development by fostering urbanization and industrial growth. However, the benefits of barbed wire were not solely technological; its primary impact came from the enhanced protection of property rights. This underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as technological changes alone may not suffice without the accompanying institutional framework. While barbed wire technology directly improved agricultural practices, its broader economic impact was amplified by the institutional changes it enabled. This interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for understanding the complex dynamics of economic development.

The literature on economic growth and social change explores various factors influencing long-term economic development, including technological advancements and macroeconomic conditions, but does not directly address the role of institutions in this context. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Pamuk (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Pamuk 2007, p.22) jointly argue that technological progress and social change are pivotal drivers of economic growth. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how technological advancements can drive economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.22) provides a complementary perspective by highlighting how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This wage gap facilitated the substitution of land and capital for labor, further driving economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.22) notes that the urban guilds were the producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe. This qualification underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in harnessing technological advancements for economic growth. The role of urban guilds in technological innovation suggests that institutions play a critical role in shaping the direction and impact of technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) both argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr highlights the importance of the accumulation of useful knowledge in driving economic growth, suggesting that institutions play a secondary role. The knowledge that fuels technological progress depends on existing techniques and often involves "analogical" thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets (1973, p.4) further elaborates on this by noting that mass application of technological innovations, many based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that drives economic growth. Yet, Kuznets also acknowledges that economic growth is closely connected with the further progress of science, which in turn is the basis for additional advances in technology. The interplay between technological progress and economic growth underscores the importance of technological advancements in shaping long-term economic development.

The interplay between technological progress and economic growth is deeply influenced by institutional frameworks. Institutions, by specifying and enforcing property rights and reducing transaction costs, facilitate the efficient use of technological innovations. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). This perspective highlights how the development of political institutions that enforce property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms can reduce transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic growth. Mokyr similarly emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which in turn depends on the knowledge of other techniques already in use (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This knowledge allows inventors to identify gaps and opportunities in the existing set of technologies, preventing potential inventors from misspending their resources.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by the need for institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Kuznets notes that technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science, which in turn is the basis for additional advances in technology (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This positive feedback loop between technological innovations and scientific discoveries underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating this process. Abramovitz extends this idea by arguing that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This suggests that institutions play a crucial role in enabling countries to catch up by providing the necessary framework for technological progress and social change.

The historical evidence shows that institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This technological advancement, facilitated by effective property rights enforcement, illustrates how institutions can drive economic growth by enabling the efficient use of new technologies. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency explanation for core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This perspective highlights the importance of considering the broader social and political context in which institutions operate, as well as the specific needs and challenges of different economies. The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. The historical evidence of technological progress in the American Plains illustrates how institutions can drive economic growth by enabling the efficient use of new technologies. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows that distributional conflicts can also shape institutions, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in economic growth.
