The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions, as a causal variable, can be understood as a driver of growth, or whether they are merely a proxy for other variables. Disagreement exists over the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars posit that institutions foster growth by providing credible commitments and reducing transaction costs. Others contend that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex, involving multiple historical paths and contingent factors. In addition, some scholars argue that institutions are a necessary but insufficient condition for growth, and that other factors, such as geography, culture, and technology, also play crucial roles. The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering development and reducing poverty. A clear consensus has not yet emerged, as the causal mechanisms and historical contexts vary widely. The question remains whether institutions can be designed and implemented to promote growth in diverse settings. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that institutional reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties and fostering long-run economic growth. They propose that the institutions governing public choice play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by specifying and enforcing property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that political institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by constraining the state's ex post behavior. This constraint improves the state's credibility in maintaining bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is pivotal for economic growth, as it reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that as economies grow more complex, institutional reliability becomes even more critical. It enables individuals to engage in impersonal exchanges with confidence, even with those they have no personal knowledge of (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability involves both formal rules and their effective enforcement, which in turn fosters a stable environment for economic activity. Various factors, including the historical and political context, can influence the efficacy of these institutions. For instance, the institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution in England illustrate how political institutions can evolve to support economic growth by providing secure property rights and a stable political environment.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies, with extractive institutions being particularly detrimental to long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The European settlers established institutions that maximized their own rents rather than promoting long-term economic development. Mortality rates faced by European settlers played a crucial role in determining the type of institutions established in the colonies. Higher mortality rates led to extractive institutions, as Europeans were less likely to settle and invest in these areas. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on the economic performance of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions is a key factor in understanding the current economic disparities among former colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions not only hindered economic growth during the colonial period but also left a lasting legacy that continues to affect economic development today. Moreover, the role of European settlers in shaping these institutions underscores the importance of political and economic factors in determining institutional outcomes. The institutional framework established during colonial rule continues to influence economic performance, underscoring the need for institutional reforms that promote long-term economic growth and development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) both highlight the importance of property rights in shaping economic outcomes. The institutions in these societies advantaged elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework not only hindered economic growth but also left a lasting legacy that continues to affect economic development today. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This mechanism underscores the role of secure property rights in promoting economic growth. The persistence of inequality in these societies suggests that the benefits of institutional reforms may be unevenly distributed. The challenge lies in designing institutions that promote economic growth while also addressing the underlying inequalities that have historically hindered development.

The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 is complex and does not follow a straightforward path, as shown by van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13). Both authors challenge the conventional view that economic growth necessarily leads to improvements in living standards. Van Zanden highlights that despite significant economic growth, there was no clear improvement in the standard of living in Europe during this period, with some studies even suggesting a decline in real wages. Komlos argues that during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being actually declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This discrepancy between economic growth and living standards can be attributed to the uneven distribution of economic benefits. The Industrial Revolution brought about significant urbanization and population growth, but these changes did not translate into improved living conditions for all segments of society. Komlos notes that there was a divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups, indicating that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed. This pattern suggests that while economic growth can drive development, it does not guarantee improvements in living standards for all. Moreover, the relative price of nutrients increased during this period, leading consumers to rearrange their consumption patterns in ways that may have negatively impacted their nutritional intake. This dynamic underscores the importance of considering the distributional effects of economic growth and the role of institutions in ensuring that the benefits are more equitably shared.

Clark and Jacks, alongside Broadberry and Gupta, discuss the Industrial Revolution and economic divergence without reference to institutional frameworks, focusing instead on technological barriers and international price arbitrage (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). Clark and Jacks examine the role of coal in driving the Industrial Revolution, finding that extraction costs for coal rose only modestly with cumulative output, leading to modest gains in total factor productivity (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). This suggests that the Industrial Revolution was driven by other factors. Broadberry and Gupta, meanwhile, argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in labour productivity and international price arbitrage (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This focus on technological barriers and price arbitrage highlights the importance of understanding the economic mechanisms at play, even if they do not directly address institutional frameworks. However, the role of institutions in shaping these economic dynamics remains a critical area for further investigation, as the absence of institutional analysis in these studies leaves a gap in understanding the full scope of factors driving economic growth and divergence.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is complemented by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.2). The development of political institutions that enforce these rules is essential for maintaining economic stability and encouraging growth. This institutional framework is crucial for reducing uncertainties and ensuring that individuals can confidently engage in economic activities with strangers, thereby fostering a complex interdependent economy (North 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the importance of credible commitments. North and Weingast show that the political institutions that emerged during the Glorious Revolution in England improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that economic actors can rely on the stability of property rights and contractual agreements. The institutional changes that occurred during this period illustrate how political institutions can complement reputation-building mechanisms to enhance economic growth. The development of efficient economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges is a direct result of these political changes, which in turn reduce transaction costs and promote economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.7).
