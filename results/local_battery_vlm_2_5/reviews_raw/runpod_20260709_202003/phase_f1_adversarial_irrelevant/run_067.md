The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or resource endowments, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the evolution of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is crucial for economic growth. Some scholars suggest that powerful groups mold institutions to maximize their rents, promoting long-run growth is not the primary goal. Still others contend that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living is not straightforward, and that other factors may be at play. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and the design of economic development strategies. Institutions are often seen as the bedrock of economic growth, but their role and the mechanisms through which they operate remain contentious. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus a live disagreement among scholars, with some focusing on the institutional changes that foster secure property rights and economic growth, while others emphasizing the role of institutions in maximizing rents for powerful groups. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a complex and multifaceted issue, with different scholars emphasizing different aspects of the relationship between institutions and economic development.

The evolution of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is crucial for economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They highlight that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This view is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges, which are essential for economic growth. The development of efficient products and factor markets, along with a medium of exchange with reliable features, allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in highly complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is essential for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange.

This reliability is achieved through the development of efficient property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which facilitate market exchanges and lower transaction costs. This complementary role of institutions in enhancing reputation mechanisms underscores the importance of political institutions in fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) document that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. Colonial institutions often aimed to maximize rents for European colonists, fostering long-run economic growth in the colonies was not the primary objective. Such institutions were maintained because they benefited the politically powerful groups, namely the European colonists, who had the capacity to enforce these arrangements. High mortality rates in regions like Africa and Latin America led to the implementation of extractive institutions, as Europeans were less inclined to settle permanently and develop institutions that would support long-term economic growth. This finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on the institutional frameworks of former colonies, which in turn shaped their economic trajectories. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, as seen in the current income disparities among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also note that the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the importance of understanding the historical roots of contemporary economic conditions.

Some argue that distributional conflicts, not institutions themselves, drove the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a view that the primary factor shaping institutions is the struggle over resource allocation between different social groups. This view posits that the institutions emerging in pre-industrial Europe were primarily shaped by power dynamics between elites and other social classes, not efficiency considerations. For example, merchant guilds, which are often portrayed as inefficient cartels, can be seen as a means for elites to maintain control over trade and resources. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the ongoing distributional conflicts that prevented more efficient alternatives from emerging. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the power struggles that underlie institutional development. This focus on distributional conflicts does not eliminate the role of efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) acknowledges the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that while distributional conflicts may drive institutional change, the efficiency of these institutions can still play a role in their persistence or replacement. This interplay between power dynamics and efficiency considerations underscores the complexity of institutional development in pre-industrial societies.

Clark and Jacks, along with Broadberry and Gupta, reassess historical economic drivers by exploring the roles of coal and early economic divergences, institutions are not the sole determinant of long-run economic growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). The role of coal in the Industrial Revolution is a key point of contention. Clark and Jacks argue that coal played a negligible role, asserting that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output, which suggests that total factor productivity in coal mining saw only modest gains during the Industrial Revolution era (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14). This perspective challenges the notion that coal was a primary driver of economic growth during this period. Broadberry and Gupta, meanwhile, argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting that the relatively low silver wage in Asia reflected low productivity in the tradable goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This early divergence suggests that factors beyond institutions, such as productivity differences, were crucial in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta's model shows that the price of nontradable commodities depends negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This interdependence of productivity across sectors underscores the complexity of economic drivers, indicating that institutional factors alone cannot fully explain long-run economic growth.

The relationship between economic growth and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 is complex and not straightforward, as van Zanden and Komlos argue (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Economic growth during this period did not necessarily translate into improved living standards. This optimistic view faces challenges from evidence showing that real wages declined over the long run, indicating uneven distribution of economic growth benefits (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Komlos reinforces this perspective by demonstrating that despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined during the Industrial Revolution due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.8). These findings suggest that while economic growth was occurring, it did not lead to universal improvements in living standards. This disconnect between economic growth and living standards underscores the importance of considering broader social and economic factors beyond simple growth metrics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to foster secure property rights and enforce market rules. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework provided a reliable structure that facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing overall economic productivity. Institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This point is reinforced by North, who argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a key factor in reducing costs per transaction and increasing the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6). The establishment of such a set of property rights allows individuals in highly complex interdependent situations to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they have no personal knowledge (North 1989, p.2). This institutional reliability is essential for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange models.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The paper contends that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not long-run growth, were a key factor in the economic development of these colonies. This institutional framework, which was shaped by politically powerful groups, had a significant impact on the economic outcomes of these regions. The findings of Acemoglu et al. support the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Institutions also play a critical role in shaping the standards of living and economic development. Van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective is reinforced by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). The differences in labour productivity and wage rates between these regions highlight the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The price of the nontradable commodity depends negatively on labour productivity in the nontradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This institutional framework, which facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs, is essential for long-run economic growth.
