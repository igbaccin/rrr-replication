The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the extent to which institutions, broadly defined as the rules and norms governing economic behavior, can drive economic development. One live disagreement concerns the relative importance of institutions versus other factors, such as technological progress or geographic endowments. Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal in facilitating economic growth by reducing transaction costs and promoting innovation. Others contend that technological advancements and economic modernization can drive growth independently of institutional changes. The debate hinges on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, and whether these mechanisms are sufficient to explain long-run growth. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6) argue that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. Institutions are at the heart of economic growth, serving as the very foundation upon which growth is built. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The political institutions that emerged during this period were crucial in specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that as the economy grows, the transaction sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, reflecting the increasing specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework governed economic exchange and ensured that the rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. North and Weingast (1989, p.7) further explain that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation system by constraining behavior in situations where reputation alone is insufficient. This institutional setup improved the state's ability to maintain its commitments, such as not expropriating wealth, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic growth. However, North (1989, p.5) cautions that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs like land, labor, and capital, is misleading because it overlooks the critical role of institutions in economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is foundational.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mortality rates faced by European colonizers determined the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents rather than long-term growth. These institutions persisted over time, creating a path-dependent trajectory that continues to affect economic performance today. The extractive nature of these institutions is evident in their design, which prioritized the interests of the colonizers over the long-term development of the colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that these institutions were shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. further show that these institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The legacy of these institutions is evident in the current economic performance of former colonies, where extractive institutions continue to hinder economic development.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements and the efficient use of innovations, which requires complementary institutional adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interdependence between technological and institutional progress is evident in the historical context of the Industrial Revolution, where institutional frameworks were essential for harnessing technological innovations effectively. Kuznets notes that the mass application of technological innovations, particularly those based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that further drives economic growth. However, Mokyr cautions that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the benefits of technological progress. This dynamic is crucial for understanding the long-term economic growth trajectory, as it underscores the necessity of institutional adaptations to support and sustain technological innovations. These adaptations are not just about facilitating the initial adoption of new technologies but also about ensuring that these technologies are used efficiently over the long run, thereby maximizing their economic benefits.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) jointly argue that the dynamics of technological advance and social change are pivotal for understanding economic growth, especially in contexts where rapid modernization occurs. This mechanism underscores how technological advancements can catalyze economic growth by fostering a higher capital-labor ratio, which in turn accelerates productivity growth. This perspective suggests that the reallocation of labor, driven by technological and social changes, is a critical factor in achieving sustained economic growth. However, Temin also notes that the business cycle can produce similar growth paths over shorter time spans, indicating that while technological and social changes are essential, they are not the sole drivers of economic growth (Temin 2002, p.3). This qualification underscores the complexity of economic growth dynamics, where multiple factors intersect to influence long-term economic performance.

Ogilvie and Domar both focus on specific economic institutions, such as serfdom and craft guilds, and argue that these institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and the efficiency of economic transactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). Ogilvie discusses how the efficiency of economic institutions is often misunderstood, noting that many pre-modern institutions have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to specific obstacles in economic transactions. Domar, on the other hand, argues that the emergence of slavery or serfdom is driven by high land-to-labor ratios and scarcity of labor. This efficiency-driven approach suggests that institutions evolve to maximize economic output under given conditions. Domar qualifies this perspective by noting that the profitability of slave versus free labor is not straightforwardly determined by technological progress or capital use. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics. For example, in a traditional society without technological progress, the end of slavery becomes more certain as the population increases and the marginal product of labor descends to the subsistence level. This dynamic highlights how institutional change is influenced by both economic efficiency and broader social and demographic factors.

Kuznets shows that technological innovation is closely connected with the progress of science, which in turn provides a positive feedback loop for further technological advances (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological progress and institutional development is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Mokyr argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating and sustaining technological advancements, which are key drivers of economic growth.

Institutional changes are shaped by broader social and demographic factors. Kuznets highlights that technological innovations are often a response to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This dynamic is further complicated by the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores how institutions can influence economic growth by creating an environment conducive to market exchanges and technological progress.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward. Abramovitz shows that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This complexity is further highlighted by the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights how the design and implementation of institutions can significantly impact economic growth, depending on the interests of the groups in power.
