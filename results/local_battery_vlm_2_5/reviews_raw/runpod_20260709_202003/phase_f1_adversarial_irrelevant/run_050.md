The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, with significant implications for understanding the trajectory of nations and regions over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others posit that institutions are merely one factor among many, or that they operate through other variables. This disagreement matters because it shapes how economists and historians interpret historical data, formulate policies, and predict future economic trends. This is a debate about the causal primacy of institutions in economic growth, not whether institutions are relevant. To understand this debate, it is crucial to examine the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and the contexts in which these mechanisms are most effective. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) report, “Total factor productivity in coalmining, 1700-1869 The evidence from consideration of the technological barriers, and from coal rents, is that extraction costs rose only modestly with cumulative output.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). They show that the success of England's economic growth was underpinned by the development of institutions that enforced rules and ensured credible commitments. These institutions complemented reputation-building strategies, enhancing their efficacy by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone was insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). Specifically, North and Weingast highlight how the Glorious Revolution marked a significant shift in political institutions, which improved the state's ability to maintain its commitments, such as not expropriating wealth. This institutional change was crucial for economic growth as it provided a stable framework for market exchanges. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, enabling individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence, even with those they had no personal knowledge of (North 1989, p.2). This confidence in outcomes, despite the increasing remoteness from personal knowledge, was essential for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) attribute the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were shaped by European settlers' interests. The settlers established institutions that maximized their own rents, promoting long-run growth. This is because politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, may resist institutional changes that would not directly benefit them, even if such changes could lead to economic development. As a result, the institutions established in colonies were often extractive, designed to exploit local resources and labor for the benefit of the colonizers. This extractive institutional framework persisted over time, hindering economic growth and development in the colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance, showing that there is a high correlation between mortality rates and European settlements, as well as between European settlements and early measures of institutions. The persistence of these institutions, which were initially designed to maximize the rents of European colonists, has had a significant impact on current income differences among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies. This suggests that even in the face of extractive institutions, there is potential for economic growth and development, given the right conditions.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective, shared by Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), challenges the notion that institutions are relevant to long-run economic growth by highlighting alternative drivers of institutional development. Ogilvie demonstrates that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts. This framework is evident in the evolution of merchant guilds, which were initially efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' in the tenth and eleventh centuries. By the twelfth to fifteenth centuries, these guilds had become inefficient cartels, illustrating how institutions can evolve from efficient to inefficient over time. This evolution suggests that the efficiency of institutions is a dynamic characteristic shaped by underlying conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this perspective to New World societies, arguing that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, highlighting how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth. Ogilvie further emphasizes that institutions are interdependent within a wider framework, and studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This interdependency suggests that the efficiency of institutions is about the broader institutional framework in which they operate. This perspective qualifies the downstream implication that efficient institutions are a prerequisite for long-run economic growth, as the efficiency of institutions is contingent on the broader institutional framework and the underlying distributional conflicts.

Komlos (1998, p.13) and Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) argue that the Industrial Revolution's economic growth did not translate into improved biological well-being for the populace. Komlos (1998, p.13) identifies that despite urbanisation and productivity gains, the life expectancy of the lower-income groups did not improve (Komlos 1998, p.13). The relative price of nutrients increased, leading to a shift in consumption patterns towards carbohydrates over protein-rich foods (Komlos 1998, p.8). This shift highlights that economic growth did not necessarily improve the nutritional status of the population. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) further substantiate this view by showing that the total factor productivity in coal mining, a critical sector during the Industrial Revolution, saw only modest gains (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.14; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.31). The implications of these findings are that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, and the institutional framework of the time failed to translate economic gains into broad-based improvements in well-being. This perspective underscores the need to consider the distributional effects of economic growth and the role of institutions in ensuring that growth benefits are widely shared.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) and Austin (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Austin 2008, p.11) share a focus on the Great Divergence, arguing that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This shared posture is a departure from the standard accounts that attribute the Great Divergence to the Industrial Revolution and European colonialism. One mechanism that Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) highlight is the role of labour productivity in the tradable goods sector, which they argue was relatively low in Asia compared to Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This labour productivity differential was a key driver of the Great Divergence, as it led to lower silver wages in Asia and higher wages in Europe, creating a differential in the cost of labour that favoured European economic growth. Bryant (2006, p.29) qualifies this mechanism by noting that the European agricultural revolution, which increased productivity and released labour for industrial employment, was a critical factor in Europe's economic growth (Bryant 2006, p.28). This pattern of structural differentiation between agriculture and industry in Europe contrasts with the Chinese pattern of integrating handicrafts within the peasant household, which limited the scope for economies of scale and capital accumulation (Bryant 2006, p.29). This structural differentiation was a key factor in the Great Divergence, as it allowed Europe to achieve higher levels of economic growth and development. This focus on structural differentiation and labour productivity highlights the importance of economic institutions and policies in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on African economic development highlights diverse historical factors, including slavery, colonial policies, and gender roles, without directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Nunn 2008, p.3). For instance, the production of slaves through domestic warfare, raiding, and kidnapping had negative impacts on subsequent development, illustrating how institutional disruptions can hinder economic progress. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development, suggesting that gender roles and institutions interacted in complex ways that are not fully captured by a simple focus on institutional disruption (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This interaction is further complicated by the fact that policies and institutions established during colonial times were designed to extract resources, which had differential gendered impacts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.18). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is multifaceted, involving the disruption of traditional institutions and the introduction of new ones that had varied impacts on different segments of society.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, with their influence extending beyond mere economic exchange to encompass the broader political and social frameworks that underpin economic activity. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drive economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is complemented by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of institutions that specify and enforce property rights is essential for economic growth, as they enable individuals to engage in complex interdependent situations with confidence in remote outcomes (North 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the interaction between economic and political factors. North and Weingast highlight that institutions complement reputation-building, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework is essential for maintaining the state's ability to uphold its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional reliability is crucial for realizing productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, allowing individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even those with whom they have no personal knowledge (North 1989, p.2).

The long-run economic growth in Africa is shaped by a multifaceted interplay of institutions, with colonial policies and institutions designed to extract resources having differential gendered impacts (Austin 2008, p.1). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting that the economic rent and growth have often been joint products in pre-colonial and colonial Africa (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who contend that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists did not necessarily maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism played an important role in the process of economic growth and the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These findings underscore the complex and varied impacts of institutions on different segments of society, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions are central to economic growth, as they specify and enforce property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, particularly in the context of colonialism. While North and Weingast emphasize the role of institutions in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2), Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists did not necessarily maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the differential impacts of colonial institutions on various segments of society, as highlighted by the varied impacts of colonial policies and institutions designed to extract resources (Austin 2008, p.1). This evidence underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of colonialism.
