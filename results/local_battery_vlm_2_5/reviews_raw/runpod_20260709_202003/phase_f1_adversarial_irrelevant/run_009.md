The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or merely ancillary factors. At the heart of this debate are two competing views: one emphasises the critical role of institutions in facilitating economic growth through efficient resource allocation and transaction cost reduction, while the other posits that technological progress and exogenous factors are the primary determinants of growth. A third view, less prominent, highlights the role of institutions in mediating technological progress and economic change, suggesting a more nuanced relationship between the two. This review examines these positions by synthesising key arguments from seminal works, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. The literature on institutions and economic growth reveals that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes, but the mechanisms through which they do so are complex and multifaceted. This literature also highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between institutions and other factors, such as technological progress and exogenous shocks. Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that the institutional legacies of colonialism are deeply rooted in the mortality rates experienced by European colonizers. This mortality-driven institutional framework was shaped by the interests of the colonizers, who established extractive institutions that maximized their rents rather than promoting long-run growth. The extractive nature of these institutions was a calculated strategy to exploit colonial resources and labor. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, the resulting institutions were designed to extract resources efficiently with minimal investment in local development. This institutional design persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on the economic performance of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions explains a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also highlight that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies.

This historical perspective underscores that institutions, as emphasized by North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2), are pivotal in driving economic growth beyond mere input considerations. Institutions, which specify and enforce property rights, reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. By lowering costs per transaction, they enable a growing proportion of exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.6) argues that as economies grow, political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should develop. This development should be evident in the emergence of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges. As a result, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. This success was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. The critical political factor was the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to or bound by these rules, a commitment that improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. The logic of these institutional changes can be applied to interpret broader historical developments, such as the institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further note that these institutions complement reputation-building and associated punishment strategies, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. The implication is that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not merely about providing a framework for economic exchange, but also about ensuring that this framework is enforced and maintained over time.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not well-served by the efficiency approach, which has been used to reinterpret premodern institutions such as serfdom and craft guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) examine specific historical institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and craft guilds, but do not directly address how these institutions relate to long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that the efficiency approach has been applied to a wide range of premodern institutions, including merchant guilds, the Mesta, noble feuds, and even vigilante justice, portraying them as efficient solutions to various obstacles to possible transactions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The implication is that these institutions may have been a response to specific economic conditions rather than a direct driver of long-run economic growth. However, Domar (1970, p.7) also notes that the relative profitability of free and slave labor is not easily determined, suggesting that the efficiency of these institutions may have varied over time and across different contexts (Domar 1970, p.6). This variability in efficiency suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth may be more complex than the efficiency approach suggests.

This finding aligns with the observation that the Industrial Revolution's economic growth did not universally translate into improved biological well-being, as Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Komlos (1998, p.8) noted. The opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology and the rise of the capital-labor ratio accelerated the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4)(Komlos 1998, p.8). However, the relative price of nutrients increased due to slower technological change and capital accumulation in agriculture, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products (Komlos 1998, p.8). This substitution effect highlights a critical mechanism through which economic growth can decouple from improvements in biological well-being. The rise in the relative price of food led to a decline in protein-rich diets, further exacerbating nutritional deficiencies. This pattern suggests that the benefits of economic growth may not be evenly distributed, with lower-income groups bearing the brunt of nutritional decline. Komlos (1998, p.8) also notes that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, depending on factors such as technological change and the elasticity of the food supply (Komlos 1998, p.12). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between economic growth, technological progress, and nutritional outcomes, indicating that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is intricately linked to the distribution of resources and the dynamics of social change.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) share a view that the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological progress are primary drivers of economic growth. Technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, and it is the growth of useful knowledge that drives this process. This process involves coordination failures that require institutional progress to complement technological advancements. Without such institutional progress, economic improvements may be unsustainable and eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets (1973, p.10) further elaborates that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments for the efficient use of innovations. This perspective underscores that technological progress is closely linked to scientific advancements, creating a positive feedback loop where mass application of technological innovations fuels further scientific progress. However, Kuznets (1973, p.10) acknowledges that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may not be abundant, highlighting a condition where the benefits of technological progress might not be evenly distributed. This qualification suggests that while technological progress is crucial, its impact on economic growth can be moderated by the institutional environment and the specific needs of different economies. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not negligible, as they facilitate the effective deployment of technological advancements.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interdependence of technological progress and institutional development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic improvement, as it requires complementary institutional progress to address coordination failures and prevent the erosion of rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.10), who highlights that the benefits of technological innovations are closely tied to the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Thus, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that technological advancements are effectively deployed and sustained over the long run. Kuznets (1973, p.10) also acknowledges that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may not be abundant, further emphasizing the need for institutions that can adapt technological progress to the specific needs of different economies (Kuznets 1973, p.10).

Institutional frameworks are essential for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, thereby promoting economic growth. North (1989, p.6) argues that the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for reducing costs per transaction and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This view is complemented by Abramovitz (1986, p.4), who suggests that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change, highlighting the importance of institutions in facilitating modernization (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is further illustrated by Ogilvie (2007, p.7), who shows that pre-industrial European institutions, such as merchant guilds and craft guilds, were designed to provide security and contract enforcement, thereby enhancing economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the case of former European colonies, where Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly impacted relative incomes and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to promote long-run growth, have had lasting effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth hinges on the interplay between technological progress and institutional development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) shows that without institutional progress, technological advancements may be limited and ultimately eliminated by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). North (1989, p.6) shows that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence of pre-industrial European institutions and the economic development of former European colonies settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutions designed to maximize rents to specific groups, rather than promoting long-run growth, have significant and lasting effects on economic outcomes.
