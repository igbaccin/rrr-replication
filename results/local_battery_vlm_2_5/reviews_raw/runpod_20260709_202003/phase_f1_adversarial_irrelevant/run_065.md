The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic performance. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins sustained economic development. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of growth, emphasizing the importance of property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability. Others contend that technological progress and social change are more critical, suggesting that institutions merely facilitate the adoption of new technologies and practices. A third group posits that distributional conflicts and power dynamics shape institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. These positions converge on the importance of institutions but diverge on the mechanisms through which they operate and the factors that drive their development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic growth or exogenous factors that shape economic performance. Institutions matter, but the question remains: how and to what extent? The answer lies in understanding the interplay between institutions, technology, and social dynamics. Institutions are not static; they evolve in response to economic and social changes, and their effectiveness in promoting growth depends on their ability to adapt to new circumstances. This evolution is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including technological progress, social change, and power dynamics. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, where their survival was uncertain. The settlers' desire to maximize short-term gains drove this pattern of institutional development. These institutions persisted over time, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these institutions is not guaranteed. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) suggest that the impact of institutions on economic performance can be understood through exogenous variation, implying that changes in institutions can alter economic outcomes. This highlights the potential for institutional reform to mitigate the long-term effects of colonial legacies, offering a pathway for economic development in regions historically hindered by extractive institutions.

Distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions may not be as crucial for long-run economic growth as previously thought (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Domar 1970, p.7). She argues that many institutions, such as merchant guilds, were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement. This efficiency-based view has been extended to various institutions, including the Mesta in Spain and noble feuds, which have been reinterpreted as solutions to transactional challenges. This suggests that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and slavery might be more about economic conditions than institutional design. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further qualify this by showing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This implies that while economic conditions may drive the initial adoption of institutions, distributional conflicts can lock in these institutions, making them resistant to change despite their inefficiency.

Abramovitz and Bryant argue that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and technological advance (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Bryant 2006, p.11). The catch-up process involves significant social changes and the dynamics of capital accumulation. Abramovitz highlights how technological opportunities can accelerate capital stock growth, reducing the technological age relative to chronological age (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic is further complicated by the broader horizon of scale-dependent technological progress, which can drive productivity growth and aggregate output (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Bryant, on the other hand, provides a historical context by discussing the distinct trajectories of European and Chinese economic development. The European pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household produced no structural differentiation between sectors, restricting the scope for economies of scale and limiting both the demand stimulus for industrialized commodity production and the capital accumulation necessary for its expansion (Bryant 2006, p.29). This historical context shows that the catch-up process is not uniform and is influenced by exogenous factors such as demographic realities and economic considerations. The catch-up process is a complex interplay of technological, social, and economic factors. Austin qualifies this by noting that the catch-up process is also influenced by the extraction of economic rents, which can be a significant source of growth in both capitalist and pre-industrial economies (Austin 2008, p.22). This qualification underscores the multifaceted nature of the catch-up process, where economic rents can drive technological innovation and investment.

The cluster of scholarship on institutions and economic growth, led by North (1989, p.6), (North 1989, p.5) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2), (North and Weingast 1989, p.7), focus on the role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The cluster illuminates how specific institutional frameworks can foster economic development through secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework, which included mechanisms for enforcing rules and constraints on state behavior, complemented reputation-building strategies and improved the efficacy of the reputation mechanism. The result was a more stable environment for economic exchanges, which in turn facilitated market development. North builds on this by emphasizing that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges reduces transaction costs, leading to a growing proportion of GNP. This institutional environment is conducive to increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. Specific historical events and technological changes have played a pivotal role in shaping regional economic development, as these events can catalyze changes in technology and property rights that drive economic growth. Hornbeck and Pamuk both emphasize that these historical factors can trigger long-term structural changes that alter economic trajectories (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). For instance, Hornbeck argues that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed farmers to better utilize their land, leading to increased agricultural productivity and economic growth. Similarly, Pamuk shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This event triggered a substitution of land and capital for labor, driving technological innovations and economic growth. Temin qualifies this by noting that unusually rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This points to the broader impact of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as these events can alter the institutional environment in ways that facilitate or hinder economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) assert that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that institutions play a secondary role. Mokyr underscores that technological progress is often driven by the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform known ideas into novel ones (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This view is echoed by Kuznets, who posits that technological progress is a direct response to specific needs and that the mass application of technological innovations constitutes much of modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This process of technological advancement requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, the institutional environment can either facilitate or hinder its economic benefits. Kuznets' observation that technological progress is closely connected with the further progress of science highlights the importance of a conducive environment for innovation (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr's caution about the need for institutional progress to complement technological progress suggests that institutions are active shapers of its economic impact. This interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complex role that institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth, where technological progress can drive growth but requires supportive institutions to sustain it.

Mokyr and Abramovitz both emphasize the interplay between technological progress and institutions in driving long-run economic growth. Mokyr highlights the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without supportive institutions, the sustainability of economic improvement is limited. Technological progress, while crucial, requires institutions to manage coordination failures and prevent rent-seeking behavior, which can erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Abramovitz extends this idea by noting that technological opportunity encourages both the growth of the capital stock and the modernization of technology (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This modernization process involves adjusting institutions to efficiently use these innovations. Kuznets' work further underscores this point by arguing that economic growth driven by technological innovations requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The mass application of technological innovations, particularly those based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that drives further technological and economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This feedback loop is crucial for sustaining long-run economic growth, as it ensures that technological advancements are continually integrated into the economic system. This integration is facilitated by institutions that adapt to and support these technological changes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elaborated by North and Ogilvie. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for supporting the increasing specialization and division of labor that characterize a growing economy. Ogilvie, on the other hand, highlights that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Pre-industrial institutions like serfdom, the community, and guilds are shaped by the power dynamics and distributional struggles within society. These institutions, while potentially inefficient, serve to manage conflicts and maintain social order, which is crucial for economic stability. This perspective underscores the complex role of institutions in economic growth, where they are arbiters of social and economic power.

The interplay between technology and institutions is further complicated by the historical and cultural context in which they operate. Bryant shows that the productivity revolution in European agriculture released labor for industrial employment, fostering economic growth (Bryant 2006, p.29). The Chinese pattern of concentrating handicrafts within the peasant household did not produce comparable growth-enhancing articulations between town and country. This historical evidence illustrates how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. The demographic realities and social arrangements in different societies play a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of institutions in supporting economic growth. This historical context is essential for understanding the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between technological determinism and institutional determinism is the case of European versus Chinese agricultural productivity. The European experience shows that institutions facilitating the release of labor for industrial employment are crucial for economic growth, while the Chinese experience illustrates the limitations of institutions that do not support such articulations (Ogilvie 2007, p.6).
