The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. The stakes are high, as the answer can redirect policy and research efforts towards either institutional reform or other growth strategies. One live disagreement in the literature is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as technological progress, are more decisive. The latter view holds that institutions are important but secondary to technological innovation. The former view contends that institutions are the fundamental factor, as they facilitate or hinder the efficient use of resources and technological advancements. The question of whether institutions are a necessary precondition for growth or a mere facilitator of other growth drivers remains open. Institutions can be a catalyst for economic growth, enabling technological progress to be harnessed effectively. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Abramovitz (1986, p.4) reports, “It needs extension and qualification.”

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework reduces transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic growth. They highlight that the commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical factor in this process (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also underscore that these institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This institutional framework ensures that the state's behavior is constrained, improving its ability to maintain commitments to constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This perspective underscores the endogenous role of political institutions in economic growth, where specific institutional features mitigate incentive problems that reputation mechanisms alone cannot address. North (1989, p.6) critiques neoclassical theories that focus primarily on population and savings as the principal determinants of economic growth, arguing that these theories overlook the crucial role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (North 1989, p.5).

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) highlight that institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe are better understood as outcomes of distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns. These institutions were shaped by the struggle over resources and power, with serfdom emerging in response to high land-to-labor ratios and scarce labor (Domar 1970, p.7). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were merely efficient solutions to economic problems. Instead, they were deeply rooted in the social and political dynamics of the time. Ogilvie's analysis of merchant guilds illustrates this point, showing that these guilds provided security and contract enforcement as club goods, rather than being inefficient cartels (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). These guilds were not just about maximizing economic efficiency but also about maintaining social order and controlling access to resources. However, Domar's analysis adds a layer of complexity by noting that the profitability of slavery or serfdom is not solely determined by technological progress or capital use (Domar 1970, p.6). This means that the economic organization of pre-industrial societies was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including the distribution of power and resources, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding the broader social and political context in which economic institutions operate.

The multifaceted nature of economic growth involves not only technological advancements but also social changes, as Komlos (1998, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) show. However, this growth was not uniformly beneficial, as evidenced by the deterioration in biological well-being during the same period. Komlos (1998, p.12) notes that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices; rather, it depends on factors such as technological change and the elasticity of the food supply. This underscores that the benefits of economic growth are not evenly distributed, and social changes, such as improvements in nourishment, are crucial for translating economic growth into broader well-being.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) examine how technological changes and historical events, respectively, shape agricultural development and wage structures. They jointly establish that exogenous shocks can catalyze institutional responses that, in turn, influence long-term economic outcomes (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) argues that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enhanced agricultural development by allowing farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This technological change enabled farmers to secure their land more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity. However, Hornbeck (2010, p.30) notes that the direct technological effects of barbed wire were not the primary drivers of these results, suggesting that the institutional changes facilitated by the technology played a crucial role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes in Europe, leading to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of the continent by 1450. This historical event triggered a substitution of land and capital for labor, driven by the relative increase in wages (Pamuk 2007, p.22). However, Pamuk (2007, p.22) also highlights that the urban guilds, rather than rural industries, were the primary producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe, qualifying the broader impact of technological change on economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This suggests that the institutional framework governing technological innovation and labor markets is critical in determining the long-term economic outcomes of exogenous shocks.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements, not by institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.10) elaborates on this by noting that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, illustrating how necessity drives invention. This mutual dependence of technological progress on existing knowledge and the specific needs of society underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) also points out that while technological progress is crucial, it requires complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Without institutional support, economic improvements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, limiting the long-term benefits of technological advancements. This interdependence between technological progress and institutional support suggests that while institutions may not be the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, they play a critical role in sustaining and amplifying the benefits of technological advancements.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions, shaped by the political and economic interests of European colonizers, significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, had lasting impacts on economic development. These institutions were not merely a consequence of geographic factors but were actively shaped by the political dynamics of the time. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, for example, was driven by the need to secure resources and maintain control, leading to persistent institutional differences that affected current income per capita. This extractive framework was further compounded by the reluctance of politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change, as they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. Consequently, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to affect economic performance, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which they were formed and their ongoing impact on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with several streams of scholarship highlighting different aspects of this relationship. Institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the efficiency of market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework reduces costs per transaction and increases the aggregate volume of exchanges, thereby fostering economic development. Kuznets complements this view by emphasizing that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is evident in the historical context, where institutions have adapted to support technological advancements and vice versa. This dynamic interaction underscores the importance of institutions in sustaining long-run economic growth.

The catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz highlights that modernization encourages rapid growth of the capital stock, which in turn accelerates technological progress (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This process is not linear but involves complex interactions between technological opportunities and institutional frameworks. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that technological progress often depends on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, which requires a supportive institutional environment (Mokyr 2005, p.12). The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr terms it, is essential for sustaining economic improvement and preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in providing the necessary conditions for technological progress and economic growth.

The historical context of institutions is crucial for understanding their impact on economic development. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations of pre-industrial European institutions, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for their formation (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective highlights the role of power dynamics and political considerations in shaping institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Hornbeck provides a specific example of how institutional changes, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing, can significantly enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This case study illustrates how institutional innovations can drive economic growth by addressing specific challenges in a historical context.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, with North showing that institutions matter by specifying and enforcing property rights (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, with Ogilvie arguing that distributional conflicts are a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the case of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, as shown by Hornbeck. This case demonstrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth by addressing specific challenges, thereby highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
