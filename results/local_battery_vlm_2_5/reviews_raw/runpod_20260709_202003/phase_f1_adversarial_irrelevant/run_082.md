The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policy and theory alike. One live disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions drive economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, while others propose that economic growth is primarily driven by factors such as population and savings. Another perspective suggests that the role of institutions is overstated, and that economic growth is better explained by other factors, such as the availability of natural resources or technological advancements. The debate highlights the need to understand how institutions interact with other factors to promote or hinder economic growth over the long term. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth, emphasizing the importance of institutions that enforce rules and constrain the state's ex post behavior. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in reducing uncertainty and promoting economic growth by providing a stable framework for market exchanges. The institutional framework underpinning economic growth is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth; the development of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is a key driver of economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This reliability is essential for reducing uncertainties associated with increasing specialization and division of labor, thereby enabling individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in the absence of personal knowledge or reciprocal exchange relationships. These institutional frameworks facilitate market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction, but also a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor. The institutional framework that supports economic growth is not just about rules governing economic exchange, but also about the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. This highlights the importance of credible commitment in economic growth, as institutions that provide a stable framework for market exchanges reduce uncertainty and promote economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, suggesting that these changes are more critical than general institutions in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanism by which colonial institutions influence economic growth is through the establishment of extractive institutions. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, the institutions established were designed to extract resources efficiently. This extractive institutional framework persists, significantly affecting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. argue that the design of these institutions was influenced by the interests of the politically powerful groups, namely the European colonists, who sought to maximize their rents rather than foster economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who show a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies, European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). This correlation underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance. However, Acemoglu et al. qualify this impact by noting that while institutions are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth; geographic factors also play a role, although to a lesser extent (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Van Zanden and Komlos show that economic development during the early modern period and the Industrial Revolution did not uniformly translate into improved living standards in Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). The optimists' view that early modern economic growth was accompanied by widespread improvements in living standards is not supported by the evidence. This disconnect can be attributed to the fact that economic growth was not evenly distributed across different sectors of the economy. For instance, agricultural productivity did not keep pace with industrial productivity, leading to a rise in the relative price of nutrients (Komlos 1998, p.8). This, in turn, led to a shift in consumption patterns, with people substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This growth did not necessarily translate into improved living standards for the majority of the population. This is because the gains from economic growth were not evenly distributed, and the benefits of increased productivity were not shared equally across different sectors of the economy. Pamuk notes that the linkages between urban and rural sectors were not always equally strong, which could lead to persistent urban-rural differences in standards of living (Pamuk 2007, p.8).

Clark and Jacks (2007, p.14) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) argue that the Industrial Revolution and the economic divergence between Europe and Asia were driven by technological barriers and international price arbitrage. This implies that the economic divergence was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in productivity and technological capabilities rather than institutional differences. However, Broadberry and Gupta also note that the price of non-tradable commodities depended negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This suggests that while technological barriers and international price arbitrage were key drivers of economic divergence, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth cannot be entirely dismissed.

Austin (2008, p.11) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) explore how historical economic growth is driven by specific mechanisms, such as technological change and property rights, which can be influenced by institutions but are not solely determined by them. Austin (2008, p.11) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the exchange-value product of Africans' labour was lower in Africa than in the places to which enslaved Africans were taken. This mechanism highlights how labour productivity and economic growth are influenced by factors such as production techniques and natural environment, which are shaped by institutions but are not the only drivers. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) similarly shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This mechanism underscores how technological change and property rights can significantly impact economic growth, even in the absence of institutional changes. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted, with institutions playing a role but not being the sole determinant. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth can vary depending on the specific historical and geographical context.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Ogilvie (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.7), argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This advantage was achieved through the implementation of policies that favored elites, such as land grants and monopolies, which restricted competition and limited opportunities for social mobility. These institutions were designed to maintain the economic and political dominance of the elite class, often at the expense of the broader population. The persistence of inequality can be attributed to the interlocking nature of these institutions, which reinforced each other and created a self-perpetuating system that was difficult to disrupt. The elite class, through their control of political and economic institutions, was able to maintain their privileged status and prevent the emergence of a more egalitarian society. This dynamic is evident in the historical records of many New World societies, where the elite class used their institutional power to maintain their dominance and resist challenges to their authority. Ogilvie points out that the efficiency of these institutions is not always straightforward. The interdependence of individual institutions within a broader framework means that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This interdependency can create a complex web of institutions that are mutually reinforcing, making it challenging to isolate the impact of any single institution on economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective highlights the need for a more holistic approach to understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, one that considers the broader institutional framework and the interactions between different institutions.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice significantly influences economic growth. North and Weingast show that the institutions governing economic exchange and their enforcement are critical for fostering secure property rights. This is evident in seventeenth-century England, where institutions like the Glorious Revolution improved the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, thus enhancing economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The institutions governing public choice are not merely supplementary to reputation-building and associated punishment strategies; they complement them by acting as constraints in situations where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.2). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth, as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others in highly complex interdependent situations (North 1989, p.2).

This divergence is evident in the price of non-tradable commodities, which depends negatively on labour productivity in the non-tradable sector and positively on labour productivity in the tradable sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.23). This shows that institutions play a crucial role in determining the productivity of different sectors of the economy, which in turn affects long-run economic growth. The institutional framework that supports economic growth is not limited to property rights and market exchanges but also includes the development of efficient products and factor markets and a medium of exchange with reliable features (North 1989, p.2).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.2).
