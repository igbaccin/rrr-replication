The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of technological progress and those who highlight the crucial role of institutions in facilitating or hindering economic growth. Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is seen as the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological progress must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutions matter because they provide the framework for economic interactions, shaping the incentives and constraints that guide individual and collective behavior. Institutions that facilitate market exchanges and enforce property rights can lower transaction costs, fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Conversely, institutions that are extractive or inefficient can impede economic development by maximizing rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the complexity of economic growth, where both factors are intertwined in a dynamic process.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers in a region influenced the type of institutions they established. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for the colonizers rather than promoting long-run growth. This institutional framework was maintained by the colonizers to ensure their political and economic dominance, even if it hindered the economic development of the colony. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) found that these institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of the colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on economic development. Acemoglu et al. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) focus on specific economic institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. They argue that these institutions can be understood as responses to distributional conflicts and specific economic conditions. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) posits that merchant guilds, for example, provided security and contract enforcement as "club goods" in their early forms, addressing information asymmetries and principal-agent problems in long-distance trade. Domar (1970, p.7) similarly argues that serfdom emerges when the land-to-labor ratio is high and labor is scarce. This perspective highlights how specific economic institutions are not just efficient solutions to transactional problems but also arise from broader economic and social conditions. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) cautions that the efficiency approach, while useful, can sometimes overlook the distributional conflicts and power dynamics that underpin these institutions. For instance, craft guilds, often seen as inefficient cartels, can also be understood as mechanisms for securing economic power and control within specific communities. This nuanced view of economic institutions suggests that their role in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both efficiency considerations and underlying power structures.

This complexity is further highlighted by the role of technological advance and social change, as Abramovitz and Temin (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Temin 2002, p.4) jointly argue. They posit that economic growth is driven by the dynamics of technological progress and social change, which together influence the rate of capital accumulation and productivity growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) explains that technological opportunities facilitate rapid progress and encourage capital stock growth, as technological advancements reduce the relative price of capital goods. This mechanism underscores how technological progress can drive economic growth by enhancing productivity and capital accumulation. Temin (2002, p.3) qualifies this by showing that labor allocation dynamics, particularly the shift from agriculture to industry, can also significantly contribute to rapid economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver, the allocation of labor resources plays a crucial role in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements. The interplay between technological progress and labor allocation dynamics highlights the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where both factors must be considered to fully understand long-run economic performance.
