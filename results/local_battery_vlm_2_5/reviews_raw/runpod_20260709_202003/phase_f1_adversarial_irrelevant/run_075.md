Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the nature of this role and the mechanisms through which it operates are subject to significant debate. The core disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions are shaped by economic growth or vice versa, and whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Some scholars argue that institutions facilitate economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights, while others contend that distributional conflicts often override efficiency considerations in shaping institutions. A third perspective posits that technological progress and institutional adjustments are interdependent and mutually reinforcing. The resolution of these disagreements has profound implications for understanding the dynamics of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to promote growth. Institutions are active agents that can catalyze or hinder economic progress. Colonial institutions shaped by European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This extractive institutional framework was influenced by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans in these colonies. This institutional legacy persists, shaping contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that differences in institutional frameworks account for a substantial portion of current income disparities among former colonies. The persistence of these institutions is actively maintained by contemporary political and economic structures. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) highlight that the institutional differences among colonies are rooted in the varied responses of European powers to the challenges posed by different mortality environments. These institutional frameworks, established to address immediate colonial needs, have had enduring effects on economic development.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22). This institutional change allowed farmers to secure their land and investments, fostering a more productive agricultural sector. This protection was crucial for agricultural development, as it allowed farmers to invest more confidently in their land. The technological improvements brought about by barbed wire, such as increased protection from others' cattle, played a significant role in driving agricultural growth. Despite these additional costs, the overall impact of barbed wire on agricultural development was positive, as it enabled farmers to better protect their investments and reduce the damage caused by livestock. This institutional change highlights how specific institutional changes can drive economic growth by enhancing property rights and reducing externalities.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7) argue that historical institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts. The presence of serfdom and craft guilds can be attributed to the need to resolve conflicts over resource distribution. Domar (1970, p.7) posits that serfdom emerged when the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor was scarce, indicating that these institutions were designed to manage the scarcity of labor and ensure that landowners could maintain control over their resources. This distributional conflict was a significant driver of institutional change. Domar (1970, p.6) further qualifies this by noting that technological progress and capital accumulation can influence the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor, suggesting that while these institutions were driven by distributional conflicts, their persistence was also influenced by economic factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.6) highlights that craft guilds, often seen as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as club goods. These institutions were designed to address specific economic challenges. The persistence of these institutions also depended on the ability of the ruling class to maintain control over the means of production and distribution of resources. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex, involving both distributional conflicts and efficiency considerations.

North's (1989, p.6) work, together with North and Weingast (1989, p.2), focuses on institutional changes introduced by colonialism but does not directly address long-run economic growth as an outcome. Both North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, with North (1989, p.6) emphasizing that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement can facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further argue that credible commitment underpins growth, which is achieved through institutions that constrain the ex post behavior of the state, thereby improving its ability to maintain bargains with constituents (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This credible commitment is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as it complements reputation-building and punishment strategies (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The specific mechanisms through which these institutions influence economic growth remain under-explored in this literature. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, their impact is not straightforward and depends on various factors, including the efficiency of market exchanges and the distribution of resources. This highlights the need for further research into the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and the conditions under which these mechanisms are most effective.

This perspective is grounded in the idea that technological advancements are deeply embedded in broader social and economic contexts. The mechanism through which technology drives growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge, which allows for the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This process involves the broader dissemination and application of knowledge, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The institutional context is crucial because it facilitates the coordination of efforts and prevents the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr notes that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. Therefore, while technological progress is a key driver, it is the interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks that ultimately determines the trajectory of economic growth. This interplay underscores the importance of institutions in supporting and sustaining the benefits of technological progress, even if technology itself is the primary engine of growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with complementary technological progress being a key component. Kuznets argues that technological advancements are central to economic growth, and these innovations require institutional adjustments for efficient use. He shows that institutions facilitate the application of technological innovations, thereby driving economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This perspective aligns with Mokyr's assertion that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustainable economic improvement. Mokyr emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, highlighting the necessity of institutions in sustaining economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Together, these sources underscore the importance of institutions in harnessing the benefits of technological progress for long-term economic growth.

Institutions also play a crucial role in reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North argues that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is essential for economic growth. He shows that as economies grow, the costs per transaction decrease due to the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges, thereby enhancing overall economic efficiency (North 1989, p.6). This viewpoint is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic development. Their analysis shows that extractive institutions, which prioritize the interests of the ruling elite, can impede long-run growth by failing to encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings highlight the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by influencing the distribution of resources and the efficiency of market exchanges.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie challenges the efficiency-based explanations of pre-industrial institutions, arguing instead that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation for their development. She shows that many pre-industrial institutions, such as merchant guilds and the Mesta, were designed to address specific economic challenges and were not necessarily efficient solutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This perspective is supported by Hornbeck's analysis of the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. Hornbeck shows that technological improvements, such as barbed wire, can significantly enhance economic development by addressing specific economic challenges (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The shared finding is that institutions, whether efficient or not, are designed to address specific economic challenges and play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.4). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which shows that technological improvements can enhance economic development by addressing specific economic challenges, regardless of whether the institutions are efficient or not.
