The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained progress. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate growth through efficiency enhancements or through mediating distributional conflicts. Institutions are seen as either the backbone of economic efficiency, ensuring that resources are allocated optimally, or as instruments that manage the power dynamics and conflicts within societies. The debate thus revolves around the fundamental mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Institutions matter in economic growth, as they create and enforce property rights, reducing transaction costs and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5). Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European mortality rates during colonialism significantly shaped the institutions that were established in colonies, which in turn affected long-run economic growth. The European settlers’ mortality rates determined the type of institutions they established in their colonies; high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for the colonists. The European settlers were unwilling to invest in institutions that would promote long-term economic growth in colonies where they were unlikely to survive. This pattern is evident in the correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the institutions that were established. The institutions that were established in these colonies had a significant impact on their economic performance, as they were designed to extract resources rather than promote economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by noting that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform, and that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted.

Distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is shared by Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Domar (1970, p.7). Distributional conflicts influenced the emergence of institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were not necessarily efficient but were the outcome of power struggles between different social groups. Ogilvie's work highlights how various institutions, from merchant guilds to vigilante justice, were often portrayed as efficient solutions to economic problems. However, she argues that these institutions were more accurately the result of distributional conflicts, where powerful groups sought to maintain their control and influence. Domar's analysis of slavery and serfdom further supports this view. He suggests that these institutions emerged when the land-to-labor ratio was high and labor was scarce, driven by the economic interests of the elite rather than any inherent efficiency. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of institutions that restricted competition and advantaged elites, as seen in the New World societies discussed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). Their analysis shows how institutions in these societies were shaped by the distributional struggles of the time, leading to policies that maintained inequality and restricted economic growth. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions is not uniform. Domar (1970, p.6) qualifies this by noting that the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor could be influenced by technological progress and capital accumulation, suggesting that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they are not the only determinant of institutional outcomes.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions are central to economic growth, facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. Their work underscores the role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and fostering economic development. They argue that institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism by acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging. This view provides an endogenous role for political institutions, where restrictions on the ex post behavior of the state improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, for example, not to expropriate their wealth (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). North (1989, p.5) critiques the neoclassical model, which describes the output of an economy as a function of the quantity and costs of a set of inputs of land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, given some production function derived from the state of technological knowledge. He argues that this formulation is largely misleading, since if that were all there were to the output of societies, they would all be rich. This critique highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they are not merely a function of inputs and technological knowledge.

Abramovitz (1986, p.4) and Temin (2002, p.4) highlight that technological progress and labor reallocation are pivotal drivers of economic growth. This dynamic not only accelerates the capital-labor ratio but also broadens the horizon of scale-dependent technological progress. Temin (2002, p.3) builds on this by noting that the business cycle can produce rapid growth paths within short time spans, aligning with the broader implications of technological progress and labor reallocation. The impact of these factors on economic growth is not uniform across all economies. Abramovitz (1986, p.4) suggests that the benefits of technological progress are more pronounced in economies undergoing modernization, where the opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology is significant. This observation underscores the importance of the initial conditions and the specific institutional context in shaping the long-run economic growth dynamics, as the benefits of technological progress and labor reallocation can be maximized in economies with the right institutional framework.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Clark and Jacks (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1) emphasize the central role that technological advancements and specific resources play in driving economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.30) identifies barbed wire fencing as a key technological innovation that significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains. Barbed wire enabled farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage, thereby fostering economic growth in the region. This technological improvement allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity, which in turn stimulated broader economic development. However, Hornbeck (2010, p.30) notes that the direct technological effects of barbed wire were not the primary drivers of these results; instead, the increased protection from others' cattle played a crucial role (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This finding suggests that while technological advancements are important, their impact on economic growth can be amplified when combined with other factors, such as improved property rights and reduced externalities. This perspective underscores the importance of both technological advancements and specific resources in shaping long-run economic growth, with the caveat that technological constraints can limit the extent to which these resources can be exploited (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.5).

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that economic growth is primarily driven by technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective posits that technological advancements and the dissemination of knowledge are critical factors in economic development, as they facilitate the efficient use of resources and drive innovation. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel solutions. This process is not merely about individual genius but also about the cumulative knowledge of existing technologies, which alerts inventors to gaps and opportunities. Kuznets (1973, p.10) supports this view, noting that technological innovations are often responses to specific needs, and their mass application can provide a positive feedback loop, further advancing both science and technology. Institutions play a role in coordinating efforts and preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors, ensuring that technological progress translates into long-term economic growth. This interplay between technology and institutions suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, institutional support is necessary for sustained economic development.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations. The interplay between technological progress and institutional support is evident in the work of Kuznets and North. Kuznets shows that modern economic growth is closely connected with the progress of science and technology, and that this progress requires institutional adjustments to be efficiently used (Kuznets 1973, p.4). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms, which facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional support is necessary for sustained economic development, as technological progress alone is insufficient to ensure long-term growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the work of Abramovitz and Mokyr. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). Mokyr emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may be unsustainable (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.4) underscores the importance of institutions in translating technological progress into long-term economic growth. Institutional support is necessary for sustained economic development, as technological progress alone is insufficient to ensure long-term growth. The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the work of Ogilvie and Clark and Jacks. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the guild system (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). Clark and Jacks show that coal output expanded in the Industrial Revolution mainly as a result of increased demand rather than technological innovations in mining, highlighting the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which institutions can be understood as a solution to efficiency problems rather than distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the role of the guild system in pre-industrial Europe, which shows that institutions can be understood as a solution to both efficiency problems and distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.6).
