The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and what factors drive sustained prosperity. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern behavior, are crucial for economic growth. One position argues that institutions are fundamental in creating the conditions necessary for economic progress, while another view posits that technological advancements and resource endowments are the primary drivers. Institutions are seen as either facilitating or hindering economic growth by influencing the efficiency of resource allocation, the protection of property rights, and the enforcement of contracts. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs strategies for economic development and institutional reform. The literature reveals that institutions play a significant role in determining the trajectory of economic growth by shaping the incentives and constraints that guide economic behavior. The institutions that facilitate growth often emerge from a complex interplay between technological progress and social norms, with the latter being shaped by historical, political, and cultural contexts. This interplay is evident in the role of technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge in economic development, as well as in the impact of institutional changes on long-run economic outcomes, as seen in historical and contemporary contexts. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that the development of political institutions plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. They posit that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Specifically, North (1989, p.6) highlights that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms led to falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of market exchanges in the economy. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges by reducing costs per transaction, thereby promoting economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) complement this view by emphasizing the importance of institutions that enforce rules and constrain the state's ex post behavior. They argue that these institutions improve the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents, such as not expropriating their wealth. This institutional framework is particularly effective in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging, thereby providing an endogenous role for political institutions in economic development. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between political institutions, property rights, and economic growth. The development of political institutions that effectively enforce property rights and constrain state behavior is essential for long-run economic growth, as they create a stable environment conducive to market exchanges and economic development. This institutional framework diminishes transaction costs and bolsters the state's credibility in upholding economic agreements.

Acemoglu et al. find that colonial institutions shaped by European settlers significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This extractive institutional framework, characterized by property rights that favor colonizers and constraints on local economic activity, stunted economic development in colonies with high European mortality rates. The institutional legacies of colonialism, therefore, are deeply intertwined with the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. exploit the variation in European mortality rates to show that these early institutional differences have persisted over time, shaping current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This persistence underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, as the extractive institutions established during colonial rule continue to influence economic performance today. The long-term effects of these institutions are evident in the current income disparities among former colonies, highlighting the critical role of institutional design in shaping economic development paths.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) both highlight the importance of property rights institutions in facilitating economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land and crops, fostering investment and productivity gains. Both sources underscore that effective property rights institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they provide the necessary incentives for investment and innovation. This is further supported by the fact that the Mesta, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners, was an efficient solution to the high cost of building fences to define property rights and to the problem of 'hold-up' threats by towns denying herds access to common resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) also observes that when circumstances shift, institutions that were once efficient may become outdated, underscoring the dynamic nature of institutional effectiveness. This dynamic aspect suggests that while property rights institutions are vital for economic development, their design must adapt to evolving economic conditions to maintain their effectiveness.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Domar (1970, p.7) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These societies, characterized by large plantations and slave labor, saw their economic structures dominated by elites who benefited from policies that limited competition and maintained their privileged status. Domar (1970, p.6) adds that the profitability of slave labor, compared to free labor, depends on various factors including technological progress and capital use. This implies that the persistence of inequality was not solely due to institutional design but also influenced by economic conditions that affected the relative profitability of slave versus free labor. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.5) further elaborate that the large plantations and the overwhelming majority of the population being black and enslaved led to highly unequal distributions of wealth and human capital. This unequal distribution, in turn, reinforced the institutions that favored the elites, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of inequality.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10) and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.54) share the view that technological advancements are the driving force behind economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.10) elaborates that technological progress is closely tied to the advancement of science and that the mass application of technological innovations fuels further scientific progress, creating a positive feedback loop (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This technological progress involves more than invention; it also encompasses the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, enabling inventors to transform known ideas into novel solutions (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode these gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that the effectiveness of technological progress in driving economic growth is contingent on the institutional framework that supports and facilitates its implementation. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional progress is critical for sustained economic growth, as institutions can either enhance or hinder the benefits derived from technological innovations.

This shows that institutions can either enhance or hinder the benefits derived from technological innovations, underscoring the importance of institutional progress in sustaining economic improvement.
