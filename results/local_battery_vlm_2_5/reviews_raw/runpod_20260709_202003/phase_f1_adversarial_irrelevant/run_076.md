The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is our understanding of how societies transition from stagnation to sustained economic advancement. Institutions, encompassing the rules and norms that govern economic interactions, are often posited as critical drivers of growth. The precise mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence are contentious. One live disagreement revolves around the nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development. Some scholars emphasize the endogenous evolution of institutions, arguing that political factors underpin economic growth by fostering secure property rights. Others focus on exogenous shocks, such as colonialism, which introduce institutional changes that can either hinder or facilitate growth. Additionally, the timing and specificity of institutional impacts are debated, with some scholars arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, while others contend that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal. This debate underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics and their multifaceted role in economic development. The interplay between endogenous and exogenous factors in shaping institutions and their subsequent effects on economic growth is a central theme that will be explored across different historical contexts. van Zanden (1999, p.2) reports, “Both explicitly criticized what Persson called the 'stagnationist' interpretation of Abel, Postan and Leroy Ladurie.” North and Weingast (1989, p.2) report, “Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows.”

This institutional evolution was particularly evident in the transformation of England's public choice governance, which North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) argue was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis highlights how political institutions can improve economic outcomes by enhancing the reliability of property rights and ensuring that economic agents can trust in the stability of their rights. This institutional framework is crucial for fostering economic growth, as it provides the necessary conditions for secure investment and economic development. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that the development of efficient economic institutions facilitates market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, which in turn supports economic growth. This institutional reliability is essential for enabling complex interdependent economic activities, where individuals can confidently engage in transactions with those they do not personally know. North (1989, p.2) further qualifies this by noting that the establishment of efficient property rights and a reliable medium of exchange are necessary for realizing the productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange. This implies that the institutional evolution in seventeenth-century England secured property rights and created a framework that supported the growth of complex economic activities, ultimately driving long-run economic development. As North (1989, p.2) explains, these institutions underpin the confidence that enables individuals to engage in economic activities with strangers, fostering a broader network of economic interactions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped current income per capita levels, suggesting that these specific institutional changes are more critical than general institutions in determining long-run economic growth. This perspective highlights that colonial institutions were often designed to extract resources and maximize rents for European colonists. These extractive institutions, shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, ensured the continuation of colonial power structures. This finding is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.2) who note that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita. These institutions persisted over time, creating a path-dependent trajectory that continues to influence economic outcomes today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by showing that institutions played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, suggesting that while extractive institutions may have hindered growth in some contexts, they also facilitated development in others. This implies that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, depending on the specific historical and contextual factors at play.

Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the key drivers shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They suggest that the institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were the outcomes of power struggles between different social groups. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of the elite, who used their political and economic power to shape institutions in their favor. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.7) points out that merchant guilds, traditionally viewed as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' in the early medieval period. The efficiency approach has been applied to a wide range of pre-modern institutions, from the Mesta in Spain to noble feuds and vigilante justice, all of which have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to various economic obstacles. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that we cannot study one institution in isolation. This interdependency suggests that the efficiency of an institution may be contingent on the broader institutional context in which it operates. This perspective is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic highlights how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that perpetuate inequality, even in the face of changing economic conditions.

Van Zanden and Komlos both highlight that economic growth does not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards. Van Zanden criticizes the optimism surrounding early modern economic growth in Europe, noting that while total factor productivity increased in some regions, real wages and living standards did not uniformly improve (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Komlos 1998, p.13). Komlos shows that during the Industrial Revolution, despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This pattern suggests that the benefits of economic growth may not be evenly distributed across society. Moreover, Komlos argues that relative price changes and the substitution of industrial commodities for food products further exacerbated the decline in nutritional standards, especially for lower-income groups. These findings suggest that the relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and mediated by factors such as income distribution and nutritional access. This complexity is further illustrated by the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups during the Industrial Revolution (Komlos 1998, p.8). This divergence underscores the importance of considering distributional effects when examining the impact of economic growth on living standards.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia, a critical aspect of long-run economic growth, was already well underway before 1800, as Broadberry and Gupta argue (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26; Austin 2008, p.11). This divergence was driven by differences in labour productivity and economic institutions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.26) show that lower silver wages in Asia reflected lower productivity in the tradable goods sector, which in turn was influenced by institutional factors. Austin (2008, p.11) elaborates on the complex interplay between labour scarcity, production techniques, and natural environmental factors, suggesting that the specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa need to be understood within a disaggregated view of factor endowments. This perspective highlights that different historical periods and paths are crucial in understanding the great divergence. The economic institutions that fostered growth in certain contexts were not necessarily optimal in others (Austin 2008, p.1). The institutions that supported economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial Africa were not always the same as those that supported growth in other regions. This nuanced understanding of institutional variation across different historical contexts is essential for comprehending the great divergence. This divergence underscores the importance of considering the specific historical paths and institutional frameworks that shaped economic growth in different regions. The implications of these findings extend to the broader study of economic development, illustrating how the interplay between institutions and economic conditions can lead to significant disparities in long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the evolution of property rights and political frameworks. North and Weingast demonstrate that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional evolution facilitated market exchanges and ensured that economic rules were enforced, thereby reducing uncertainties and promoting growth (North 1989, p.2). The development of these institutions allowed for the emergence of political structures that could enforce property rights and maintain economic stability, essential for long-term economic growth. This is evident in the development of efficient product and factor markets, as well as the establishment of a reliable medium of exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The institutional changes at the time of the Glorious Revolution in England illustrate how political institutions can enhance economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Broadberry and Gupta support this view by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by differences in institutional frameworks and economic conditions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.26). This divergence highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long run, as different regions developed distinct institutional frameworks that influenced their economic trajectories.

Institutional frameworks also played a critical role in the economic development of other regions, such as Africa. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional variation across different historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These changes had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions, illustrating how institutions can either promote or hinder economic growth. The specific reasons for low labour productivity in Africa are most plausibly understood by considering choice of production techniques in the context of a disaggregated view of the factor endowment, including specific aspects of the natural environment (Austin 2008, p.11). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between institutions and economic conditions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity and reducing uncertainties (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or to different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the development of efficient product and factor markets, as well as the establishment of a reliable medium of exchange, which allowed for the emergence of political structures that could enforce property rights and maintain economic stability (North and Weingast 1989, p.7).
