The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms, conventions). The central disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive economic growth. One position emphasizes institutions as the primary determinant of economic growth, arguing that efficient institutions facilitate economic activity by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. Another position posits that technological progress, rather than institutions, is the mainspring of economic growth, with institutions playing a complementary role. A third position suggests that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, and that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to the interests of powerful groups.

The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they primarily facilitate growth or are shaped by other factors. Institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5), but the mechanism by which they do so is contested. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.”

The emphasis on political institutions in specifying property rights and providing enforcement is a key aspect of the argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as North and Weingast (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who underscore the importance of credible commitment from the regime or sovereign to enforce these rules. The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies this, where political institutions played a critical role in securing property rights and fostering economic growth by improving the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. This political foundation for economic growth is further supported by the fact that these institutions complement reputation-building and punishment strategies, enhancing the efficacy of the reputation mechanism. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) also note that these institutions complement reputation-building, acting as a constraint in circumstances where reputation alone is insufficient to prevent reneging.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains, as Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Domar (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Domar 1970, p.7)  both show. Barbed wire enabled farmers to better protect their property rights by delineating their land and reducing externalities from livestock damage. Hornbeck (2010, p.4) finds that barbed wire's effects are a combination of direct technological improvements and increased protection from others' cattle. Domar (1970, p.6) notes that the profitability of slave labor compared to free labor depends on various factors, including technological progress and capital use. This suggests that the introduction of barbed wire, by enhancing property rights and reducing externalities, likely increased the profitability of free labor in agricultural settings. This implies that while barbed wire may have increased the profitability of free labor, it may not have been a decisive factor in the end of slavery, but rather part of a broader set of economic and social changes.

Long-term structural changes, such as shifts in relative wages and technological advancements, played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes in pre-industrial Europe. Pamuk (2007, p.22) and Komlos (1998, p.8) share the posture that structural changes initiated long-term economic dynamics, such as wage gaps and well-being. Pamuk posits that the Black Death triggered a substitution of labor for land and capital, as wages rose relative to rents and capital prices (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This shift was driven by technological advancements, notably in urban guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This suggests that while technological progress and capital accumulation were occurring, the benefits were not uniformly distributed, and the relative price of nutrients increased. The implications of this economic divergence are significant, as Komlos notes that the relative price of food declined in the twentieth century despite rapid population growth (Komlos 1998, p.12). This indicates that the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply are crucial factors in determining the economic outcomes of long-term structural changes.

Distributional conflicts are posited as the primary drivers shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that the "elder" merchant guilds of the tenth and eleventh centuries were efficient providers of security and contract enforcement as 'club goods'. She challenges the prevailing view that many pre-modern institutions were efficient solutions to economic problems. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) notes that the Mesta, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners, and even the noble feud, have been reinterpreted as efficient solutions to various economic obstacles. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) support this view, arguing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The efficiency of these institutions is not always straightforward. The greater efficiency of very large plantations and the overwhelming fraction of populations that came to be black and slave made the distributions of wealth and human capital extremely unequal (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This suggests that while distributional conflicts may drive the formation of institutions, the resulting institutions can perpetuate inequality, potentially hindering long-run economic growth.

Mokyr's emphasis on the primacy of technological progress in driving economic growth, shared with Kuznets, posits that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the key driver of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.10). This perspective suggests that technological advancements are the primary catalysts for sustained economic development. Mokyr highlights that technological progress often relies on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, processes that do not necessarily depend on institutional support (Mokyr 2005, p.12). Kuznets further elaborates that the mass application of technological innovations, many of which are based on recent scientific discoveries, provides a positive feedback loop that drives economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This interplay between technological progress and economic growth indicates that institutions may play a secondary role in facilitating the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets also notes that the stock of innovations suitable for less developed countries may be limited, suggesting that the benefits of technological progress are not universally accessible without institutional adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.10). This qualification underscores the importance of institutions in ensuring that technological advancements are effectively utilized across different economic contexts, even if they are not the primary drivers of growth.

Acemoglu et al. and their colleagues highlight that colonialism led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persistently affected the economic growth of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rates experienced by European colonizers in these regions played a crucial role in shaping the institutions established. This pattern is evident in the correlation between European mortality rates and the types of institutions established, which in turn influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these extractive institutions has had profound effects on economic development, with formerly poor colonies experiencing significant economic growth and industrialization as a result of institutional changes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The impact of these institutions is not uniform. The design of institutions to maximize rents for European colonists indicates that the economic benefits of institutional changes are contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they occur.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the broader development of economic reasonableness. This is evident in the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Mokyr emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets further elaborates on this by noting that economic growth driven by technological advancements requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This alignment between technological progress and institutional development highlights the necessity of complementary institutional frameworks for sustained economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge, as Mokyr argues, is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who notes that the mass application of technological innovations, closely connected with the progress of science, provides a positive feedback loop for economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4) underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the efficient use of technological innovations, thereby driving long-run economic growth.

The historical and political contexts in which institutions are designed play a crucial role in determining their impact on economic growth. North underscores that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.5). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The design of institutions to maximize rents for European colonists indicates that the economic benefits of institutional changes are contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they occur (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This contingency is further illustrated by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they are designed and implemented. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement regarding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains, which significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This historical evidence shows that the effectiveness of institutions in driving economic growth depends on their ability to address specific challenges and opportunities within a given context.
