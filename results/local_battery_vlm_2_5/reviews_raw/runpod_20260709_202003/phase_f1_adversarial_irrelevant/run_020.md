The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping how societies evolve and why certain regions experience sustained economic progress while others stagnate. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as technological advancements or demographic changes, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions create the necessary frameworks for economic activity, while others posit that institutions merely facilitate growth driven by exogenous factors. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce contracts can foster economic growth by providing a stable environment for investment and innovation. Weak institutions can hinder growth by creating uncertainty and discouraging investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They identify the Glorious Revolution as a pivotal moment, where the political changes led to the establishment of institutions that ensured the state's commitment to maintaining property rights. These institutions complemented reputation-building mechanisms by providing constraints that prevented reneging on agreements. This institutional framework allowed the state to maintain its commitments to constituents, thereby enhancing the overall economic environment. This institutional reliability is crucial as it allows individuals to have confidence in their dealings with others, even in complex interdependent situations. North (1989, p.6) also notes that this institutional evolution requires a medium of exchange with reliable features, alongside efficient product and factor markets. This highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional requirements for economic growth, where reliable institutions are necessary but not sufficient on their own. The establishment of such a set of property rights allows for the realization of productivity gains associated with impersonal exchange, thereby fostering long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. posit that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is primarily due to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who often resisted institutional changes that would not directly benefit them, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions. This dynamic is evident in the high correlation between European mortality rates, which influenced settlement patterns, and the subsequent establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on long-term economic development. The persistence of these institutions highlights the critical role that initial institutional frameworks play in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

The institutions in New World societies with greater inequality are shown to have restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Hornbeck 2010, p.34). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Hornbeck (2010, p.34) both underscore how institutional frameworks shaped by initial inequalities can impede long-term economic growth. This institutional bias towards elites created barriers to entry for new competitors, limiting the dynamism of the economy. Hornbeck (2010, p.34) illustrates how agricultural development can be enhanced when property rights are secure and externalities are reduced. In societies with high initial inequality, the institutions that evolve often fail to protect property rights effectively for the majority. This failure to secure property rights can hinder agricultural development and, by extension, broader economic growth. The persistence of these inequalities over time underscores the critical role that initial institutional frameworks play in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Ogilvie argues that the evolution of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe was significantly shaped by distributional conflicts (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; Ogilvie 2007, p.7). These institutions, including serfdom, the community, and various guilds, were efficient solutions to economic problems and were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. Ogilvie's work, along with Lindert and Williamson's analysis of inequality trends, highlights how technological and demographic changes could lead to secular inequality trends, which in turn influenced institutional development (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interplay between technological imbalance and labor-force growth created conditions where certain institutions might emerge to address rising inequalities. Ogilvie cautions that the efficiency approach to understanding these institutions is limited. Institutions are often part of a broader framework and their efficacy can be context-dependent (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie’s framework suggests that the interdependency of institutions within a wider system means that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness implies that the long-term economic impact of these institutions is not straightforward, as their effects are mediated through complex social and political dynamics. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth must be understood within the broader context of societal power structures and historical contingencies.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and related arguments about economic divergence highlight that historical contexts and specific economic conditions are pivotal in understanding long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.29). These works emphasize that the relationship between economic institutions and growth is mediated through complex historical dynamics. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the relative economic positions of countries are a result of their colonial histories, is critiqued for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not adequately account for the specific economic conditions and historical contexts that shaped long-run economic growth in different regions. The thesis assumes that the economic impact of institutions is uniform across different historical periods, which is not supported by the evidence. For instance, the economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa; the kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others (Austin 2008, p.1). The historical context of Africa, for example, was characterised by scarcity of labour in relation to cultivable land as well as to capital (Austin 2008, p.11). This historical context shaped the institutions that emerged in Africa and influenced their impact on long-run economic growth. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth must be understood within the broader context of societal power structures and historical contingencies. van Zanden (1999, p.2) and Komlos (1998, p.13) both challenge the notion that economic growth directly translates into improved standards of living. Van Zanden critiques the optimistic view of early modern economic growth in Europe, arguing that the relationship between economic development and living standards was not straightforward. He highlights the debate between those who emphasize long-term productivity growth and those who point to the decline in real wages. Meanwhile, Komlos argues that despite the economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being actually declined. This decline was due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment, factors that were not adequately addressed by aggregate purchasing power metrics. The mechanism linking economic growth to living standards involves the distribution of income and the relative prices of nutrients. Komlos shows that as the relative price of nutrients increased, consumers shifted their consumption patterns, substituting industrial commodities for food products and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This shift had significant implications for biological well-being, particularly for lower-income groups. Van Zanden's critique of the optimistic view of early modern economic growth underscores the complexity of the relationship between economic development and living standards. This complexity is further illustrated by Komlos' findings on the divergence in life expectancy between upper and lower income groups, highlighting the importance of considering distributional effects when assessing the impact of economic growth on living standards. The role of institutions in shaping this relationship remains implicit, yet crucial, as the distribution of resources and the relative prices of goods are influenced by the institutional framework.
