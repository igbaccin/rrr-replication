The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are crucial as they can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as technology or geographical advantages, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions are essential for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for efficient market transactions and property rights enforcement. Another position contends that technological progress and innovation are the primary drivers of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role. A third position, while acknowledging the importance of institutions, suggests that distributional conflicts and political power dynamics are more critical in shaping economic institutions than efficiency considerations. This review will examine these positions and their implications for understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions are vital to economic growth because they influence the sustainability of technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.54) reports, “It recognized the possibility of what we might call today coordination failures and suggested policies to rectify them.” Kuznets (1973, p.10) reports, “Finally, it may well be that, despite the tremendous accumulation of material and social technology, the stock of innovations most suitable to the needs of the less developed countries is not too abundant.”

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the security of property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This commitment is manifested in institutions that constrain the state's ex post behavior, such as preventing expropriation of wealth. These institutions complement reputation-building mechanisms, improving their efficacy in situations where reputation alone is insufficient (North and Weingast 1989, p.7). The Glorious Revolution in England exemplifies how institutional changes can enhance the state's ability to maintain its part of bargains with constituents. This institutional framework fosters increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges and thus economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.6) further argues that the neoclassical model, which describes economic output as a function of inputs and technological knowledge, is misleading because it overlooks the role of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.5). Institutions thus play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to secure property rights and efficient market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. show that colonial institutions designed by European settlers were pivotal in shaping the long-run economic outcomes of their former colonies. This institutional legacy is evident in the current economic performance of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mechanisms through which these institutions operate are complex. For instance, the European settlers' mortality rates influenced the type of institutions they established; high mortality rates led to the creation of extractive institutions, which prioritized the extraction of resources over long-term economic growth. This institutional framework, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, persisted over time and significantly impacted the economic development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.2). The persistence of these institutions is a critical factor in understanding the current income disparities between former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the institutions established during colonial times continue to influence economic performance today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This enduring impact underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development in shaping economic outcomes.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Pamuk (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Pamuk 2007, p.22) share a focus on the impact of property rights and technological change on economic development. This technological innovation allowed farmers to protect their investments more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity. Similarly, Pamuk (2007, p.22) shows that the Black Death in Europe led to long-term structural changes, including a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The increased wages relative to rents and the price of capital drove substitution of land and capital for labour, highlighting the role of technological and institutional changes in shaping economic outcomes. The impact of these changes was not uniform across regions, as evidenced by the differential effects on low-woodland areas during the period from 1880 to 1900 (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). Increased protection from externalities also played a crucial role in these changes, alongside the direct technological effects of innovations like barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). Pamuk (2007, p.22) illustrates this point by noting that urban guilds were the producers of new technology in pre-industrial Europe, not rural industries (Pamuk 2007, p.22). This suggests that the interplay between technological innovation and institutional frameworks, such as guilds and property rights, was instrumental in driving economic development. The significance of secure property rights in facilitating agricultural development and technological change underscores the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are posited as the primary drivers shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, more so than institutional frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.7; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, often criticized for their inefficiency, were actually efficient solutions to specific problems faced by their members. For example, craft guilds provided security and contract enforcement as 'club goods' (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). This efficiency, however, was not the primary driver of their formation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) support this view, arguing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions were rooted in the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital, which led to the dominance of large slave plantations and a population primarily composed of slaves of African descent (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This pattern of inequality and its institutional manifestations had profound implications for economic development, as it restricted competition and limited the opportunities for technological innovation and economic growth. Therefore, the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions cannot be overlooked when examining the long-run economic growth in pre-industrial Europe.

Mokyr argues that technological progress is driven by the accumulation and recombination of existing knowledge, a process that often relies on "analogical" thinking where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of a knowledge base that can be built upon and adapted over time. Kuznets complements this view by highlighting that economic growth is intrinsically linked to technological innovations, which in turn are closely connected with scientific progress (Kuznets 1973, p.4). The implication is that the accumulation of useful knowledge creates a positive feedback loop, where technological advancements fuel further scientific discoveries, driving economic growth. However, Mokyr cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement could be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors, which highlights that while technological progress is crucial, it is not sufficient on its own (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, while technological advancement and knowledge growth are essential drivers of economic development, they must be supported by institutional frameworks that prevent the erosion of economic gains.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary frameworks that support technological advancements and mitigate rent-seeking behaviors. Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological progress is a key driver of economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes that technological progress is crucial for economic development, but it must be supported by complementary institutional progress to prevent rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets similarly highlights that technological innovations are closely connected to the progress of science, which in turn fuels further technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Kuznets 1973, p.4) underscores the importance of institutions in harnessing the benefits of technological progress for sustained economic growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions facilitate this process are still debated. North argues that institutions matter because they define property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial European institutions supports this, showing that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for powerful groups, not to promote long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). The historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains shows that technological advancements can enhance economic development by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4). This empirical case supports the broader claim that institutions are critical for leveraging technological progress to drive economic growth. Acemoglu et al. provide further evidence that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This finding illustrates the profound impact of institutions on economic outcomes, as the extractive institutions designed by European colonists hindered long-run growth. The disagreement centers on the precise role of institutions in promoting economic growth. North argues that institutions define property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6), while Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions can hinder long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The historical evidence from the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains settles this disagreement by demonstrating that institutions can enhance economic development by enabling better protection of property rights and reducing externalities (Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
