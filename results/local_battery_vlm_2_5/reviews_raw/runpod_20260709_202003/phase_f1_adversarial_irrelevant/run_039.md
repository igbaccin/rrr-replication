The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high because this question bears directly on the efficacy of policy interventions aimed at fostering economic development. On one side, scholars argue that institutions are pivotal in determining economic outcomes, emphasizing the importance of property rights, contract enforcement, and governance structures. On the other side, there are those who contend that technological progress and exogenous factors, such as geography and natural resources, are the primary drivers of economic growth. This debate is further complicated by the recognition that institutions themselves are shaped by historical, political, and social contexts, making it challenging to isolate their effects. The central question is whether institutions are the primary engine of economic growth or merely a byproduct of other underlying factors. This debate is crucial for understanding how policies can be designed to foster sustainable economic development. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share a finding that the institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and catalyzing economic growth. North (1989, p.6) posits that efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore the importance of political institutions that enforce rules and constrain the state's ex post behavior, thereby enhancing the state's credibility in maintaining agreements with constituents. This institutional framework can be seen as a mechanism for mitigating coordination failures and fostering economic growth. The specific institutional changes during the Glorious Revolution illustrate how political institutions can endogenously support economic development by improving the efficacy of the reputation mechanism. North (1989, p.6) complements this by highlighting that the neoclassical model, which focuses on inputs and production functions, is insufficient for explaining economic growth, as it overlooks the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective is crucial for understanding how policies can be designed to foster sustainable economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions significantly shaped current income per capita in former colonies. Their research demonstrates that the mortality rates experienced by European colonizers influenced the type of institutions they established. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains rather than foster long-run economic growth. This institutional framework was not conducive to economic development, as it prioritized the extraction of resources over investment in productive capacities. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on contemporary economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) argue that technological progress is the primary driver of economic growth. This progress is fueled by the accumulation of useful knowledge, which enables the development of new technologies and the improvement of existing ones. Mokyr elaborates that technological advances often rely on the recombination of existing techniques and analogical thinking, where inventors transform familiar ideas into novel innovations. This process is not merely about individual genius but also about the collective knowledge base that informs and inspires new inventions. Abramovitz further underscores that technological opportunities not only drive productivity growth but also encourage the rapid accumulation of capital, as technological progress reduces the relative price of capital goods. This dynamic creates a positive feedback loop where technological advance and capital accumulation reinforce each other, driving sustained economic growth. However, Mokyr cautions that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, institutional progress is more contingent and subject to setbacks. This implies that while technological advances provide a robust foundation for economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on complementary institutional developments. Thus, the interplay between technological progress and institutional factors is crucial for long-term economic development.

Hornbeck (2010, p.30) and Ogilvie (2007, p.7) together argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They demonstrate that institutions like merchant guilds, often seen as inefficient cartels, were actually efficient providers of security and contract enforcement during the tenth and eleventh centuries. However, Ogilvie notes that this efficiency-centric view has been applied to a wide range of premodern institutions, including those that were traditionally vilified for blocking economic progress. This perspective suggests that the role of economic institutions in economic development is more complex than the efficiency-centric view suggests. For instance, the Mesta, a guild-like association of shepherds and sheep-owners in pre-modern Spain, has been reinterpreted as an efficient solution to property rights and resource access issues. Even noble feuds and vigilante justice have been framed as efficient solutions to contract enforcement problems in pre-modern societies. This indicates that the efficiency of institutions can be context-dependent and subject to reinterpretation based on changing conditions. Hornbeck's work on barbed wire fencing in the American Plains illustrates that technological improvements can enhance property rights protection and reduce externalities, thereby facilitating agricultural development. This suggests that the efficiency of institutions can be enhanced through technological advancements, which in turn can drive economic growth. However, Hornbeck also notes that the direct technological effects of barbed wire do not drive the main results, implying that the broader institutional framework plays a crucial role in determining the impact of technological changes. Therefore, the interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional efficiency, and technological progress is crucial for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Structural changes during periods of economic transition, such as industrialization, can have profound effects on long-term economic performance. Komlos (1998, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.22) examine how these changes can alter wage structures and biological well-being, influencing economic outcomes. The relative price of nutrients increased during the Industrial Revolution, leading consumers to substitute industrial commodities for food products, and carbohydrates for protein-rich foods. This shift in consumption patterns was driven by technological changes and capital accumulation in agriculture lagging behind industrial advancements, as well as diminishing returns to labor in food production. Urban guilds played a pivotal role in technological innovations, producing new technology that was more capital-intensive. The guilds' influence on technological progress highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic growth. However, Komlos (1998, p.12) notes that population growth does not necessarily lead to a rise in food prices, as it depends on factors such as the nature of technological change and the elasticity of the world's food supply. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between structural changes, institutional frameworks, and long-term economic performance. The impact of technological advancements on economic growth is contingent upon the broader institutional context in which they occur.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.4) both highlight that technological progress is a key driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that technological progress and institutional progress are complementary (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Technological progress alone cannot sustain economic improvement without institutional progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Institutional progress ensures that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economic system, preventing rent-seeking behaviors that could erode gains. This is supported by the shared finding that technological progress is contingent on the institutional context (Abramovitz 1986, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.12). Abramovitz (1986, p.4) shows that technological progress encourages the growth of the capital stock, which in turn drives economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This dynamic is further illustrated by the fact that technological progress reduces the price of capital goods relative to labor, thereby accelerating the growth of the capital stock (Abramovitz 1986, p.4). This shows that institutions play a critical role in facilitating the adoption and dissemination of new technologies, thereby driving long-term economic growth.

The importance of institutions in economic growth is further emphasized by the work of North (1989, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, which reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is essential for the efficient allocation of resources and the promotion of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the importance of institutions that promote inclusive growth rather than extractive practices. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the significant role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This is consistent with the broader finding that institutions shape economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources and opportunities (North 1989, p.6; North 1989, p.5).

Institutional frameworks also play a crucial role in addressing distributional conflicts and promoting economic efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) and Hornbeck (2010, p.30) provide insights into how institutions can address distributional conflicts and promote economic efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.7) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.7). This is supported by the finding that institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.6). Hornbeck (2010, p.30) shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.30). This highlights the importance of institutions in addressing distributional conflicts and promoting economic efficiency. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the introduction of barbed wire fencing, which shows that institutions can significantly enhance economic development by addressing distributional conflicts and promoting economic efficiency (Hornbeck 2010, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.4).
