The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some regions thrive economically while others stagnate. The debate centres around whether cultural factors, through their influence on institutions, are the primary drivers of economic growth. On one side, scholars argue that cultural traits, such as trust and social norms, shape institutions in ways that either foster or hinder economic development. On the other side, there are those who contend that institutions are shaped more by historical and geographic factors, with culture playing a secondary role. This disagreement underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between culture and institutions in the context of long-run economic growth. The debate centers on the mechanisms that shape institutions and their relative importance compared to other factors. This question is central to understanding the persistence of economic disparities across regions. Culture and institutions are intertwined, and their relationship is dynamic. The task is to discern how culture influences institutions and, through them, economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This cultural milieu was particularly influential in the context of Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) show that ideas, as specific manifestations of culture, were key to economic growth. They argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge propelled economic growth. This cultural dynamic is evident in the rhetoric and institutions that emerged in countries like the Netherlands, France, and even Spain, where new ideas fostered economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates that the diffusion of useful knowledge spread through various channels such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences. This widespread dissemination of knowledge facilitated the Great Enrichment. The role of tacit knowledge, which was often embodied in skills and personal transmission, further underscores the importance of cultural factors. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that the diffusion of tacit knowledge was also influenced by the technology and costs of codification, highlighting that while tacit knowledge was essential, its transmission required specific conditions. Therefore, the cultural milieu that fostered the spread of ideas and knowledge was crucial for economic growth.

This security was critical for both the long-term capital market and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. These scholars show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had a lasting impact on current income per capita. The mechanism operates through the persistence of these institutions over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate a strong correlation between settler mortality and the quality of institutions, which in turn affects economic performance. The implication is that the institutional legacy of colonialism is a key factor in understanding current economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these extractive institutions, therefore, has significant implications for the economic development of former colonies. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial times continues to influence economic outcomes, independent of cultural factors.

The great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Africa is well-documented, with studies showing varying levels of per capita income and urban development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This divergence is evident in the differing trajectories of wages and prices in Europe and Asia. In Europe, higher agricultural labour productivity led to a shift in labour from agriculture to industry and services, driving economic growth. However, this pattern did not hold universally; for instance, in southern India, high agricultural productivity did not translate into lower food prices or increased economic development. The reasons for this discrepancy are complex, involving factors such as investment patterns and institutional frameworks. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was due to significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This investment was facilitated by institutional changes that supported economic development, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This finding suggests that the economic performance of African economies was more nuanced than previously characterized, with some regions experiencing growth tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability underscores the importance of understanding the specific conditions that drove economic development in different regions, independent of cultural factors.

The complexity of catch-up processes in economic growth involves both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz's work highlights that institutional changes and technological advancements do not follow a linear path; instead, they are influenced by a multitude of factors including social competence and the adoption of best-practice technologies. Abramovitz emphasizes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, and social competence for exploiting advanced methods was initially limited. This limitation suggests that the catch-up process involves both adopting new technologies and developing the social and institutional capacity to effectively utilize them. Austin supports this view by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic approach underscores the importance of endogenous factors in shaping economic growth, highlighting that simple convergence hypotheses are insufficient to explain long-run growth patterns. However, Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, implying that the effectiveness of institutions can vary over time. This variability further complicates the catch-up process, as institutions that are favorable for economic growth in one period may not be as effective in another. The nuanced interplay between exogenous factors, technological advancements, and social changes underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, suggesting that a holistic understanding of these dynamics is essential for explaining long-run growth patterns.

This instability, in turn, hindered the development of strong institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. The centrality of institutions in economic growth is a shared finding (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). In seventeenth-century England, North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, showing how institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The Bank of England began private operations shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, providing a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt and its subsequent private operations highlight the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This approach emphasizes Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the course of economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, contra geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The results provide support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies, and via this channel, account for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
