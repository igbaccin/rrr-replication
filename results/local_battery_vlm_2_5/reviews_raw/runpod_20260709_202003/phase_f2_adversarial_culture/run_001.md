The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. The debate centers on whether cultural factors are merely epiphenomena of deeper institutional structures, or whether they are independent variables that shape institutions and, through them, economic outcomes. One position posits that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run growth. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that cultural norms can motivate economic behavior and shape institutional frameworks. This view is challenged by those who argue that institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors such as geography or mortality rates, with culture playing a secondary or even negligible role. This tension underscores the need to carefully examine how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic growth.

Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. She cites Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks are more likely to have institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability. This perspective is supported by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate the gains from trade and technological change that underpin modern economies. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. Together, these arguments suggest that cultural factors, by shaping institutional frameworks, can play a crucial role in driving long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey and Ogilvie (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that economic change is driven by ideas and ideologies that shape the economic landscape. This cultural shift was a profound change in how people thought about their economic activities, not merely a response to material interests. McCloskey (2015, p.10) illustrates this with the example of France, where the "Franco-French War" reflects a deep ideological conflict that hindered economic growth. This cultural influence extended to micro-level behaviors, demonstrating the pervasive impact of ideas on economic change, not just macro-level institutions. This interplay between ideas and institutions underscores the complexity of economic change, where cultural beliefs set the stage for institutional development but do not operate in isolation.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective is further supported by North's examination of the English financial revolution, which highlighted the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can facilitate economic growth by providing a stable market for both public and private debt. The financial revolution fostered a secure environment for long-term capital markets, which are essential for sustained economic development, and reduced transaction costs. This institutional framework, by ensuring the security of property rights, laid the groundwork for economic growth and political freedom in England. The precise mechanisms by which these institutions influenced economic outcomes are complex and multifaceted. North and Weingast's analysis underscores the necessity of institutions in economic growth, but it also acknowledges that other forces, such as cultural factors, may have played a role in shaping these institutions and their effects. The interplay between institutions and cultural beliefs is intricate, and while institutions provide the structural framework for economic growth, cultural factors can influence how these institutions are perceived and utilized.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development highlights the deep-rooted influence of historical institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had enduring effects on current income per capita. This pattern is rooted in the historical settlement practices of European colonizers, who established institutions that maximized their rents, not fostering long-run growth. The correlation between settler mortality and the type of institutions implemented highlights how initial colonial practices shaped enduring economic institutions. These extractive institutions, designed to benefit European colonists, persistently hindered economic development in the colonies. The persistence of these institutions is a critical determinant of contemporary economic performance, not merely a historical artifact. This historical legacy underscores the significance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The impact of these institutions is not uniform across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on the mechanisms through which these institutions were maintained, noting that they were shaped by powerful groups who benefited from the status quo. This dynamic suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions is a product of ongoing political and economic power struggles, not just a matter of historical inertia. The interplay between colonial institutions and local power dynamics highlights the complexity of institutional change and its long-term economic implications.

The growth of useful knowledge is a key driver of economic growth, as Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) have shown. The diffusion of knowledge across borders facilitated technological advancements that transcended national boundaries, creating a shared intellectual movement that drove economic progress. This diffusion was actively facilitated through various means, including periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, not merely a passive process. The significance of tacit knowledge, which is often embodied in skills and expertise, further underscores the importance of personal transmission and training in the dissemination of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Abramovitz (1986, p.12) highlights that the social competence for exploiting advanced technologies was initially limited, particularly in the earlier years and among latecomers. This limitation suggests that the effective utilization of knowledge requires the societal capacity to absorb and implement it, not only its availability. Consequently, the role of culture in dominating institutions becomes evident as it shapes the absorption and application of technological advancements, influencing long-run growth through the dynamics of knowledge dissemination and societal competence.

The economic contributions of women, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Goldin, reveal the multifaceted role of gender in shaping long-run growth dynamics. They show that women's roles in economic development have been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa and during transformative periods in the U.S. (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that the colonial period codified gender roles, which had long-term consequences for gender production and economic growth in Africa. In the U.S., Goldin identifies a quiet revolution in women's employment and education, driven by changes in household production technology and institutional shifts. One specific mechanism through which women's contributions have influenced growth is the transformation of labor supply and demand. For instance, the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century and the growth of educational institutions altered the supply of potential workers, thereby impacting economic growth. This dynamic is further qualified by Goldin, who notes that changes in household production technology and institutional barriers also played a crucial role in shaping women's labor supply. These factors collectively illustrate how women's economic contributions, shaped by cultural norms and institutional changes, have been instrumental in driving long-run growth. This influence underscores the broader theme of how cultural and institutional dynamics intersect to shape economic development, a theme that carries into the next section.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, underscores the enduring impact of historical cultural attitudes on economic development. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies and led to significant population losses. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation. The adverse effects of the slave trade on state centralization further exacerbated this issue. Nunn and Wantchekon found that the slave trade caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, combined with the cultural mistrust, has created a challenging environment for economic growth. Nunn further elucidates that Africa's history of slavery has contributed to modern corruption, which is another significant barrier to economic development (Nunn 2008, p.1). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape African economic outcomes through both cultural and institutional channels.

Cultural beliefs and values fundamentally shape institutions, driving long-run economic growth. A shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6) is that cultural beliefs and values shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife in medieval Europe, influenced the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the role of women in economic development, showing that cultural beliefs about gender roles significantly impacted economic production systems. Cultural beliefs and values are not static; they evolve over time and are influenced by historical events and institutions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical context underscores the importance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping institutions and economic growth. The specific mechanisms through which cultural beliefs and values influence institutions remain a subject of debate. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, showing that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, demonstrating the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth. North and Weingast further argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The persistence of cultural mistrust in Africa, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, highlights the enduring impact of historical events on cultural beliefs and values. The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on trust in Africa, as measured by Nunn and Wantchekon, settles the disagreement on the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.
