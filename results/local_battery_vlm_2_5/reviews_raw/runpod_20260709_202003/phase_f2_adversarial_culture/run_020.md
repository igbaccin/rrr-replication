The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors in shaping economic institutions, which in turn drive long-term economic performance. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, and whether these cultural factors are endogenous or exogenous to the economic system. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can significantly impact economic growth by facilitating coordinated actions and encouraging institutions that favor growth. Others contend that the impact of culture on economic institutions is overstated, and that more direct factors, such as institutional frameworks and transaction costs, play a more decisive role. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors should be seen as primary drivers of institutional change or as secondary effects of underlying economic and political structures. This disagreement is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that the institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights and promoting economic growth. Their analysis highlights the English financial revolution, which significantly lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, both of which were crucial for fostering a stable market for public debt. This stability, in turn, provided a positive externality for the growth of private debt markets (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutions that emerged during this time played a necessary role in facilitating economic growth and political freedom, underscoring the importance of secure property rights in long-run economic development. The evolution of these institutions faced numerous challenges. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, for instance, necessitated a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, illustrating the complex interplay between political and economic factors. The implications of this institutional evolution are profound, suggesting that while other forces might have eventually led to England's success, the presence of these institutions accelerated and solidified this progress.

The impact of distributional conflicts often overshadows the role of culture in shaping these institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the institutional framework of pre-industrial Europe was shaped by the struggle between elites and commoners over resources. This struggle resulted in institutions that maintained the balance of power, even if they were not always efficient. For instance, serfdom and guilds were not designed to maximize economic output but to allocate resources and mitigate conflicts. This perspective challenges the view that institutions are shaped by cultural beliefs or efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further elaborates on the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that institutions like the family system, labor markets, and credit markets were interlinked and influenced each other. This interdependency highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need to study institutions as part of a broader system. Austin (2008, p.25) qualifies this view by noting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutions may vary over time. This implies that while distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping institutions, their effects were not uniform and could change with different historical contexts. The dynamic nature of institutional evolution underscores the importance of considering multiple factors and their interactions in understanding long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Broadberry and Gupta, highlight that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These institutions, shaped by initial factor endowments, led to societies with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. The persistence of inequality was reinforced through policies that limited economic opportunities for the lower classes, thereby maintaining the status quo. This dynamic is evident in the contrasting development paths of Latin American and North American societies. Sokoloff and Engerman note that while both regions started with abundant land and natural resources, the institutional frameworks that evolved in Latin America were more conducive to maintaining elite dominance. Broadberry and Gupta further illustrate this point by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, indicating that institutional differences played a crucial role in economic development long before industrialization. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated varied across regions, with some societies experiencing more pronounced effects of inequality on economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta underscore that the advanced parts of India and China had grain wages comparable to those in north-western Europe, but significantly lower silver wages, reflecting different institutional arrangements and their impacts on economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This suggests that the relationship between inequality and economic growth is complex and context-dependent, influenced by the specific institutional frameworks in place.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas, ideologies, and the diffusion of useful knowledge are primary drivers of economic change. They identify intellectual shifts as the key mechanism for economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.10) traces this to the cultural struggles and rhetoric that shaped new circumstances and ideas, which in turn fostered economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) describes how tacit knowledge, which includes skills and implicit forms of knowledge, was essential to the economic growth of Europe. This tacit knowledge was transmitted through personal training and communication, highlighting the role of social interactions in disseminating ideas. Mokyr (2005, p.56) argues that the diffusion of this knowledge across Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. These mechanisms underscore the importance of cultural and intellectual exchanges in driving economic growth. This shows that the diffusion of knowledge was a crucial factor in the Great Enrichment, and that the diffusion of knowledge was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. They contend that differences in European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita. This is illustrated by the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as between early institutions and those present today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) further demonstrate that the impact of these institutions on economic performance is highly significant, with a substantial effect on income per capita. The institutional framework established during colonial times, therefore, played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlighting the extractive nature of many colonial institutions. This extractive character suggests that the economic benefits of colonial institutions were not evenly distributed and often favored the colonizers at the expense of local populations.
