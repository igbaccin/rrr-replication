The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth has been a contentious issue among scholars. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are primary drivers of economic growth or secondary to institutional frameworks. Some researchers argue that cultural norms and values directly influence economic outcomes by shaping institutions, while others posit that institutions are the primary determinants of growth. The disagreement is rooted in differing interpretations of historical data and the mechanisms through which culture and institutions interact. At the heart of this debate is the question of whether cultural values and norms are endogenous to institutional frameworks or whether they exert an exogenous influence on economic development. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Nunn and Wantchekon suggest that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

Institutions are highlighted as a fundamental determinant of economic growth, as North (1989, p.1); (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) assert. The institutional framework, shaped by historical events and colonial policies, significantly influences economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn affected current income per capita. This historical path dependency underscores the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic development. North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to foster long-run growth. This implies that the institutional framework can be manipulated to serve the interests of the ruling elite, potentially stifling economic development.

North and Weingast, along with Sokoloff and Engerman, assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework facilitated the growth of a stable market for public debt, which in turn provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets. This security allowed for the development of a robust financial system, which was essential for sustaining long-term economic growth. However, Sokoloff and Engerman qualify this by noting that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was influenced by the institutions that evolved, which often advantaged elites and restricted competition, thus affecting the course of development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This implies that while secure property rights and stable financial institutions are necessary for economic growth, the distribution of power and wealth within a society can significantly influence the effectiveness of these institutions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) share a critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views, arguing that these perspectives oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis assumes that early economic and political development led to subsequent stagnation, while Eurocentric views posit that Europe's unique cultural and institutional features drove its economic ascendancy. Both approaches overlook the dynamic and endogenous processes that shaped economic development in different regions. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, for instance, overlooks the complex interplay between institutions, technologies, and cultural norms that influenced economic growth. Austin underscores that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes secure property rights as key to long-term economic growth, struggles to explain why some countries adopted growth-enhancing institutions while others did not. This limitation highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutions to shape economic outcomes. Bryant builds on this by asserting that the revisionist position, which dismisses Eurocentric views, is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. Bryant argues that effective causality in economic development is governed by a causal connectedness that spans longer-term processes, including the accumulation of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms. This perspective suggests that cultural factors, such as legal-juridical developments and civic urbanization, played a crucial role in Europe's economic trajectory. Bryant (2006, p.35) also notes that the revisionist preference for focusing on shorter-term processes can lead to explanatory distortions. This implies that a comprehensive understanding of long-run growth requires considering both immediate and long-term cultural and institutional dynamics.

The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has persisted and impacted long-term development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust emerged as a consequence of the exploitative and violent nature of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and undermined cooperative norms. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has been linked to contemporary economic underdevelopment, as it impedes social capital formation and hampers collective action. A specific mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy operates is the weakening of state institutions. This instability has persisted into the present, contributing to ongoing economic challenges. Nunn (2008, p.27) also notes that the adverse effects of the slave trade extend beyond institutional weakness. The legacy of mistrust has also manifested in high levels of corruption, further hindering economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8) jointly establish that women have played a pivotal role in economic development, shaping both pre-colonial and colonial economies. The contribution of women to economic growth is significant. This role involves labor, as well as cultural and institutional factors that influence economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack describe how colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production. These constructs may still reflect the sexual division of labor established during the colonial period, indicating that cultural norms and beliefs can be as enduring as pre-colonial social norms. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) illustrate this through the Akan state formation in the 17th century, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth in a matrilineal society. Goldin (2006, p.8) shows that changes in social norms concerning women's family and career began in the late 1960s and 1970s, highlighting the transformative impact of cultural shifts on economic participation. These shifts were driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. Goldin (2006, p.3) further notes that changes in household production technology and institutional barriers also altered female labor supply. These cultural and institutional factors highlight the complex interplay between gender roles, economic development, and long-run growth.

This historical investigation reveals that the impact of institutions on development is not uniform across regions and time periods. This perspective is shared by Broadberry and Gupta (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The historical context is essential because it reveals that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions. This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and fosters development-inducing institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). However, the new economic history of Africa, while focusing on institutional reforms to alleviate poverty, does not explicitly address how culture might dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. This omission is significant because cultural factors can profoundly influence the effectiveness of institutional reforms. For instance, cultural norms and values can shape how institutions are perceived and implemented, thereby affecting their impact on economic development. This interaction between culture and institutions is crucial for understanding the long-run growth dynamics in Africa.

Culture's role in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and economic trajectories of nations. Institutions, as the primary drivers of economic growth, are fundamentally shaped by cultural factors. Acemoglu et al. show that the determinants of European settlement in colonies, influenced by mortality rates, significantly affected the institutions established there (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical context underscores how cultural perceptions of risk and mortality shaped institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced long-run economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, exemplifies how institutional changes reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings highlight that cultural norms and values, by shaping institutional frameworks, play a pivotal role in determining long-run economic outcomes.

The interaction between culture and institutions is further elucidated by the role of trust within societies. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, accounting for reduced interpersonal trust and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy of mistrust influences contemporary economic outcomes by affecting the effectiveness of institutional reforms. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the need to consider the dynamic and endogenous nature of cultural and institutional interactions over time. Austin's perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.1) underscores the importance of understanding how cultural factors influence the adoption and effectiveness of institutions that drive economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement about the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the persistence of institutional frameworks shaped by cultural norms. For instance, the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, provides a clear example of how institutional changes reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence shows that cultural factors, such as trust and risk perceptions, are integral to the effectiveness of institutional reforms. The measurement type used here is the historical analysis of institutional changes and their economic impacts, a case that both North and Nunn and Wantchekon have already used (North 1989, p.9; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
