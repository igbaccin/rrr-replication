The role of culture in shaping institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the identification of the primary drivers of economic growth and the extent to which cultural factors can influence institutional development and economic performance over the long term. There is a live disagreement between those who argue that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions and those who contend that other factors, such as geographical conditions or extractive institutions, are more influential. Some scholars posit that cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, foster institutions that promote economic growth. Others emphasize the role of extractive institutions established during colonial periods, which persist and significantly affect current economic outcomes. Understanding the role of culture in institutional development is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at promoting economic growth. This debate underscores the need to explore how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive long-run economic development. In this context, the interdependency of institutions within broader frameworks is a key factor to consider. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) argue that the growth of useful knowledge and ideas, as specific manifestations of culture, were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Ideas and ideologies, rather than institutions or interests, shaped the economic landscape by fostering innovation and productivity. This new rhetoric, grounded in cultural shifts, created new circumstances that further reinforced the growth of useful knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates on this by highlighting that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was crucial. The sharing of ideas through periodicals, translations, and international conferences ensured that useful knowledge was widely accessible. This diffusion was not merely a matter of institutional change driven by elite interests, as McCloskey (2015, p.10) critiques in the work of Cheung, but a broader cultural phenomenon. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further notes that much of this knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practices that were transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge, while harder to codify, was integral to the economic growth of the period. The interplay between explicit and tacit knowledge underscores the importance of cultural factors in driving economic change.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with Pamuk, discuss the economic divergence between regions of Europe and Asia before 1800, as well as structural changes initiated by events like the Black Death (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This divergence was driven by the varying trajectories of agricultural productivity and institutional change. Broadberry and Gupta argue that in the prosperous parts of Asia, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower. This discrepancy suggests that the purchasing power over tradable goods and services was not uniform across these regions. The lower silver wages in Asia indicate that the economic development in these regions did not translate into a comparable standard of living as in north-western Europe. This divergence was influenced by high levels of investment in agriculture, which in turn led to high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). However, this higher productivity did not result in an abundance of grain and low food prices in Europe, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This shift highlights the role of institutional changes and labour mobility in driving economic development. Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, contributing to the rise of rural industries. The scarcity and high cost of labour ushered in an era of labour-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These structural changes, initiated by the Black Death, played a crucial role in the economic divergence between regions. The role of culture in driving these structural changes and long-run growth remains unexplored. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further research on how cultural factors influence economic development and institutional change.

North and Weingast, together with Hornbeck, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights played a crucial role in fostering economic growth, particularly in seventeenth-century England and the American Plains (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). In England, the financial revolution was pivotal in this process, as it provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt. The institutions that facilitated the growth of a stable market for public debt were instrumental in this transformation. The Bank of England, for instance, began private operations shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, illustrating the interconnectedness of public and private financial markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework secured property rights and created an environment conducive to economic growth and political freedom. On the American Plains, the introduction of barbed wire fencing similarly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. The role of social norms in property rights enforcement, as seen in Ellickson's analysis of a modern California county, suggests that cultural factors may also play a significant role in economic development. This finding qualifies the emphasis on institutional frameworks by highlighting the importance of social norms in property rights enforcement.

North (1989, p.1) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) highlight the pivotal role of institutions in shaping economic growth, emphasizing their impact on transaction costs and the allocation of resources. North (1989, p.1) posits that institutions are crucial for lowering transaction costs, which in turn facilitates economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights and fostering long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). These institutions, shaped by the need to maximize rents for European colonists, were designed to extract resources efficiently (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). As a result, the institutional frameworks that emerged in these colonies have significantly affected their economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This perspective underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic development, highlighting that the legacies of colonial rule continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade has left a lasting impact on African economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust. This culture of mistrust, ingrained through centuries of exploitation and violence, has persisted and continues to affect economic outcomes. The slave trade's legacy is evident in the weakened state institutions that struggle to enforce property rights and contracts, which are crucial for economic growth. Furthermore, the historical accounts of the slave trades highlight how this instability led to a lack of state centralization, which is a critical determinant of current economic performance. The fragmented states, a direct result of the slave trade, have been unable to implement effective policies and institutions that could foster economic growth. This historical legacy of mistrust and instability, therefore, continues to hinder Africa's economic development, illustrating how the slave trade's impact extends beyond immediate economic losses to shape long-term institutional and cultural frameworks.

Ogilvie and Austin argue that distributional conflicts are the primary driver of pre-industrial economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie contends that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by the power struggles among different social groups. This perspective aligns with Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the current economic conditions of African countries are largely due to their historical institutions. Austin argues that this thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors that influenced the economic development of Africa, including the dynamic responses of Africans to their environments (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical interplay is a matter of institutional adoption and how these institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies further supports this view by showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality tended to advantage elites, who used government policies to restrict competition and maintain their dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This mechanism highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutions that perpetuate inequality, thereby influencing long-term economic development. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical distributional conflicts on contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions are dynamic frameworks that evolve in response to cultural influences. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework means that cultural values and beliefs can significantly impact economic outcomes. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) demonstrates that cultural beliefs about the afterlife and social capital can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, such as guilds and communes. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects contemporary economic and political institutions. This cultural mistrust, stemming from historical events, highlights how deeply ingrained cultural norms can perpetuate institutional frameworks that hinder economic growth. The enduring impact of historical distributional conflicts on contemporary economic outcomes underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutions over the long run. The historical evidence from these studies shows that cultural influences have lasting effects on institutional development and economic performance.

Institutional frameworks are inherently complex and interconnected, with cultural beliefs playing a pivotal role in their evolution. Cultural values can shape the rules and norms that govern economic interactions, thereby influencing long-term economic development. For example, the English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, reflects how cultural and institutional changes can lead to secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical transformation shows that institutions, when supported by cultural norms, can foster economic development. The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of cultural values on long-term economic outcomes. The measurement of trust levels and historical events, such as the slave trade in Africa, provides empirical evidence of how cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks and economic performance. This historical evidence shows that cultural influences are a fundamental factor in the development of institutions that drive long-run economic growth.
