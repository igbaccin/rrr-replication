The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the explanation of persistent economic disparities across regions and over time. One live disagreement centers around whether cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic institutions and growth, or whether institutions are shaped by other factors such as geography, technology, or political structures. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. Others posit that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates or the slave trade, which in turn influence long-run economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or a secondary factor influenced by other determinants. This tension is critical for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. The role of culture in shaping institutions and economic growth is a complex and multifaceted issue, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on the mechanisms through which culture exerts its influence. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective emphasizes the role of institutional change in facilitating economic development, rather than cultural factors. North (1989, p.1) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further illustrate that the institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and the issuance of public debt, played a critical role in providing the necessary conditions for economic growth and political freedom. These institutional developments were pivotal in creating a stable environment for long-term capital markets and economic growth. However, while North and Weingast emphasize the role of institutions in fostering economic growth, it is important to recognize that cultural factors, such as religious beliefs and social norms, can also influence economic outcomes. The interplay between institutions and culture is complex, and both factors can contribute to economic development in different ways.

Acemoglu et al. contend that colonial institutions are a fundamental cause of long-run economic development. This perspective, shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48), is rooted in the idea that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have lasting effects on economic outcomes. A key mechanism through which colonial institutions influence long-run development is the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had enduring impacts on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that high settler mortality led to the establishment of institutions that prioritized resource extraction over sustainable development. This institutional legacy continues to shape current economic conditions, as evidenced by the strong correlation between settler mortality and contemporary institutional quality. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this by noting that these institutions are often shaped by politically powerful groups who may not prioritize long-term economic development. The persistence of these institutions indicates that their initial design and the interests they serve have had a profound and lasting impact on economic trajectories. This highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutional frameworks on long-run economic development, independent of cultural factors.

This ideological transformation was a cultural struggle that reshaped societal values and norms. The cultural shift that occurred in countries like the Netherlands and Britain, which embraced a bourgeois and anti-aristocratic ethos, enabled a new rhetoric to take hold, which in turn fostered economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This stream contends that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions was the negotiation of rights and privileges among elites, commoners, and guilds. For instance, the craft guilds and merchant guilds, which were central to the economic life of medieval Europe, emerged as a result of conflicts over the control of markets and the regulation of trade. These guilds were tools used by different groups to secure their economic interests and maintain their social status. Ogilvie's analysis shows that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The downstream implication of this perspective is that the institutions that emerged from these conflicts were not necessarily efficient or growth-promoting. Rather, they were often designed to protect the interests of powerful groups and maintain the status quo. Ogilvie acknowledges the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may not capture the complexity of institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This implies that while distributional conflicts were a significant factor, they were not the only force shaping economic institutions.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This dynamic is particularly evident in Africa, where environmental responses and technological adaptations shaped economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). They note that in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change drove agricultural investment, leading to high agricultural labour productivity. This productivity did not result in low food prices due to labour migration from agriculture into industry and services. This suggests that institutional changes, driven by cultural and technological factors, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are that culture, through its influence on institutions, can dominate economic paths and growth trajectories.

This mistrust is a contemporary force shaping African economies. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this legacy, showing that the slave trade undermined state centralization and political stability, leading to fragmented states. This political fragmentation, in turn, hindered the development of effective institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. The impact of the slave trade extends beyond political instability. This underscores the enduring influence of historical events on present-day economic and political conditions, highlighting the importance of long-term cultural and institutional factors in shaping economic development.
