The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is whether culture is a force that shapes institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. Central to this debate is the question of whether cultural values and beliefs influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by the cultural beliefs of the societies that create them. The former camp views institutions as evolving to maximize economic efficiency, while the latter emphasizes the role of cultural norms and values in determining institutional structures. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms underlying economic growth and development, as it challenges the notion that institutions are purely rational constructs and suggests that cultural factors play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This is because institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. North (1989, p.1) also notes that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). North (1989, p.1) also notes that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution. Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that distributional conflicts shape economic institutions, with efficiency or cultural factors playing a role. Ogilvie demonstrates this through the lens of pre-industrial Europe, where institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by the interests of elites seeking to maintain their power and wealth. These elites were not driven by efficiency considerations alone; instead, they manipulated institutions to preserve their advantageous positions. The persistence of these institutions, despite their inefficiencies, is a direct result of this power dynamic. The distributional conflicts Ogilvie highlights are not confined to pre-industrial Europe. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and maintaining inequality. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework must be considered. Institutions do not operate in isolation; rather, they are part of a complex system where changes in one area can have ripple effects throughout the entire framework. This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they are just one piece of a larger puzzle that includes cultural, technological, and institutional factors. The interplay of these factors creates a dynamic system where economic institutions are shaped by a multitude of influences, not just distributional conflicts.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They propose that the European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita. The impact of these institutions is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as the significant 2SLS estimate of the impact of institutions on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). However, Acemoglu et al. also note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the long-term economic effects of colonial institutions are complex and multifaceted.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and Bryant (2006, p.35) adds that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. These sources together establish that the dynamic and endogenous factors of economic development are not adequately captured by institutional evolution alone. The causal connectedness between past and present is governed by the enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms that have accumulated through prior developments (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Thus, the institutionalist approach, while emphasizing the political delivery of secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, may not fully account for the complex interplay of these factors. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic, partly endogenous, version of institutional evolution suggests that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, and that the adoption of growth-favorable institutions in some countries but not in others cannot be explained by institutionalist theory alone (Austin 2008, p.25). This view is qualified by Bryant (2006, p.35), who argues that the European passage to modernity was facilitated by a massive fissure within Latin Christendom that refashioned not only the institutional orders of Europe, but the mentalities of its inhabitants as well (Bryant 2006, p.4). This suggests that the cultural and institutional changes that underpinned European economic growth were interdependent and mutually reinforcing, and that the role of culture in shaping long-run growth is complex and multifaceted.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) both focus on technological and structural changes that affect economic growth, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing and the impact of the Black Death. These studies do not directly address how culture influences long-run growth, but they do highlight how technological innovations and structural changes can enhance productivity and economic development. This is consistent with Pamuk's (2007, p.24) findings that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, and contributed to the rise of rural industries. These findings demonstrate that technological and structural changes can have profound effects on economic growth by altering the incentives and opportunities available to economic actors. These changes also have cultural implications. Pamuk (2007, p.5) notes that the Black Death brought about a significant shift in the economic landscape of Europe, which in turn influenced the cultural norms and social structures of the time. This suggests that while technological and structural changes are important drivers of economic growth, they are also intertwined with cultural factors that shape long-run development.

This highlights the need to consider the broader social and cultural contexts in which technological and structural changes occur. During the pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa, women were integral to community reproduction and economic activities, especially in matrilineal societies like Asante, where their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Their role in economic growth was foundational, challenging views that marginalize women's economic contributions. This dynamic was not confined to Africa; in the U.S., a quiet revolution transformed women's employment and education, altering social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career roles (Goldin 2006, p.8). This transformation was driven by exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions, which significantly altered female labor supply and occupational patterns (Goldin 2006, p.3). The interplay between cultural norms and economic growth highlights the enduring impact of historical gender dynamics on contemporary economic development.

The skill premium and standard of living in European economic development do not directly address the fundamental cause of long-run growth (van Zanden 2009, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2). The skill premium, which measures the relative earnings of skilled versus unskilled workers, serves as a proxy for the returns to human capital investment. This premium can incentivize education and skill acquisition, thereby fostering economic growth. The relationship between the skill premium and economic development is complex and context-dependent. This dynamic suggests that the skill premium is intertwined with broader economic and demographic trends, not an isolated determinant of economic growth. This qualification underscores the nuanced role of the skill premium in economic development, indicating that while it may be a predictor of long-term economic performance, it is not a sufficient condition for sustained growth. The interplay between the skill premium and other economic factors, such as population growth and agricultural productivity, highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development and the need for a comprehensive understanding of its determinants.

Nunn and Wantchekon identify a culture of mistrust as a critical legacy of the slave trade in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This culture of mistrust, a direct product of the slave trade, has had a profound and lasting effect on social capital and economic development. The slave trade's impact on trust levels can be attributed to the political instability and weakened state structures that resulted from the transatlantic slave trade. This mistrust is a deeply ingrained cultural norm that has been shaped by historical events, not merely a byproduct of contemporary political or economic conditions. Nunn (2008, p.27) supports this perspective by noting that the slave trade caused long-term political instability, which in turn resulted in fragmented and weak states. This political instability has had a detrimental effect on economic development, as it has hindered the establishment of strong and effective institutions. The legacy of the slave trade involves the immediate loss of human capital, as well as the long-term erosion of trust and the weakening of state institutions. These historical factors have created a challenging environment for economic growth, one that is marked by mistrust and institutional fragility.

(2001, p.27) both highlight how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores that culture influences the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity.

North (1989, p.1) similarly points out that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.1).
