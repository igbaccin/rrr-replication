The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors can be understood as drivers of institutional change, or if they are merely epiphenomenal to deeper structural forces. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values shape institutions and, consequently, economic growth. Others contend that institutions are primarily shaped by economic factors, with culture playing a secondary role. The intellectual stakes involve understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and the potential for cultural interventions to influence growth trajectories. The disagreement centres on the causal primacy of cultural factors versus economic determinants in shaping institutions that foster long-run growth. This debate is crucial for policy implications, as it informs whether cultural change can be leveraged to promote economic development or if efforts should focus on structural economic reforms. The interplay between culture and institutions is not a simple one, and the live disagreement centres on whether cultural factors can drive institutional change and, by extension, economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was pivotal in creating a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the growth of a market for private debt (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution in England thus played a critical role in long-run economic success, as it provided a large and positive externality for the development of a stable market for private debt. The institutions that emerged during this period were essential for making economic growth and political freedom possible. The creation of the Bank of England, for instance, enabled the intermediation of public debt, which was a foundational step in fostering a stable market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Despite these advancements, the extent to which other forces would have led to similar economic development under different institutional frameworks remains a subject of debate. Williamson's work, though controversial, supports the thesis that the institutions governing public choice in England were necessary for the country's long-run success.

The legacy of colonialism extends beyond immediate economic impacts, fundamentally altering the institutional frameworks of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule were shaped by European settlers' mortality rates, leading to the creation of extractive institutions in high-mortality regions. These institutions, designed to maximize colonial rents, were not conducive to economic development. The persistence of these institutions has significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism is clear: high mortality rates among Europeans led to extractive institutions, which in turn hindered economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which in the colonial context were European colonists. This qualification underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, as these institutions were not oriented towards fostering long-run growth. Thus, the legacy of colonial institutions remains a critical factor in understanding the economic trajectories of former colonies.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These scholars highlight that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. The persistence of institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds can be attributed to the ability of dominant groups to maintain their control and extract rents. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with various markets and welfare systems, contributing to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that the institutional framework as a whole, shaped by distributional conflicts, played a crucial role in economic development. The persistence of inequality in New World societies indicates that the institutions shaped by these conflicts could also hinder growth by restricting competition and maintaining elite advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This underscores the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development, highlighting how institutions shaped by these conflicts can have enduring effects on economic outcomes.

The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) argue persists and affects long-term economic development. This mistrust is a legacy of the slave trade's destabilizing effects on African societies. These fragmented states, in turn, struggle to provide the stable institutional framework necessary for sustained economic growth. Persistent mistrust and political instability have tangible economic consequences. This historical legacy of mistrust and instability creates a challenging environment for modern economic development, as trust and stable institutions are fundamental for fostering economic growth. The interplay between historical mistrust and contemporary economic underdevelopment underscores the complex and enduring effects of the slave trade on African societies.

Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex processes of economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). While this thesis suggests that colonial institutions are the primary cause of current economic disparities, Austin and Broadberry and Gupta argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This divergence was driven by endogenous factors, such as institutional changes and investment in agriculture. Austin's critique of the 'simple geography' thesis highlights the importance of dynamic, context-specific factors in shaping economic development paths. This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gupta's argument that the great divergence was a gradual process influenced by various factors, including investment and institutional changes. Their shared finding suggests that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was the result of a complex interplay of factors over time. This perspective does not fully address the role of culture in shaping long-run growth, as it primarily focuses on economic and institutional factors.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both posit that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change, particularly in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Their shared finding suggests that the economic transformation during this period was fundamentally driven by intellectual shifts. McCloskey (2015, p.10) highlights the role of cultural and ideological changes, noting that the "Great Enrichment" was facilitated by a new rhetoric that created new circumstances, which in turn fostered further rhetorical and economic developments. This perspective underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping economic growth, as the ideological environment influenced the adoption of new economic practices and institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.56) complements this view by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was crucial, as it enabled the transfer of tacit knowledge and skills that were essential for economic development. This diffusion was facilitated by personal transmission and training, which highlights the role of social and cultural networks in disseminating knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the codification of knowledge was a function of technology and costs, suggesting that the economic benefits of knowledge diffusion were not uniformly distributed. This qualification points to the importance of considering the economic and technological context in which cultural factors operate. Cultural factors, therefore, played a significant role in shaping long-run growth by facilitating the diffusion of knowledge and ideas.

The role of culture in shaping institutions and fostering long-run growth is evident in the interplay between social norms, economic practices, and institutional frameworks. Cultural factors, such as trust and social capital, significantly influence the efficiency of economic institutions. For example, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how features like trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, thereby improving societal efficiency. These 'civic' cultures, characterized by altruistic and horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. This is shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Similarly, the historical analysis of European economic institutions by Ogilvie shows how cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interdependency of institutions within a broader framework underscores the importance of considering the wider institutional context in which cultural factors operate (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The role of cultural factors in shaping institutions and facilitating long-run growth is clear, but the extent to which these factors operate within or are constrained by the broader economic and technological context remains.
