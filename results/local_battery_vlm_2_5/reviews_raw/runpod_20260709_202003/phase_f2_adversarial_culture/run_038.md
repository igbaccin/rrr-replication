The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography. At stake is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values influence the establishment and evolution of institutions that foster economic development. The live disagreement centers around whether cultural factors are fundamental causes of long-run growth or whether they are merely epiphenomena of other, more proximate determinants. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs directly shape economic institutions, while others contend that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or political dynamics. The debate hinges on whether culture operates through institutions to drive economic growth, or if institutions are shaped by other factors and culture is incidental. This question is pivotal because it determines how economists and historians should approach the study of economic development, particularly in understanding the persistence of economic disparities across regions and over time. A clear understanding of the role of culture in shaping institutions is essential for designing effective policies aimed at promoting economic growth. The debate extends to whether cultural factors are antecedent to institutional development, or if they are a result of institutional changes. The relationship between culture and institutions is complex and multifaceted, with various scholars proposing different mechanisms through which culture influences institutional development and economic growth. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, a claim supported by North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They demonstrate that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional change reflected an increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast assert that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success. The implication is that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional perspective aligns with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North points out that the neoclassical model, which has served as the basis of economic reasoning for most scholars in the Western world, holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. This institutional framework underscores the importance of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs in driving economic growth, a mechanism that operates independently of cultural factors.

Distributional conflicts were a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In pre-industrial Europe, the economic institutions that emerged were often the result of power struggles rather than cultural preferences. For example, the persistence of serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the elite's desire to maintain control over labor and resources. This dynamic is echoed in the New World, where institutions in societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. For instance, Latin American economies were characterized by extreme wealth disparities from their inception, with institutions evolving to reflect and reinforce these inequalities. This pattern underscores how institutions that emerge from distributional conflicts can significantly impact long-run growth. The interdependencies between different institutions within a broader framework add complexity to this picture. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that individual institutions cannot be studied in isolation, as they are interconnected within a wider system. This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts may drive the formation of institutions, their impact on growth is mediated by the complex interactions within the institutional framework.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective highlights the pivotal role of cultural shifts in fostering economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further elaborates that the diffusion of useful knowledge, rather than institutional frameworks, was the mainspring of economic growth. This knowledge was codified and also embodied in tacit forms, transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that cultural and ideological changes can drive the dissemination of knowledge, which in turn fuels economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that the diffusion of knowledge was not uniform and was influenced by national differences in style and emphasis (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This qualification suggests that while cultural shifts are crucial, their impact on economic growth can vary across different national contexts. Thus, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is nuanced, with cultural shifts driving knowledge dissemination and economic growth, but with varying effects across different contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persisted over time. These institutions, in turn, had a profound impact on current income per capita, illustrating how historical institutional changes can shape long-term economic performance. The relationship between settler mortality and institutional quality is robust, with a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions. This finding underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to these institutional changes, highlighting the critical role of institutions in economic growth and industrialization. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions are crucial, their impact on economic development can be complex and multifaceted. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, offer a significant explanation for long-run economic outcomes, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors driving economic development, compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This critique highlights that the thesis, which posits that institutions shaped by colonialism are the primary determinants of long-run economic outcomes, overlooks the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional evolution. For example, Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the African experience demonstrates a more nuanced interaction between environmental factors and technological responses, challenging the notion of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic view implies that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth depends on how these institutions interact with local conditions and historical contexts. Bryant (2006, p.35) further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of cultural norms and social relations, which are mediated inheritances from the past and crucial for any transition to industrial modes of production (Bryant 2006, p.4). These factors, together with technological advancements and legal-juridical developments, create a complex web of influences that shape economic growth.

The cluster of scholars Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Goldin (2006, p.8) address various factors influencing economic growth, but they do not explicitly focus on how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. They share a focus on the complex interplay between technological advancements, social changes, and economic dynamics. Abramovitz highlights the erratic nature of productivity levels and the limited social competence for exploiting advanced technologies, particularly in the earlier years and among latecomers (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Goldin, on the other hand, discusses the role of social norms and institutional changes in altering female labor supply and occupational shifts, noting that these changes were driven by various exogenous factors (Goldin 2006, p.3). One specific mechanism that emerges from their work is the role of social competence and norms in shaping economic outcomes. Abramovitz's observation about limited social competence for exploiting advanced technologies suggests that cultural factors, such as social norms and institutional arrangements, play a crucial role in determining economic growth. Goldin's analysis qualifies this by showing that exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions, also significantly influence labor supply and occupational shifts. This points to a broader implication: while cultural factors are important, they interact with exogenous changes in complex ways that shape economic growth. These interactions highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of how culture and institutions co-evolve to drive long-run economic growth.

The slave trade has left a profound legacy in Africa, shaping cultural attitudes and institutions in ways that persist today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) find that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust and political instability, which in turn hindered economic development. This political instability is a critical factor in understanding Africa's current economic underdevelopment, as it has led to a lack of state centralization and weakened institutions. The historical legacy of the slave trade has not only shaped cultural attitudes but also influenced the development of institutions, which are fundamental to long-run economic growth. However, the impact of the slave trade is not limited to cultural attitudes and institutions; it also has implications for contemporary economic outcomes. This underscores the complex interplay between historical events, cultural attitudes, and long-run economic growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted, with cultural attitudes shaping institutions that, in turn, drive economic development. Cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both highlight how cultural factors can lead to the establishment of institutions that either foster or hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline. This indicates that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Cultural attitudes also play a crucial role in the development of institutions that govern public choice and secure property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution. Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). North (1989, p.1) and Austin (2008, p.2) build on this by showing that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North (1989, p.1) argues that the neoclassical model holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs, but that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter (North 1989, p.1). Austin (2008, p.2) further shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the importance of institutional evolution in long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

The legacy of historical events, such as the slave trade, also plays a significant role in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, affects the development of institutions that foster economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) also show that the slave trade can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trade is still felt today in the form of economic underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). Austin and Nunn (2008) both demonstrate that the impact of the slave trade is not limited to cultural attitudes and institutions; it also has implications for contemporary economic outcomes. Austin argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. (2002) call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the course of economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on contemporary economic outcomes shows that the legacy of historical events continues to shape the development of institutions and economic growth in Africa.
