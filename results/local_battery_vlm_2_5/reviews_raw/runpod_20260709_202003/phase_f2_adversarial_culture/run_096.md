The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth presents a complex interplay between cultural beliefs and economic development. At stake is the intellectual debate on whether cultural factors are instrumental in shaping institutions that drive economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or institutional frameworks, hold more explanatory power. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which cultural beliefs and values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. On one side, scholars argue that cultural factors are pivotal in creating institutions that facilitate economic growth and political accountability. On the other side, researchers contend that the impact of culture is overshadowed by other determinants, such as the extractive nature of institutions imposed by colonial powers or the endogenous responses of populations to their environments. The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs can alter the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, thereby influencing economic performance over the long run. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical roots of economic disparities and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. Culture plays a critical role in economic institutions, explaining why some societies thrive while others stagnate (Author, Year, p.N). (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The role of women in economic growth highlights how cultural norms and gender dynamics significantly influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods were substantial, challenging views that marginalize their role. They show that colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had lasting impacts on gender relations and production systems, which continue to reflect the sexual division of labor established during colonial times. This dynamic is not confined to Africa; Goldin notes that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. This revolution was driven by changes in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career, which began to shift in the late 1960s and 1970s. These changes were preconditions for others, such as college majors, professional school enrollment, and occupational change. The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, including the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century and the growth of educational institutions. These changes made "nice" jobs available and greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women. The role of women in economic growth thus underscores the importance of cultural norms and gender dynamics in long-run economic development, showing how these factors can both constrain and enable economic progress.

The slave trade's legacy in Africa underscores how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders cooperation and investment in public goods. For example, Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect on trust in Africa. Nunn highlights that this political instability has led to weakened and fragmented states, which are less capable of providing public goods and enforcing property rights. This political instability has also contributed to current underdevelopment in Africa (Nunn 2008, p.1). Nunn also recognizes that the slave trade is not the only factor contributing to Africa's underdevelopment (Author, Year, p.N). Other factors, such as colonialism and more recent events, also play a role. Nevertheless, the legacy of the slave trade in shaping cultural attitudes and political institutions remains a critical factor in understanding Africa's long-run economic development.

North and Weingast  argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). These scholars demonstrate that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the institutions governing economic interactions, not merely by population and savings (Author, Year, p.N). Through the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, England experienced a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in fostering a stable market for public debt, which provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Although the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, it is important to note that other forces, such as technological change and better resource allocation, also contributed to economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Ogilvie (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) focus on the role of institutions in shaping development paths and distribution, but do not directly address how culture influences long-run growth. They show that institutions can be shaped by factors such as inequality and distributional conflicts, which in turn affect economic growth. In New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, characterized by policies that benefited the elite, created a cycle where inequality was maintained and reinforced through institutional design. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) similarly highlights that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, shaped by conflicts over resource distribution, influenced economic growth by determining the allocation of resources and opportunities. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the entire institutional system, including labour markets, credit markets, and welfare systems, contributed to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency implies that the institutional framework as a whole, not just individual institutions, is crucial for understanding economic development. The role of culture in shaping these institutions and their impact on long-run growth is still an open question (Author, Year, p.N).

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run growth, and Acemoglu et al. support this view with empirical evidence showing that extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mechanism through which these institutions affect growth is via their impact on the allocation of resources and opportunities, which in turn influences economic performance. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance, finding a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This relationship suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial times continues to shape economic outcomes in the present day. Acemoglu et al. qualify this perspective by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic development, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth is contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they were established (Author, Year, p.N).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This critique highlights the importance of considering the period-specific nature of institutions and their impact on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) also rejects the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development, and instead advocates for a more dynamic, endogenous approach that considers Africans' responses to their environments and the technologies available to them. This dynamic approach underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with their environments over time. Bryant contends that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This perspective emphasizes the interconnectedness of historical transitions and the role of enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms in facilitating industrial modes of production and imperial hegemony. Bryant argues that the revisionist preference for shorter-term and immediate processes is prone to explanatory distortions, highlighting the importance of considering the long-term accumulation of resources and opportunities in understanding historical transitions. The shared claim of this stream is that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect, as they fail to account for the complex interplay between institutions, environments, and historical transitions. This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with their environments over time.

These findings underscore the importance of cultural factors in institutional development.
