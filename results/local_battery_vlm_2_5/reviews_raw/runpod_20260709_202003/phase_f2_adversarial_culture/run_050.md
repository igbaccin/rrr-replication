The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth presents a complex and contested intellectual terrain. The stakes involve understanding whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of economic growth and whether institutions are shaped by cultural norms and beliefs. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which cultural influences dominate institutions versus other factors such as geographical determinants or institutional efficiency. One side argues that cultural beliefs directly shape economic institutions, influencing efficiency and growth. The opposing side posits that the efficiency of institutions is the primary determinant of economic growth, with cultural beliefs playing a secondary role. This debate is crucial for comprehending the underlying mechanisms of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks is central to this discussion, as it determines how societies organize their economic activities and achieve long-term prosperity. Understanding the role of culture in shaping institutions is essential for policymakers aiming to promote economic growth, as it highlights the importance of cultural factors in institutional design and efficiency. The relationship between cultural beliefs and economic institutions is a critical area of study, as it sheds light on the complex interplay between societal values and economic outcomes. McCloskey and Mokyr both argue that ideas and ideologies, as components of culture, were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey's work shows that the Great Enrichment was driven by a cultural shift in rhetoric, which fostered new economic circumstances. This is exemplified by the contrast between the uniformed, patriotic engineers of France and the more pragmatic, business-oriented institutions in places like the Netherlands and Britain. Mokyr's work underscores that the growth of useful knowledge was a key driver of economic growth. This knowledge was codified and transmitted through personal interactions and tacit forms, facilitating its spread across borders. The shared finding is that ideas, not merely institutions or interests, were the key drivers of economic change. The spread of useful knowledge was not confined to national borders. Mokyr contends that the diffusion of knowledge within the Western world was rapid and thorough, facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. This diffusion suggests that the economic growth spurred by new ideas was a shared phenomenon across Northwestern Europe, not limited by national boundaries. McCloskey's analysis of the "Franco-French War" illustrates how cultural and ideological struggles within nations could either impede or accelerate economic growth. This internal struggle highlights the dynamic nature of cultural influence on economic development, where the rhetoric and ideologies of the time could either stifle or promote growth. For example, Mokyr's emphasis on tacit knowledge highlights a crucial mechanism through which ideas spread and fostered economic growth. This tacit knowledge, often embedded in skills and practices, was transmitted through personal interactions and training, making it a vital component of economic development. The implication is that the dissemination of ideas and knowledge, whether codified or tacit, was essential for economic growth. This dissemination was influenced by various cultural and ideological factors.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) assert that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They identify the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England as a pivotal moment, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs. This institutional change increased the security of property rights over time, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The implication is that secure property rights, underpinned by robust institutions, provided a stable environment for economic activities, fostering growth by encouraging investment and innovation. North's emphasis on the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs aligns with Weingast's analysis of the English financial revolution, which highlights how institutions can create large and positive externalities, such as the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework, which secured property rights and reduced transaction costs, was essential for long-term economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). When European powers colonized various regions, the mortality rates they faced influenced the type of institutions they established. High mortality rates led to the implementation of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize short-term gains for the colonists. These institutions, characterized by poor property rights and high transaction costs, persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The impact of these institutions is evident in the correlation between early colonial institutions and current economic performance, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutional frameworks. When European colonies were established, these institutions were shaped to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. also found that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these extractive institutions has profound implications for economic development, as they create barriers to growth by discouraging investment and innovation. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the critical role of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on economic development. The slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists to this day. This mistrust was created by the slave trade's disruption of social structures and the exploitation of ethnic divisions. The slave trade also caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability and fragmentation in turn led to a lack of state centralization, which is a key determinant of economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) argues that state centralization is positively correlated with real per capita GDP. The slave trade's adverse effect on economic development is not solely due to its impact on state centralization. It also had a direct effect on economic institutions, such as property rights and contract enforcement, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.1) argues that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. This corruption, in turn, can hinder economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to influence economic outcomes in Africa, underscoring the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This perspective is shared by Pamuk (2007, p.24), who attributes the early divergence to long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death. The Black Death brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, contributing to the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations. This mechanism led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death's role in this divergence is significant because it demonstrates how a historical event can have long-lasting economic effects, shaping the institutional landscape and technological advancements in Europe. The Black Death's impact on institutional change and technological innovation underscores how historical events can influence economic development by altering the conditions for investment and innovation. The timing of economic divergence before 1800 is crucial because it challenges views that attribute this divergence solely to later developments, such as the Industrial Revolution. The role of culture in this context is evident in how historical events shaped the institutional landscape and technological advancements, which in turn influenced economic growth.

Austin argues that institutional explanations for economic growth oversimplify causation, and this critique is shared by Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutionalist approach, which posits that secure private property rights are key to long-term economic growth, often fails to account for the dynamic and endogenous factors that influence institutional evolution. This simplification can lead to an incomplete understanding of why certain institutions that appear favorable for economic growth are adopted in some countries but not in others. Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, overlooks the complex interplay of factors that shape economic development. This critique is supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework, arguing that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. Ogilvie contends that the European family system, for instance, was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. This dynamic interplay of institutions can lead to divergent economic outcomes, as seen in the economic performance of northern and southern Italy since medieval times. The cultural beliefs and values associated with these regions, such as trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, played a significant role in shaping the institutional landscape and economic growth. This underscores how cultural factors can influence the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game, affecting the efficiency of the economy. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Ogilvie's emphasis on the interdependency of institutions highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of culture in shaping economic institutions and long-run growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. Beliefs and values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, significantly influence the structure and equilibrium of collective action games, affecting economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural factors are intertwined with institutional frameworks, as shown by the interdependency of institutions within a broader system. For instance, the European family system was interlinked with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, all of which contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency underscores how cultural elements, such as trust and social networks, can foster institutions that promote economic growth. The significance of cultural beliefs is further highlighted by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust and horizontal social networks, tend to have institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions is also evident in the historical context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that exogenous cultural theories, such as those proposed by Max Weber, provide a framework for understanding how cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions. For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, which in turn affected the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shows that cultural beliefs can have a direct impact on the institutional landscape and, consequently, on economic growth. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is also recognized by studies of European economic institutions, which highlight the importance of looking at entire institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The cultural approach to understanding economic institutions is further supported by the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the long-term impact of cultural and institutional factors on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is aligned with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, particularly in the context of transaction costs. North points out that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of growth, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. With positive transaction costs, institutions become crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutions that reduce transaction costs and promote economic efficiency.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. This, in turn, facilitated economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The interdependency of institutions is evident in the parallel development of a market for public debt and a market for private debt, both of which were critical for England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This highlights how cultural factors, such as trust and social networks, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability.

This highlights the long-term impact of cultural factors on economic institutions and performance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of mistrust in Africa today underscores the enduring impact of cultural factors on economic performance.
