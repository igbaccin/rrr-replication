The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development. At stake is the explanation of persistent economic disparities between regions and the identification of the most effective pathways to sustainable growth. A key disagreement centers around whether cultural factors directly influence institutional development and economic performance, or whether institutions evolve independently of cultural influences. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values shape the economic institutions that underpin growth, while others posit that institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors such as geography or technological advancements. A third group contends that cultural factors are antecedents that trigger institutional changes, rather than direct causes of economic growth. This debate underscores the need to unpack the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic development to better understand the drivers of long-term growth. The question of whether cultural factors are the dominant influence on institutions, or merely a complementary force, remains unresolved. Culture and ideas were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, as McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) both argue. The economic growth that occurred in this period was not merely a result of institutional changes driven by elite interests, but rather a consequence of a shift in ideologies that valued commerce and innovation. McCloskey describes this shift as a "new rhetoric" that fostered a change in circumstances, which in turn reinforced the new rhetoric (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth, noting that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was a critical factor in the Great Enrichment (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This knowledge was not just codified in texts, but also embodied in tacit skills and practices that were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that the spread of ideas and ideologies was facilitated by the mobility of people and the ease of communication, which allowed for the rapid dissemination of both explicit and tacit knowledge. This process was not uniform across Europe, as different regions had varying levels of openness to new ideas and practices. For instance, Mokyr notes that the diffusion of knowledge was more pronounced in regions where there was a greater emphasis on practical application and innovation. This regional variation in the adoption of new ideas and practices highlights the complex interplay between culture, knowledge, and economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which are fundamental to economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This institutional change provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The secure property rights and reduced transaction costs fostered by these institutions created an environment conducive to long-term capital investment and economic expansion. The pace and decisiveness of this growth were contingent on the specific institutional arrangements that emerged. The financial revolution in England, for example, was a pivotal moment that accelerated economic growth by providing a stable and secure financial system (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, characterized by secure property rights and efficient financial markets, laid the groundwork for sustained economic development.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) posit that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. The mortality rates of European settlers influenced the type of institutions implemented in the colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional framework, characterized by extractive policies, persisted over time and shaped the economic trajectory of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17) show that there is a high correlation between settler mortality and the establishment of these institutions, which in turn have a significant impact on current income per capita. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, had a profound and lasting effect on the economic development of these regions. This perspective highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, even as other factors may also play a role in shaping economic outcomes.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency or cultural factors, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights the role of power dynamics and the interests of elites in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie posits that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were primarily driven by the struggle between different social groups to secure their interests. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is evident in the way institutions were structured to maintain the power of elites, even at the expense of overall economic efficiency. The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding the long-run impact of institutions on economic growth. Ogilvie acknowledges the complex interdependencies within institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This recognition underscores the need to consider institutions as part of a broader system, rather than in isolation, when evaluating their impact on economic development.

The economic development and structural changes in Europe during specific historical periods, such as the impact of the Black Death on wages and institutions, and the Industrious Revolution preceding the Industrial Revolution, underscore the complex interplay between demographic shifts, institutional adaptations, and economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.24). The Black Death, for instance, led to significant labor shortages, which in turn drove up wages and altered institutional frameworks, particularly in regions like northwest Europe. The Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, further catalyzed economic growth by releasing labor reserves to the market and yielding practical functions to market-supplied services (de Vries 1994, p.18). This period saw a transformation in urban networks, establishing a framework for regional economic development that could support industrial growth (de Vries 1994, p.5). The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living during this period was not straightforward. For example, the development of silver wages reflected economic development in various parts of Europe, but this relationship was complex and did not always translate into continuous growth in GDP per capita (van Zanden 1999, p.6). The economic development and structural changes in Europe during these periods highlight the multifaceted nature of long-run growth, where cultural norms and institutional adaptations play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

The literature on institutional reforms and the economic history of Africa identifies the role of institutional variables as pivotal in explaining development and poverty, with a particular focus on the gradual nature of institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Bryant 2006, p.35). Institutions, in this view, are deeply rooted in historical contexts that extend beyond the colonial period, into precolonial and even prehistorical times. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that effective institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions. This congruence underpins the legitimacy of governments and enhances their capacity to foster development-inducing institutions. Conversely, low levels of congruence can lead to patrimonial politics, regionalism, and corruption, which hinder economic development. Bryant (2006, p.35) underscores the importance of cultural norms and social relations, arguing that transitions to industrial modes of production are enabled by accumulated resources and opportunities from the past. This perspective highlights that cultural and institutional legacies are crucial for understanding long-run growth. This implies that while institutional reforms are essential, their effectiveness is contingent on a deeper understanding of the cultural and historical contexts in which they operate.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Stern (2004, p.3) both focus on how technological innovations and environmental changes influence economic development, rather than addressing the role of culture in long-run growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Stern 2004, p.3). They argue that these factors can significantly alter economic development trajectories by reshaping resource allocation and productivity. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains enabled farmers to protect their property rights and reduce livestock damage, thereby enhancing agricultural development. This technological innovation allowed farmers to better control their resources and mitigate externalities, demonstrating how technological advancements can drive economic growth by improving efficiency and productivity. In a similar vein, Stern argues that shifts in industrial output mix during economic development can lead to changes in pollution intensities. Early stages of development see a shift from agriculture to heavy industry, increasing emissions, while later stages witness a transition to services and lighter manufacturing, which have lower emissions per unit of output. This environmental dimension further illustrates how technological and environmental factors can influence economic development. However, Stern also notes the potential for economic growth to eventually improve environmental conditions, suggesting a nuanced relationship between these factors and long-run growth. This perspective highlights the importance of considering both technological innovations and environmental changes in understanding economic development, even if these factors do not directly address the role of culture.
