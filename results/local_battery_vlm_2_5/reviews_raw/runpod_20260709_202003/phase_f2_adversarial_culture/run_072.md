The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography. At stake is understanding whether cultural factors, which shape institutions, are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geographic determinants, play a more significant role. Scholars have argued that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. For instance, some contend that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, and that the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline, thus showing that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions. Others have proposed that institutions matter in economic growth, and that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This debate highlights the need to understand the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Institutions played a key role in reducing transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) further clarify that the institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England, shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, began private operations. This development underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth. North (1989, p.1) observes that the neoclassical model, which has been the foundation of economic reasoning for most scholars, is valid only under the stringent assumption of zero transaction costs. This assumption overlooks the role of institutions in economic growth, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between institutions and economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that women have played a pivotal role in economic growth and development, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial Africa and in the United States during the 20th century. Their work underscores the significant contributions of women to economic development, highlighting how cultural norms and gender roles shaped economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack show that the sexual division of labor established during the colonial period in Africa continues to influence gender production, reflecting the enduring impact of colonial constructs on economic dynamics. In the U.S., Goldin (2006, p.8) illustrates that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics, driven by shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's roles in the workforce. Exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and advancements in educational institutions catalyzed these changes. This transformation had profound implications for women's economic participation and life satisfaction, as well as for broader economic growth. The role of women in economic development varies across regions and time periods. While Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) highlight the indispensable role of women in Akan state formation and community reproduction, Goldin (2006, p.3) points out that institutional changes, such as the availability of part-time work and the removal of barriers to married women's employment, were crucial in altering female labor supply. These findings suggest that cultural norms and institutional changes interact in complex ways to shape women's economic contributions, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of culture in economic growth and development.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift was driven by the long-term political instability resulting from the slave trades, which weakened and fragmented states. The political fragmentation that ensued led to a lack of central authority and weakened institutions, which in turn fostered a culture of mistrust. The resulting political instability and weakened states further hindered economic growth by creating an environment where long-term investments were risky and less likely to be made. The legacy of this mistrust has had far-reaching consequences for economic development, social cohesion, and governance. The impact of the slave trade on trust levels is evident in contemporary African societies, where low levels of trust continue to impede development. For example, the lack of trust in institutions and fellow citizens can deter cooperation and collective action, which are crucial for economic growth. Furthermore, the mistrust engendered by the slave trade can exacerbate corruption, as individuals may be less likely to trust and support institutions that are meant to enforce rules and regulations. This dynamic can create a vicious cycle where mistrust leads to weakened institutions, which in turn reinforces mistrust. In this context, the historical legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African societies by perpetuating a culture of mistrust that hinders economic and social development.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share the finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These conflicts can determine the structure of institutions by influencing how resources and power are distributed within a society. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions, in turn, reinforced the power of elites and perpetuated inequality. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that the persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the resistance of elites to changes that might undermine their dominant position. This resistance is a mechanism by which distributional conflicts maintain the status quo, even when more efficient institutions are available. The downstream implication of this mechanism is that the persistence of inequality can be self-reinforcing, as those in power use their influence to maintain institutions that favor their interests. This dynamic is further qualified by the observation that the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework can make it difficult to implement changes that might disrupt the existing power structure. This pattern is evident in pre-industrial England, where the community-based welfare system was interdependent with other institutions, creating a framework that furthered economic growth but also reinforced existing inequalities (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which persist in shaping current economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The causal link between colonial institutions and economic performance is strengthened by the observation that European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, led to the establishment of institutions that either fostered or hindered growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The persistence of these institutional legacies indicates that the reversal of fortune is a consequence of enduring institutional frameworks shaped by historical events, not just initial geographic conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further support this by showing that institutions introduced during colonialism played a crucial role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, thereby accounting for significant income differences today. Austin (2008, p.25) notes that the African experience suggests a dynamic and endogenous process, where Africans' responses to their environments also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. This dynamic interplay between institutional legacies and endogenous responses underscores the complexity of the reversal of fortune, indicating that while colonial institutions set the stage, local adaptations and responses also significantly influenced economic trajectories.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping the institutions that drive long-term economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions underscores the enduring impact of cultural and institutional legacies on economic outcomes. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks is further illustrated by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical evidence supports the idea that cultural factors, by shaping institutional frameworks, can have profound and lasting effects on economic development.

The dynamic relationship between culture and institutions is also evident in the African context. Austin highlights that the African experience suggests a more dynamic and endogenous process, where Africans' responses to their environments also played a role in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack, who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The role of women in economic development is a critical aspect of the broader cultural framework that influences institutional outcomes. This shared finding (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Austin 2008, p.25) illustrates the importance of considering cultural factors in understanding the long-run growth trajectories of different regions.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture plays a crucial role in shaping institutions that drive long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the influence of culture is exogenous or endogenous. Austin shows that Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of how African women's contributions to economic growth reflect the dynamic and endogenous nature of cultural influences on institutional outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).
