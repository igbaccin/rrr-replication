The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The debate centers around whether cultural factors, such as norms and beliefs, shape institutions that drive economic growth, or whether institutions are primarily influenced by exogenous factors like geography or technology. One position posits that cultural norms and beliefs, shaped by historical experiences such as the slave trade, can persist and influence contemporary economic outcomes. This view emphasizes the enduring impact of cultural legacies on institutional development and economic performance. Another position argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, with cultural factors playing a secondary or even negligible role. This view suggests that institutions, once established, can foster economic growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. The disagreement revolves around the relative importance of cultural factors versus institutional frameworks in determining long-run economic growth. This issue is crucial for understanding the dynamics of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to promote growth. The resolution of this debate could significantly influence economic theory and policy-making, particularly in regions with a history of institutional underdevelopment. The evidence from different streams of scholarship reveals a complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They focus on the role of the financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, and its impact on reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This financial revolution was a pivotal moment that facilitated economic growth and ensured political freedom. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn fostered the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The secure property rights and reduced transaction costs enabled by these institutions were fundamental to the long-term economic success of England. By creating a stable environment for investment and economic activity, these institutional changes laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in driving economic development, highlighting how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can catalyze growth. North (1989, p.9) also notes that the decline in transaction costs was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, indicating that external pressures can also play a role in shaping institutional evolution. This interplay between institutional changes and external pressures reveals the complex dynamics that underpin economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and knowledge were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective posits that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in fostering growth. McCloskey notes that the elite's acquisition of information about better institutions stemmed from a broader cultural struggle, which he refers to as the 'Franco-French War' (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This cultural dynamic was instrumental in driving economic change, as it led to the development of new rhetoric, which in turn shaped new circumstances. Mokyr elaborates on this idea by highlighting the role of useful knowledge in economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within the Western world was a key factor in the economic enrichment of the period. This diffusion was facilitated by various means, including periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr further notes that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practices that were transmitted through personal training and communication (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge was crucial for economic growth, as it complemented codified knowledge and was essential for the development of new technologies and practices. The implication of this mechanism is that the dissemination of ideas and knowledge, both codified and tacit, was a critical factor in driving economic growth during this period. This perspective underscores the importance of cultural dynamics in shaping economic development, highlighting how the dissemination of ideas and knowledge can catalyze growth.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental to long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates in colonies determined the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize colonial rents. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism involves the establishment of institutions that reflect the interests of the colonizing power. For instance, Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term gains over long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, designed to extract resources efficiently, hinders economic growth by failing to encourage investment and innovation. The downstream implication is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic performance, as these institutions are resistant to change and perpetuate the extractive practices of the colonial era. Acemoglu et al. also acknowledge that the impact of these institutions is not uniform across all colonies, suggesting that other factors, such as local resistance or post-colonial policies, may modify their effects (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is indirectly addressed by examining how different historical periods and paths influence economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share the view that the impact of institutions on economic growth is period-specific and influenced by historical contexts. Specifically, Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the course of development. This dynamic interaction between environment and societal responses is crucial for understanding how institutions evolve over time. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) add that the differentials in paths of development are of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have attracted attention from economic historians and economists more generally. They emphasize that conventional economic factors have not been ignored, but the explanations offered for the contrasting records in growth have most often focused on institutions and highlighted the variation across societies in conditions relevant to growth. One downstream implication of this focus on institutions is that the persistence of inequality is influenced by the institutions that evolved in response to different environmental and historical contexts.

Broadberry and Gupta, together with van Zanden (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2), argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This early modern great divergence is marked by significant differences in economic development between north-western Europe and Asia, with Europe experiencing higher levels of investment in agriculture and industry. This investment led to increased agricultural labor productivity and economic growth in Europe, a pattern not mirrored in Asia. Van Zanden highlights that the economic development of various parts of Europe, including the role of silver wages, reflected broader economic trends (van Zanden 1999, p.6). Van Zanden also notes that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living is complex, challenging simple interpretations of early modern economic growth. For example, the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture, as noted by Parthasarathi, was driven by significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This investment led to high levels of economic development and low silver wages, illustrating the intricate interplay between economic factors and long-term growth. Despite these early economic differences, the role of culture in shaping long-run growth remains a critical area for further investigation.

The slave trade's impact on African development illustrates how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic outcomes by reducing the efficacy of economic transactions and the willingness to engage in collective action. The slave trade also had a profound impact on state centralization, leading to long-term political instability and weakened states. This political instability further hindered economic development, as states became fragmented and unable to provide the necessary infrastructure and institutions for growth. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability continues to affect Africa's economic development, as these factors hinder the establishment of effective institutions and the implementation of economic policies. The negative impact of the slave trade on African development highlights the importance of understanding the long-term effects of historical events on cultural attitudes and economic outcomes.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, emphasizing the necessity of institutions in facilitating economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The legacy of historical events, such as the slave trade, continues to influence cultural attitudes and economic outcomes in Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust hinders the establishment of effective institutions and the implementation of economic policies, thereby affecting Africa's economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The negative impact of the slave trade on African development highlights the importance of understanding the long-term effects of historical events on cultural attitudes and economic outcomes. These effects are evident in Africa's current underdevelopment, as the slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability continues to affect economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the differences in European mortality rates and their impact on colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and European settlements, as well as between European settlements and early measures of institutions, underscores the enduring influence of historical factors on institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of understanding the long-term effects of historical events on cultural attitudes and economic outcomes.

The persistence of inequality in New World societies is another example of how culture shapes institutions and economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The differentials in paths of development in Latin America and other regions have long been of central concern to scholars, who have highlighted the variation across societies in conditions relevant to growth such as the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These differences in institutions raise the challenge of explaining where the differences in institutions come from, underscoring the importance of understanding the role of culture in shaping institutions and economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which culture shapes institutions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, which highlights Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available (Austin 2008, p.25).
