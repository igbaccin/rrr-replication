The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental question: do cultural values and beliefs shape institutions in ways that drive long-term economic growth, or are institutions shaped primarily by other factors, such as geographic conditions or political dynamics? Two prominent positions exist. Some scholars argue that culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, influencing economic outcomes through norms, values, and social capital. Others contend that institutions are more directly shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates or colonial policies, which in turn determine economic performance. This debate is critical because it informs our understanding of the underlying mechanisms that drive economic growth and development. Understanding the role of culture in institutional development can help policymakers design more effective strategies for fostering economic growth. The stakes are high: misdiagnosing the causes of economic stagnation can lead to ineffective policies, perpetuating poverty and underdevelopment. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are shaped by cultural values, suggesting instead that they are the result of power struggles and economic interests. The implications of this view are significant, as it shifts the focus away from cultural factors and towards the political and economic dynamics that shape institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as proposed by Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), are not merely exogenous but are influenced by the complex interplay of cultural and economic factors. These institutional changes were driven by European settlers' responses to local conditions, particularly mortality rates, which shaped the type of institutions established. The high mortality rates in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective contrasts with the view that culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. The institutional framework established by colonial powers, influenced by settler mortality and the resulting institutional design, has persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that the African story indicates a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus complicated by the endogenous responses of colonized populations to their environments.

The drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, as argued by McCloskey and Mokyr, were deeply influenced by ideas and ideologies, which suggests that the role of culture in shaping institutions is more nuanced than previously thought (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective is anchored in the notion that the growth of useful knowledge was a pivotal force in economic development. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the importance of tacit knowledge, which was often transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge was crucial for technological advancements and economic growth. McCloskey emphasizes that the cultural struggle and ideological shifts were essential in creating new circumstances that facilitated economic growth. For instance, he points out that in countries like France, the cultural and ideological environment was more conducive to economic growth due to the new rhetoric that emerged from the bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries, such as the Netherlands and Britain (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This new rhetoric was a significant factor in the Great Enrichment. The interplay between ideas, ideologies, and tacit knowledge thus created a dynamic environment where economic growth thrived. de Vries adds a nuanced perspective by emphasizing the Industrious Revolution, which preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This Industrious Revolution was characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, which were driven by the same ideas and ideologies that McCloskey and Mokyr highlight (de Vries 1994, p.18). This suggests that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is more complex than a straightforward institutional determinism.

The role of distributional conflicts, as highlighted by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman, suggests that the economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by power dynamics rather than cultural factors alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective highlights that the institutions that emerged in pre-industrial Europe were largely a result of conflicts over the distribution of resources and power. These institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were deeply influenced by the struggle between different social groups. For example, the craft guilds and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe were structured in ways that reflected the power dynamics between artisans, merchants, and other economic actors. This dynamic is evident in the way these guilds regulated entry, prices, and production, often to the detriment of efficiency but to the advantage of those in power. The institutional framework that emerged from these conflicts was not always conducive to economic growth. For instance, the guilds' restrictive practices could stifle innovation and competition, which are key drivers of economic development. The persistence of these institutions suggests that they served important functions for the dominant groups. This perspective challenges the notion that culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth by highlighting the role of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions. The interdependencies between different institutions further complicate this picture. Ogilvie contends that institutions are interdependent within a broader framework, and studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. For example, the European family system was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, all of which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency means that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is part of a complex web of interactions. This complexity is further underscored by the fact that the institutional frameworks that emerged from these conflicts could have both positive and negative effects on economic growth. While some institutions, such as those that facilitated the accumulation of capital, could promote growth, others, such as those that restricted competition, could hinder it. Therefore, the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is multifaceted and requires a nuanced understanding of the broader institutional framework.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their argument is grounded in the notion that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn reflected increased security of property rights. This security was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that emerged during this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, which was crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The role of these institutions in fostering economic growth involved creating a stable market for public debt, which had broader implications for the economy.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) show. This mistrust, stemming from the historical trauma of the slave trade, has enduring effects on economic development. The legacy of the slave trade includes weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, has been a barrier to economic growth. The slave trade's impact on state centralization is critical, as recent research highlights the importance of state development in determining current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this by noting that slavery can be seen as a source of precolonial origins for modern corruption (Nunn 2008, p.1). This corruption, in turn, undermines the very institutions that are crucial for fostering economic growth. The historical trauma of the slave trade thus has a cascading effect, from mistrust to political instability to corruption, all of which hinder economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and other scholars in this cluster (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2) suggest that economic development and divergence between Europe and Asia were already significant by 1800, driven by factors such as urbanization and institutional changes. Pamuk (2007, p.24) builds on this by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, which contributed to the rise of rural industries (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Pamuk (2007, p.24) further qualifies this by showing that the Black Death ushered in an era of labour-saving technological innovations, which likely exacerbated these regional disparities (Pamuk 2007, p.5). The economic divergence between Europe and Asia thus involved complex interactions between demographic shocks, institutional changes, and technological advancements, shaping the long-run economic trajectories of these regions.

The influence of institutional reforms on African economic trajectories has been a central theme in the literature. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) share the finding that African economies were significantly shaped by historical economic patterns and institutional changes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This finding is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who argues that institutional variables are crucial in explaining development in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further contends that effective institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions, enhancing government legitimacy and fostering development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This alignment is particularly important in regions with a strong precolonial history, where contemporary institutions must be congruent with historical ones to avoid regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. The importance of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions highlights the nuanced role of historical legacies in shaping current economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutional reforms and historical economic patterns in Africa, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these dynamics could inform more effective economic policies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) highlight the pivotal role of women in economic growth and development, particularly during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that African women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities, with their bloodlines defining the transmission of office and wealth in matrilineal societies. This dynamic is evident in Akan state formations, where women's contributions were crucial to community survival and economic development. The significance of women's roles in these societies underscores the importance of cultural and social norms in shaping economic outcomes. Goldin (2006, p.3) extends this perspective by examining the transformations in women's employment and education in the U.S., noting that exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions altered female labor supply. These shifts were part of a quiet revolution that transformed women's roles in the economy. The persistence of gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) suggests that these transformations may be contingent on specific cultural and historical contexts. The long-term consequences of these gender dynamics highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs and social norms influence economic development.

Bryant (2006, p.35) and van Zanden (2009, p.24) share a focus on long-term economic performance and the skill premium (Bryant 2006, p.35; van Zanden 2009, p.24). Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that the transition to industrial modes of production is underpinned by a causal connectedness that accumulates through prior developments, including cultural norms. Van Zanden (2009, p.24) supports this by noting that the skill premium is linked to labor productivity in agriculture, which is in turn connected to population growth. This implies that cultural factors, such as those governing labor productivity and population dynamics, play a crucial role in shaping the skill premium and, consequently, long-term economic performance. van Zanden (2009, p.24) also highlights the protective mechanisms in the urban sector that can shield against declines in rural real wages, indicating that the impact of cultural factors on the skill premium is not uniform across different sectors of the economy. This nuanced relationship between culture and the skill premium underscores the need for a more detailed examination of how cultural beliefs and social norms influence economic development, particularly in the context of long-term growth and the skill premium.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal in understanding long-run economic growth. Institutions, as defined by North, are the rules of the game, including formal rules and informal constraints that shape human interaction (North 1989, p.1). Cultural beliefs and values significantly influence these institutions, as seen in Putnam's theory of social capital, which highlights how trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence extends beyond micro-level behaviors to macro-level economic outcomes. For instance, the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, as discussed by Ogilvie, underscores how cultural norms can shape entire institutional systems, such as the European family system and community-based welfare systems, which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interplay between culture and institutions is further evidenced by the role of the Bank of England in fostering secure property rights, which North and Weingast argue was critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The historical evidence shows that cultural factors have directly influenced the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as the financial revolution in England. This revolution, characterized by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, in turn, facilitated long-term economic growth by providing a stable market for public and private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions is also evident in the role of African women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods were shaped by cultural norms and gender roles (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). These cultural factors not only influenced economic outcomes but also had long-term consequences for gender production and the sexual division of labor (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This synthesis shows that cultural beliefs and social norms are fundamental in shaping institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement concerns the relative importance of cultural factors versus other determinants, such as geographic or climatic conditions. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across different historical periods and regions, which reveals the enduring impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions and long-term growth.
