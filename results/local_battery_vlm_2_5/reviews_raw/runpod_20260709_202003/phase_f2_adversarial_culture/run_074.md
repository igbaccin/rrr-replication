The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic growth. This debate centers on two main positions: those who argue that cultural factors are crucial in determining institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, and those who contend that other factors, such as geographical or institutional determinants, play a more significant role. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with some scholars emphasizing the direct impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, while others focus on the indirect effects through institutional frameworks. The resolution of this debate has implications for understanding the historical trajectories of different regions and the potential paths for economic development. Cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions by influencing the structure of collective action games, as seen in the case of guilds in medieval Europe. This influence can have long-term effects on economic performance, as demonstrated by the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy, attributed to differences in cultural beliefs and associated institutional manifestations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that these changes had a profound effect on current economic performance, with the nature of institutions imposed by colonial powers being a key determinant. This dynamic is evident in the correlation between settler mortality and the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) present evidence that high settler mortality rates led to the implementation of institutions that maximized rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-run growth. This institutional framework, designed to extract resources, has persisted and continues to influence economic performance today. Austin (2008, p.25) qualifies this perspective by highlighting the dynamic and endogenous nature of African responses to their environments. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role, the interaction between these institutions and local adaptations must be considered for a complete understanding of economic development. The complexity of this interaction underscores the need for a nuanced approach to studying the long-run effects of colonial institutions on economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts, not cultural factors, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. They propose that these conflicts arose from the unequal distribution of resources and power, leading to institutions that favored the elite at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This perspective is illustrated by the evolution of institutions in New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to institutions that reinforced this inequality. For instance, in societies with high inequality, elites used their political power to implement policies that restricted competition and maintained their privileged status, thereby perpetuating inequality over time. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for long-run economic growth. Ogilvie qualifies this perspective by emphasizing the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor, the overall institutional framework, including cultural and efficiency considerations, must also be considered to fully understand economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) report, “Economists traditionally emphasize the pervasive influence of factor endowment, so the qualitative thrust of this approach may not be entirely novel (Baldwin, 1956; Lewis, 1955; Domar, 1970).”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was pivotal in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. The role of institutions in this context is underscored by the fact that the financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further cementing the economic benefits of secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). However, the impact of these institutions was not merely economic; they also played a necessary role in fostering political freedom, highlighting the interconnected nature of economic and political institutions in long-term development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust, fostered by the historical trauma of slave trade, has had profound effects on African societies, shaping contemporary social norms and economic interactions. For example, the slave trade caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability has been shown to correlate with current economic performance, as countries with a history of state development tend to have higher real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). This finding suggests that the legacy of the slave trade, through its impact on state centralization and political stability, continues to influence economic outcomes in Africa. The relationship between the slave trade and mistrust is complex. The malaria transmission index, which provides a close approximation to historical conditions, suggests that the environmental factors may also play a role in shaping trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This indicates that while the slave trade is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of contemporary trust levels in Africa.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by institutional changes and urbanization. This, in turn, facilitated the movement of labor from agriculture to industry and services, which is a pattern that is absent in the southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe. These economic changes in Europe were accompanied by urbanization and the rise of cities like London, which became economic hubs. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) corroborates this pattern, noting that the development of silver wages in Europe reflected broader economic trends. Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this divergence by highlighting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes. These changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to the guilds, contributed to the rise of rural industries and labor-saving technological innovations. This suggests that while institutional changes and urbanization were key drivers of economic development in Europe, external shocks like the Black Death also played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) share a focus on how historical economic patterns and institutional reforms have shaped African economies. They highlight the significance of institutions in driving economic development, with Broadberry and Gardner emphasizing that African economies, especially Anglophone ones, had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further supports this perspective by noting that effective institutions, especially those congruent with indigenous ones, enhance economic growth. This congruence underlines the legitimacy of governments and fosters development-inducing institutions. However, Hopkins also points out that low levels of congruence can lead to patrimonial politics, regionalism, and corruption, which hinder economic progress. This suggests that while institutional reforms are crucial, their effectiveness depends on their alignment with preexisting cultural and historical contexts.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that precede and facilitate economic growth but do not directly address culture as the fundamental cause (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). De Vries (1994, p.18) demonstrates that the Industrious Revolution involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns, which laid the groundwork for the Industrial Revolution. This period saw a significant shift in economic behavior, with households releasing labor reserves to the market and adopting new consumption patterns. This knowledge was not confined to written records but also included skills and expertise transmitted through personal interactions. The diffusion of this knowledge facilitated economic growth by enhancing productivity and innovation. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies the impact of codified knowledge by noting that tacit knowledge remained essential, highlighting the importance of personal transmission and training in the dissemination of useful knowledge. This underscores the complexity of economic growth, where both formal and informal knowledge systems play critical roles. The interplay between increased labor input, new consumption patterns, and the growth of useful knowledge created a fertile ground for economic development, even if the cultural underpinnings of these phenomena remain less explored.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8) identify significant contributions by women to economic growth and development, both in pre-colonial and colonial Africa and in the U.S. during the 20th century. In Africa, women played indispensable roles in the reproduction of communities, particularly in matrilineal societies like Asante, where their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth. This dynamic highlights the economic and social value of women's roles, which were integral to the functioning and growth of these societies. In the U.S., a quiet revolution transformed women's employment and education, driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions. These shifts altered female labor supply and challenged traditional social norms concerning women's family and career roles. However, Goldin (2006, p.3) qualifies the pace of this transformation, noting that institutional changes and technological advancements in household production also played crucial roles. This underscores that while women's contributions to economic growth were substantial, the underlying mechanisms and conditions varied across different historical and cultural contexts.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie highlights that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth due to the positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illustrated by Austin, who notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This indicates that cultural factors, along with institutional frameworks, are integral to understanding long-run economic growth. The Bank of England's financial revolution, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions is also evident in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, entire institutional frameworks must be considered. The significance of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions is further supported by Austin's observation that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Austin 2008, p.25).

This agreement is grounded in the recognition that institutions play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
