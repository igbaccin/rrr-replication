The central question of long-run economic growth remains a contentious issue among scholars. The debate centers on whether institutions dominate the growth process, and if so, what role culture plays in shaping these institutions. The literature presents a live disagreement about the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth: some scholars argue that culture directly influences the establishment and persistence of institutions that drive economic growth, while others contend that institutions evolve independently of cultural factors. Some scholars propose that culture shapes institutions through historical and social processes, while others argue that institutions are primarily shaped by economic and political factors. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a critical question because it influences how we understand the mechanisms of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. Understanding the interplay between culture and institutions is essential for devising effective strategies to promote economic growth in diverse cultural contexts. This interplay can be understood through examining the historical determinants of institutions and the cultural factors that influence their evolution. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The role of women in economic growth highlights how cultural norms and social dynamics, particularly gender relations, significantly influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, with gender relations established during these times having long-term consequences for gender production. This dynamic is evident in matrilineal societies like Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth. The role of women in economic growth is further underscored by Goldin's analysis of the U.S., where shifts in women's employment, education, and family dynamics were driven by changes in social norms and expectations regarding future work. These changes represented a quiet revolution that transformed women's roles in the economy. The increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century and the growth of educational institutions from the 1910s to the 1940s are key factors that altered female labor supply and made "nice" jobs available. However, changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also played a significant role, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women. This pattern suggests that cultural and social dynamics, including gender relations, are crucial in shaping long-run economic development.

The slave trade's impact on African economic development underscores the enduring influence of historical events on shaping cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability has been particularly detrimental to Africa's economic development. This political instability and lack of state centralization have had profound effects on economic performance, as evidenced by the positive correlation between state antiquity and real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). The persistence of this mistrust varies across Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon note that while the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is widespread, its effects are moderated by other factors, such as contemporary economic conditions and recent historical events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This suggests that while the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is a significant factor in Africa's economic underdevelopment, it is not the sole determinant.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional evolution is closely tied to the development of financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, not only facilitated economic growth but also played a pivotal role in the development of a stable market for public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This, in turn, provided a significant positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, fostering a more robust economic environment. The institutions that emerged during this period were instrumental in creating a stable framework for economic activities, which was essential for long-term growth. North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the financial revolution was not merely a response to economic conditions but a critical factor in shaping them, underscoring the importance of institutions in economic development. The impact of these institutions varied across different regions or historical periods. The stability and effectiveness of these institutions varied, and their influence on economic growth was contingent on the broader historical and political context. This historical contingency underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, highlighting that while institutions are crucial, their effects are mediated by broader historical and political factors.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism's institutional changes are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates is a key mechanism through which colonialism influenced economic growth. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the correlation between mortality rates, European settlements, and current institutions is strong. The institutional framework introduced by colonial powers, therefore, had a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectories of former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic development, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on growth is not straightforward.

The new economic history of Africa focuses on institutional reforms as a means to alleviate poverty, but it does not explicitly address how culture might dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective is shared by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), who argue that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 look similar to the stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe rather than the developing north-western parts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). The implication is that the historical trajectory of institutional development in Africa, shaped by both colonial and precolonial influences, plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of current institutional reforms. The role of culture in shaping these institutions is not explicitly addressed. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective in promoting growth if they are congruent with indigenous institutions, highlighting the importance of cultural congruence in institutional effectiveness (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary, their success in promoting long-run growth may depend on how well they align with the cultural context of the societies they aim to transform.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) examine how different historical paths and institutional frameworks have influenced economic development, particularly focusing on inequality. They jointly establish that the historical institutional frameworks of a society can be traced to its unique, dynamic responses to environmental and technological conditions. The historical path of a society's institutional development is a product of how societies adapt to their environments over time. This suggests that the historical institutional frameworks of a society are not merely a function of static geographical factors, but rather a product of how societies adapt to their environments over time, ultimately shaping their economic development. This dynamic relationship between historical paths and institutional frameworks can lead to persistent inequality, as seen in the New World societies where greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies.

Cultural factors play a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17) show that European settlement patterns, influenced by mortality rates, led to the establishment of institutions that persistently affect current income levels. These historical institutional frameworks, shaped by cultural responses to environmental challenges, have enduring effects on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.25) argues that the impact of institutions on economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that the relationship between culture, institutions, and economic development is dynamic and evolving. This dynamic is further illustrated by North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9), who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and secure property rights, which are crucial for long-term economic development.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) demonstrate that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in England's long-run success. This period saw the development of new financial instruments that dramatically declined the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights. The implications of this are significant, as the institutions established during this period provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, thereby fostering economic growth.

Cultural beliefs and social norms also significantly influence economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects social capital and interpersonal trust, which are essential for economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4; 2014, p.6) further illustrate this point by showing that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their role in the reproduction of communities and the transmission of wealth highlights the importance of cultural beliefs and social norms in shaping economic development.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus multifaceted. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is that the impact of institutions on economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). The historical evidence of the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, settles this disagreement by showing that the institutions established during this period provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, thereby fostering economic growth.
