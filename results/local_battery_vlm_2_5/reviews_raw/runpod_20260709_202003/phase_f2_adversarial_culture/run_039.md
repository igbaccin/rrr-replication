The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is the fundamental question of what causes societies to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political stability. One stream of thought posits that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. This perspective suggests that cultural factors can shape collective action and motivate individuals to adhere to rules that promote economic growth. A competing perspective posits that cultural impact is overshadowed by other factors, including geographic conditions and the extractive nature of institutions imposed by colonial powers. These rival explanations often downplay the role of culture, focusing instead on exogenous shocks and institutional frameworks that directly influence economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are merely epiphenomenal or if they are fundamental drivers of institutional change and economic growth. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the historical trajectories of different societies and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic development. A key part of this debate is how cultural beliefs interact with institutional frameworks to either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The institutions governing public choice and property rights are critical to economic growth because they reduce transaction costs, enabling specialization and division of labor. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast posit that the institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). While the financial revolution and the development of new institutions were pivotal, it is important to note that the evolution of these institutions was not inevitable but rather a product of specific historical and political contexts.

Ogilvie contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, which highlights that institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, demonstrating that institutional frameworks are influenced by the distribution of power and resources rather than cultural beliefs alone. Ogilvie emphasizes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework must be considered to understand the evolution of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that the institutional frameworks that emerged in pre-industrial Europe were not isolated phenomena but were intricately linked to the broader social and political contexts. Ogilvie's framework, which emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts, may not fully account for the nuanced ways in which cultural factors can interact with institutional development. This interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a more holistic understanding of the factors that drive long-run growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were ideas and ideologies, not institutional frameworks. This perspective highlights the importance of intellectual shifts in driving economic growth. The "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" like the Netherlands and France, for example, fostered new rhetoric that generated new circumstances, which in turn created a feedback loop that facilitated the Great Enrichment, a period marked by sustained economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr's work on the growth of useful knowledge complements McCloskey's ideas, emphasizing that the diffusion of knowledge across nations was a key factor in economic development. Mokyr argues that the transmission of knowledge, both codified and tacit, was crucial for economic growth, as it allowed for the widespread adoption of new technologies and practices (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This diffusion was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, all of which contributed to the spread of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.56). However, Mokyr also notes that the codification of knowledge was a function of the technology and costs of codification, as well as the payoff to efforts to do so, indicating that the process of knowledge diffusion was not straightforward and required significant investment (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial drivers of economic change, the practical implementation of these ideas required substantial resources and infrastructure.

The complexity of economic growth and technological advance is influenced by various exogenous factors and social dynamics, but the role of culture as a dominant factor in long-run growth is not explicitly addressed in this cluster (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz and Austin both underscore the multifaceted nature of economic growth, highlighting that it is not solely determined by institutional frameworks or geographical factors. Austin, similarly, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating that economic development is shaped by a dynamic interplay of environmental responses and technological adaptations (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interplay suggests that cultural factors, which influence how societies respond to their environments and adopt technologies, play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific mechanisms through which culture influences these processes remain under-explored in this cluster. Austin notes that African responses to their environments, utilizing various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic, partly endogenous approach to economic development highlights the importance of cultural adaptations in shaping economic outcomes. The implication is that cultural factors, by influencing how societies adapt to their environments and technologies, can significantly impact long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The mortality rates faced by European colonizers significantly influenced the type of institutions established in their colonies. High mortality rates led to the implementation of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, shaped by the initial conditions of European settlement, persisted over time and had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the strong correlation between early institutions and current institutions, which in turn significantly affects income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The legacy of these extractive institutions is evident in the economic performance of former colonies, where the institutional framework continues to hinder economic development. The implication is that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a lasting impact on the economic growth of former colonies, shaping their economic trajectories in profound ways.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and impacts long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Both authors highlight that the slave trade weakened state institutions and fostered political instability, which in turn hindered economic growth. The slave trade disrupted traditional political structures, leading to fragmentation and a lack of centralized authority. This institutional decay is a key mechanism through which the slave trade affected economic development. The weakened state institutions were unable to provide the necessary infrastructure and security for economic activities to thrive. This institutional legacy is compounded by the cultural mistrust that developed as a result of the slave trade. The persistence of this mistrust undermines social capital, which is crucial for economic development. The slave trade's impact on African economic development is thus multifaceted, involving both institutional decay and cultural shifts that continue to affect economic outcomes today.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Culture, as a determinant of social capital, influences the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Shared findings (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27) show that cultural beliefs and values, particularly those related to trust and reciprocity, can shape institutions that foster economic growth. For example, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how civic cultures encourage institutions that promote economic development and political accountability. This aligns with the argument that trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, thereby improving societal efficiency. Similarly, the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the European family system, labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interlinked, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. In this context, cultural values and institutions work together to drive economic growth. The historical evidence of different institutional frameworks shows that cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, are integral to the economic performance of societies.

The impact of culture on institutions is not limited to economic growth but also extends to political accountability. Putnam's theory of social capital (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) suggests that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust and norms of reciprocity, are more likely to develop institutions that promote political accountability. This is supported by the argument that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. The cultural approach, therefore, provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how institutions and culture interact to shape long-run economic growth. Cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, are integral to the economic performance of societies. The historical evidence of different institutional frameworks shows that cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, are integral to the economic performance of societies. This is evident in the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times, which can be attributed to cultural beliefs and values with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions are crucial because the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. This perspective is supported by the role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as shown by North and Weingast (1989, p.30). The precise remaining disagreement is over the specific mechanisms by which institutions and culture interact. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the development of new financial instruments and the decline in transaction costs, which reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).
