The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in the field of economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic growth over extended periods. Two main positions exist in the literature. One position argues that culture, through its influence on institutions, shapes long-term economic outcomes. This perspective posits that cultural beliefs and values can either foster or hinder economic growth by influencing the creation and maintenance of institutions. The other position contends that institutions are primarily shaped by economic and political factors, and that culture plays a secondary role at best. This perspective suggests that institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, and that cultural factors are more a reflection of institutional frameworks than a cause. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors fundamentally shape institutions, or whether institutions are shaped by other factors and culture is merely a byproduct. This disagreement is crucial for understanding the mechanisms behind economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote development. Institutions and culture are not static entities; they evolve over time and interact in complex ways, making it essential to understand their dynamic relationship. Culture's role in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic growth is a multifaceted issue that requires a nuanced examination of historical and contemporary evidence. The interplay between culture and institutions is not a one-way street; instead, it involves a continuous feedback loop where cultural beliefs influence institutions, and institutions, in turn, shape cultural norms. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The development of institutions in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1), underscores the significance of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs in fostering economic growth, building on the cultural and institutional dynamics that shaped these outcomes. This institutional framework, particularly the creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of financial instruments, played a critical role in reducing the costs of transacting and securing property rights over time. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that the institutions governing public choice provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, thereby supporting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated are complex. The role of these institutions in fostering economic growth underscores the importance of secure property rights and the reduction of transaction costs in long-run economic development.

Distributional conflicts, not just cultural factors, are a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie (2007, p.12); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4)). Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie's analysis challenges the idea that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, are the primary drivers of economic institutions. The efficiency argument posits that these institutions arose to maximize economic output, but Ogilvie contends that they were often the result of power struggles between different social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This suggests that economic institutions are deeply intertwined with the power dynamics of the societies in which they exist. The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework underscores the complexity of economic development. Ogilvie notes that the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interconnected with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system. This interconnectedness implies that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient for understanding economic growth. However, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated are complex, and further research is needed to fully understand their impact on long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. They trace how the mortality rates experienced by European settlers influenced the types of institutions established in colonies. High mortality rates led to the creation of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonizers. This is because the political power of colonizers and their desire for immediate rents shaped institutional design. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) further show that the extractive institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates are strongly correlated with current economic outcomes. This correlation underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth. The design of these institutions was not solely driven by economic efficiency. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that politically powerful groups often resist institutional changes that do not directly benefit them, even if those changes could promote long-term growth. This resistance further entrenches extractive institutions, perpetuating their negative effects on economic development. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the deep and lasting influence of colonial institutions on long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) examine how economic divergence is shaped by institutional evolution across different historical periods, with the latter arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant by 1800. The timing and nature of institutional changes are crucial. Austin (2008, p.2) stresses the dynamic, endogenous responses of Africans to their environments, highlighting the role of technology and institutional adaptation (Austin 2008, p.25). This points to a complex interplay between endogenous factors and exogenous shocks. Pamuk (2007, p.24) illustrates this interplay by showing how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including labour-saving technological innovations and greater mobility (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These changes contributed to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. However, Pamuk (2007, p.24) also notes that the timing of these changes is crucial, as the Black Death's impact was not immediate but unfolded over decades (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This suggests that the long-run effects of such shocks depend on the specific institutional and economic contexts in which they occur, which can either amplify or mitigate their impact on growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) both assert that institutional reforms are pivotal for African economic development. The new research programme begins with the hypothesis that institutional variables are more significant than other variables in explaining development. As applied to Africa, the hypothesis further assumes that institutional change is gradual. This assumption calls for historical investigation because current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) align with this perspective, arguing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, which often overlooked the role of historical institutional factors. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional contexts that can either amplify or mitigate the impact of economic shocks on growth. The effectiveness of these institutions in promoting growth depends on their congruence with indigenous institutions. High degrees of congruence underline the legitimacy of governments and enhance their ability to foster institutions that induce development. Low levels of congruence have the opposite effect and create conditions that favour patrimonial politics, which lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). These findings suggest that while institutional reforms are crucial, their success depends on how well they align with the historical and cultural contexts of African societies.

The role of women in economic development is a vital aspect that intersects with cultural and institutional factors, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) share. Women's economic contributions are deeply embedded in cultural norms and institutional structures, shaping and being shaped by them. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight that colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had enduring impacts on gender relations and economic production in Africa. These constructs, along with pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs, influenced the sexual division of labor, which in turn affected economic development. Goldin (2006, p.8) similarly points out that shifts in women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. were driven by changes in social norms and expectations. These shifts were catalyzed by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes were intertwined with broader institutional and cultural contexts. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) illustrate how, in matrilineal Akan societies, women's roles in reproduction and wealth transmission were crucial for community survival and economic stability. This underscores the importance of understanding the cultural and institutional contexts in which women operate, as these factors significantly influence their economic contributions and, by extension, long-run growth. The interplay between cultural norms, institutional structures, and women's economic roles highlights the complexity of factors that drive economic development, suggesting that policies aimed at enhancing women's economic participation must consider these interconnected dynamics.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, show that the slave trade left a lasting legacy of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of enslavement and exploitation, has persisted across generations, shaping contemporary social and economic interactions. The slave trade's impact on trust is a dynamic force that continues to influence African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's effects on trust are particularly relevant because trust is a crucial factor in economic development, facilitating cooperation, reducing transaction costs, and fostering social capital. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization indicates that the slave trade also undermined state institutions, leading to long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, hindered economic development by creating an environment of uncertainty and weak governance. The legacy of the slave trade encompasses institutional weaknesses that continue to impede economic growth. The persistence of these historical legacies underscores the importance of addressing both cultural and institutional factors in promoting long-term economic development in Africa.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. The cultural beliefs and values of societies can significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates that cultural factors, such as Catholic beliefs about the afterlife, motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, thereby affecting economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence extends to broader institutional frameworks, as seen in the interdependence of European family systems, labor markets, and credit markets, which collectively contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlement patterns, driven by cultural and institutional factors, led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The legacy of these cultural and institutional frameworks underscores their enduring impact on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by the work of North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30). North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further supporting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Austin 2008, p.2) shows that institutions are critical for long-term economic growth and that cultural factors significantly influence their development and effectiveness.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the historical and contemporary dynamics of economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) both show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of cultural and gender dynamics in economic institutions (Austin 2008, p.25; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant long-term consequences for gender production and economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The stickiness of these colonial constructs may explain counterfactual evidence from economic dependency models, which have found that breadwinner wives continue to play a crucial role in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The persistence of these historical legacies underscores the importance of addressing both cultural and institutional factors in promoting long-term economic development in Africa.
