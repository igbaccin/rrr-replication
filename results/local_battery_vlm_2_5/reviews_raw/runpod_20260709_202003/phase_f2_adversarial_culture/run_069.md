Culture's role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue. Scholars debate whether cultural factors are fundamental causes of economic growth or merely epiphenomenal to deeper structural forces. One position emphasizes culture's direct influence on institutional frameworks, arguing that shared beliefs and values can foster or hinder economic growth by shaping institutions. The other position challenges this view, suggesting that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geographical conditions or technological advancements, with culture playing a secondary role. This disagreement is central to understanding the dynamics of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. Ogilvie and North both acknowledge that institutions are critical for economic growth, but they diverge on the extent to which culture influences these institutions. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. Ogilvie, on the other hand, contends that cultural beliefs can directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. This tension sets the stage for exploring how culture interacts with institutions to drive long-run economic growth. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The role of women in economic growth highlights how cultural norms and gender dynamics significantly influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). This perspective is supported by the quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the United States, as described by Goldin (2006, p.8). The transformation in women's roles was driven by shifts in social norms and expectations, which in turn influenced women's life satisfaction and career prospects. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) detail how women's roles in Akan state formation were indispensable for community reproduction. This dynamic underscores the broader cultural significance of gender roles in economic development. However, Goldin notes that these changes were not merely evolutionary but were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology. These factors altered female labor supply and made part-time work more available, thereby facilitating women's entry into the workforce. The implication is that cultural shifts, both endogenous and exogenous, play a crucial role in determining women's economic contributions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth. Cultural norms and gender dynamics thus emerge as key factors in shaping economic development trajectories, with women's roles serving as a critical mechanism through which these cultural influences manifest.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) and McCloskey (2015, p.10) argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This period saw a significant shift in economic thought, where the growth of useful knowledge became the mainspring of economic growth. McCloskey posits that the "new rhetoric" of the bourgeoisie, which emphasized innovation and economic betterment, played a crucial role in fostering an environment conducive to growth. This rhetoric reflected a cultural shift that altered societal norms and values. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that the diffusion of knowledge spread rapidly through various means, including periodicals, translations, and international conferences. This widespread dissemination of knowledge facilitated the Great Enrichment, as Mokyr terms it, by making tacit knowledge more accessible and applicable across different regions. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that the codification of knowledge was constrained by the technology and costs of the time, meaning that tacit knowledge remained an essential part of the economic landscape. This highlights a nuanced view of economic growth, where cultural shifts and the dissemination of ideas are intertwined with technological and economic factors, shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels, a phenomenon that Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) show is linked to Africa's current economic underdevelopment. The slave trade undermined state centralization, creating long-term political instability and weakened states, which in turn hindered economic development. The process of state centralization is crucial for economic growth because it enables the establishment of institutions that facilitate trade, enforce contracts, and provide public goods. Nunn illustrates that the historical impact of the slave trade on state centralization can be seen through the lens of current economic performance. For instance, countries with a history of state development, measured by their experience with state-level institutions, tend to have higher real per capita GDP. This historical legacy of weakened states, stemming from the slave trade, has left a lasting imprint on Africa's economic trajectory, constraining its ability to achieve sustained growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run economic development. They show that European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates established inclusive institutions that fostered economic growth, while high mortality rates led to extractive institutions that hindered development. This pattern is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as the significant impact of these institutions on income per capita. The historical legacy of these institutions persists, shaping contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further illustrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes. This institutional perspective highlights the enduring effects of colonial policies on economic development paths. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists. Thus, the economic benefits of colonial institutions are contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which they were established.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. Through the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, England experienced a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which reflected increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, by providing a stable market for public and private debt, fostered economic growth and political freedom. The financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional evolution underscores the importance of secure property rights in economic development, contrasting with views that emphasize cultural factors as the primary drivers of economic growth. The specific historical and political contexts in which these institutions were established are crucial for understanding their impact on economic development. These institutions were shaped by the fiscal crises and political dynamics of the time (North 1989, p.9).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) share a focus on the economic divergence and development in Europe, highlighting the role of institutional changes and urbanization in driving growth. This divergence was evident in wage structures, with north-western Europe experiencing higher silver wages, which conferred greater purchasing power over tradable goods and services. This institutional framework, however, did not uniformly lead to abundance and low food prices, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this by showing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that contributed to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These structural changes included greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional reforms in guilds, as well as labour-saving technological innovations. This suggests that while institutional changes were pivotal, they were also influenced by broader economic and demographic shifts, such as those triggered by the Black Death. These findings underscore the complex interplay between institutional changes, urbanization, and economic growth in Europe, highlighting how these factors collectively shaped long-run economic development.

The literature on African economic performance and institutional reforms highlights the significance of institutional variables in explaining development outcomes, but it does not directly address how culture influences long-run growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This approach underscores the gradual nature of institutional change, which necessitates historical investigation to understand the roots of current institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) also notes that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth is enhanced when they are congruent with indigenous institutions, a factor that underlines the legitimacy of governments and enhances their ability to foster development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance and highlights the importance of factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in shaping economic growth. However, the role of culture in institutional reforms remains an under-explored area, suggesting that future research could benefit from integrating cultural factors into the analysis of long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Bryant (2006, p.35) argue that the evolution of institutions in the New World was heavily influenced by initial conditions of inequality, which in turn shaped long-term economic development. This perspective highlights how initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. Bryant (2006, p.35) builds on this by emphasizing the role of cultural norms and social relations in enabling transitions to industrial modes of production, suggesting that the cultural context is crucial for understanding institutional evolution. However, while Bryant acknowledges the importance of cultural factors, he also underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how these factors interact with economic and political institutions over time. This interaction is complex, as cultural norms can both facilitate and hinder institutional reforms depending on the specific historical and social context. The downstream implication is that cultural factors, though crucial, are not deterministic. Instead, they interact dynamically with economic and political institutions, shaping long-term growth trajectories in ways that require careful historical and contextual analysis. Thus, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains an area that demands further exploration and integration into broader theories of economic development.

The downstream implication is that while technological and agricultural developments drive long-run growth, the societal and cultural context in which these innovations are implemented can significantly influence their impact.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the importance of endogenous factors and dynamic responses to environmental challenges, while Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows how distributional conflicts influenced the formation of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were deeply embedded in the social and political fabric of the time. Austin (2008, p.25) further qualifies this by noting the period-specific nature of institutional impacts on economic growth, implying that the effectiveness of institutions can vary over time and across different contexts.
