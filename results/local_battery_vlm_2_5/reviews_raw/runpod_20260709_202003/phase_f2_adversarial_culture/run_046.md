The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in the study of economic development. At stake is the ability to understand and predict economic outcomes across diverse societies. One position argues that culture shapes economic institutions directly, influencing growth trajectories through the values and beliefs it instills. This view posits that cultural norms can either facilitate or hinder economic progress by determining the efficiency and structure of institutions. Another position maintains that institutions are shaped by other factors, such as mortality rates and geographical conditions, and that culture plays a secondary role at best. This disagreement hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or merely a complementary factor. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policy-makers aiming to foster economic growth, as it informs whether cultural interventions can significantly alter institutional frameworks and, by extension, economic performance. The debate over the role of culture in economic growth is central to the broader question of how societies develop and prosper over the long term. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The impact of culture on economic growth extends beyond individual beliefs, influencing the very institutions that govern societies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that gender dynamics, particularly the role of women, are integral to understanding long-run economic development. The authors show that African women were crucial to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women's roles in society were essential to the reproduction of communities. This is evident in matrilineal societies like Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth. The colonial period introduced new gender constructs that altered the dynamics of gender relations, with long-term consequences for economic production. These colonial constructs may explain why certain economic dependency models find that breadwinner wives continue to be a significant factor in economic outcomes. The gendered division of labor established during colonial times has persisted, reflecting the enduring influence of cultural norms and gender relations on economic development. The role of women in economic growth is further underscored by Goldin (2006, p.3) findings on the quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. Exogenous changes, such as the increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, altered female labor supply and transformed women's roles in the economy. This transformation resulted from specific changes in social norms and institutional frameworks. These findings highlight how cultural shifts and gender dynamics can drive long-run economic growth, shaping the economic landscape in profound ways.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust, rooted in historical exploitation, continues to shape contemporary social and economic interactions. This is a living legacy that influences current behaviors and attitudes. The slave trade's impact on African economic development is profound, as it led to long-term political instability and weakened state structures. This instability, in turn, hindered economic growth and development. The relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability resulted in weakened and fragmented states, which are crucial determinants of current economic performance. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are not limited to historical periods. Recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability continues to shape African economies today.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change, not interests or institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey contends that the economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by a cultural struggle that fostered new rhetoric, which in turn created new circumstances (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting the diffusion of knowledge through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The dissemination of knowledge facilitated the spread of new ideas and ideologies, which then influenced economic development. Mokyr acknowledges that much of the knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills, making personal transmission and training crucial for its spread (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge, while essential, posed challenges in codification and dissemination, qualifying the straightforward diffusion of ideas and ideologies. The interplay between codified and tacit knowledge shaped the economic landscape, illustrating the complex role of ideas and ideologies in driving economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show that the evolution of institutions in 17th-century England, particularly the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The development of a stable market for public debt provided a large positive externality for the parallel growth of a market for private debt, further stimulating economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth. These scholars find that the differences in European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita. The mortality rates of European settlers in the colonies are strongly correlated with the presence of European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutions. This correlation suggests that the establishment of institutions was influenced by the ability of European settlers to survive and establish themselves in the colonies. The impact of institutions on income per capita is significant, with a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, may persist even when they do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains.

Distributional conflicts and inequality shape economic institutions, focusing on culture as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). In pre-industrial Europe, institutions such as serfdom and guilds were driven by conflicts over the distribution of resources and power. These institutions reflected the interests of powerful groups. Similarly, in the New World, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism of institutional development driven by distributional conflicts is further complicated by the interdependencies between different institutions within a broader framework. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that institutions are interconnected, and studying one in isolation may overlook these interdependencies. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, which collectively contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. Thus, the institutional framework shaped by distributional conflicts and inequality has profound implications for long-run economic growth, as these institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic development depending on how they allocate resources and power.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) both examine the impact of foreign trade and export production on economic development, noting that these factors significantly influenced economic growth in both Europe and Africa. They show that the rise of foreign trade and export markets facilitated economic growth by stimulating productivity and resource allocation. These variations influenced the timing and scale of economic growth, underscoring the importance of institutional flexibility and adaptability in harnessing the benefits of foreign trade. Pamuk (2007, p.24) further illustrates this point by demonstrating how the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These changes, including increased mobility, flexibility, and institutional change within guilds, contributed to the rise of rural industries and labor-saving technological innovations. The role of culture in these structural changes is less clear. Pamuk (2007, p.24) argues that the Black Death brought about greater institutional change to the guilds, but the specific cultural factors that drove this change are not explicitly addressed. This suggests that while foreign trade and export production are crucial drivers of economic growth, the cultural underpinnings of these economic activities remain an area for further investigation.

The Industrious Revolution and agricultural development are significant historical phenomena that preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution, but these papers do not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (de Vries 1994, p.18; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This period saw a shift in economic activities, with households releasing labor reserves to the market and yielding practical functions to market- and government-supplied services. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) examines the role of property rights and social norms in agricultural development, highlighting how these factors enabled farmers to better protect their land and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This suggests that the Industrious Revolution was a social phenomenon, where cultural norms played a crucial role in shaping economic behaviors. The specific cultural factors that drove these changes remain elusive. The exact mechanisms by which culture influenced these norms and, in turn, economic growth, are not explicitly addressed. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further investigation into the cultural underpinnings of economic activities during this period.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both highlight that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are not straightforwardly explained by environmental obstacles or simple geographical factors. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, endogenous version of economic development, where Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories. Cultural and institutional factors, along with geographical ones, played a significant role in economic development. The dynamic interaction between culture and environment is further supported by the fact that high levels of investment in southern Indian agriculture led to high levels of economic development and low silver wages. This investment resulted from cultural and institutional factors. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that the cultural underpinnings of economic activities can change over time. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth evolves with historical and institutional contexts.

The economic development and skill premiums in Europe are closely linked to long-term economic performance, and this relationship is reflected in the differences in the skill premium (van Zanden 2009, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This is supported by the observation that the wages of unskilled workers, which reflected labor productivity in agriculture, were linked to population growth (van Zanden 2009, p.23). The development of silver wages thus reflected, up to a point, the economic development of the various parts of Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between economic indicators and actual living standards, highlighting that economic growth does not always equate to improved living conditions.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal for long-run growth. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. concur that institutions are deeply influenced by cultural beliefs and historical contexts. Ogilvie shows that institutions like guilds and communities in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by cultural beliefs, such as Catholic views on the afterlife, which influenced economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European mortality rates, a proxy for cultural and environmental factors, significantly impacted the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This interplay between culture and institutions underscores that economic growth is about the cultural framework that supports or hinders institutional development.

The historical evolution of institutions reveals that cultural factors are integral to their formation and persistence. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, was critical for economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional change was underpinned by cultural shifts that supported secure property rights and reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). Austin complements this view by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, showing that different historical paths and periods must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). These perspectives indicate that cultural factors are central to the institutional frameworks that drive long-run growth.

The influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions is further illustrated by the role of women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This contribution was deeply rooted in cultural norms and social structures, such as the matrilineal society of the Akan, where women's roles were indispensable to community reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This cultural context shaped economic institutions and practices, highlighting that cultural factors are active agents in economic development.

Institutions are deeply embedded in cultural frameworks. North and Weingast show that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise role of culture in this process remains a subject of debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the detailed comparison of institutional frameworks across different regions and periods, such as the contrast between northern and southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This comparison shows that cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, are key to understanding economic performance and long-run growth.
