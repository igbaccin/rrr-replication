The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether cultural factors, such as social norms, values, and beliefs, fundamentally shape the institutional frameworks that drive economic growth. This debate pits those who see culture as a primary force shaping institutions against those who view institutions as primarily determined by other factors, such as geography or economic efficiency. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the dynamics of collective action. For example, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' posits that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, tend to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability. This perspective suggests that cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, can explain economic performance over long periods. Some scholars argue that culture's influence on institutions is frequently exaggerated, and that other elements, like geographic conditions or economic efficiency, often play a more substantial role. This debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth, as well as the importance of considering the historical and contextual factors that shape these relationships. The question of whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional change, or whether they are secondary to other forces, remains a critical area of inquiry. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) establish that the slave trade in Africa instilled a persistent culture of mistrust, which continues to affect contemporary trust levels. This cultural legacy is a significant factor in African economic development. The slave trade, which involved the forced removal of millions of Africans, disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment of suspicion and distrust. The long-term effects of this mistrust are profound, as it permeates social interactions and economic exchanges. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) further illustrate this by showing the critical role of women in maintaining social and economic stability during colonial periods. They highlight that the matrilineal societies, where women were crucial for the transmission of wealth and office, were particularly vulnerable to the disruptions caused by the slave trade. The slave trade diminished the population and disrupted the social fabric, resulting in a lasting culture of mistrust. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development by hindering cooperation and collective action, which are essential for growth. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape African societies, influencing contemporary economic outcomes and social dynamics.

Acemoglu et al. assert that colonial institutions, shaped by European settler mortality rates, are a crucial determinant of current economic outcomes, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) showing that these institutions accounted for a significant fraction of current income differences. High mortality rates led Europeans to create extractive institutions designed to maximize colonial rents and promote long-run growth. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize immediate gains over long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions, in turn, hindered economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation, and by concentrating wealth and power in the hands of a few. This pattern of institutional design, driven by the need to maximize short-term benefits for European colonists, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) stress that these institutions' effects vary with the initial conditions and the specific historical context of each colony. This variability suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, their effects were mediated by other factors, including local cultural and geographical conditions.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the growth of useful knowledge, fostered by new ideas and ideologies, is the primary driver of economic growth. These ideas did not merely reflect interests but actively reshaped them. McCloskey highlights that economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by a cultural struggle that transcended mere elite interests. The new rhetoric fostered by this cultural struggle made new circumstances, which in turn reinforced the new rhetoric, creating a virtuous cycle that led to economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was readily diffused within the Western world. This diffusion was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. Mokyr also points out that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and expertise, and moved about through personal transmission and training. This tacit knowledge was crucial for economic growth, as it complemented codified knowledge and was essential for technological advancements. Mokyr also recognizes that knowledge diffusion was not uniform, and national differences in style and emphasis within the West did exist. This qualification suggests that while the growth of useful knowledge was a significant driver of economic growth, local cultural and institutional factors also played a role in shaping economic outcomes.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This dynamic perspective acknowledges that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their environments. This perspective is shared by Bryant (2006, p.35), who argues that a causal connectedness governs every transition from past to present, and that the revisionist preference for restricting effective or salient causality to shorter-term and immediate processes is prone to explanatory distortions. This implies that long-term economic growth is not merely a function of immediate institutional changes, but rather a complex interplay of cultural, social, and technological factors that evolve over time. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and cultural conditions that shape economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They highlight that the evolution of institutions, particularly those related to public finance, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional framework fostered economic growth and contributed to political freedom. This institutional stability was essential for the development of both public and private debt markets, which in turn supported long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24; North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast's analysis highlights that the institutional changes in England were proactive in shaping economic conditions. This proactive role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The institutional framework in seventeenth-century England provided a stable environment for economic activities, which was critical for sustained growth. The specific historical and cultural conditions that enabled these institutional changes must also be considered. These conditions include the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This trade laid the groundwork for the subsequent financial revolution and the secure property rights that followed. Thus, the institutional framework in seventeenth-century England was a product of economic needs and specific historical and cultural factors that facilitated its development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts and the resulting institutional frameworks are key to understanding economic development. This is a critical insight, as it highlights how economic institutions are deeply embedded in social and political structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were the result of distributional conflicts. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of elites, even if they hindered overall economic productivity. They were shaped by the cultural beliefs and values of the time, which reinforced the status quo. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to the New World, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to maintain elite power. They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to institutions that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. This pattern is evident in the government policies that advantaged elites, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape. This has significant implications for understanding long-term economic growth, as it suggests that institutions designed to maintain elite power can stifle economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further notes that these institutions are interdependent, forming a complex framework that can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Understanding this interdependency is crucial for grasping how economic institutions evolve and their impact on long-term economic development. The implication is that long-term growth is not just a matter of institutional design but also of the underlying power dynamics and cultural values that shape those institutions.

This shows that cultural values and institutions are interdependent and that their interaction is essential for long-term economic growth.
