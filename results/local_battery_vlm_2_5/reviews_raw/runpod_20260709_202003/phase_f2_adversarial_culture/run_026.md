The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustained economic growth. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values significantly shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. They propose that cultural factors can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic performance. However, other scholars contend that the impact of culture on institutions is often overstated, suggesting that other factors such as geographic conditions, economic endowments, and historical events may play a more significant role in shaping institutions and long-term economic growth. The live disagreement centers on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or if it is merely one of many factors that influence economic outcomes. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth, as it challenges conventional wisdom and opens new avenues for research and policy-making. The debate over the role of culture in shaping institutions and economic growth is particularly relevant in the context of historical institutional development, where cultural beliefs and values have been shown to influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey (2015, p.10) describes how the "cultural struggle" in France, for example, was characterized by a rhetorical battle that ultimately influenced economic growth. This cultural shift was not merely a matter of institutional change driven by elite interests, but rather a complex interplay of ideas and ideologies that reshaped societal norms and economic behaviors. Mokyr (2005, p.56) adds that the growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was essential for economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) emphasizes that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by various means, including periodicals, translations, and personal communications. This widespread dissemination of knowledge suggests that the cultural and intellectual environment of Europe was conducive to the exchange of ideas, which in turn drove economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the transmission of tacit knowledge, which was often embodied in skills and practices, required personal interactions and training. This highlights the importance of social and cultural networks in facilitating the spread of knowledge that was critical for economic development. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in how ideas and ideologies influenced economic behaviors and the dissemination of knowledge.

The early modern period saw significant economic divergence between Europe and Asia, which was already evident before 1800. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2) both argue that the prosperous regions of Asia resembled the stagnating parts of Europe rather than the rapidly developing northwestern Europe. The lower silver wages in Asia indicate a lower purchasing power over tradable goods and services, suggesting differences in economic development and institutional frameworks. van Zanden (1999, p.6) highlights the role of England and London in the eighteenth century, noting that silver wages reflected broader economic developments across Europe. This suggests that regional economic dynamics, including the role of major urban centers, played a crucial part in shaping early modern economic growth. The specific cultural and institutional factors that drove these economic differences remain less clear.

Acemoglu et al. posit that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which emphasizes the role of colonial institutions in economic development, offers a crucial explanation for long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Austin 2008, p.2). This thesis highlights that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, particularly those that shaped property rights and governance structures, were pivotal in determining the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, often stifled long-run economic growth in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Austin also acknowledges that the thesis helps in overcoming limitations of earlier institutionalist approaches, particularly in explaining why certain institutions that foster economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic view of institutional change suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-term growth is influenced by endogenous factors within the colonized societies. This nuanced perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate, highlighting that cultural factors may play a role in shaping institutional outcomes and, consequently, economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts are more important than cultural factors in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They show that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by distributional conflicts between elites and non-elites. This is illustrated by the persistence of serfdom, guilds, and other institutions that restricted competition and advantaged elites in both regions, which Ogilvie attributes to the "interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework" (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these institutions was due to the power dynamics between elites and non-elites. Sokoloff and Engerman show that the same dynamic was at play in the New World, where institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This suggests that cultural factors are not the primary drivers of institutional change, but rather that distributional conflicts play a more significant role in shaping economic institutions.

Institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can dramatically lower transaction costs, thereby enhancing economic productivity and growth. These institutional developments not only supported long-term capital markets but also provided a positive externality for the growth of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the impact of institutions on economic performance is significant, with a strong correlation between early institutions and current economic outcomes. The persistence of institutions is not always straightforward. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note that while institutions like the Bank of England were pivotal, other forces might have eventually led to similar outcomes, albeit more slowly and less decisively. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other economic factors, highlighting that while institutions are necessary for growth, they may not always be sufficient on their own.

Nunn and Wantchekon, together with Nunn, show that the slave trade had deep and enduring effects on African development by shaping cultural attitudes, such as mistrust. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, contributing to Africa's reduced economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a significant factor in the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, underscoring the profound impact of historical events on contemporary cultural norms. The persistence of this mistrust highlights how historical events can shape long-term economic trajectories, complementing the idea that institutions are pivotal in economic growth. The relationship between the slave trade and state centralization reveals a more complex picture. Nunn and Wantchekon note that the slave trade led to long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability further exacerbated the economic underdevelopment in Africa. The impact of the slave trade on state centralization is consistent with the historic accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, affected economic performance, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The impact of the slave trade on state centralization and the resulting political instability is a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic underdevelopment.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run growth. Trust and social norms, as cultural factors, significantly influence economic institutions and efficiency. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe shows that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, develop institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, explain the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times. This cultural approach to understanding economic institutions is supported by empirical evidence showing that societies with strong social capital tend to have more efficient economic institutions and better economic performance. The importance of social capital in fostering economic growth is further underscored by the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. Ogilvie notes that the European family system, community-based welfare system, and nuclear family system were interdependent with labour markets, land markets, and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu and Johnson's cross-country comparisons suggest that favourable contracting institutions are partially interdependent with favourable property rights institutions, further emphasizing the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights the complex interplay between cultural factors, institutions, and economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North argues that the neoclassical model, which holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs, fails to account for the role of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). He points out that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter, and gains from trade are the key to the wealth of nations. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, which shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, underscoring the importance of institutions in economic growth. This historical evidence supports the view that cultural factors, through their influence on institutions, play a significant role in long-run growth.

While the role of culture in shaping institutions is well-established, there remains a disagreement over the specific mechanisms by which culture influences economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This cultural mistrust, in turn, affects economic performance by undermining social capital and the development of efficient economic institutions. However, Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis fails to account for the dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, which considers Africans' own responses to their environments and the technologies available to them (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on state centralization and the resulting political instability, which affected economic performance. The impact of the slave trade on state centralization is consistent with the historic accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, affected economic performance, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on state centralization and the resulting political instability provides a clear example of how cultural factors, through their influence on institutions, can affect economic growth.
