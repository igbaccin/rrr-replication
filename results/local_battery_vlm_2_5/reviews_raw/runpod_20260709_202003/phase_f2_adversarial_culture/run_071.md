The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors, such as norms, beliefs, and values, can influence the establishment and evolution of institutions that foster economic growth. One live disagreement revolves around the primacy of culture versus other factors, such as geography or institutions themselves, in determining economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values can directly shape institutions, leading to economic growth or stagnation. Others contend that the impact of culture is more indirect, mediated through institutions that are themselves shaped by other factors, such as colonial experiences or mortality rates. This debate is crucial for understanding the deep-rooted factors that influence economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth.

The interdependence between institutions and economic growth is a central theme in this debate. North argues that institutions matter significantly in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. He asserts that the neoclassical model, which assumes zero transaction costs, fails to account for the real-world importance of institutions. North contends that with positive transaction costs, institutions play a critical role in facilitating economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Austin, who highlights the importance of institutional evolution and policy experience in understanding long-term economic growth. Austin notes that economic historians view the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe. This shared finding underscores the enduring influence of institutions on economic growth, as institutions shape the economic landscape over extended periods (North 1989, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2).

This pervasive culture of mistrust, instilled by the slave trade in Africa, has been shown to persistently influence social and economic dynamics, as evidenced by the work of Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4). This mistrust was a deeply ingrained cultural norm that persists to this day. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.15) show that the legacy of the slave trade accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect on trust, underscoring the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary social attitudes. The implications of this mistrust extend beyond social interactions, influencing economic behavior and institutional effectiveness. Austin (2008, p.25) notes that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of geographical approaches to economic development, where Africans' responses to their environments have been shaped by historical technologies and institutions. This dynamic interaction highlights how the culture of mistrust, rooted in the slave trade, continues to influence economic outcomes by affecting the adoption and effectiveness of institutions.

North (1989, p.1) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) share the view that institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The role of institutions in economic growth is particularly evident in the context of colonialism. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate how colonial institutions influenced current income per capita by examining the impact of European settler mortality rates on institutional development. These institutions, shaped by the needs and interests of colonial powers, often prioritized extractive practices over long-term economic development. This dynamic is evident in the institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond colonial contexts. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) illustrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This suggests that the institutional framework established during this period played a necessary role in England's long-run success, underscoring the importance of institutions in economic development. This is not to say that institutions alone determine economic growth; rather, they interact with other factors, such as culture, to shape economic outcomes.

Building on this, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) delve into how economic institutions and inequality have molded pre-industrial societies. They argue that the interplay between economic institutions and inequality significantly influenced the development of these societies. The institutions in the New World, for example, were structured to advantage elites, who used various government policies to restrict competition and maintain their dominant positions. This process was driven by the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized these societies from their inception. These institutions, in turn, shaped the long-term development paths of these societies. However, Pamuk (2007, p.24) qualifies this by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, suggesting that exogenous shocks can also play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions and inequality. The impact of economic institutions on long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, involving not only the direct effects of these institutions but also their interaction with other factors such as exogenous shocks.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) highlight that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. In the advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower. This suggests that the economic development in Asia was not as robust as in Europe, despite similar grain wages. van Zanden (1999, p.2) supports this idea by claiming that economic development in Europe did not necessarily translate into improvements in the standard of living, as evidenced by the stagnation in silver wages. This pattern indicates that economic growth in pre-industrial societies was not uniform and that the standard of living did not always improve in tandem with economic development. The divergence in economic development between Europe and Asia before 1800 was influenced by factors such as institutional changes and investment in agriculture.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Hornbeck (2010, p.6) present a contrasting view, suggesting that many Anglophone African economies exhibited stronger economic performance in the early twentieth century than previously acknowledged. These economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and export markets, with growth patterns influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This linkage to foreign trade and export markets suggests that the development of agricultural sectors was crucial for economic performance, as export production drove growth in many countries. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) supports this view by highlighting the importance of property rights in agricultural development, demonstrating that social norms and legal responsibilities can enhance economic productivity. The variability in growth patterns across Africa indicates that the effectiveness of these factors depended on local conditions and institutions. This variability underscores the complexity of agricultural development and its impact on economic performance, as local factors could either facilitate or hinder growth. The role of culture in shaping long-run growth is thus intricately tied to the specific institutional and economic contexts of different regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) underscore the critical role of women in economic growth and development, both in Africa and the U.S., thereby challenging views that have historically overlooked their contributions. The role of women in economic development is evident in the significant contributions made by African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. In matrilineal societies like Asante, women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities, as their bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Women's economic roles extended to critical areas such as mineral resource management and trade. This dynamic highlights how women's economic contributions were integral to the broader economic development of these societies. The economic contributions of women during these periods were foundational to the economic systems. In the U.S., a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics, driven by changes in social norms and institutional frameworks (Goldin 2006, p.3). These transformations were marked by significant shifts in expectations regarding future work and life satisfaction. The increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions were key drivers of these changes, altering female labor supply and occupational patterns. The extent to which these transformations have fully integrated women into economic leadership roles remains a subject of debate.

Culture plays a critical role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as shown by multiple streams of scholarship. Ogilvie describes how cultural beliefs and values, such as those embedded in 'civic' cultures, foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and stimulating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework highlights the complexity of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate the gains from trade and technological change (North 1989, p.1). The role of culture is further underscored by the example of African women, whose contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This shows that cultural norms and social structures can either facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on how they shape institutions.

The impact of culture on institutions is also evident in the historical context of European colonization. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how cultural and institutional legacies can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.2). This is supported by North's observation that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in securing property rights and fostering economic growth in England settles the debate on the period-specific relationship between institutions and economic growth.
