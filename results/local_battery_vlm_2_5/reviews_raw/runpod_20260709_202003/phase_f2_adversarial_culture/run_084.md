The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional scholarship. The stakes are high, as understanding this dynamic can illuminate pathways to sustained economic development. The debate centers on whether cultural factors, such as shared norms and values, directly influence the formation and effectiveness of institutions that drive growth, or whether other factors, such as geographical determinants or exogenous shocks, play a more decisive role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions by altering the structure of collective action games, thereby influencing economic efficiency and growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Others posit that the impact of culture on institutions is mediated by other factors, such as the historical experiences of societies and the specific institutional frameworks they develop in response to these experiences (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with some studies suggesting that cultural factors can either reinforce or undermine the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the long-term economic performance of societies and for designing policies that can foster sustainable growth.

This perspective, however, overlooks the role of cultural norms and values that underpinned these ideas and ideologies. McCloskey contends that the shift in rhetoric, which emphasized bourgeois values and dishonorable aristocratic values, played a significant role in fostering economic growth in the Netherlands, Britain, and France, and eventually in Spain (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This rhetorical shift facilitated a new set of circumstances that, in turn, generated further rhetorical changes, ultimately leading to the Great Enrichment. Mokyr further elaborates on the mechanism of knowledge diffusion, highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge was pivotal for economic growth. He underscores the role of tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training, as a critical component of the knowledge economy. This tacit knowledge, along with codified knowledge, moved freely within Europe, contributing to the intellectual movement that drove economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr also observes that knowledge diffusion varied among nations. The impact of this intellectual movement was most pronounced in the West, where the Enlightenment had taken root, and where the free exchange of ideas was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56).

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting an increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in establishing a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the growth of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The resulting financial stability and secure property rights provided a conducive environment for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The implication is that the institutional framework, rather than cultural factors alone, was critical in driving economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) assert that colonial institutions are a fundamental cause of long-run economic development. They argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, rather than cultural factors, played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of colonies. Mortality rates faced by European colonizers influenced the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonizers, while low mortality rates resulted in institutions more conducive to long-term economic growth. These institutional differences persisted over time and had significant effects on current income per capita. The impact of these institutions is evident in the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as between early institutions and those in place today. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) found a significant relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, suggesting that institutional legacies are a key determinant of economic performance. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial experiences, continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-term growth, which further underscores the critical role of institutions in economic development.

Distributional conflicts, not cultural factors, are the fundamental driver of economic institutions, shaping long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie demonstrates that pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, serfdom, the community, and various guilds, can be better explained by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie also shows that these institutions were interdependent within a broader framework, such as the European family system and community-based welfare systems, which further influenced economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate on this by examining New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic underscores that distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that favor specific groups, thereby influencing long-run economic trajectories. For example, the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be traced back to the initial conditions that favored elites, which in turn shaped the institutions that governed these societies. This institutional framework, influenced by distributional conflicts, can have lasting effects on economic development, highlighting the critical role of these conflicts in shaping long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are rooted in different historical paths and institutional evolutions. Both authors highlight that the institutional frameworks that fostered economic growth in certain regions were not uniformly adopted across different parts of the world. This divergence is evident in the varying responses to environmental and technological challenges, which influenced the adoption of institutions conducive to economic development. The institutional frameworks that supported economic growth in Europe and its overseas 'off-shoots' were not merely a result of secure private property rights but also involved complex interactions between technology, policy, and investment. This dynamic institutional evolution highlights the importance of cultural and historical contexts in shaping the adoption of institutions that drive long-run economic growth. These findings underscore that the cultural and historical contexts that shaped institutional frameworks were critical in determining the long-run economic trajectories of different regions.

Nunn and Wantchekon's study establishes that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a key factor in the long-term economic development of African countries. The slave trade decimated African populations, leading to a lack of trust in institutions and among individuals. This effect is not merely an artifact of colonial rule or contemporary conditions but a direct legacy of the slave trade. Nunn shows that slave exports had a detrimental effect on state centralization, leading to political instability and weakened states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, hindered economic development by creating an environment where institutions were unable to effectively support growth. Nunn argues that the slave trades led to a cycle of underdevelopment, where political instability and weak institutions perpetuated economic stagnation (Nunn 2008, p.1). This cycle is not easily broken, as the legacy of mistrust and weak institutions continues to impact economic development. The persistent mistrust and weak institutions are a significant barrier to economic growth, as they hinder the ability of African countries to implement effective policies and attract investment.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. In pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts were crucial in forming core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by cultural beliefs and values (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's decline in these beliefs led to the decline of guilds. This cultural influence extended to social capital, where trust, norms, and networks facilitated coordinated actions and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust and reciprocity, developed institutions that fostered economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Across different regions, the impact of culture on institutions is profound. In Europe, the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were a result of European settlement patterns, which were influenced by mortality rates faced by soldiers, bishops, and sailors in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions underscores the enduring impact of historical cultural and institutional factors on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework further emphasizes the role of culture in shaping economic institutions. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights that institutions cannot be studied in isolation; rather, entire institutional frameworks must be considered (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was crucial for England's long-run success, showing how institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth and highlights the role of culture in shaping these institutions.

The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical mistrust and weak institutions are a significant barrier to economic growth, as they hinder the ability of African countries to implement effective policies and attract investment.
