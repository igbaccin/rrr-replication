The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is whether cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, can influence institutions in a way that shapes economic growth over the long term. The debate centers around two main positions. One position posits that cultural factors, by shaping institutions, have a profound impact on long-term economic growth. The other position argues that institutions are shaped primarily by exogenous factors such as geography or historical events, and that culture plays a secondary role.

The disagreement hinges on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions, shaped by cultural factors, create a stable environment for economic activity, thereby fostering growth. Others contend that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, and that culture's role is more indirect and less significant. This debate is crucial for understanding the underlying mechanisms of economic development and for informing policy interventions aimed at promoting growth. The role of culture in shaping institutions and its impact on long-term economic growth remains a live question because it challenges the dominant view that institutions are shaped primarily by exogenous factors. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Nunn (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27) argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality restricted competition and advantaged elites, contributing to the persistence of inequality. They show how these institutions evolved in response to the factor endowments of New World societies, which were characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality was perpetuated through various government policies that favored elites, such as those that restricted competition and consolidated political power. The mechanism through which inequality persisted was the concentration of wealth and political power in the hands of a few, which allowed them to shape institutions in their favor. This dynamic is evident in the historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). The downstream implication of this institutional environment is that it created a vicious cycle where inequality was both a cause and a consequence of underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that the slave trades' adverse effects on economic development extended beyond the colonial period into the post-colonial era. McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the growth of useful knowledge and the dissemination of ideas were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This period saw a profound cultural shift where new ideologies and knowledge systems emerged, fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. For instance, the dissemination of knowledge through periodicals, conferences, and personal communications facilitated the diffusion of useful knowledge across different regions, transcending national boundaries. This cultural shift involved more than institutional change; it was a fundamental transformation in how knowledge was perceived, valued, and applied. Mokyr (2005, p.14) elaborates on this by noting that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and practices that required personal transmission and training. This tacit knowledge, while difficult to codify, played a crucial role in economic growth, highlighting the importance of cultural factors in the dissemination and application of knowledge. Cultural struggles within societies, such as the 'Franco-French War' in France, could also impede economic growth by preserving conservative ideologies that resisted change. This suggests that while the dissemination of useful knowledge was a significant driver of economic growth, cultural resistance and ideological conflicts could mitigate its effects. Despite these challenges, the overall impact of ideas and ideologies in driving economic change during this period is undeniable, as it laid the groundwork for the Great Enrichment.

This cultural legacy of mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has profound implications for economic development. The mistrust, a direct legacy of the slave trade, has eroded social capital, which is crucial for economic growth. This erosion of trust has made it difficult for communities to cooperate effectively, hindering collective action and investment in public goods. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that the sexual division of labor established during the colonial period continues to influence gender production, reflecting the sticky nature of pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. This persistent mistrust and entrenched gender roles have created a complex web of challenges that impede economic development in Africa.

Their shared perspective underscores that the institutional changes in Europe were driven by a complex interplay of factors including investment, technology, and geographical responses. This dynamic environment fostered high agricultural productivity and labor mobility, which in turn stimulated economic growth. The investment in agriculture led to increased labor productivity, but this did not result in abundant grain and low food prices because labor shifted from agriculture into industry and services. The historical paths of Europe and Asia diverged significantly, with Europe experiencing institutional changes that facilitated economic growth, while Asia's institutional frameworks did not undergo similar transformations.
