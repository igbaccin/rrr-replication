The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. Scholars disagree on whether culture directly influences economic growth through institutions, or if institutions evolve independently of cultural factors. Some argue that cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions, fostering growth by encouraging trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Others contend that economic institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography or technology, with culture playing a secondary or even negligible role (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies. The live disagreement centers around whether cultural beliefs directly influence economic institutions, or if institutions are shaped by other factors and culture merely reinforces existing institutional frameworks. The first stream of literature directly addresses the influence of culture on institutions and economic growth, while the second stream proposes rival causes that do not operate through cultural variables. The third stream identifies antecedents that flow into the causal variable of culture, offering complementary explanations.

North & Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution of the late 17th century, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutional changes have nuanced implications. While the financial revolution was pivotal, other forces might have eventually led England to success, albeit more slowly and less decisively. The institutional framework that emerged in England provided a robust foundation for economic development, highlighting the importance of secure property rights and efficient financial systems in driving long-term growth.

Ideas and ideologies, not just institutions, were pivotal drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (Bryant 2006, p.35; McCloskey 2015, p.10). The cultural and ideological shifts in Europe were fundamental in shaping the economic trajectory. These shifts included the rise of bureaucratized, territorially consolidated polities, legal-juridical developments that extended rights and protections to persons and their claims to property, and the onset of a vibrant print culture. These elements were interconnected and reinforced each other, creating a dynamic environment conducive to economic growth. The cultural struggle in France, for instance, highlighted the deep-seated ideological conflicts that influenced economic policies and outcomes. This cultural dynamic was evident across Europe, where the interplay of ideas and ideologies drove economic change. These cultural and ideological factors evolved over time, creating new circumstances that further drove economic growth. The role of culture in driving economic change is nuanced. While cultural factors were crucial, they were not the sole determinants of economic growth. Other factors, such as technological advancements and institutional changes, also played significant roles. For instance, the rise of bureaucratized, territorially consolidated polities, as legitimized by representational and constitutional compacts with the governed, was a critical factor in driving economic growth. This highlights the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth.

Distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency or cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). They posit that institutions are shaped by the power dynamics between different groups in society, with elites often using their influence to create institutions that benefit them. In the New World, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) illustrate how extreme inequality led to the establishment of institutions that favored elites and restricted competition, thus perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework, which prioritizes elite interests, can hinder economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader societal advancement. The persistence of these institutions, despite their potential inefficiency, underscores the significant role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonialism led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly impacted current income per capita. The mechanism begins with European mortality rates determining settlement patterns, which in turn shaped the colonial institutions that persisted over time. High mortality rates in colonies led to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This historical institutional framework, grounded in colonial experiences, highlights how the initial conditions of colonial rule set the stage for enduring economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) illustrate that the correlation between settler mortality and current institutions is robust, underscoring the lasting impact of these early institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that these institutions may be designed to maximize short-term gains for the ruling elite. This nuance suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions, while detrimental to economic growth, can be understood as a strategic response by colonial powers to secure their interests.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This thesis suggests that institutions in Africa were designed to maximize short-term gains for the ruling elite. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth is a complex and evolving process. Austin (2008, p.2) shares the rejection of what Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) call the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. This divergence is evident in the differing levels of economic development and institutional change in Europe and Asia. In the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change is usually seen as bringing about investment in agriculture, leading in turn to high agricultural labour productivity. This higher agricultural labour productivity did not lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between institutions, technology, and economic development, suggesting that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a multifaceted and evolving process.

The role of women in economic development is often overlooked in mainstream economic narratives, yet it is pivotal in understanding long-run growth. The gendered division of labor and cultural norms significantly influence economic outcomes. For instance, the historical role of women in Akan societies, where matrilineal systems defined wealth transmission, underscores how cultural beliefs can shape economic structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic is not confined to pre-colonial Africa; in the U.S., the transformation of women's employment and education from the mid-20th century onward was driven by shifts in social norms and technological changes (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes in women's roles altered labor supply and demand, influencing broader economic patterns. These shifts have long-term consequences that are complex and multifaceted, as evidenced by the enduring impact of colonial gender constructs in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The interaction between cultural norms and economic institutions is a recurring theme, suggesting that the role of women in economic development is a fundamental aspect of long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon, together with Nunn, highlight how the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has enduring effects on contemporary social and economic outcomes. The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to long-term political instability and weakened state structures (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, hindered the development of strong, centralized states capable of implementing effective economic policies. The resulting fragmented political landscape has persisted, affecting Africa's economic trajectory. The legacy of the slave trade also manifests in modern corruption, as historical experiences of exploitation have eroded trust in institutions (Nunn 2008, p.27). This institutional weakness, coupled with persistent mistrust, creates a challenging environment for economic growth and development. The historical impact of the slave trade on cultural attitudes and political structures in Africa thus continues to shape long-term economic and social outcomes, with mistrust and institutional frailty acting as persistent barriers to growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions by altering the dynamics of collective action. For instance, Ogilvie shows that the Catholic belief system motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's shift in beliefs led to the decline of these guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic illustrates how cultural shifts can reshape economic institutions over time. Similarly, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework further underscores the role of culture in economic growth. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system, labour markets, and credit markets were interconnected, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural attitudes towards these institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective aligns with the idea that cultural and institutional frameworks evolve over time, influencing long-term economic outcomes.
