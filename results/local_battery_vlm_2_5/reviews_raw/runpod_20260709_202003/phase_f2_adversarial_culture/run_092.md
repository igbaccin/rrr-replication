The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development scholarship. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors in shaping economic institutions and, through them, economic trajectories. Some scholars contend that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby driving long-run growth. Others argue that cultural factors, while relevant, are not the primary determinants of institutional development and economic growth. A third group posits that cultural factors operate through other mechanisms, such as distributional conflicts or social capital, to shape institutions and economic outcomes. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth, as it challenges simplistic views that attribute economic development solely to geographic or institutional factors. The interplay between culture and institutions is a dynamic process that evolves over time. North (1989, p.1) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) jointly highlight that institutions play a fundamental role in shaping economic growth. North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are inherent in any economic system. The mechanism through which institutions affect growth is by reducing transaction costs, as North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9) illustrates through the example of the Bank of England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and fostered economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify this by noting that institutions can be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth, implying that the impact of institutions on growth is contingent on their design and the interests they serve.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency or cultural factors, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are designed to maximize efficiency or driven by cultural beliefs. Ogilvie contends that the formation of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds was motivated by the interests of powerful groups seeking to maintain their economic and political advantages. This distributional conflict approach highlights that institutions are shaped by the struggle for resources and power among different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea, arguing that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the design of institutions is contingent on the distribution of power and the interests of dominant groups. However, Ogilvie acknowledges the complexity of institutional frameworks, noting that different institutions are interdependent and may resist change from external influences (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency implies that institutional change involves the complex interplay of various institutions within a broader framework. The implications of this perspective suggest that understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions requires a nuanced approach that considers the power dynamics and interdependencies within institutional frameworks.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) show that historical events can create enduring cultural legacies, such as mistrust in Africa, which continue to influence trust levels today. The slave trade disrupted social structures, fostering an environment where trust in others became a liability. This mistrust, in turn, hindered the development of cooperative institutions essential for economic growth. The enduring impact of this historical trauma is evident in contemporary African societies, where low levels of trust persist. This cultural legacy of mistrust is an active force shaping current economic and social dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) further illustrate the deep-seated nature of these cultural norms by highlighting the role of women in pre-colonial and colonial African societies. The matrilineal societies of the Akan people, for instance, showcase how cultural beliefs and social norms have persisted despite external influences. The transmission of wealth and office through matrilineal lines underscores the resilience of cultural practices. These enduring cultural norms, including the mistrust engendered by the slave trade, create a complex interplay with institutional frameworks. The legacy of mistrust affects interpersonal relations and institutional trust, which is crucial for economic development. This dynamic underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors, shaped by historical events, influence institutional frameworks and long-run growth.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique is shared by Bryant, who argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent (Bryant 2006, p.35). Both authors emphasize that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis fails to account for the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic development, which involves complex interactions between technology, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms. Austin notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay is crucial because it highlights how local responses to environmental challenges can shape economic outcomes, a mechanism that is overlooked by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. However, Bryant qualifies this by asserting that without the enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms, there can be no phased conversion to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony, regardless of the availability of "windfall" natural resources (Bryant 2006, p.4). This qualification underscores the importance of a holistic approach that considers multiple factors, including cultural norms, in understanding long-run economic growth. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks is thus a critical factor in shaping economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and Hornbeck (2010, p.6) demonstrate that specific institutional changes and technological innovations, such as the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England and the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, significantly influenced property rights and economic development. These institutional changes fostered secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize the critical role of the financial revolution in England's long-run success, highlighting how institutions governing public debt markets provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets. This institutional framework enabled the growth of a stable market for public debt, which was instrumental in securing property rights and promoting economic growth. The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains similarly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed for more effective control over property, thereby facilitating economic development. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) notes that in some contexts, such as modern California, social norms can play a more significant role than strict legal responsibilities in encouraging property control. This suggests that while institutional changes and technological innovations are crucial, the effectiveness of property rights enforcement can also depend on prevailing social norms. The interplay between institutional frameworks and cultural norms thus emerges as a critical factor in shaping economic development.

Structural changes in the economy and women's roles, as highlighted by Goldin (2006, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.24), can transform long-run growth by altering the labor supply and skill distribution. Goldin and Pamuk both highlight how exogenous changes can initiate significant shifts in economic structures. Goldin identifies several exogenous factors that influenced women's employment, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. These changes altered the supply of labor and influenced women's educational and occupational trajectories. These shifts were driven by scarcity and high labor costs, which in turn reshaped the economic landscape. One key implication of these structural changes is the emergence of new skill premiums and wage gaps, as seen in the divergence between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5). However, these changes also highlight the complex interplay between exogenous shocks and endogenous responses, as the increased demand for skilled labor and the rise of new industries were not immediate but evolved over time. This evolution suggests that while structural changes can drive long-run growth, the process is often gradual and influenced by a multitude of factors.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run growth, as seen in the interplay between cultural beliefs and economic institutions. Ogilvie highlights how cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is evident in the impact of Catholic beliefs on medieval craftsmen's adherence to guild rules, which in turn affected economic outcomes. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that cultural factors, such as mortality rates, influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the enduring impact of cultural beliefs on institutional frameworks and economic growth.

The evolution of institutions is deeply intertwined with cultural and historical contexts, as shown by North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England. They argue that the financial revolution, driven by institutional changes, played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This period saw the development of new financial instruments and the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. North emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that focus on population and savings (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This shows that cultural and institutional changes can drive long-run growth by fostering secure property rights and reducing transaction costs.

Cultural beliefs and values also shape economic institutions by influencing social capital and trust. Ogilvie discusses Putnam's theory of social capital, which refers to features of social organization such as trust, norms, and networks that can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam contends that societies with high social capital, characterized by 'altruistic' or 'civic' cultures, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times. This dynamic version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach shows that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, emphasizing the role of gender and cultural norms in shaping economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).
