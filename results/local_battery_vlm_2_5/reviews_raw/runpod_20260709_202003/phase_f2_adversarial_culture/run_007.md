The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. This perspective posits that cultural norms can enhance societal coordination and trust, which in turn facilitate economic development. Some scholars argue that culture influences institutions indirectly, with other factors such as power distribution and historical context playing a mediating role. This debate is crucial because it addresses whether cultural interventions can be effective tools for economic development or if they are merely secondary to more fundamental economic and political forces. The disagreement hinges on whether cultural beliefs can independently shape institutions that drive long-run growth, or if they are merely epiphenomena of deeper economic and political structures. The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. A clearer understanding of the role of culture in institutional development could inform more effective policies aimed at fostering economic growth. The discussion begins with the perspective that cultural beliefs and values directly shape institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, particularly in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This period saw a significant shift in economic thought, where ideas about liberty and innovation took center stage. McCloskey notes that the "cultural struggle" in France, for example, was pivotal in shaping economic outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.10). Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth, stressing that the dissemination of knowledge was critical for economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This useful knowledge was not merely codified in texts but also embodied in tacit forms, such as skills and practices, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implications of this knowledge dissemination were profound, as it facilitated the spread of innovative ideas and practices across borders, contributing to the Great Enrichment. This underscores the complex interplay between ideas, institutions, and culture in driving long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) contend that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. The persistence of this mistrust has far-reaching implications for economic development. This suggests that the historical legacy of the slave trade continues to shape contemporary societal norms and economic outcomes. This historical instability has left a lasting impact on Africa's economic performance, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is a crucial determinant of current economic performance. Recent empirical studies suggest that Africa's history can explain part of its current underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). These studies focus on the link between countries' colonial experience and current economic development, highlighting the enduring effects of historical events on contemporary economic conditions. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade continues to influence trust levels in Africa, shaping the economic landscape and hindering development.

North and Weingast, along with North, establish that institutions governing public choice are pivotal in economic growth, particularly in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). North elaborates that this institutional framework was crucial for developing a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of private debt markets (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions provided a large, positive externality, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast observe that these institutions were crucial for economic growth but not the sole drivers (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). They suggest that other forces, such as technological advancements and resource allocation, also played a role in England's economic success. This highlights that while institutional frameworks are fundamental, they operate in conjunction with other economic factors to drive long-run growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) share the finding that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This divergence is marked by differences in urban development and real wages, which were influenced by institutional and economic factors. This disparity reflects the differing levels of economic development and purchasing power over tradable goods and services. This economic development was not uniform across Europe; the stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe had lower standards of living compared to the more prosperous north-western regions. The economic development in Europe was influenced by institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture, which in turn increased agricultural labour productivity. These institutional changes did not uniformly lead to high productivity and low food prices, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services, creating a complex interplay between economic factors and institutional frameworks. The role of culture in this economic divergence remains ambiguous.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) share the view that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective, which challenges the primacy of culture in economic development, posits that European colonial powers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions, intended to maximize rents for European colonists and not to foster long-term growth, endured over time and notably influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This institutional framework, shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions evolved and interacted with local contexts, suggesting that the dynamic interplay between institutions and economic development is complex and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further support this view by demonstrating that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies, accounting for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional perspective, while acknowledging the importance of geography, emphasizes that the impact of institutions on economic development is mediated by historical and political factors, which shape the institutional landscape in ways that persist over time.

This historical context sets the stage for understanding how cultural legacies, such as those stemming from the slave trade, can shape contemporary economic outcomes. Ogilvie, along with Sokoloff and Engerman, contend that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, molded core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, where extreme inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective challenges the notion that cultural beliefs alone can explain institutional structures and their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie highlights the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that studying institutions in isolation overlooks their complex interactions and the resulting economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This framework does not comprehensively address the role of cultural beliefs in influencing individual behaviors and collective actions within these institutions.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. Cultural beliefs influence individual behaviors and collective actions within institutions, thereby affecting economic outcomes. For example, the Protestant Reformation in Europe led to the decline of guilds, as Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated craftsmen to follow guild rules, whereas the disappearance of these beliefs with the Reformation caused guilds to decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. This perspective is supported by Putnam's theory of 'social capital,' which refers to features of social organization, such as trust, norms, and networks, that can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam argues that some societies are characterized by high social capital because of their 'altruistic' or 'civic' cultures, which in turn encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The theory of social capital is also supported by the work of North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they provide the framework for economic activity (North 1989, p.1). North's work highlights the importance of secure property rights and the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.1).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is complex. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework suggests that studying institutions in isolation overlooks their complex interactions and the resulting economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the impact of institutions on economic performance is not solely determined by cultural beliefs, but also by the broader institutional framework in which they operate. This is further supported by the work of Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work highlights the importance of considering the dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, which suggests that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This shows that the role of culture in shaping institutions is complex and multifaceted, and that it is important to consider the broader institutional framework in which cultural beliefs operate.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further complicated by the fact that cultural beliefs can also be shaped by institutions. For example, the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This shows that institutions can shape cultural beliefs, which in turn influence individual behaviors and collective actions within institutions. This perspective is supported by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast's work highlights the importance of institutions in shaping cultural beliefs and the resulting economic outcomes. The financial revolution in England, for example, led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This shows that the role of culture in shaping institutions is complex and multifaceted, and that it is important to consider the broader institutional framework in which cultural beliefs operate.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth. North and Weingast show that the financial revolution in England played a critical role in England's long-run success, as it provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).
