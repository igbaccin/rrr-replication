The role of culture in shaping institutions has long been debated as a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and norms directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. Others contend that the impact of culture on institutions is overstated, with institutional frameworks evolving primarily through economic and political mechanisms. This disagreement is significant because it challenges the very foundations of institutional theory, which posits that institutions are the primary determinants of economic performance. The stakes are high, as the resolution of this debate could fundamentally alter our understanding of economic development and the policies that promote it. For instance, if culture is indeed a primary driver of institutional evolution, then policies aimed at fostering economic growth might need to focus on cultural transformation rather than mere institutional reform. If institutions evolve independently of cultural influences, policy should concentrate on direct institutional changes. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the interplay between culture and institutions in the context of long-run economic growth. The relationship between cultural beliefs and the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity is complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The institutional changes in England, which secured property rights and fostered economic growth, were driven by the evolution of public choice institutions, as argued by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North explains that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, played a critical role in the country's long-run success. North and Weingast posit that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. North and Weingast also highlight that the institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. This development was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as it provided a stable financial environment that encouraged investment and economic activity. North and Weingast note that shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, the Bank of England began private operations. This shift underscores the importance of institutional evolution in supporting economic growth. The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by North’s argument that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of growth, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. With positive transaction costs, institutions matter significantly, as they facilitate the gains from trade that are key to the wealth of nations. This perspective contrasts with views that attribute economic development primarily to other factors, such as cultural beliefs or exogenous shocks. North’s emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic growth highlights the need for secure property rights and a stable financial environment to support long-term economic development.

However, Ogilvie and Austin contend that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency or cultural factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe highlights that distributional conflicts among elites were a key driver in shaping economic institutions. She argues that the institutions of serfdom, the community, and guilds were maintained to preserve the power and wealth of elites, rather than for efficiency or cultural reasons. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were efficient responses to economic conditions or the result of cultural beliefs. Austin extends this argument by critiquing the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes economic development primarily to exogenous factors such as geography. Austin argues that this thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach underscores the importance of endogenous factors in economic development. Austin also notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This qualification highlights the complexity of institutional change and the need to consider the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions operate. The dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and endogenous factors in shaping economic institutions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the fundamental causes of long-run growth.

Beyond interests and institutions, McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) emphasize that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective underscores the power of cultural narratives in shaping economic outcomes. McCloskey illustrates this dynamic through the "Franco-French War," a cultural struggle that hindered economic growth in France due to entrenched ideologies. This ideological resistance was not merely about institutional constraints but about deeply held beliefs that resisted change, even in the face of evident success in neighboring countries. Mokyr complements this view by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in economic growth. He notes that the diffusion of knowledge across borders was facilitated by international networks and personal communications, highlighting the importance of cultural exchange in driving economic progress. The tacit nature of much useful knowledge meant that its dissemination depended on personal interactions and training, underscoring the role of cultural transmission in economic development. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that the codification of knowledge was a function of technology and costs, suggesting that while cultural factors are crucial, they operate within a broader economic context. The interplay between cultural narratives and the diffusion of useful knowledge thus offers a nuanced understanding of the fundamental causes of long-run growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Broadberry and Gupta, highlight how institutions shaped by inequality have influenced economic development across different regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Sokoloff and Engerman explore how the initial conditions of New World societies, marked by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power, led to the evolution of institutions that reinforced these disparities. These institutions, often designed to protect elite interests, restricted competition and hindered economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta corroborate this perspective by demonstrating that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, highlighting how institutional differences contributed to varying trajectories of economic development. This divergence was significantly influenced by institutional frameworks that either facilitated or impeded investment and productivity. The institutional differences, as Broadberry and Gupta show, had tangible effects on economic outcomes, such as agricultural productivity and labour mobility. The implications of these institutional frameworks extend beyond economic metrics. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the persistence of inequality and the resulting institutional structures have had lasting impacts on social and political landscapes, further entrenching disparities over time. This interplay between inequality, institutions, and economic development underscores the complex role that cultural and institutional factors play in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant of long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, as shown by Acemoglu et al., had a profound impact on current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is because the mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies directly influenced the type of institutions established, which in turn shaped long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, shaped by the initial conditions of colonial settlement, highlights the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. This perspective suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial rule has had lasting effects on economic development, independent of cultural factors.

The slave trade in Africa has had a profound impact on the continent's economic development, with the culture of mistrust it fostered emerging as a rival explanation to culture as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this culture of mistrust persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. They complement this finding by noting that their evidence aligns with studies that have found historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as the independence of cities in Italy during the eleventh to fourteenth centuries. This suggests that the historical determinants of cultural norms are not unique to Africa but are a broader phenomenon (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn builds on this by arguing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, has had a significant adverse effect on economic development. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability are consistent with empirical research showing that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. However, the relationship between the slave trade and economic development is complex, as Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, suggesting that the impact of the slave trade on economic development is not solely through its effect on culture but also through its impact on institutions (Nunn 2008, p.1).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is significant, as it influences the development of economic and political frameworks. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions, such as guilds, which in turn affect economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence is evident in the development of social capital, which facilitates coordinated actions and improves societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that cultural beliefs and values, shaped by historical experiences such as the slave trade, can persist and affect current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical impact of the slave trade on African economic development is complex, as it has shaped both cultural and institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These cultural influences are not isolated but are interdependent with other institutions, forming a broader framework that affects economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these cultural influences is evident in the long-term effects on economic development, as shown by the enduring impact of the slave trade on African economies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Cultural beliefs and values also play a crucial role in the development of secure property rights, which are essential for economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This is supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and facilitate economic activity (North 1989, p.1). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The historical evidence shows that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, highlighting the importance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping institutions that foster economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
