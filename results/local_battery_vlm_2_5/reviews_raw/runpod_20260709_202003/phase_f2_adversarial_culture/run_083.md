The role of culture in shaping institutions and driving long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors, such as beliefs and values, influence the establishment and evolution of institutions that foster economic growth. Institutions, in turn, are widely understood to be a fundamental cause of long-term economic performance. The live disagreement centers on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development and economic growth, or if other factors, such as geopolitical conditions or economic efficiency, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure of collective action games. Others contend that the impact of culture on economic growth is secondary to other factors, such as extractive institutions established during colonial periods or the endogenous development of institutions in response to local conditions. The debate hinges on understanding the mechanisms through which culture interacts with institutions to influence economic outcomes. This interplay is crucial for comprehending the persistent economic disparities observed globally. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a stable environment for public and private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The development of new financial instruments during this period reflects the increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth. This institutional framework supported economic development and played a necessary role in achieving political freedom. The financial revolution in England illustrates how institutional changes can create positive externalities, fostering both public and private debt markets. The implications of this institutional framework are profound.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective challenges the notion that economic growth is solely driven by institutions or interests. McCloskey illustrates this point by describing how France's economic growth was stifled by conservative ideologies, despite the country's potential to follow the examples set by the Dutch and British. Mokyr complements this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. Useful knowledge encompasses both codified knowledge and tacit knowledge, which includes skills and expertise transmitted through personal interactions. This knowledge diffuses across borders, fostering economic growth in interconnected European nations. Mokyr notes that the diffusion of knowledge varies across the globe. The Enlightenment, for instance, had a profound impact on the Western world but did not similarly affect other regions. This suggests that while ideas and knowledge are crucial drivers of economic growth, their impact is mediated by cultural and historical contexts, which can either facilitate or hinder their dissemination and application. The ideas of the Enlightenment, for example, were more readily adopted in countries with cultural and institutional environments conducive to innovation and change.

This is particularly evident in the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) propose that European mortality rates in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persist to this day. These institutions, in turn, have a profound impact on current economic performance. The mechanism by which this occurs is through the establishment of settler institutions, which are directly correlated with contemporary economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) highlight a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions. This relationship underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have long-lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) note that the role of institutions in economic growth varies. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which did not always encourage economic development. This implies that the extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered long-run growth, as these institutions were aimed at extracting resources (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Distributional conflicts play a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions, providing a crucial explanation for the institutional frameworks that govern societies, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The institutional design of serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be understood through the lens of distributional conflicts. These institutions were shaped by the struggles between different social groups vying for resources and power. For instance, craft guilds in medieval Europe were the result of conflicts between artisans and other social classes. This perspective highlights that the institutional landscape is deeply influenced by the power dynamics and resource allocation struggles within society. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutions is not always straightforward. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework complicates the study of any single institution in isolation. This interdependency suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is multifaceted and requires a holistic understanding of the institutional framework. This insight underscores the need to consider the broader context in which institutions operate, as the effects of distributional conflicts can be both direct and indirect.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and related studies highlight the complex interplay between historical paths, institutions, and economic performance in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This thesis suggests that the historical trajectories of African economies were shaped by a combination of endogenous responses to environmental challenges and exogenous factors such as colonial policies. Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance and underscores the importance of considering local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in understanding economic growth. By highlighting the dynamic nature of African economies, these studies emphasize the need for a more nuanced understanding of the factors that influence long-run growth. This qualification suggests that the role of culture in shaping institutions and economic growth may be more complex and multifaceted than previously thought.

The impact of the slave trade on African development highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, which is a key factor in understanding the reduced levels of trust in African societies. This mistrust, in turn, hinders economic development by making it difficult for individuals to cooperate and engage in productive activities. The reduced levels of trust can be attributed to the historical trauma of the slave trade, which has left a lasting impact on African societies. The role of culture in shaping institutions is further supported by the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. This interdependency is crucial for understanding how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). Both institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The implication is that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic outcomes. The paper argues that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, are the primary drivers of reduced trust in African societies or if other factors, such as the establishment of extractive institutions, play a more significant role (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on African development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) and the empirical comparison of European mortality rates and institutional establishment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) settles this disagreement by showing that both cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth.
