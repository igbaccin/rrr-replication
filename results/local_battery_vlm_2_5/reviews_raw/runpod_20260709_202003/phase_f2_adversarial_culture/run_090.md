The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate. At stake is whether cultural beliefs, values, and norms significantly shape the institutional frameworks that underpin economic growth or whether other factors, such as extractive institutions or geographic determinants, play a more decisive role. Scholars have proposed rival explanations for long-run growth, with some arguing that cultural beliefs and values influence economic institutions and efficiency, while others point to the impact of European mortality rates on the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies. The debate hinges on whether culture operates as a trigger for institutional frameworks that foster growth or if other factors, such as the reversal of fortune thesis, provide a more accurate explanation. This literature review will explore these competing explanations and their implications for understanding the role of culture in long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. They shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) found that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This indicates that the initial conditions of a society can shape its institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic outcomes. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex, as institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize their own interests over long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) point out that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to certain groups rather than to maximize long-run growth. This implies that the initial conditions and the interests of powerful groups can lead to the establishment of institutions that hinder economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) advance the notion that economic institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups. In pre-industrial Europe, distributional conflicts between serfs and landowners, or between craftsmen and merchants, influenced institutions such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions; they were shaped by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that these differentials in institutions persisted over time, affecting the course of development. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) warns that studying one institution in isolation may miss the interdependencies within the wider institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the complex interplay between different institutions must also be considered. The role of culture in shaping institutions may be less direct than previously thought, as the interests of powerful groups and the resulting distributional conflicts seem to be the more fundamental drivers of institutional change.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial legacies shaped long-run development, oversimplifies causal pathways by conflating different historical periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective challenges the notion that long-run growth can be attributed to a singular institutional or geographical factor, as it highlights the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic development. Austin's critique underscores the importance of considering how institutions evolve over time and the role of endogenous factors in shaping economic outcomes. This historical context is pivotal, as it indicates that the institutional frameworks in place before colonial rule had a lasting impact on economic trajectories. Austin further elaborates that the African experience, in particular, illustrates a more complex interplay between environment and economic development, where local responses and technological adaptations shaped economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interaction challenges the idea of unchanging environmental obstacles and emphasizes the role of endogenous factors in economic development. However, Austin also notes that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, implying that the impact of institutions can vary over time. This qualification highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions influence long-run growth, considering both their historical context and the dynamic nature of economic development.

The long-term impacts of historical institutions in Africa, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), underscore the enduring influence of institutional variables on development outcomes. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who highlight the significance of political institutions in shaping economic growth patterns. The interplay between local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions has been pivotal in determining the timing and scale of growth in export production across African economies. This dynamic interplay underscores the enduring influence of historical institutions on economic development. However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) also notes that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth is contingent on their congruence with indigenous institutions, a factor that can enhance government legitimacy and foster development-inducing policies. The complexity of this relationship is further complicated by the potential for low congruence to foster patrimonial politics, regionalism, and corruption. This nuanced understanding of institutional effectiveness in Africa highlights the need for a more detailed examination of how cultural factors, which are not directly addressed in this stream, might interact with institutions to influence long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa engendered a deep-seated culture of mistrust, which has endured and continues to shape contemporary social dynamics. This historical trauma has left an indelible mark on African societies, fostering an environment where trust is scarce and social cohesion is weak. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) complement this view by highlighting the role of women in maintaining community cohesion during periods of intense social disruption, such as the slave trade. They argue that despite the significant contributions of women in maintaining social order and economic development, the legacy of mistrust has persisted, influencing contemporary gender dynamics and economic outcomes. This enduring mistrust has implications for economic development, as it can hinder cooperation, investment, and overall economic growth. Furthermore, the persistence of this cultural norm of mistrust can exacerbate existing economic inequalities and hinder the effectiveness of institutions designed to promote growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) note that the gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles established during the colonial period have had significant long-term consequences for gender production, which may continue to reflect the sexual division of labor established during the colonial period. These cultural norms and beliefs may explain counterfactual evidence from economic dependency models, which have found that breadwinner wives continue to be a significant factor in economic dependency.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Hornbeck (2010, p.6) show that economic and social changes in the U.S. were driven by technological and institutional shifts, with the former focusing on women's employment patterns and the latter on agricultural development. The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). Similarly, women's employment patterns shifted due to exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes altered female labor supply and made part-time work readily available, which in turn dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women. The quiet revolution in women's employment, education, and family dynamics was not merely evolutionary or stalled. It was a complex interplay of social norms, changes in household production technology, and institutional changes that made "nice" jobs available (Goldin 2006, p.8). However, these transformations did not directly address how culture influenced these changes or long-run growth, as social norms were not the primary focus of these studies. Nonetheless, the role of social norms in encouraging ranchers to control cattle in modern California suggests that cultural factors may have played a more significant role than initially acknowledged (Hornbeck 2010, p.6).

Culture is a pivotal factor in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as it influences the development and functioning of economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values, such as those promoting trust and norms of reciprocity, can foster institutions that encourage economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that institutions, shaped by historical and cultural factors, significantly impact economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, as Ogilvie argues, underscores the importance of cultural elements in creating an environment conducive to growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in institutional development is further highlighted by North and Weingast, who demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective is supported by Austin's observation that the institutional evolution of older industrial economies is deeply rooted in historical patterns and paths, which include cultural factors (Austin 2008, p.2). North also emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights, which are essential for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.1).

The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth is also evident in the African context. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women's contributions to economic growth were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the role of cultural norms in shaping economic activities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This cultural influence is further illustrated by the impact of gender-based colonial constructs on the sexual division of labor, which had long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The historical evidence from Africa, as well as the broader institutional framework discussed by Ogilvie, shows that cultural factors are integral to the development of institutions that support long-run economic growth.
