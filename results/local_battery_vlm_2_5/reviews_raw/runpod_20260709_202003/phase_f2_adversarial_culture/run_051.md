The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional development, which in turn shapes economic growth, or if other factors, such as geographical determinants, technological advancements, or political structures, play a more significant role. One key position argues that cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. This perspective is exemplified by the idea that cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, facilitate coordinated actions and encourage institutions that favor economic growth. Another position posits that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as secure property rights, is crucial for fostering economic growth, and that cultural factors are secondary to these institutional developments. The disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional development or if other factors, such as geographical determinants or political structures, play a more significant role. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for understanding the mechanisms of economic growth and the potential for policy interventions to stimulate development. A key question is whether cultural factors are endogenous to institutional development or if they operate independently to shape economic outcomes. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This decline was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This decline was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This period saw a shift in the way property rights were secured, with the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War leading to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions laid the groundwork for economic growth by fostering secure property rights, but the pace and decisiveness of this growth were contingent on the specific institutional arrangements that emerged.

Austin argues that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional path dependency is rooted in the settler mortality rates that determined the type of institutions imposed by colonial powers. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, while low mortality rates facilitated the establishment of more inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin elaborates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin qualifies the downstream implications of these institutional changes by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the course of economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). However, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The slave trade in Africa has had a profound and enduring impact on the continent's culture and institutions, with mistrust being a notable and persistent outcome of this historical trauma (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, identify that the slave trade has led to a culture of mistrust that significantly impairs contemporary trust levels. This cultural shift is a living legacy that continues to shape African societies today. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that this mistrust is a direct result of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment of distrust. This persistent mistrust, in turn, has had far-reaching effects on economic development. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust is a significant factor in the reduced trust levels observed in Africa today. Nunn further elaborates on this by showing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability has had a detrimental effect on state centralization, which is crucial for economic development. Nunn's findings suggest that the slave trade's impact on state development is a key factor in Africa's current underdevelopment. The long-term effects of the slave trade on political instability and state centralization are interconnected with the broader issue of trust, highlighting the complex interplay between culture and institutions in shaping long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) propose that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency or cultural factors, are the primary driver behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie's analysis challenges the notion that the craft guilds, merchant guilds, and serfdom systems were established for efficiency, suggesting instead that these institutions were shaped by the interests of dominant groups seeking to maintain their power and wealth. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's examination of New World societies, where institutions were structured to maintain elite dominance and inequality. For example, in colonial societies with high levels of inequality, elites used their political power to implement policies that restricted competition and maintained their privileged status. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality, where institutions evolved to protect the interests of the ruling class rather than to promote broad-based economic development. Ogilvie notes that the interdependence of institutions complicates this view. She argues that institutions like the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England were interdependent with other systems such as labour and credit markets, and this interconnected framework may have contributed to economic growth. The implication is that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, the broader institutional framework also plays a crucial role in shaping long-run growth, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how these factors interact. This view suggests that cultural factors, often overlooked in institutional analysis, might still play a significant role in shaping the broader institutional framework.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, with institutional changes and urban development playing crucial roles in this process (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This productivity did not translate into lower food prices because labour shifted from agriculture into industry and services. The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to the guilds, which contributed to the rise of rural industries (Pamuk 2007, p.24). However, van Zanden (1999, p.2) cautions that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that while institutional changes were significant, they did not uniformly improve living standards across Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This implies that the impact of institutional changes on long-run growth may have been regionally specific, influenced by factors such as urban development and wage gaps.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) share a focus on the significance of institutional variables in shaping African economic development, positing that historical institutional changes have had profound effects on economic growth in Africa. This pattern of growth was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.8) extends this analysis by arguing that effective institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions, enhancing government legitimacy and fostering development. This congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions creates conditions that support economic growth, while low levels of congruence can lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This perspective suggests that the success of institutional reforms in Africa may depend on their ability to integrate with pre-existing cultural and institutional frameworks. This implies that while institutional reforms are crucial, their effectiveness in promoting long-run growth may be contingent on their congruence with existing cultural and institutional frameworks.

The role of women in economic growth and development is significant, as African women made substantial contributions during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). This dynamic underscores the importance of women's contributions to economic development, challenging views that marginalize their role. Goldin (2006, p.8) also emphasizes the transformative changes in women's employment and education in the U.S., noting that shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career began in the late 1960s and 1970s. These changes were preconditions for occupational shifts, such as the increased enrollment of women in professional schools and changes in college majors. The specific mechanisms by which these changes occurred in the U.S. may not directly translate to African contexts, as the structural and cultural environments differ significantly. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) point out that colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, and these constructs may be as influential as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. This suggests that while women's contributions to economic growth are undeniable, the pathways through which these contributions manifest can vary widely depending on historical and cultural contexts.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that precede and facilitate economic growth, but do not directly address culture as the fundamental cause (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). De Vries (1994, p.18) demonstrates that the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a precursor to the Industrial Revolution. This shift was not merely about technological advancements but involved a broader transformation in societal behavior and economic practices. The diffusion of this knowledge across Europe facilitated a shared intellectual movement that transcended national boundaries. This diffusion was not just about the spread of technical knowledge but also about the transmission of tacit knowledge through personal interactions and training. This mechanism underscores the importance of social networks and cultural exchange in the dissemination of knowledge. However, Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the codification of knowledge was influenced by technological and economic factors, indicating that the process was not solely cultural but also driven by practical considerations. This suggests that while culture plays a role in the growth of useful knowledge, it is part of a broader set of factors that include economic and technological conditions. Understanding the interplay between these factors is crucial for comprehending the role of culture in economic growth.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both highlight how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the decline of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation led to the dissolution of guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European mortality rates, which reflect cultural and institutional adaptability, significantly affected the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, in turn, persist and affect current income per capita, illustrating the long-term impact of cultural factors on economic growth. Together, these findings demonstrate that cultural beliefs and values are integral to the institutional frameworks that support economic growth.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further underscores the role of culture in economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North (1989, p.1) both emphasize the importance of understanding institutions as part of an interconnected system. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the European family system, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North (1989, p.1) similarly points out that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This interdependency shows that cultural factors, by influencing the development of institutions, create a framework that supports economic growth. Furthermore, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This evolution illustrates how cultural and institutional changes can create a positive externality for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the dynamic nature of cultural and institutional changes must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic nature is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4), who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the role of cultural and gender dynamics in economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The specific historical evidence of women's contributions to economic growth in Africa, as measured by their roles in reproduction and community building, settles the disagreement about the role of culture in economic growth. This evidence shows that cultural factors, including gender roles and beliefs, are integral to the institutional frameworks that support economic growth.
