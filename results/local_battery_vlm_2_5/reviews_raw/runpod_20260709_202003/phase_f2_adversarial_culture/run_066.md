The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The stakes of this intellectual debate are high because the answer will shape how we understand and address persistent economic disparities across regions and countries. A key disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development, which in turn influence economic growth, or if other factors, such as geographic or institutional determinants, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values shape institutions by influencing collective action and economic efficiency, thereby fostering growth. Others propose that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates or the slave trade, which then determine long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth is complex, with some studies suggesting that cultural factors act as antecedents to institutional development, while others view institutions as the primary drivers of economic performance. The resolution of this debate will provide critical insights into the mechanisms underlying economic growth and development. The evolution of institutions in response to cultural factors and their subsequent impact on economic growth is a central theme that carries across these debates. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework, particularly the financial revolution marked by the creation of the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This development was pivotal in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further amplifying economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This underscores the necessity of institutions in fostering economic growth and political freedom. The role of institutions in economic development has many facets. North (1989, p.1) highlights that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants of growth, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. This observation underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, as they address the realities of positive transaction costs. The implications of these institutional developments extend beyond economic growth, influencing political stability and the evolution of modern economies.

Distributional conflicts are posited as a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors, challenging the notion that culture dominates institutions in shaping long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutions over time. The implications of these distributional conflicts extend beyond economic institutions to political stability. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient, and that the entire institutional framework must be considered. This interdependency highlights the complexity of institutional development and the need to understand the broader context in which institutions operate.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the legacy of colonialism is a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. Extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, did not encourage economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions, which were not conducive to long-run growth, can be attributed to the reluctance of the politically powerful groups to initiate institutional change, as they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. This reversal in relative incomes was driven by the institutional changes that occurred during the colonial period. These changes had a significant impact on the economic growth and industrialization of the formerly poor colonies. This correlation highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on current economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share the perspective that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic change. This perspective challenges the traditional view that economic growth is primarily driven by institutional frameworks. McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that the cultural shifts in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were crucial for the Great Enrichment, highlighting the role of intellectual changes in economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.56) supports this idea by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) shows that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. This diffusion spread across the Western world. This type of knowledge was often transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships, highlighting the importance of social and cultural factors in the dissemination of knowledge. The role of culture in economic growth involves the ideological and intellectual shifts that drive economic change.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both challenge the notion that the reversal of fortune and the great divergence are primarily driven by simple geographic factors or institutional changes. They argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, and that this process was influenced by a complex interplay of factors including investment, technology, and institutional change. These arguments highlight the importance of a dynamic, endogenous approach to understanding economic development, where endogenous factors such as investment in technology and institutions play a crucial role. The argument that the divergence was already underway before 1800 is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's (2006) finding that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 look similar to the stagnating parts of Europe. This suggests that the divergence was a result of endogenous factors such as investment in agriculture and technology. Austin further qualifies this by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This implies that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the course of economic development. This dynamic approach also raises questions about the role of culture in shaping these responses and the institutional changes that followed.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is seen as a rival explanation to broader cultural factors influencing growth. The mechanism through which this mistrust developed is tied to the long-term political instability caused by the slave trades, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, led to a weakened institutional framework, which is a crucial determinant of current economic performance. The link between the slave trade and modern corruption further underscores the enduring impact of this historical trauma on Africa's economic development.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure of collective action games. For instance, Ogilvie explains that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to the decline of these guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, explain economic performance differences, such as the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This influence of culture on institutions is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding the role of culture in economic growth. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need to consider the dynamic, partly endogenous, responses of Africans to their environments (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions is essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical evidence underscores the importance of cultural and institutional frameworks in driving long-run economic growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is settled by the historical evidence of the English financial revolution. North and Weingast show that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The historical evidence of the English financial revolution settles the disagreement by showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, thereby dominating long-run growth.
