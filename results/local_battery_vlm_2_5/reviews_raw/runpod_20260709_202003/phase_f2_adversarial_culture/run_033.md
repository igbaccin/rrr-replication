The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural values and beliefs are the primary drivers of institutional development, which in turn fosters economic growth, or if other factors, such as geographical determinants or institutional frameworks, play a more significant role. The debate revolves around two main positions: one that emphasizes the primacy of cultural factors in shaping institutions, and another that highlights the importance of exogenous factors and institutional frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and North (1989, p.1) both argue that institutions play a critical role in economic growth, but they differ in their emphasis on cultural factors. These positions suggest that cultural factors and institutional frameworks are both crucial in understanding long-run economic growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of how culture interacts with institutions to drive economic development.

They were pivotal in shaping economic institutions and transcending narrow elite interests (McCloskey 2015, p.10). This perspective emphasizes that the cultural narrative and intellectual discourse of the time resulted from a broader ideological transformation, not merely responses to accumulated information and calculated interests. This transformation was evident in the "Franco-French War" and the subsequent ideological battles that reshaped economic institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The interplay between cultural narratives and institutional frameworks underscores the importance of a holistic approach to understanding long-run economic growth, where ideas and ideologies are as fundamental as institutional changes.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) identify that the development of these institutions was a response to specific historical and political contexts, not merely a product of cultural narratives. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was a political and social transformation that provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The role of institutions in this context is crucial because they provided a framework that ensured the protection of property rights over the long term, a necessary condition for sustained economic growth. The institutions that emerged during this period created a positive externality that facilitated both public and private economic activities, underscoring the interconnected nature of political and economic developments. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, highlighting the interdependence of public and private economic activities. This institutional framework fostered economic growth and laid the groundwork for political freedom, illustrating the profound impact of institutional changes on long-run economic and political outcomes.

This argument shifts the focus from the cultural narratives to the institutional legacies of colonialism, which Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue are more influential than cultural explanations for growth. These authors demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates had a lasting impact on current income per capita. The mechanism through which this occurs is the persistence of these institutions over time, shaped by politically powerful groups that maximize their own rents. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) underscore that these 'equilibrium institutions' are often extractive, designed to benefit the colonizers. The implications of this institutional framework are profound, as it stifles economic development and creates a cycle of dependency and underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) note that the impact of these institutions on income per capita is significant, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial-era policies on modern economic outcomes. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic trajectories in former colonies, illustrating the deep and lasting influence of historical institutional changes on long-run growth.

Cultural institutions evolve in response to societal changes, and this evolution is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) share a posture that re-evaluates historical economic institutions and paths, challenging simplistic views of causation in economic development. They argue that the institutional legacies of colonialism, such as those discussed by Acemoglu et al., are not the sole determinants of economic trajectories. Instead, the dynamic interplay between cultural beliefs and economic institutions is a critical factor. This interplay can be seen in the way cultural values and norms influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, which in turn influenced the efficiency of the economy. This mechanism shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can significantly impact economic outcomes by shaping the rules and norms that govern collective action. Austin (2008, p.25) extends this idea by noting that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. This suggests that cultural adaptations to environmental challenges can drive economic growth, even in the face of seemingly unfavorable institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) cautions that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework must be recognized. This means that studying one institution in isolation may not provide a complete picture of its impact on economic growth. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth requires an analysis of the entire institutional framework, including the cultural beliefs and values that underpin it. This holistic approach is essential for grasping the complex interplay between culture and institutions in shaping economic trajectories.

The economic divergence between Europe and Africa, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), exemplifies the complex interplay between cultural factors and institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories. This divergence can be attributed to differences in institutional change and investment in agriculture, which led to varying levels of economic development. These patterns of growth and development were influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The role of culture in shaping these institutional frameworks and economic trajectories remains largely unexplored in these studies. This gap underscores the need for a more comprehensive analysis that integrates cultural factors to fully understand the long-run economic growth of these regions.

The pervasive culture of mistrust resulting from the slave trade in Africa, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27), illustrates how cultural factors can shape institutional frameworks and social dynamics, thereby influencing economic development. This cultural shift is a persistent force that influences contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this by showing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, has adverse effects on economic development. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing political instability are consistent with recent empirical research, which shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. This implies that the institutional frameworks shaped by the slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability continue to hinder Africa's economic growth. The relationship between the slave trade and economic development is complex and multifaceted. While the slave trade undeniably had adverse effects, it is also important to consider other factors that may have contributed to Africa's underdevelopment. For instance, the role of colonialism and more recent events cannot be overlooked. The slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development is a critical aspect of understanding the continent's long-run growth trajectory.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is pivotal to understanding long-run economic growth. Cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. For instance, Ogilvie shows that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon reveal that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and affects current economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings highlight that cultural factors are fundamental drivers of institutional development. Austin underscores the dynamic nature of cultural influences, showing that African responses to their environments, shaped by available technologies, ultimately changed economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further supported by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate specialization and division of labor, which are key to improved productivity and technological change (North 1989, p.1). Ogilvie notes that the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework means that cultural influences are part of a complex system that collectively affects economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. For example, North and Weingast demonstrate that the financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England, played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This highlights how institutions that secure property rights and facilitate economic transactions are essential for sustained growth. Acemoglu et al. further support this by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores the long-term impact of institutional frameworks on economic performance. The role of culture in this process is evident in the way that cultural beliefs and values shape the development and effectiveness of these institutions. For instance, the cultural norms of trust and reciprocity, as highlighted by Ogilvie, can encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the financial revolution in England. This event, as North and Weingast show, played a critical role in England's long-run success by providing a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical evidence demonstrates the direct impact of cultural influences on institutional development and economic growth, showing that cultural factors are fundamental drivers of institutional development.
