The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether cultural factors are fundamental causes or merely epiphenomena. One position holds that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. Proponents of this view argue that cultural norms can shape institutions in ways that either facilitate or hinder economic development. This perspective is championed by those who see cultural factors as integral to understanding the long-term economic performance of societies. On the other hand, some scholars contend that cultural factors are secondary to other determinants, such as institutions themselves or geographic endowments. This view posits that while culture may have some influence, it is the institutional framework that ultimately governs economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs are autonomous forces that shape institutions or if they are merely reflections of deeper structural factors. This question is not merely academic; it has profound implications for understanding the trajectories of economic development and for designing policies that foster growth. The stakes are high, as the answer to this question can inform how societies approach economic and institutional reform. The debate over whether cultural factors are primary drivers or secondary influences in long-run economic growth is ongoing. The resolution of this debate may lie in understanding how cultural beliefs interact with other factors, such as institutions and geographic conditions. The interplay between culture and institutions is a critical area of study, as it can provide insights into the mechanisms that drive economic growth and development. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) share a perspective that institutions are pivotal in economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and low transaction costs. For North and Weingast, the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. North (1989, p.9) explains that these institutional developments reflected an increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The role of institutions in securing property rights presents complexities. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader economic and political context. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) provide further nuance by highlighting how the institutions that fostered economic growth also facilitated political freedom, indicating that the benefits of institutional development extend beyond economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) challenge the notion that cultural beliefs alone can explain the development of core economic institutions, emphasizing instead the role of distributional conflicts. These conflicts were driven by the distribution of resources and power, which resulted in institutions that favored the interests of elites. This perspective is supported by the observation that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who show that societies with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power developed institutions that reinforced these inequalities. The implications of this perspective are significant. If distributional conflicts are the primary driver of institutional development, then cultural factors may play a secondary role in shaping long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework suggests that institutions cannot be studied in isolation. In other words, while distributional conflicts may be a primary driver, the broader institutional framework must also be considered. This nuance underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) shift the focus from institutions to the proliferation of ideas and ideologies, suggesting that cultural factors may have played a more indirect role in economic growth. McCloskey illustrates this by contrasting the institutional change theories of Cheung and North, who posit that institutional change is driven by information and interests, with the reality of economic change being driven by rhetoric and ideology. He argues that the 'Great Enrichment' was driven by the changing rhetoric and ideology in countries like the Netherlands, France, and even Spain. Mokyr expands on this by highlighting the role of knowledge diffusion in economic growth. He argues that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth, and that this knowledge was diffused across Europe through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. This diffusion of knowledge was not hindered by national boundaries, as the knowledge was readily accessible to all who sought it. Mokyr notes that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practices. This tacit knowledge was transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships, highlighting the importance of social networks and institutions in the diffusion of knowledge. This underscores the complex interplay between ideas, knowledge, and institutions in driving economic growth. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus complicated by the role of ideas and ideologies in shaping economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) demonstrate that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia by 1800 was influenced by urban development and institutional changes, which were themselves shaped by cultural and historical contexts. They contend that the prosperous regions of Asia during this period resembled the stagnating parts of Europe. This divergence is evident in wage structures, where grain wages in advanced parts of India and China were comparable to those in northwestern Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This wage disparity suggests that the purchasing power over tradable goods and services was lower in Asia, indicating underlying economic differences. Pamuk (2007, p.5) provides a complementary perspective, arguing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that contributed to the wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450. These changes included declining interest rates, increased labor mobility, and institutional reforms within guilds, all of which fostered an environment conducive to labor-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). The implications of these structural changes are profound, as they highlight how exogenous shocks like the Black Death could catalyze institutional adaptations that drove economic growth in specific regions. The role of culture in shaping these institutional responses remains a critical area for further investigation, as the mechanisms through which cultural factors influence economic outcomes are not fully elucidated in this literature.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, contrasting with cultural explanations (Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that high European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, establishing a direct link between colonial institutions and economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the enduring impact of colonialism on economic development. This mechanism is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes. The institutional framework introduced by colonial powers shaped the economic trajectories of these regions, leading to persistent income differences. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the relationship between institutions and economic performance is complex, with institutions shaped by politically powerful groups that may not always prioritize economic development. This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions influenced economic growth, acknowledging that the impact of these institutions varied across different contexts.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade led to the removal of millions of Africans from their homes and created long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, hindered the development of strong, centralized institutions that could support economic growth. This implies that the legacy of the slave trade, through its impact on political stability and institutional development, continues to affect economic outcomes in Africa today. The extent to which this mistrust culture translates into contemporary economic outcomes is subject to further qualification.
