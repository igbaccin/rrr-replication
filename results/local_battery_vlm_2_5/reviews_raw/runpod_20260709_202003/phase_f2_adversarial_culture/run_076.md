The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the explanation for persistent economic disparities across regions and historical periods. The debate centers on whether cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, thereby driving growth, or whether other factors, such as institutional frameworks or exogenous shocks, are more decisive. One position argues that cultural norms and values influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, leading to divergent economic outcomes. This view posits that societies with civic cultures, characterized by trust, reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, tend to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability. Another position contends that the impact of culture is overstated, and that institutional frameworks, shaped by historical events and exogenous factors, are the primary drivers of economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the importance of interdependencies within institutional frameworks for understanding economic performance, focusing on cultural influences. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the dynamics of economic development and the potential for policy interventions. The interdependency of culture and institutions in shaping long-run growth is a critical area of inquiry. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) argue that economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was primarily driven by ideas and ideologies. In particular, McCloskey contends that the shift from mercantilism to liberalism was a pivotal moment in fostering economic growth. This transformation involved a cultural shift that redefined the very nature of economic activity, alongside elite interests or institutional changes. The ideas that took root in this period spread widely beyond elite circles. Mokyr underscores this point by showing that the growth of useful knowledge was a key driver, facilitated by the diffusion of ideas across borders. This diffusion involved tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training, as well as codified knowledge. The spread of useful knowledge was therefore a function of both written records and personal transmission, highlighting the role of social networks in disseminating ideas. However, Mokyr also notes that the payoff to efforts to codify knowledge depended on the technology and costs of codification, suggesting that the diffusion of ideas was not frictionless. The implication is that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, their impact was mediated by the practical constraints of knowledge transmission. This mediation process underscores the complexity of how cultural shifts translate into economic growth, as the ideas must be not only generated but also effectively disseminated and applied.

North and Weingast argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and spurring economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The implication is that the evolution of these institutions was a necessary condition for economic development. This perspective underscores the idea that secure property rights, facilitated by robust institutions, create a stable environment where economic actors can confidently invest and innovate. The development of a stable market for public debt, for instance, provided a positive externality for the growth of private debt markets, further reinforcing economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North and Weingast's analysis highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that cultural factors alone cannot account for long-run growth. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, shared by Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48). Colonial institutions were shaped by the mortality rates faced by European settlers, which in turn influenced the type of institutions established in these colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, characterized by extractive policies, persisted over time and significantly impacted current income per capita. The relationship between settler mortality and current institutions is strong and statistically significant, indicating that the colonial legacy of institutions continues to influence economic performance today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Austin qualifies the implications of this institutional framework by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform across all former colonies and may vary depending on the specific historical and institutional context of each country. As such, the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former European colonies, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional changes on long-run growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts and institutional inequality have been pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of New World societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These conflicts, rooted in the unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power, have influenced the evolution of institutions that either fostered or hindered economic growth. This institutional framework, which favored elites, created a self-reinforcing cycle where policies and institutions continued to disadvantage the majority, thereby stunting economic growth. One specific mechanism through which this occurred was the restriction of access to education and political participation, which further entrenched the power of the elite class (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie highlights that these institutional frameworks are interdependent with other economic and social structures, such as labor markets and credit systems (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on long-run growth. However, the role of culture in shaping these institutional frameworks remains under-explored, suggesting that while distributional conflicts and institutional inequality are crucial, they do not fully account for the cultural dimensions that might also influence long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack show that women have played a crucial role in economic growth and development in Africa, both during pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as in the U.S. (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). These contributions are often overlooked in broader discussions of economic growth, yet they highlight the significant impact of cultural norms and gender roles on economic development. The role of women in economic growth is deeply intertwined with cultural and social structures, which shape both the opportunities and constraints they face. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth, making them indispensable to the reproduction of communities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This cultural framework not only underscores women's economic contributions but also illustrates how cultural norms can influence long-run growth by determining who participates in the economy and how. Goldin's analysis of the U.S. reveals that shifts in women's employment and education were driven by exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). These factors altered female labor supply and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women, illustrating how cultural and institutional changes can create new economic opportunities. These transformations also faced resistance from entrenched social norms. For example, the quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. was met with resistance from social norms concerning women's family and career, which began to change only in the late 1960s and 1970s (Goldin 2006, p.8). This resistance highlights how cultural beliefs can both facilitate and hinder economic growth, depending on how they interact with institutional changes.

The divergence between Europe and Asia predates 1800, challenging views that attribute this gap to later developments (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This perspective highlights that the economic development of Europe and Asia was not uniform, with Europe experiencing more rapid growth, particularly in its northwestern regions. The divergence was driven by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture and industry, leading to higher productivity and economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that in northwestern Europe, institutional changes led to high agricultural labor productivity, which in turn drove economic development. This institutional framework allowed for the movement of labor from agriculture to industry and services, contributing to overall economic growth. Meanwhile, van Zanden (1999, p.2) posits that there was almost continuous growth of GDP per capita in Britain between 1068 and 1800, suggesting that economic development in Europe was not a sudden phenomenon but a gradual process. This view underscores the importance of long-term institutional changes in driving economic growth. The role of culture in this divergence remains less clear. While institutional changes were crucial, the cultural factors that supported or resisted these changes are not directly addressed in these studies. This gap in the literature suggests that future research should explore how cultural beliefs and norms interacted with institutional changes to shape long-run economic growth in Europe and Asia.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a persistent legacy that continues to shape African economies. The slave trade undermined state centralization and political stability, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, has had profound economic consequences. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization highlights how the slave trade's impact on political institutions has had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of this mistrust culture, coupled with the adverse effects on state development, underscores the deep and enduring impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory. This historical legacy of mistrust and political instability continues to shape African societies, influencing contemporary economic and political dynamics.

The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a central theme in the literature. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both underscore the significance of cultural beliefs and values in influencing economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, can motivate economic behaviors and shape institutions like guilds, which in turn affect economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates, which are culturally influenced, led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) highlights how cultural factors can deeply influence the institutional framework, which in turn affects economic development. These institutions, whether guilds or colonial governance structures, are embedded within cultural contexts that shape their function and impact.

Austin and North further elaborate on the institutional framework by emphasizing the dynamic nature of institutions and their interdependence. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that institutions evolve over time in response to various factors, including cultural influences (Austin 2008, p.2). North similarly argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. He shows that institutions like the Bank of England, which emerged from a financial revolution, played a crucial role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9) underscores the importance of understanding institutions as dynamic entities that are influenced by cultural and historical contexts.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show how the financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, highlighting the interconnectedness of cultural, institutional, and economic factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Akyeampong and Fofack also contribute to this discussion by highlighting the significant role of African women in economic growth and development, both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show that the gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production and economic development, further emphasizing the cultural dimensions of economic institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6) illustrates how cultural beliefs and gender roles are integral to the institutional framework that drives economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture plays a crucial role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the influence of culture on institutions is primarily through direct economic motivations or through broader social and historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence of the financial revolution in England, which provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, settles this disagreement. This historical case shows that cultural beliefs and values, embedded within broader social and historical contexts, directly influence economic institutions and drive long-run growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
