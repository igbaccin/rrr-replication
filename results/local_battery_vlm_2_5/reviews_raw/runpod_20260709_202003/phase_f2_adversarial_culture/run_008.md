The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values shape the institutional frameworks that underpin economic performance. One position argues that cultural factors, such as trust, norms, and social networks, facilitate coordinated actions and encourage institutions that favour economic growth. This view posits that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action games. Another position contends that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geographic conditions and mortality rates, which determine the type of institutions established in a region. This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on economic performance can be estimated by exploiting differences in these exogenous factors as a source of variation. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are a primary driver of institutional development or if they are secondary to other exogenous determinants. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic growth and for designing policies aimed at fostering development in contemporary societies. The debate on the role of culture in institutional development is closely linked to the broader question of how institutions themselves influence long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They established that the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This revolution was both an economic and a political phenomenon, driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The institutions that emerged from this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, which was crucial for long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The role of institutions in economic growth is complex. The financial revolution's impact on economic growth was not immediate; it took time for the institutions to mature and for their effects to be fully realized. North (1989, p.1) acknowledges that while institutions are critical, their influence on growth is mediated by other factors, including the evolution of technology and changes in resource allocation. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own.

Mokyr (2005, p.56) and de Vries (1994, p.18) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, highlighting the role of technological and intellectual advancements. This perspective emphasizes that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, facilitated the Industrial Revolution by establishing a framework for regional economic development. This framework was a precursor that enabled industrialization. The growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was crucial in this process. Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that much of the knowledge was embodied in skills and tacit knowledge, which moved about through personal transmission and training. This tacit knowledge was essential for the diffusion of useful knowledge across Europe, contributing to the broader intellectual movement that drove economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.18) cautions that while the Industrious Revolution released labor reserves to the market, it also led to the dissolution of traditional household functions, potentially destabilizing the reproduction function. This destabilization could limit the long-term sustainability of economic growth driven by useful knowledge.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This period saw a profound shift in economic thought, where new ideas about commerce, industry, and property rights began to take hold. McCloskey (2015, p.9) describes how this cultural shift was a complex interplay of new rhetoric and changing circumstances. The ideas of the Enlightenment, which emphasized reason, individualism, and progress, played a crucial role in shaping economic policies and practices. These new ideas challenged traditional notions of economic stagnation and paved the way for unprecedented growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) supports this view by highlighting that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited in the earlier years, suggesting that the diffusion of useful knowledge was contingent on the broader cultural and ideological context. This interplay between ideas, institutions, and economic growth underscores the complex nature of cultural influence on long-run economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Goldin, highlight that the role of women in economic growth has been significant, shaping long-run economic development through cultural norms and gender dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). The gendered division of labor, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, was crucial for economic productivity. Women were indispensable to community reproduction and the transmission of wealth in matrilineal societies, such as the Akan state, where women's bloodlines defined the inheritance of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This cultural framework underscores how gender dynamics influence economic systems, with women's roles in reproduction and community building directly tied to economic development. Goldin notes that these shifts were catalyzed by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These exogenous factors, including changes in household production technology and institutional barriers, illustrate how cultural and institutional frameworks can both enable and constrain women's economic roles. This interplay between cultural norms, institutional changes, and women's economic participation underscores the complex nature of gender dynamics in long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run growth. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, influenced by European settlers' mortality, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The impact of institutions on economic performance is robustly supported by the high correlation between settler mortality and current institutions. This correlation is underpinned by a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, which in turn influences income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) illustrate this relationship through a 2SLS estimate, showing a significant impact of institutions on income per capita. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may not benefit directly from economic gains or may fear losing political power with institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these extractive institutions do not encourage economic development, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic outcomes.

This cluster critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views, arguing that these perspectives oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Specifically, Austin (2008, p.2) argues that this approach has difficulty testing the institutionalist approach quantitatively and insufficiently explains why certain institutions that were apparently favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. Austin (2008, p.25) further supports this critique by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach considers Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changing their economic trajectories. Bryant (2006, p.4) builds on this by arguing that any attempt to overturn the reigning comprehension of the key determinants in the European trajectory, from ancient beginnings to modern consequences, would appear to be an audacious, unpromising venture. This implies that the complex interplay of culture, institutions, and economic growth is not easily reducible to simple geographical or institutional factors. The downstream implication is that a nuanced understanding of long-run growth requires considering the dynamic interplay of cultural, institutional, and geographical factors, which may not be captured by oversimplified theses.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that distributional conflicts are central to the formation of core economic institutions. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These policies, which favored elites, were often driven by the need to manage conflicts over resource distribution and maintain political stability. This dynamic is evident in the way European institutions evolved to balance the interests of various social classes, with economic efficiency often taking a backseat to distributional concerns. The downstream implication is that the interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design can significantly shape long-run growth trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the institutional framework as a whole may be more relevant to economic growth than individual institutions. This highlights the need for a holistic approach to understanding the role of institutions in long-run growth, one that considers the complex interplay of cultural, institutional, and geographical factors.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident by 1800, marking a significant departure from the views of 'world historians' who often attribute this divergence to later industrial developments. This early divergence is characterised by the lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living. The mechanism underlying this divergence involves the role of institutional change and investment in agriculture. Broadberry and Gupta observe that in northwestern Europe, institutional changes drove agricultural investment, leading to higher productivity. Labor shifted from agriculture to industry and services, so this did not result in abundant grain and low food prices (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This pattern contrasts with the stagnating regions of Europe and Asia, where economic development did not translate into comparable improvements in the standard of living. The specific role of silver wages is highlighted as a critical factor in this divergence, as it conferred purchasing power over tradable goods and services. Van Zanden's analysis reinforces this, noting that the development of silver wages reflected broader economic trends in Europe, particularly in England and London (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This economic performance in Europe and Asia before 1800 underscores the complex interplay between economic development and the standard of living, which can be further qualified by the role of cultural factors in shaping institutional frameworks and economic outcomes.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) together focus on specific technological and structural changes in agriculture that influenced long-term economic development. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity, which in turn contributed to broader economic growth. The role of social norms in property rights enforcement is also highlighted, as ranchers were encouraged to control cattle through norms. This mechanism of social norms is also found in Pamuk's work, where the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, and contributed to the rise of rural industries. Scarcity and the high cost of labor also ushered in an era of labor-saving technological innovations. This suggests that cultural factors, such as social norms and institutional changes, played a significant role in shaping the economic outcomes of these technological and structural changes in agriculture. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus underscored by these findings, as cultural factors influenced both the adoption of new technologies and the enforcement of property rights.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27) identify how the slave trade shaped African development through the lens of cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which persist and affect long-term economic outcomes. This impact is seen in the enduring political instability and weakened states that resulted from the slave trades, which in turn hindered economic performance. The slave trade's legacy of political instability and mistrust is a key mechanism through which it influenced economic development. This political instability, in turn, has fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Mistrust is not the only cultural factor at play.

The new economic history of Africa, as championed by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), highlights the importance of institutional variables in explaining development. This literature emphasises that current institutions in Africa are deeply rooted in historical legacies that extend beyond colonial rule into precolonial and even prehistorical times. This historical depth of institutions underscores their gradual evolution and the need for a nuanced understanding of how past events shape present economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) posits that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions. This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster development-inducing institutions. This variation suggests that while institutional congruence is crucial, other factors also play significant roles in economic development. Englebert's (Hopkins 2009, p.18) work on ethnic fragmentation further elaborates on this point, arguing that high levels of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions can reduce regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutional reforms in Africa depends on their alignment with historical institutions and their ability to address contemporary challenges such as ethnic fragmentation and political instability. Therefore, understanding the historical context of institutional development in Africa is essential for designing effective policies that promote long-run growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is pivotal for understanding long-run growth, as cultural beliefs and values shape the institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder economic development. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, illustrating how non-economic cultural beliefs influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin supports this view, emphasizing that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences of older industrial economies are crucial for understanding long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2).

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further underscores the significance of cultural influences on institutional development. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system, labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural factors, by shaping these institutions, play a critical role in economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, which led to a decline in transaction costs and the development of new financial instruments, illustrates how cultural and institutional changes can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The historical context of institutional development in Africa provides further evidence of the role of culture in shaping institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender roles and cultural beliefs in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they shape the transaction costs and property rights that underpin economic development (North 1989, p.1). The role of culture in dominating institutions is evident in the historical development of African societies, where cultural beliefs and values have shaped the institutional frameworks that govern economic activity.

The fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply rooted in the interplay between culture and institutions. The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise mechanisms by which culture influences institutions remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, highlighting the role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, which highlights Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available (Austin 2008, p.25). This approach underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutional development and economic growth.
