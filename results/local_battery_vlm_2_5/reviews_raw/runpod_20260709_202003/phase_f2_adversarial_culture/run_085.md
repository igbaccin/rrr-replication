The role of culture in shaping institutions and driving long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural values and beliefs can serve as the primary drivers of institutional development and, consequently, economic growth. On one side, scholars argue that cultural factors, such as social capital and trust, are pivotal in fostering institutions that promote economic efficiency and growth. This perspective posits that cultural norms can influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby shaping long-term economic performance. On the other side, scholars contend that institutions are primarily shaped by economic factors, such as factor endowments and mortality rates, which drive institutional development and economic growth independently of cultural influences. This debate hinges on whether culture is a foundational force that underpins institutional development or merely a secondary factor influenced by economic and political conditions. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the mechanisms of economic growth and the potential for policy interventions to foster development. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, and understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the long-term trajectories of economic growth. The key question is whether cultural values are antecedent to institutions, or whether institutions are shaped by economic factors that render cultural influences secondary. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They demonstrate that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was critical for enhancing the security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). The institutions that facilitated this revolution played a necessary role in enabling economic growth and political freedom. The creation of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, further bolstering economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The implications of these institutional changes reach beyond immediate economic benefits. Jeffrey Williamson's work highlights that while other forces might have eventually led to England's success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without the institutional framework established during this period (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were a catalyst for immediate growth and a foundational element that ensured sustained economic development.

Ogilvie and others (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are the key to explaining the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These conflicts shaped institutions like serfdom and guilds, which were efficient responses to economic conditions and the result of power struggles among elites and commoners. The institutions that emerged from these conflicts were often inefficient but served to maintain the status quo and distribute resources in favor of the ruling elites. The distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe were not just about economic efficiency; they were about power and control. This is evident in the persistence of institutions like serfdom, which restricted the mobility of labor and maintained a hierarchical social structure. This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are always efficient responses to economic conditions. Ogilvie highlights that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). If institutions are interdependent, then studying them in isolation can be misleading. The European family system, for instance, was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions. This interconnected framework contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The persistence of these institutional frameworks often hindered economic growth by maintaining inequalities and restricting competition.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This struggle involved a new rhetoric that created new circumstances, which in turn fostered new rhetoric, ultimately leading to the Great Enrichment. Mokyr (2005, p.56) asserts that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.14) further elaborates that much of the useful knowledge in eighteenth-century Europe was tacit, moving about through personal transmission and training. This tacit knowledge, along with codified knowledge, was crucial for economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.9) provides a concrete example of this mechanism: the privileged young engineers in France marching under a banner inscribed with a motto that would strike others as hilariously antique and unbusinesslike. Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies the implications of this mechanism by emphasizing that the diffusion of knowledge within the West was less about national differences and more about commonalities. The growth of useful knowledge, both tacit and codified, spread widely, driving economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and de Vries (1994, p.18) highlight that technological advances and social changes, such as the Industrious Revolution, played a pivotal role in economic growth by increasing labor input and altering consumption patterns. The Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, involved a significant increase in labor input and new consumption patterns, setting the stage for further economic development. This period saw a shift in how labor was utilized, with households releasing their labor reserves to the market and yielding practical functions to market- and government-supplied services, which fundamentally altered the economic landscape (de Vries 1994, p.18). This transformation was about technological progress and social changes that enabled greater productivity and economic growth, as Abramovitz notes, with productivity levels becoming more stable over time and social competence for exploiting advanced methods increasing (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). De Vries (1994, p.18) emphasizes that Europe's urban network, connecting its many cities in systems of commercial interaction, was an achievement of the early modern period, establishing a framework for regional economic development in which industrial growth could occur. The Industrious Revolution facilitated the Industrial Revolution by creating a more dynamic and interconnected economic environment. The shared finding among these authors is that the Industrious Revolution was a critical precursor to the Industrial Revolution, setting the stage for the latter's success. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the broader social and technological changes that underlie economic growth.

Colonialism's impact on economic growth was driven by the institutional changes it introduced. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies determined the type of institutions established, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. The high mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions persisted over time, creating a framework that hindered economic development. This persistence is evident in the strong correlation between early institutions and current institutional quality, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is complex. The extractive nature of these institutions meant that they were not designed to encourage economic development. The institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who aimed to maximize their rents. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism thus had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies, creating a legacy that continues to influence economic performance today.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, show that the slave trade has had a persistent negative impact on African economic development through its effects on culture and institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts trust levels. This cultural shift is a rival explanation to the broader claim that culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a multifaceted issue. Institutions, as defined by North, are the rules of the game in a society (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and the development of new financial instruments, which were critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shows that the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework is essential for understanding economic development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the craft guild, and that exogenous cultural theories can influence the structure of economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of 'social capital' suggests that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This indicates that cultural beliefs and values can shape institutional frameworks that promote economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This demonstrates that historical factors can influence the development of institutions that either promote or hinder economic growth.

The legacy of mistrust, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, has had a profound impact on economic development in Africa, as mistrust makes it difficult for people to cooperate and work together (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the initial conditions and the institutions that evolve from them play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural beliefs and values, or historical factors such as mortality rates and initial conditions, are the primary drivers of institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the impact of the slave trade on trust in Africa, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon, which demonstrates the enduring influence of cultural factors on institutional development and economic growth.
