The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional scholarship. At stake is the very nature of economic development, with different scholars advocating for cultural, institutional, or geographic explanations. The disagreement centers around whether cultural factors, such as norms and beliefs, are the primary drivers of economic growth by shaping institutions, or if institutions themselves are the key determinants, shaped by exogenous factors like geography or mortality rates. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy and development strategies, as it influences how resources are allocated and interventions are designed. For instance, if culture is the primary driver, policies aimed at changing cultural norms might be prioritized. If institutions are the primary cause, efforts to reform institutions directly could be more effective. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, with scholars like Ogilvie and North highlighting the importance of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions and growth, while others like Acemoglu et al. and Austin emphasize the role of exogenous factors and the evolution of institutions over time. The dynamic interplay between culture and institutions, and their respective roles in long-run growth, is a crucial area of inquiry. This debate is particularly salient when considering the historical and contemporary development trajectories of different regions, as it sheds light on the underlying mechanisms that drive economic growth and stagnation. A comprehensive understanding of this relationship is essential for crafting effective policies that can foster sustainable development. The question of whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of economic growth through their influence on institutions, or if institutions themselves are the key determinants shaped by exogenous factors, is a central theme in the literature. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The role of institutions as fundamental determinants of economic growth is well-established (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). North argues that institutions are a critical factor in economic growth, challenging traditional neoclassical theories that focus on population and savings. North contends that the neoclassical model, which assumes zero transaction costs, fails to account for the real-world impact of institutions. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that mortality rates in European colonies influenced the development of extractive institutions, which in turn affected current income per capita. These institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth. In former European colonies, these groups designed institutions to maximize their own benefits, fostering economic development. This dynamic underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic outcomes, even when they are extractive. Acemoglu et al. qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development, as they are often designed to maximize rents for powerful groups, promoting long-run growth. Thus, the institutional framework established during colonial periods continues to influence economic trajectories, highlighting the enduring significance of institutions in shaping economic growth.

North and Weingast, along with Sokoloff and Engerman, highlight the significance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth and shaping societal outcomes, a perspective that contrasts with cultural determinism (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, for instance, was pivotal in securing property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This was achieved through the development of a stable market for public debt, which created a positive externality for the private debt market (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional evolution was a deliberate outcome of public choice institutions that prioritized economic development. Similarly, in the New World, the institutional frameworks that emerged were heavily influenced by the initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality. These institutions, shaped by the colonial environment, played a critical role in perpetuating inequality by favoring elites and restricting competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The enduring impact of these institutions underscores their role in economic development, as they continue to influence economic trajectories long after their initial establishment. This perspective is nuanced by the recognition that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness in promoting growth can be constrained by the extent of inequality and the power dynamics they embody. Thus, the institutional framework not only drives economic growth but also reflects and reinforces societal inequalities, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes.

The slave trades in Africa had a profound adverse effect on economic development, suggesting that historical events like slavery can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn highlight that the slave trades caused a culture of mistrust and political instability, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has had a significant impact on economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation. Moreover, the slave trades led to long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. These states were unable to provide the necessary public goods and services that are crucial for economic growth. Furthermore, the history of slavery has been linked to modern corruption, which further hampers economic development. The extent to which these historical events continue to affect economic development is qualified by the recognition that contemporary factors, such as colonial boundaries and malaria transmission, also play a role. The ongoing impact of these factors suggests that while historical events like slavery have had a significant adverse effect on economic development, they are not the sole determinants of Africa's current underdevelopment.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both argue that the spread of ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic changes in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Mokyr (2005, p.56) aligns with this perspective by highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. This useful knowledge was codified and tacit, moving through personal transmission and training, and it was diffused widely across the Western world. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further elaborates that the diffusion of knowledge was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which made national differences less important than the commonalities in the generation of useful knowledge. The implication is that the diffusion of ideas and knowledge was deeply intertwined with cultural and intellectual shifts. Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies this by noting that the codification of knowledge was a function of technology and costs, and tacit knowledge remained an essential part of the knowledge base. This qualification suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, the mechanisms of their diffusion and the role of tacit knowledge were also significant factors in economic growth. The interplay between ideas, institutions, and culture thus shaped the economic trajectory of Northwestern Europe.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) argue that the Great Divergence between the West and the East was already underway before 1800, driven by structural changes and economic developments. Pamuk (2007, p.24) highlights how the Black Death in the mid-14th century initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death reduced the labor force and brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to the guilds, contributing to the rise of rural industries and labor-saving technological innovations. These changes were demographic and fundamentally altered the economic landscape. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) support this view by showing that in the advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in northwestern Europe, but silver wages, which conferred purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were substantially lower. This wage disparity reflects broader economic differences that were already evident by the early modern period. This productivity did not result in an abundance of grain and low food prices because labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. The implication is that while structural changes and economic developments were crucial, the role of culture in shaping these changes and their long-term impact on economic growth remains an open question.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping pre-industrial European institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups, and by efficiency considerations. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development is further complicated by the interdependencies of various institutions within a wider framework. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that the interdependency of individual institutions, such as the European family system and the community-based welfare system, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interconnectedness suggests that economic growth was driven by the broader institutional framework. The role of culture in shaping these institutions and their long-term impact on economic growth remains under-examined. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further research into the cultural dimensions of economic institutions and their contributions to long-run growth.

Economic transformations and property rights have influenced societal changes, such as women's employment and agricultural development (Goldin 2006, p.8; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). Goldin and Hornbeck establish that exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly altered labor supply and agricultural development. These changes were driven by shifts in relative demand, technological advancements, and institutional reforms, which collectively transformed social norms and expectations regarding women's roles in the workforce and the protection of property rights (Goldin 2006, p.3; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). One mechanism through which these transformations occurred was the alteration of social norms and expectations, which in turn influenced individual behaviors and societal structures. For instance, the growth of educational institutions and the availability of part-time work for married women altered female labor supply, while the introduction of barbed wire fencing enabled farmers to better protect their property rights. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) qualifies this pattern by noting that in some settings, social norms can encourage behaviors that go beyond strict legal responsibilities. This suggests that while property rights and economic transformations are crucial, the underlying cultural and social norms play a significant role in shaping their impact on long-run growth. Thus, the interplay between economic transformations, property rights, and cultural norms is essential for understanding the broader institutional framework that supports long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify the causes of long-run growth by compressing historical periods and paths, and focusing on environmental obstacles (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) argue that this approach fails to account for the dynamic, endogenous responses of societies to their environments and the complex interplay of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms. This perspective suggests that long-run growth is a product of a complex interplay of factors that evolve over time. Bryant (2006, p.35) qualifies this view by noting that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. This implies that while cultural norms and social structures play a significant role in shaping long-run growth, they are not the sole determinants.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) discusses how cultural norms and beliefs, such as those related to the afterlife, can influence economic institutions and efficiency. For instance, Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, which in turn shaped the economic structure. This perspective is echoed by North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24), who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is recognized by some studies of European economic institutions, suggesting that these cultural and institutional factors are interlinked and contribute to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of cultural norms in economic growth is further supported by the work of Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4; 2014, p.6), who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This shows that cultural norms and social structures, which include gender roles, play a significant part in shaping economic institutions and long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.25) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective suggests that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, involving various historical periods and paths. The shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) shows that the role of culture in shaping economic institutions and long-run growth is significant and complex. The precise mechanisms by which cultural norms influence economic growth remain a subject of ongoing debate.

The underlying point of agreement is that culture significantly influences institutions that drive long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the role of cultural norms in shaping economic institutions and long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, involving various historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence and empirical comparison that settles the disagreement are the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as shown by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4). This evidence demonstrates that cultural norms and social structures, including gender roles, play a significant part in shaping economic institutions and long-run growth.
