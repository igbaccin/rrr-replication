The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the intellectual struggle between scholars who view culture as a fundamental driver of institutional development and those who see it as a secondary factor. Some scholars contend that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. This perspective posits that cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, can enhance the efficiency of societal coordination, which in turn supports economic development. In opposition, other scholars argue that geographical factors, such as mortality rates and climate, are more critical in shaping institutions that drive economic growth. This debate is significant because it determines whether policies should focus on cultural reforms or address geographical constraints. The question of how culture influences institutions and economic growth remains unresolved, with scholars divided on the primacy of cultural versus geographical factors. This disagreement underscores the need to explore the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and long-run economic growth, as well as the mechanisms through which these factors interact. This mistrust, in turn, has a detrimental effect on economic development. The slave trade's impact on African development is not limited to the past. It continues to shape current economic and social dynamics. For example, the mistrust generated by the slave trade can hinder cooperation and reduce the effectiveness of institutions. This is because mistrust can make it difficult for people to work together and trust in the institutions that govern their society. This can lead to a vicious cycle of underdevelopment, where mistrust hinders economic growth, which in turn reinforces mistrust. The historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as the slave trade, are a key factor in understanding the origins of trust. These historical determinants can have long-lasting effects on economic and social development. This is because they shape the cultural attitudes and norms that govern behavior in society. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust is a clear example of how historical events can shape cultural attitudes in ways that have long-lasting economic and social impacts.

The institutional framework established in seventeenth-century England, as analyzed by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30), shows how secure property rights can drive economic growth. This institutional evolution was marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically declined the cost of transacting and reflected increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutions provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt was pivotal in this process. This institutional framework provided the foundation for a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of a market for private debt. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes can create positive externalities that drive economic growth. However, the precise mechanisms by which these institutions fostered economic growth are complex and multifaceted, involving not just the financial sector but also the broader political and economic landscape.

This perspective shifts the focus from cultural determinants to the role of distributional conflicts in shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as discussed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4). Ogilvie argues that the persistence of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the power struggles between different social groups, rather than any efficiency gains these institutions might have provided. This dynamic is evident in the interplay between social classes and the state, where elites often used their political influence to maintain institutions that perpetuated their economic advantages. For instance, Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were shaped by elite interests, which restricted competition and maintained the status quo. This pattern of institutional persistence through distributional conflicts has significant implications for economic development. Ogilvie cautions that the interconnectedness of institutions within a broader framework can complicate efforts to isolate the effects of any single institution (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) challenge this view, asserting that ideas and ideologies were the primary catalysts for economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. These ideas catalysed economic growth by fostering an environment where useful knowledge could thrive. This perspective highlights the role of cultural shifts in promoting economic change, as the dissemination of ideas and ideologies enabled the growth of useful knowledge. Mokyr's analysis underscores that the diffusion of this knowledge spread throughout Europe via periodicals, translations, and personal communications. This diffusion was crucial for the Great Enrichment, as it allowed for the widespread adoption of new ideas and technologies. However, Mokyr also notes that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practices that were not easily codified or transmitted through written documents (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge required personal transmission and training, which could limit its diffusion and impact on economic growth. The interplay between codified and tacit knowledge reveals that while ideas and ideologies were powerful drivers of economic change, the effectiveness of their transmission depended on the cultural and social context in which they were embedded. This context could either facilitate or hinder the spread of useful knowledge, thereby influencing long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) extend this argument by emphasizing the role of colonial institutional changes as a pivotal factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers influenced the type of institutions established in their colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The relationship between European mortality rates and institutional outcomes is strong, providing a robust basis for understanding how colonial experiences shaped long-term economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize their own interests over economic development. This implies that the institutional legacies of colonialism have profound and enduring effects on economic performance.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) share the view that historical paths and institutional evolutions are complex and varied. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia reflect distinct historical trajectories and institutional changes. These trajectories are influenced by the interplay between geography, technology, and endogenous responses to environmental challenges. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the African experience illustrates a dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach emphasizes how Africans' responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, ultimately shaped their economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further illustrate this complexity by noting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They argue that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 resembled the stagnating regions of Europe. This divergence was driven by differences in institutional change, investment in agriculture, and labor productivity. The implication is that institutional evolutions were not uniform across regions and were influenced by a multitude of factors, including geographical and technological conditions, as well as endogenous responses to these conditions. The complexity of these interactions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on long-run growth.

Culture's role in shaping institutions and influencing long-run growth is multifaceted. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both highlight the influence of cultural beliefs and values on economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, can motivate economic behaviors and institutional structures. In pre-industrial Europe, Catholic beliefs influenced the rules of guilds, and the Protestant Reformation's shift in beliefs led to guilds' decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to distinct institutional frameworks in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These frameworks were influenced by the cultural and social contexts of the colonizers. Together, these studies demonstrate that cultural factors can shape institutions by influencing the rules and behaviors that underpin them, and that these institutions, in turn, affect long-run growth by determining the efficiency and productivity of economic activities.

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks further underscores culture's role. Ogilvie and North both emphasize the interconnectedness of institutions. Ogilvie notes that institutions are interdependent within a wider framework, making it necessary to study entire institutional systems rather than individual institutions in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and secure property rights, which are critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This interconnectedness means that cultural influences on one institution can have ripple effects across the entire institutional framework, thereby shaping long-run growth trajectories. For example, the English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, both of which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shows how cultural shifts, such as those driven by the Protestant Reformation, can lead to institutional changes that foster economic growth.

Cultural factors also influence long-run growth by shaping social capital and trust. Ogilvie and Nunn and Wantchekon both discuss the role of social capital and trust in economic performance. Ogilvie mentions Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that features like trust, norms, and networks can improve economic efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it hinders the formation of social capital and the efficient functioning of institutions. The persistence of these cultural factors over centuries highlights their enduring impact on long-run growth. The historical evidence shows that cultural beliefs and values shape institutions, which in turn influence long-run growth through their effects on social capital and trust. The empirical comparison of Northern and Southern Italy by Putnam, and the historical analysis of the slave trade by Nunn and Wantchekon, provide specific examples of how cultural factors can shape institutions and long-run growth.
