The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors versus institutional frameworks in understanding economic growth trajectories. One prominent position argues that cultural norms and beliefs, shaped by historical experiences such as colonialism and the slave trade, significantly influence the development of institutions and, consequently, economic growth. This perspective posits that cultural factors can either facilitate or hinder institutional development, thereby affecting long-term economic performance. In contrast, another position emphasizes the primacy of institutional frameworks, suggesting that the quality of institutions, such as property rights and governance structures, directly determines economic growth, with culture playing a secondary or mediating role. The debate centers on whether cultural factors are independent variables that shape institutions or dependent variables that are shaped by institutions. The resolution of this debate has implications for policy and theoretical frameworks in economic development, as it informs whether interventions should target cultural change or institutional reform. The question of how culture interacts with institutions in driving long-run growth remains a critical area of inquiry, with significant implications for understanding the economic trajectories of nations. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional development is a key area where these positions converge and diverge. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, driven by high European mortality rates, significantly impacted long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Acemoglu et al. and North and Weingast both highlight that the institutional frameworks established in colonial settings were crucial in shaping economic trajectories. High mortality rates among European settlers led to the creation of extractive institutions, which were designed to exploit local resources rather than foster sustainable development. These institutions, characterised by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities for the local population, persisted over time and significantly hindered economic growth. North and Weingast illustrate that the financial revolution in England, which facilitated secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, was pivotal in fostering economic growth. This contrasts with the extractive institutions in colonies, which lacked similar mechanisms for promoting economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman build on this by explaining that the initial inequality in New World societies, shaped by extractive institutions, persisted over time. They argue that these institutions advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality and impeding economic growth. This dynamic underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic performance, as the initial conditions set by extractive institutions created a lasting legacy of inequality and underdevelopment.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.27) share a focus on how institutional variables and historical events, such as slavery and colonial rule, have profoundly shaped Africa's development trajectory (Hopkins 2009, p.8)(Nunn 2008, p.27). These scholars underscore the significance of institutional change, suggesting that current institutions are deeply rooted in precolonial history and even prehistory. This historical depth implies that understanding Africa's economic development requires a long-term perspective that considers the gradual evolution of institutions. For instance, the legacy of slave trades has had a lasting impact on state centralization and political stability, which are crucial for economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrates that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, has fostered conditions that hinder economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further elaborates that effective institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions, underscoring the importance of congruence between contemporary and traditional institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster development-inducing institutions. However, the historical legacy of slavery and colonial rule has created a complex web of institutional challenges that persist today. Nunn (2008, p.27) explicitly connects Africa's history of slavery to modern corruption, illustrating how historical events have shaped contemporary institutional environments (Nunn 2008, p.1). This historical perspective is crucial for understanding the root causes of Africa's underdevelopment and for informing institutional reforms that can address these deep-seated issues.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) both show that shifts in women's roles have significantly influenced economic performance across regions. Goldin identifies a series of exogenous changes that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. These changes, including increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions, altered the labor supply and created new opportunities for women. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that these shifts were the result of specific historical and institutional factors. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that regional economic performance was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. These patterns of growth and decline were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The implications of these findings extend to the broader understanding of economic development, suggesting that institutional changes and shifts in labor dynamics can drive significant economic growth. The complex interplay of these factors suggests that understanding the role of women's roles in economic performance requires a nuanced view of historical and institutional contexts.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) contend that the growth of useful knowledge and ideas were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey argues that the cultural struggle, or the "Franco-French War, " played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth. This struggle, driven by new rhetoric and ideologies, fostered an environment conducive to economic development. Mokyr builds on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. This useful knowledge was not confined to written texts but also included tacit knowledge, which was crucial for economic progress. Tacit knowledge, often embodied in skills and practices, was transmitted through personal interactions and training, highlighting the importance of social networks and cultural exchange in disseminating knowledge. However, Mokyr cautions that the diffusion of useful knowledge was not uniform across all regions, suggesting that cultural and institutional factors played a role in determining which regions benefited most from this knowledge.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. They share a view that the 'simple geography' thesis is inadequate, which posits that unchanging environmental obstacles solely determine economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) offers a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of economic development, suggesting that Africans' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately drove changes. This dynamic view highlights that economic development is shaped by the endogenous responses of populations to their environments. This implies that the institutional frameworks in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering sustainable economic development. Therefore, the impact of colonialism on institutional changes in former colonies was complex and multifaceted, involving both exogenous and endogenous factors.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may not always prioritize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is aligned with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast, was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic development.
