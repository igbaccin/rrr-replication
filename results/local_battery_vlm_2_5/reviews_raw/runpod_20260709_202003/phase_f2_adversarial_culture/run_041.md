The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors in shaping institutional frameworks that foster economic growth. The debate hinges on whether cultural beliefs and values independently influence economic institutions, or whether institutions are primarily shaped by other factors such as geography, technology, or political dynamics. One position argues that cultural norms and values, such as trust and reciprocity, can facilitate coordinated actions and encourage institutions that favor economic growth. This perspective is often associated with the concept of 'social capital', which emphasizes the role of cultural beliefs in improving societal efficiency. For instance, Putnam contends that 'civic' cultures, characterized by high levels of trust and horizontal social networks, have led to superior economic performance in regions like northern Italy since medieval times. Another position posits that cultural beliefs and values are not the primary drivers of institutional change. Instead, it suggests that economic institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as mortality rates, which influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in mediating the impact of exogenous shocks on economic performance. The disagreement revolves around the extent to which cultural factors can be considered a fundamental cause of long-run growth, or whether they are merely one of many factors that influence institutional development. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of public choice institutions in England during the seventeenth century was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This financial revolution was a pivotal moment in English economic history, as it facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional change provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt were critical in fostering economic growth and political freedom. The role of cultural factors in shaping these institutional developments is still debated.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) extend this analysis by asserting that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run growth. They show that European colonies with high mortality rates among settlers developed extractive institutions, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The mechanism involves the initial conditions of European settlement, which shaped the institutions established in these colonies. Mortality rates shaped the types of institutions implemented, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This pattern is supported by a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this perspective by highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin suggests that African responses to their environments, utilizing available technologies, played a role in shaping institutional outcomes. This dynamic view implies that while initial institutional conditions were crucial, the subsequent evolution of institutions was influenced by endogenous factors, including local responses and technological adaptations. This nuanced perspective underscores the complex interplay between initial conditions, institutional development, and long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) challenge this perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts, not efficiency or cultural factors, shaped the fundamental economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These authors argue that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the elite's desire to maintain their power and control over resources. Ogilvie's analysis of the craft guilds supports this perspective, arguing that they were established to restrict competition and maintain the elite's control over resources. This distributional conflict mechanism is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, which shows that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism suggests that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were designed to maintain the elite's control over resources. Ogilvie acknowledges that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework may complicate this picture, as different institutions may resist the encroachment of distributional conflicts and promote growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) further complicate the picture by emphasizing that ideas and knowledge, not just institutions, were pivotal drivers of economic growth. McCloskey posits that the shift in ideologies and rhetoric in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. This intellectual transformation was a catalyst for institutional changes. The focus on ideas and ideologies underscores the role of cultural shifts in shaping economic trajectories. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth, noting that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe facilitated the Great Enrichment. The spread of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was crucial for economic development. Mokyr underscores that the transmission of tacit knowledge, often through personal interactions and training, was as vital as written knowledge. This mechanism of knowledge diffusion, combined with the ideological shifts McCloskey discusses, suggests that cultural and intellectual changes were fundamental drivers of economic growth. Mokyr acknowledges that the codification of knowledge was also influenced by technological advancements and the payoff from efforts to codify tacit knowledge. This qualification indicates that while ideas and knowledge were primary drivers, technological progress played a complementary role in the economic growth of Northwestern Europe.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) (Nunn 2008, p.27) provide a historical example of how cultural attitudes, shaped by events like the slave trade, can have lasting economic impacts. This historical trauma, combined with political instability, led to weakened and fragmented states. The mechanism of mistrust is one of the most important determinants of long-term economic development. The slave trade's impact on economic development includes the long-term cultural and political consequences. Nunn (2008, p.1) notes that corruption is another enduring legacy of slavery, which can further hinder economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon's (2011, p.4) evidence highlights the importance of historical events in shaping cultural attitudes that influence long-term economic outcomes. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the deep historical roots of cultural attitudes and their impact on economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) posit that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. Their findings, shared with van Zanden (1999, p.2), highlight that high productivity in agriculture and the associated economic development in Europe did not uniformly lead to higher standards of living. This observation underscores the complexity of economic development, where increased productivity does not always translate into widespread prosperity. For instance, in England, high agricultural productivity did not necessarily result in lower food prices or an abundance of grain. Instead, labor migrated from agriculture into industry and services. The divergence in economic growth between Europe and Asia during this period is also reflected in the differing trajectories of urban development and real wages. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the high productivity of agriculture in southern India was driven by significant investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This investment did not lead to the same economic outcomes as in Europe, where institutional changes drove agricultural investment and productivity. This disparity suggests that the cultural and institutional frameworks in Europe and Asia played crucial roles in shaping economic development, even before the industrial revolution. The divergence in economic growth between Europe and Asia highlights the importance of understanding the deep historical roots of cultural attitudes and their impact on economic development.

The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a critical area of study in economic history. Acemoglu et al. and North show that institutions governing property rights and public choice are essential for economic development. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional framework allowed for the development of a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, highlighting the necessity of institutions in economic growth. The English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was critical (North 1989, p.9). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This framework was crucial for early industrialization and capitalism.

The cultural underpinnings of these institutional frameworks are also significant. Ogilvie highlights how cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural approach shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. The cultural approach to economic institutions is exemplified by Putnam's theory of 'social capital', which refers to those features of social organization, such as trust, norms, and networks, that can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Trust, norms, and networks are key features of social organization that can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4). The historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior are crucial for understanding the origins of trust, which is a key determinant of economic growth. The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This mistrust is a significant factor in the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust in Africa today.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the historical and empirical evidence presented. Institutions matter in economic growth by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). The cultural underpinnings of these institutional frameworks are also significant, as cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the cultural approach provides a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, which has been recognized by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The historical evidence shows that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.
