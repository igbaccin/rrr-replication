The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors in shaping economic institutions and, consequently, economic performance over the long term. One position holds that cultural values and beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. This view posits that cultural norms can either facilitate or hinder the development of institutions that support economic growth. Another position, however, argues that the impact of culture on institutions is indirect and mediated through other factors, such as distributional conflicts or exogenous shocks. This debate underscores the complexity of the relationship between culture, institutions, and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive long-term economic development. The resolution of this debate will enhance the predictive power of economic models and inform policy interventions aimed at fostering sustainable growth. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They identify the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as pivotal in reducing transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights over time. North (1989, p.1) elaborates on this by highlighting that these institutions enabled the emergence of a stable market for public debt, which had a positive externality on the development of a market for private debt (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the necessary conditions for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The implications of these developments are significant, as they underscore the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth and political freedom. This nuanced perspective highlights that while institutions played a critical role, the path to economic growth was multifaceted and involved various interconnected factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World were fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency or cultural factors. These conflicts led to the creation and perpetuation of institutions that favored elites. For instance, in the New World, the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to the development of institutions that restricted competition and maintained elite advantages (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This pattern is evident in the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe as well, where Ogilvie observes that the interdependency of institutions, such as the European family system and community-based welfare systems, contributed to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The role of cultural factors in shaping these institutions is complex. This indicates that while distributional conflicts are a primary driver, cultural factors can also play a significant role in the development of economic institutions. This interplay between distributional conflicts and cultural factors highlights the complex nature of institutional development and its impact on long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. These authors highlight that European mortality rates in colonies influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persisted over time. This mechanism is supported by a strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, which in turn significantly affects income per capita. These institutions continue to shape contemporary economic performance. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, highlights the enduring effects of colonial institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, who may resist changes that do not directly benefit them. This dynamic illustrates how institutional changes due to colonialism can lock in economic disparities, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant of long-run growth.

The critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) underscores the need for a more balanced view of cultural and institutional interactions. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the institutional legacies of colonialism are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, compresses different historical periods and paths, leading to an oversimplification of causation. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that this approach fails to account for the dynamic and endogenous processes that shape economic development. This is evident in the African story, where Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the course of economic development. This dynamic, endogenous process is a key mechanism through which culture interacts with institutions to shape long-run growth. Bryant (2006, p.35) highlights that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, further underscoring the complexity of the relationship between culture and institutions. The revisionist preference for restricting effective or salient causality to shorter-term and immediate processes is prone to explanatory distortions. Long-run growth is influenced by both institutional legacies and the cultural and social dynamics that shape these institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that culture is the primary determinant of long-run growth, suggesting instead that the interaction between culture and institutions is a more nuanced and complex process.

The pivotal role of women's economic participation, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8), indicates that cultural norms surrounding gender can significantly influence long-run growth dynamics. Women's contributions to economic development are fundamentally intertwined with the cultural and institutional frameworks that shape growth. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that pre-colonial and colonial periods saw women as indispensable to community reproduction, particularly in matrilineal societies where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This underscores the importance of cultural norms in shaping economic roles and institutional structures. Goldin's analysis of the quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. illustrates how shifts in social norms and institutional changes can transform economic participation and growth (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes include the increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions, which altered female labor supply and made part-time work more accessible. Goldin's work further shows how changes in household production technology and the removal of barriers to the employment of married women facilitated women's entry into the workforce. This dynamic suggests that institutional changes, driven by cultural shifts, can significantly impact economic growth by altering labor supply and demand. This implies that while cultural and institutional changes can drive growth, the persistence of certain norms and roles can act as a barrier to sustained economic development.

The enduring effects of the slave trade on African economic development, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) (Nunn 2008, p.27), illustrate how historical cultural legacies can persistently hinder economic progress. This mistrust, fostered by the exploitative nature of the slave trade, created a social environment where cooperation and collective action became challenging. The historical trauma of the slave trade led to a persistent lack of trust among individuals, which in turn hindered the development of strong institutions and effective governance. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability is a crucial factor in understanding the current economic underdevelopment of Africa, as it has impeded the establishment of stable and effective institutions necessary for sustained growth. The legacy of slavery is evident in the high levels of corruption and weak state institutions that persist today, as Nunn (2008, p.1) notes, the history of slavery can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment. However, it is important to acknowledge that while the slave trade played a significant role, other factors such as colonialism and recent events have also contributed to Africa's economic challenges. Thus, addressing the legacy of the slave trade requires a multifaceted approach that considers the complex interplay of historical, political, and social factors.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, with cultural beliefs and values influencing the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie explains that distributional conflicts, more than efficiency, account for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective is supported by Richardson's contention that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital highlights that cultures characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. Putnam argues that these 'civic' cultures explain the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial, as recognized by studies of European economic institutions. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further emphasized by North's argument that institutions matter due to positive transaction costs, contrary to neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. North points out that the neoclassical model holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs, but with positive transaction costs, institutions become critical. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast also highlight the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England as crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show how the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, providing a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This underscores the importance of cultural and institutional factors in driving long-run growth.

The legacy of slavery and colonialism has significantly impacted African economic development, with cultural factors playing a crucial role in shaping institutions. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of African economic development. This perspective is supported by the role of African women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as argued by Akyeampong and Fofack. They show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The enduring impact of cultural and institutional factors on long-run growth is evident in the persistent effects of historical institutions and the contributions of African women to economic development.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the interdependency of cultural beliefs, values, and economic institutions. The underlying point of agreement is that cultural factors significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, as shown by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which historical institutions, such as those established during colonialism, persist and affect current income per capita, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of African economic development, as highlighted by Austin. This is shown by the measurement of European mortality rates and their correlation with current institutions, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run growth.
