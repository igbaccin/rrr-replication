The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development scholarship. At stake is the explanation for why some societies prosper economically while others stagnate, with implications for policy and theory. The debate centres on whether cultural factors are primary drivers of economic growth or whether they are secondary to other institutional and structural factors. Some scholars argue that culture shapes institutions, influencing economic outcomes. Others contend that institutions emerge from political and economic processes, with culture playing a secondary or even negligible role. Still others propose that cultural factors are antecedents to institutional development, which in turn drives economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They trace the establishment of secure property rights to the financial revolution that began with the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. This revolution was a direct result of the evolving political institutions that provided the necessary stability for economic growth. This institutional framework, which secured property rights and fostered economic growth, was contingent on the political stability achieved through the Glorious Revolution of 1688. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) also acknowledge that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. This institutional stability, however, was not a foregone conclusion. North notes that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for the institutional changes that followed. The evolving institutions governing public choice, therefore, were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth in seventeenth-century England.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) identify ideas and ideologies as the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey posits that the change in rhetoric and ideologies, especially the rise of bourgeois values, played a crucial role in fostering economic growth. McCloskey maintains that this cultural shift was a driving force in its own right, not merely a response to institutional changes. Mokyr supports this perspective by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr notes that the diffusion of knowledge across borders was facilitated by periodicals, translations, and international conferences, which highlights the role of ideas in driving economic change. This perspective challenges the notion that culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth, suggesting instead that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers. Mokyr further qualifies this view by noting the importance of tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge, while not codified, was essential for economic growth, suggesting that the diffusion of ideas was not limited to written texts but also included practical skills and know-how. This implies that the spread of ideas and knowledge was a complex process involving both formal and informal channels, underscoring the multifaceted nature of economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. They highlight that the extractive institutions established by European colonizers, designed to maximize rents for colonists, have had enduring effects on current economic performance. The impact of these institutions can be traced through the historical mortality rates faced by European settlers, which influenced the type of institutions implemented. These mortality rates, in turn, correlate strongly with contemporary institutional quality, which is a key determinant of current income per capita. This relationship suggests that the institutional framework imposed during colonial times has persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further elaborate that the high correlation between settler mortality, early institutions, and current institutions underscores the long-lasting effects of colonial policies. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not necessarily conducive to economic development, as they are shaped by politically powerful groups. This implies that the institutional legacy of colonialism has been a critical factor in determining the economic outcomes of former colonies, often at the expense of long-run growth.

The dynamic interplay between technology, society, and institutions suggests that economic growth is influenced by a complex web of cultural factors. For instance, Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Austin (2008, p.2) emphasize that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This complexity is evident in the erratic nature of productivity levels, which are influenced by the gap between existing and best-practice technology, and the limited social competence for exploiting advanced methods. This limited social competence is a crucial antecedent to the institutional framework of a country, and its impact on long-term economic outcomes is a downstream implication of the historical legacy of technological and social change. Austin (2008, p.2) further argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, thereby failing to account for the dynamic, partly endogenous nature of economic development. This dynamic nature indicates that economic development is influenced by the responses of societies to their environments, not merely by static environmental obstacles. This perspective aligns with the idea that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight distributional conflicts as a key factor in shaping economic institutions, contrasting with efficiency-based explanations. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutions is evident in the evolution of pre-industrial European economic systems. Ogilvie argues that economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds were shaped by power struggles between different social groups, not by efficiency considerations. For instance, craft guilds, which limited competition, were sustained by the collective action of artisans who benefited from restricting entry into their trades. This dynamic is further illuminated by Sokoloff and Engerman, who show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, thereby perpetuating inequality. In these societies, the institutional framework was designed to protect the interests of the powerful, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This institutional bias towards elites restricted competition and innovation, thereby impeding long-run growth. The implications of this institutional bias are profound, as Ogilvie notes that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. The entire institutional framework must be considered to understand its impact on economic development. This interdependency indicates that the role of culture in shaping institutions is mediated by the complex interplay of power dynamics and institutional design.
