The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what propels societies toward sustained economic progress. One side of the debate posits that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. This perspective is exemplified by scholars who argue that cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, can enhance economic performance by facilitating coordinated actions. Another perspective posits that culture's influence on institutions is filtered through additional factors, such as distributional conflicts or the interplay between institutions within a broader framework. This view contends that the efficiency of institutions is better explained by these conflicts and frameworks rather than by cultural beliefs alone. The interaction between these positions highlights the complexity of understanding how culture interacts with institutions to drive long-run growth, with implications for policy and theoretical development. The debate over the role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is pivotal for understanding the dynamics of economic development. The question of whether cultural beliefs directly influence institutional efficiency or whether this influence is mediated through other factors remains unresolved. This unresolved question is central to the live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the direct impact of culture on institutions and those who see culture as one of many factors influencing institutional efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1), underscores the enduring influence of cultural beliefs on institutional development. Their work highlights the importance of the financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and increased security of property rights. This institutional framework was essential for the development of both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North (1989, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, as noted by North and Weingast (1989, p.24). While the financial revolution was a critical factor in England's long-run success, it is important to note that other forces might have eventually led to similar outcomes, albeit more slowly and less decisively. This underscores the necessity of institutions in facilitating economic growth and political freedom.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both examine the role of economic institutions and distributional conflicts in shaping pre-industrial societies. They posit that economic institutions evolve primarily due to distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations. Ogilvie highlights that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by conflicts over wealth and power. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were simply efficient responses to economic conditions. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to New World societies, showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. Distributional conflicts thus played a crucial role in determining the institutional frameworks that governed economic activity. Ogilvie advises that the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework must be acknowledged. Institutions such as the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interlinked with other economic institutions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is complex and multifaceted, influencing not just individual institutions but entire institutional frameworks.

The divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gupta and van Zanden (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This early divergence challenges views that attribute the economic gap solely to post-1800 developments. The mechanism underlying this divergence is the differential impact of economic institutions on productivity and wages. In Europe, institutional changes, particularly in agriculture, led to increased productivity and higher wages, driving economic growth. This process was more pronounced in north-western Europe, where institutional reforms facilitated investment in agriculture, which in turn boosted labour productivity. Van Zanden observes that this productivity did not result in abundant grain and low food prices, as labor shifted from agriculture to industry and services (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This pattern of labour migration and sectoral shifts was crucial in driving Europe's economic development. In Asia, particularly in advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower, indicating a different trajectory of economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This divergence in wages and productivity highlights the role of economic institutions in shaping long-run growth trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) show that women have played a pivotal role in economic growth and development, both in pre-colonial Africa and in the United States. They highlight that African women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities, particularly in matrilineal societies like Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth. This dynamic was crucial for economic development, as women's roles were integral to the functioning of these societies. In the United States, a quiet revolution transformed women's employment and education, altering social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career roles. For example, the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early 20th century made "nice" jobs available, and the growth of educational institutions greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. These shifts were driven by exogenous changes, including technological advancements in household production and institutional changes that made part-time work more accessible. These changes in women's roles and economic contributions varied across different regions and periods. For instance, the turning point for earnings relative to comparable men occurred around 1980, indicating that the economic gains for women were not immediate but rather a result of gradual changes in social norms and institutional frameworks.

The slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust culture is a legacy of the slave trade's disruptive effects on African societies, which undermined political institutions and created long-term political instability. As Nunn argues, the slave trades caused political instability and weakened states, which in turn led to fragmented political structures. This political fragmentation had a direct impact on the development of economic institutions. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability are consistent with recent empirical research showing that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's legacy of political instability and weakened states has had a lasting impact on African societies, contributing to the underdevelopment of economic institutions and the persistence of mistrust. This mistrust culture, in turn, has had a profound impact on economic development, as it undermines the ability of individuals and institutions to collaborate effectively. The historical legacy of the slave trade has had a profound impact on African societies, shaping their economic institutions and cultural norms in ways that continue to affect their development today.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) share the view that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is mediated through the persistence of these institutions over time. The persistence of extractive institutions can be explained by the fact that these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth, a pattern that can be traced to the historical legacy of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also note that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, which means that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth may vary depending on the historical context. This variation in the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is a key insight that qualifies the broader claim about the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run growth.

Culture plays a fundamental role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run growth, as shown by the interdependence of cultural beliefs and economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This shows that cultural beliefs and values are integral to the development of institutions that support economic growth. The precise role of culture in this process remains a subject of debate. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.25) provides a dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, suggesting that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17) demonstrate that the factors determining whether Europeans could settle in the colonies have a significant effect on institutions today. The key insight is that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies depending on the historical context, and that the long-run effects of these institutions are mediated by cultural beliefs and values (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence of European colonization shows that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions underscores the importance of understanding the historical and cultural context in which institutions operate.
