The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One position holds that cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, significantly influence the development of institutions that govern economic activity. This perspective emphasizes that cultural traits can either facilitate or hinder the establishment of effective institutions, which in turn drive long-term economic growth. Some scholars posit that institutional development is mainly influenced by exogenous factors, such as geographic conditions or historical events, and cultural influences. This view suggests that the impact of culture on institutions is secondary to these more immediate determinants. The debate hinges on whether culture is a critical driver of institutional change or a mere byproduct of other, more fundamental forces. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policy-makers aiming to foster economic growth in developing regions. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex and multifaceted, with both endogenous and exogenous factors at play. The question of how culture shapes the institutions that govern long-run growth is central to this debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” North (1989, p.1) reports, “Coase pointed out that the neoclassical model, which has served as the basis of economic reasoning for most scholars in the Western world, holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs; but that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These institutions were central to the financial revolution that led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn supported long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that emerged in England during this period, such as the Bank of England, played a critical role in providing a stable market for public debt, which in turn fostered the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, was a pivotal moment in economic history. The secure property rights and low transaction costs that resulted from this revolution were foundational for economic growth. This framework does not fully explore the role of culture in shaping these institutions. This omission leaves open the question of how cultural factors might have influenced the development of these institutions and their impact on economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers' mortality rates determined the type of institutions that were established in colonies, which in turn influenced long-run economic development. The mortality rates of European settlers in a colony had a significant impact on the kind of institutions that were established. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists and to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were not conducive to economic development and persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). A key mechanism through which these extractive institutions persisted is the political power of the colonizers, who designed institutions to maximize their own rents and to promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective challenges the notion that culture dominates institutions in the context of long-run economic development, as the institutional framework established by colonial powers had a more direct and enduring impact on economic outcomes. The implications of these findings extend beyond colonial contexts, suggesting that institutional design by powerful groups can have lasting effects on economic development, regardless of cultural factors.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, challenging interest-only theories (Mokyr 2005, p.56; McCloskey 2015, p.10). McCloskey develops this perspective through the lens of cultural and ideological shifts, which he posits as the primary drivers of economic growth, and institutional frameworks or elite interests. Mokyr also supports the idea that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This knowledge was codified in textbooks or technical drawings, and also embodied in tacit forms such as skills and know-how (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of this knowledge across Europe facilitated the Great Enrichment, as McCloskey describes it, where new ideas and ideologies created new circumstances that further fostered economic growth. This process involved the accumulation of information, and cultural and ideological shifts that made new ideas possible and valuable. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the diffusion of knowledge was not uniform across all nations, and that the institutional and cultural differences between nations were less important than the commonalities that allowed for the spread of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between culture, institutions, and economic growth, suggesting that while culture may not dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth, it plays a crucial role in shaping the conditions under which economic growth can occur. In this way, the role of culture in economic development is a facilitator of the diffusion of ideas and knowledge.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already pronounced before 1800. The authors contend that the prosperous regions in Asia during this period were more economically similar to stagnating parts of Europe than to the rapidly developing northwest. The key to this divergence lies in the institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture and industry, leading to higher productivity and economic growth. These changes were driven by specific historical events, such as the Black Death, which initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5). These structural changes included greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional reforms in guilds, as well as labor-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). The economic divergence was thus shaped by a confluence of historical events and institutional changes that favored the northwest over other regions. The role of culture in these changes remains implicit. While the institutional changes and technological innovations are well-documented, the cultural factors that may have influenced these developments are less clear. This suggests that while historical events and institutional changes were crucial, cultural factors may have played a supporting role in shaping the conditions for economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and de Vries (1994, p.18) share the view that technological advances and social changes are pivotal in shaping economic growth. They argue that technological progress is a complex interplay of social and institutional factors. Abramovitz highlights how productivity levels are influenced by both technological advancements and the social competence to exploit these advances. This dynamic is evident in the historical context of the Industrial Revolution, where technological progress was closely linked to social changes. For instance, de Vries discusses the Industrious Revolution, a period characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns. This revolution preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution, illustrating how social changes can drive technological progress and economic growth. The role of culture in these dynamics remains implicit. Abramovitz notes that institutional changes can limit growth, suggesting that cultural factors might influence these institutional dynamics. This indicates that while technological advances and social changes are crucial, cultural factors may play a supporting role in shaping the conditions for economic growth.

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects long-term development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust persists in Africa. This mistrust has been identified as a critical barrier to economic development because it hinders social cohesion and collaborative efforts. They argue that this mistrust is a direct result of the historical trauma inflicted by the slave trade, which has left deep-seated cultural scars. Nunn (2008, p.27) complements this by linking the slave trade to political instability and weak state centralization. The legacy of the slave trade includes weakened and fragmented states, which are less capable of fostering economic growth. This political instability further exacerbates the challenges of economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.15) observe that the persistence of the malaria transmission index, which has remained stable over time, indicates that historical conditions, such as those created by the slave trade, continue to influence contemporary Africa. This suggests that the cultural and institutional legacies of the slave trade are deeply entrenched and continue to shape the economic landscape of Africa today.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions and, consequently, long-run economic growth. The legacies of historical events, such as the slave trade, have deeply influenced contemporary African institutions and economic development. For example, the persistence of the malaria transmission index indicates that historical conditions, including those created by the slave trade, continue to influence contemporary Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This shows that the cultural and institutional legacies of the slave trade are still shaping Africa's economic landscape. The mistrust culture developed during the slave trade persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust culture has significantly impacted African institutions and economic development, highlighting the enduring influence of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding underscores the importance of institutions in determining long-run economic growth. The high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and European settlements, as well as between early institutions and institutions today, further emphasizes the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic development in former colonies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) also highlight the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflects increased security of property rights and is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shows that the development of institutions that foster secure property rights is essential for long-run economic growth. The financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, further highlighting the importance of institutions in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is clear. Institutions matter in economic growth, and the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings as principal determinants, holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). This shows that institutions are critical for economic growth, as they play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise mechanisms by which culture influences institutions remain a subject of debate. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This shows that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic development in former colonies. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, which do not encourage economic development, further highlights the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The empirical comparison of current income differences between former colonies and European settlers shows that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
