The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain economic growth over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, thereby driving growth, or if other factors such as geographical determinants, institutional frameworks, or specific historical events play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action. Others contend that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for economic growth, suggesting that studying individual institutions in isolation may be misleading. Additionally, the impact of European mortality rates on the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies and the subsequent effects on current income per capita highlight the enduring influence of historical factors on economic development. Furthermore, the role of women in economic growth and development, particularly in African societies, challenges traditional views that marginalize their contributions. The interplay between cultural beliefs, institutional frameworks, and historical events thus shapes the fundamental causes of long-run growth, with cultural factors often acting as a trigger for institutional changes that drive economic development. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective emphasizes the importance of institutional change in facilitating economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights, both public and private (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in creating a stable market for public debt, which provided a significant positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Consequently, the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would have eventually led to England's success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The implication of this institutional framework is that the evolution of secure property rights and economic growth is not merely a function of cultural factors, but rather a result of institutional changes that fostered economic development. This institutional perspective underscores the necessity of secure property rights in driving economic growth, a theme that can be further explored through the lens of cultural influences on institutional development.

Acemoglu et al. assert that institutions established during colonialism were a fundamental determinant of long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). European settlements in colonies with low mortality rates led to the establishment of institutions that fostered economic growth, while high mortality rates resulted in extractive institutions that hindered development. The political and economic interests of the colonizers shaped this institutional framework, designing institutions to maximize their rents and promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications of this are that the persistence of these institutions over time significantly influenced current economic outcomes, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This institutional perspective underscores the importance of considering the historical context of institutional development when examining long-run economic growth. This perspective challenges the notion that culture dominates institutions in this context, highlighting the role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of core economic institutions, not cultural factors. These conflicts arise from the unequal distribution of resources, which in turn shapes the institutions that govern economic activity. In pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the structure of guilds can be attributed to the power dynamics between elites and the lower classes, not cultural beliefs about efficiency or social capital. In the New World, the initial abundance of land and natural resources led to societies characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved, such as those governing property rights and government policies, which advantaged elites and restricted competition. These distributional conflicts are deeply rooted in the economic and political structures of societies, not merely cultural artifacts. Ogilvie underscores the interconnectedness of institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that studying one institution in isolation may overlook the complex interdependencies that drive economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This perspective challenges the notion that culture dominates institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, highlighting instead the critical role of distributional conflicts in institutional development.

The slave trade has cultivated a culture of mistrust in Africa that persists and hinders long-term development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a legacy of the slave trade's impact on political and economic stability. The slave trade led to the weakening and fragmentation of states in Africa, resulting in long-term political instability. This instability, in turn, has been linked to reduced economic performance, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is a significant determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are not limited to political instability. The slave trade also had a negative impact on economic development through its effect on state centralization. The slave trade caused a negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. The slave trade also had a negative impact on economic development through its effect on corruption. Slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, which has been linked to reduced economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.1). The slave trade's impact on economic development is not solely due to its effect on state centralization and corruption. The slave trade also had a negative impact on economic development through its effect on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has been linked to reduced economic performance, as trust is a key determinant of economic growth. It is important to note that the impact of the slave trade on economic development is not limited to its effect on trust. The slave trade also had a negative impact on economic development through its effect on education and democracy. This suggests that the slave trade's impact on economic development is complex and multifaceted, and that it is not solely due to its effect on trust.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) share a posture that historical and institutional paths are pivotal in explaining economic divergence. This shared perspective highlights the importance of long-term institutional evolution in shaping economic outcomes, but it does not directly address cultural determinants. Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes the dynamic, partly endogenous nature of Africans' responses to their environments, suggesting that institutional changes are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including cultural responses. This dynamic interaction is further supported by Pamuk (2007, p.24), who demonstrates that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The dynamic nature of institutional change and its relationship with cultural factors remains under-explored. This gap in the literature suggests that while historical and institutional paths are crucial, the role of cultural determinants in shaping these paths and their impact on long-run growth warrants further investigation.

The role of women in economic development is an important aspect that intersects with cultural and institutional factors, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue. Both sources highlight that women's economic contributions have been shaped by cultural norms and institutional changes, which have in turn influenced long-run economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate how gender roles in pre-colonial and colonial Africa were integral to the economic system, with women's labor and social roles being crucial to community reproduction and economic stability. This dynamic is evident in the matrilineal societies of Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of wealth and office, underscoring their indispensable role in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Goldin's analysis of the U.S. economy shows that shifts in women's employment and education were driven by exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and institutional changes that facilitated part-time work and employment of married women (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes in women's roles were preconditions for broader socio-economic transformations, altering expectations and norms regarding women's family and career dynamics. Goldin notes that these changes were not linear or uniform, as they were influenced by evolving social norms and institutional frameworks (Goldin 2006, p.8). This nuanced view of women's economic contributions highlights the complex interplay between cultural norms, institutional changes, and long-run economic growth.

The Eurocentric view of economic history, as articulated by Bryant and Hopkins, posits that the rise of industrial and imperial modes of production in Europe was predicated on a complex interplay of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms that accumulated over time (Bryant 2006, p.35; Hopkins 2009, p.8). They assert that the development of Europe’s unique trajectory was driven by a multitude of factors, including civic urbanization, legal reforms, agricultural and commercial innovations, and military advancements (Bryant 2006, p.4). These factors collectively enabled the transition to modern industrial and imperial forms of hegemony. Bryant's perspective underscores that this transition was a gradual process rooted in the cultural and institutional legacies of the past. This historical continuity highlights the importance of long-term cultural and institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Hopkins introduces a nuanced dimension to this view by emphasizing the significance of institutional congruence with indigenous structures in promoting growth. This perspective suggests that effective institutional reforms must align with preexisting cultural and social norms to achieve sustained economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This alignment is crucial for enhancing the legitimacy of governments and fostering institutions that induce development. The implication is that cultural congruence is a necessary condition for the successful implementation of institutional reforms aimed at long-run economic growth.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is evident in the long-run growth trajectories of societies. Ogilvie's analysis highlights the significance of cultural beliefs in influencing economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's disappearance of these beliefs caused guilds to decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural shift illustrates how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Cultural values and beliefs, therefore, are integral to the institutional frameworks that promote economic growth.

Cultural congruence between institutions and preexisting social norms is crucial for effective institutional reforms. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective aligns with Austin's observation that the institutionalist approach emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence from England shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The historical evidence of England's financial revolution supports the idea that cultural congruence is essential for the successful implementation of institutional reforms aimed at long-run economic growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by the role of African women in economic growth and development. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This contribution challenges views that marginalize their role and shows that gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impacts on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The historical evidence from Asante society, where women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities, underscores the importance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The matrilineal society of Asante, where women's bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth, illustrates how cultural norms can influence economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that cultural beliefs and values play a significant role in shaping institutions that promote long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which cultural congruence is necessary for the successful implementation of institutional reforms. Ogilvie's analysis shows that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The historical evidence from England's financial revolution and the role of African women in economic development settles this disagreement. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence shows that cultural congruence is a necessary condition for the successful implementation of institutional reforms aimed at long-run economic growth.
