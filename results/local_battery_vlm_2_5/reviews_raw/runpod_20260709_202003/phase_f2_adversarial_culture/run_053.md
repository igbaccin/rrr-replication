The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development, with scholars debating whether cultural factors are antecedents or epiphenomena. One camp argues that cultural norms and values shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. Another camp posits that institutions themselves are the primary drivers, with culture playing a secondary role. A third camp views culture as a consequence of institutional arrangements. The disagreement hinges on whether culture is an independent variable that influences institutional development or a dependent variable shaped by institutional frameworks. The stakes are high, as understanding the causal dynamics between culture and institutions can inform policies aimed at fostering economic growth and development. McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that culture, as embodied in ideas and ideologies, fundamentally shaped long-run growth by driving economic change. McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that the "Great Enrichment" in Europe was driven by a new rhetoric that fostered new circumstances, which in turn shaped new rhetoric. Mokyr (2005, p.56) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He argues that the diffusion of knowledge within the Western world, facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, was a critical factor in economic growth. One specific mechanism through which culture drove economic change was the diffusion of ideas and knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.56) highlights that much of the useful knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills and practical know-how. This tacit knowledge moved about through personal transmission and training, underscoring the importance of cultural and social networks in disseminating economic ideas. The implication is that cultural factors, such as the openness to new ideas and the presence of social networks facilitating knowledge diffusion, played a crucial role in economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further qualifies this by noting that the payoff to efforts to codify knowledge depended on the technology and costs of codification, suggesting that the economic environment also influenced the diffusion of knowledge. This interplay between cultural factors and economic conditions highlights the complex role of culture in driving long-run growth.

This perspective is reinforced by the financial revolution in England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The implications of these institutional changes were profound, not only in accelerating economic growth but also in fostering political freedom. This institutional evolution provided a robust framework for economic development, suggesting that the role of culture in economic growth may have been overstated in other accounts. The legacy of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes is well-documented. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had a lasting impact on current income per capita. High mortality rates deterred European settlement, leading to the implementation of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for colonial powers rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This institutional framework, characterized by weak property rights and high transaction costs, hindered economic development in these regions. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights the profound and enduring effects of colonial practices on economic performance. The mechanism through which colonial institutions affect current economic outcomes is through the persistence of extractive institutions that stifle economic growth. However, the impact of these institutions is not uniform; the political dynamics within colonies also play a crucial role in shaping the long-term effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that equilibrium institutions can be extractive if they are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if this inhibits long-run growth. This implies that the persistence of extractive institutions is not solely due to geographic factors but also to the political and economic incentives of colonial powers.

The slave trade played a pivotal role in shaping African cultural attitudes, which in turn influenced long-term economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, a phenomenon that persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has had profound implications for African societies. It has eroded social capital, which is crucial for economic development. The slave trade also contributed to political instability and weakened state structures, further hindering economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27). The political instability resulting from the slave trade led to fragmented states, which are less capable of implementing effective economic policies. This fragmentation is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the ability of states to provide public goods and enforce property rights. Nunn further elaborates that the slave trade may have contributed to modern corruption, exacerbating the challenges faced by African economies (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) show that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They argue that the advanced parts of India and China, rather than mirroring the prosperous regions of Europe, resembled the stagnating parts of Europe. This divergence is evident in grain wages, which were comparable across these regions, but silver wages, which reflected purchasing power over tradable goods and services, were significantly lower in Asia. Pamuk (2007, p.5) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1) illustrates how the Black Death contributed to this divergence by initiating long-term structural changes, such as increased labor mobility and flexibility. These changes led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. This wage gap, in turn, facilitated labor-saving technological innovations, further propelling economic development in northwestern Europe. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not adequately capture the complexities of economic development by simplifying the interaction between historical periods and paths. This simplification overlooks the dynamic interplay between environmental factors and societal responses, which can significantly alter economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that African societies' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately reshaped their economic paths. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) reinforce this dynamic by highlighting how different factor endowments in New World societies led to varied levels of inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions. These institutions, characterized by policies that favored elites, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that it can stifle economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader participation and innovation.
