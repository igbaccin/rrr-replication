The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the question of whether cultural factors shape economic trajectories by influencing the institutions that govern economic activity. On one hand, scholars emphasise the role of institutions in economic growth, arguing that these institutions are shaped by cultural factors. On the other hand, others argue that economic growth is primarily driven by other factors, such as geography or technology, and that institutions are merely a secondary effect. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions were the fundamental factor shaping long-run economic growth. The mechanism behind this is the differential mortality rates of European settlers in colonies. These mortality rates determined the type of institutions that were established. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for European colonists. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that these institutions persist today, significantly affecting current income per capita. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) provide empirical evidence supporting this mechanism, demonstrating a strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that the persistence of extractive institutions is due to geographic factors and the political power dynamics that favored European colonists. These dynamics ensured that institutions were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers. The implication is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on contemporary economic disparities.

McCloskey and Mokyr both argue that the growth of ideas and knowledge was the primary driver of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). These scholars suggest that the dissemination of useful knowledge was critical for economic development. McCloskey notes that the cultural struggles and new rhetoric in bourgeois societies fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. This process involved a broader cultural shift that embraced new ideas and ideologies. Mokyr elaborates on this by highlighting the role of tacit knowledge, which was often transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge was crucial for technological advancements and economic progress (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that the spread of ideas and knowledge, facilitated by cultural and social dynamics, played a pivotal role in driving economic growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between culture, ideas, and economic growth, suggesting that while ideas are fundamental, their impact is mediated by cultural and institutional contexts.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both highlight the complexities of economic growth and technological change, focusing on institutional dynamics and social competence. Abramovitz shows that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. He observes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, and that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was limited, particularly in earlier years and in more recent latecomers (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This limitation suggests that the ability to adopt and implement new technologies is not uniform across societies, implying that cultural and institutional contexts play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gupta note that in the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower, indicating different economic development paths (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This observation underscores the idea that economic growth is influenced by cultural and institutional factors that shape how societies respond to and implement new technologies. This qualification implies that while cultural and institutional contexts are important, they are not static and can evolve over time, affecting long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The development of secure property rights was pivotal as it enabled long-term investments and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution's impact on property rights security and economic growth underscores the importance of institutional evolution in fostering long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that the slave trade has had a profound and enduring impact on African development, shaping cultural attitudes such as mistrust that continue to influence long-term economic outcomes. The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to political instability and weakened states, which in turn hindered economic development. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability are consistent with the findings that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust, which persists to this day, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust can impede economic growth by reducing cooperation and social capital, which are crucial for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.1) argues that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, highlighting how historical events can shape cultural attitudes that persist and influence long-term economic outcomes. This cultural legacy of mistrust and corruption, stemming from the slave trade, continues to affect African development, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical roots of cultural attitudes in shaping long-term economic outcomes.
