The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development scholarship. At stake is the explanation for why some societies prosper while others stagnate. One position emphasises the primacy of institutions, arguing that they shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment and innovation. This view holds that institutions which protect property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for growth. Another position, meanwhile, highlights the role of culture in shaping institutions and economic outcomes. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or whether cultural factors, through their influence on institutions, play a more fundamental role. The causal mechanisms linking culture to institutions and ultimately to growth remain an open question. Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon share a finding that historical events can shape cultural attitudes that persist over time and influence economic outcomes. This suggests that culture, through its impact on institutions, can indeed play a significant role in long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They identify a critical mechanism in the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, which reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution, marked by the financial revolution, was pivotal in creating a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the growth of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth by ensuring the security of long-term property rights, a dimension that is crucial for both capital markets and overall economic development (North 1989, p.9). This implies that while cultural factors may influence economic development, the institutional framework is the primary driver of long-run growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and knowledge, not institutions, are the primary drivers of economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.10) posits that the "Great Enrichment" in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by a cultural shift in rhetoric and ideology that fostered economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) explains that knowledge in the 18th century was often tacit and embodied in skills, which were transmitted through personal training and communication. This tacit knowledge was crucial for economic growth, as it moved about through personal transmission and training. This emphasis on the role of ideas and knowledge in driving economic growth suggests that cultural factors, such as the spread of useful knowledge and the development of new ideologies, were instrumental in fostering long-run growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that the diffusion of knowledge was a global phenomenon, affecting the entire world where the Enlightenment took root. This implies that while cultural factors may have influenced economic development, they did so on a global scale, challenging the notion that culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth in specific regions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. They contend that the nature of institutions established by colonial powers, particularly in regions with high mortality rates, had a lasting impact on economic performance. The mechanism through which this occurred is the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. The high correlation between settler mortality and the establishment of extractive institutions highlights how these institutions were shaped by the colonial experience. The persistence of these institutions over time has had significant implications for current income per capita in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes. This suggests that the legacy of colonialism, through the establishment of extractive institutions, has been a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that equilibrium institutions may not encourage economic development. This implies that the institutional framework established during colonial times may have been designed to serve the interests of the colonizers. The downstream implication is that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by the interplay between colonial policies and the economic interests of the colonizers.

Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of early modern economic divergence in Europe highlights that institutional changes in the developing parts of northwestern Europe led to high agricultural labor productivity, but this did not necessarily translate into abundant grain and low food prices (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This finding is supported by Pamuk's examination of the Black Death's impact, which shows that the pandemic initiated long-term structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to the guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These shifts contributed to the rise of rural industries and ushered in an era of labor-saving technological innovations. This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between institutional changes and economic development in shaping long-run growth.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa illustrates how cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, can persist and influence long-term economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn (2008, p.27) also demonstrates that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. This effect is mediated by the long-term political instability and weakened state institutions that resulted from the slave trades. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization highlights how the slave trade undermined the development of strong, centralized states, which are crucial for economic growth. The impact of the slave trade on African economic development is thus not limited to the immediate loss of human capital but extends to the long-term cultural and institutional legacies that continue to affect economic outcomes. These legacies include the persistence of mistrust and the challenges posed by weak state institutions, both of which hinder economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share the posture that different historical and institutional paths have led to varying levels of inequality. The different levels of inequality are a function of the institutions that evolved in the New World societies, which were influenced by the factor endowments of these societies. These institutions, in turn, shaped the distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality. This approach highlights how Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the course of development. This dynamic interplay between institutions and inequality is further qualified by the observation that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This qualification underscores the complexity of the institutional paths to inequality and the need for a nuanced understanding of how different historical and institutional contexts shape economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the way cultural norms influence the evolution of institutions over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.24) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy has significant implications for the institutions that govern economic activity in contemporary Africa. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional development is further illustrated by the work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4; 2000, p.2), who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic highlights how cultural and institutional factors interact to shape long-term economic trajectories. The continuity of these cultural and institutional legacies is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.17), who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This evidence underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes. The dynamic interplay between institutions and inequality is further qualified by the observation that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This qualification underscores the complexity of the institutional paths to inequality and the need for a nuanced understanding of how different historical and institutional contexts shape economic outcomes. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in economic growth and development (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which cultural norms influence the evolution of institutions over time (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the legacy of the slave trade in Africa, which has had a profound impact on the institutions that govern economic activity in contemporary Africa.
