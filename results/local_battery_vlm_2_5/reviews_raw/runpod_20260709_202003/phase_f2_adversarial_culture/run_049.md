The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the intellectual project of understanding the fundamental drivers of economic growth, which is essential for designing effective policies and interventions. One live disagreement concerns the extent to which cultural factors, such as beliefs and values, shape economic institutions and, through them, economic performance. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. Others contend that the impact of culture is mediated through other factors, such as institutions governing public choice and property rights. A third group posits that cultural factors are less significant than other determinants, such as geographic conditions or exogenous shocks, in shaping economic institutions and growth. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors operate as independent variables or are subsumed under broader institutional frameworks. The resolution of this debate has implications for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. This interplay is crucial for comprehending why some societies have thrived economically while others have lagged behind. The question of cultural influence on institutions and growth is thus central to the broader inquiry into the determinants of economic development. Institutions are highlighted as a fundamental determinant of economic growth, as North argues that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the cost of transactions, with secure property rights and effective financial systems reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. North illustrates this with the example of the Bank of England, whose establishment in 1694 led to a significant decline in transaction costs and facilitated long-term capital markets (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by evidence showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast, along with Hornbeck, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This institutional development is exemplified by the financial revolution in England, which played a critical role in the country's long-run success. The institutions that facilitated the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a significant positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This dynamic highlights how secure property rights, underpinned by robust institutions, can drive economic growth by encouraging investment and reducing transaction costs. Hornbeck's analysis of agricultural development on the American Plains further illustrates this point, showing how the introduction of barbed wire fencing enhanced property rights and reduced externalities from livestock damage, thereby promoting agricultural growth. This implies that while institutions are fundamental, their impact on economic growth can be modulated by cultural factors that influence how property rights are enforced and respected.

Distributional conflicts are a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than cultural factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies are shaped primarily by the distribution of power and wealth. Ogilvie demonstrates that the institutional frameworks of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe were influenced by the distributional conflicts between different groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) expand on this by showing that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This pattern suggests that the institutional structures which facilitate long-run growth are not merely a product of cultural beliefs or social norms but are deeply rooted in the power dynamics and distributional conflicts within societies. This means that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth is not determined by cultural factors alone but is significantly influenced by the underlying power structures and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie's work (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) further illustrates this by showing that institutions are interdependent within a wider framework, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutions is not isolated but part of a broader system.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and impacted trust levels. This culture of mistrust is a legacy of the historical trauma inflicted by the transatlantic slave trade, which has had enduring effects on social capital and economic development. The slave trade disrupted social structures and fostered an environment where cooperation and trust were undermined. This mistrust is not merely a cultural artifact but a deep-seated response to historical injustices that continue to shape contemporary social dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack support this view, highlighting how colonial constructs and the codification of gender roles during this period have had long-term consequences for gender production and economic dynamics. These gender-based constructs, which continue to influence contemporary economic systems, reflect the enduring impact of historical power dynamics on social norms and economic outcomes. While the slave trade's legacy of mistrust has persisted, it is important to note that contemporary economic development is also influenced by other factors, such as the role of women in economic production. Akyeampong and Fofack show that women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities in Asante, for their bloodlines in this matrilineal society defined the transmission of both office and wealth. This indicates that while mistrust is a significant factor, the economic contributions of women also play a crucial role in shaping economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) report, “The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles discussed in this article had significant impact on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production.” Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) report, “Given the mounting evidence of the importance of trust, our contribution here is in helping to understand and explain its origins.”

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (2006, p.35) both challenge simplistic explanations of economic development, arguing that historical causation is more complex and dynamic than often portrayed (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). Austin emphasizes that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting that African responses to their environments played a significant role. This dynamic, endogenous approach suggests that African economic development was not solely determined by exogenous factors but was shaped by local adaptations and technological advancements. Bryant (2006, p.35) further argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, emphasizing the interconnected nature of historical transitions. This perspective implies that the legacy of past developments, including technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms, fundamentally shapes economic trajectories. However, Bryant (2006, p.35) notes that the revisionist preference for shorter-term causality can lead to explanatory distortions, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding of long-term historical processes is necessary for accurate analysis.

Although the literature on social and demographic changes influencing economic growth does not directly address the role of culture in long-run growth, it provides valuable insights into the broader structural shifts that can indirectly shape cultural norms and institutional frameworks. Goldin (2006, p.8) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) both (Goldin 2006, p.8; Pamuk 2007, p.24) identify how exogenous changes in social and economic conditions can drive significant shifts in demographic patterns and labor markets. during the early 20th century (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes in labor supply and occupational patterns are not merely economic phenomena; they are deeply intertwined with cultural norms and social expectations regarding women's roles in society. Pamuk (2007, p.24) similarly highlights how the Black Death in Europe led to long-term structural changes, including greater mobility and institutional changes in guilds, which in turn fostered labor-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These structural changes not only altered economic conditions but also reshaped social dynamics and cultural practices. The interplay between demographic shifts and cultural norms suggests that while these changes do not directly address cultural determinants of long-run growth, they illustrate how broader structural shifts can create conditions that influence cultural norms and institutional frameworks over the long term. This interplay underscores the importance of considering the multifaceted nature of economic development, where cultural factors are not isolated but are part of a complex web of influences.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth, as it influences the norms, values, and social practices that underpin economic development. Cultural beliefs and values, such as those emphasized by Putnam's theory of social capital, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of cultural factors in creating conditions that support long-term economic development. For instance, the "civic" cultures in northern Italy, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, have been linked to superior economic performance since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, highlight how cultural norms can shape economic outcomes over the long term.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further illustrates the role of culture in dominating institutions. Ogilvie’s analysis of the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England shows how these institutions were interconnected with labour markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural norms influence not just individual institutions but entire institutional frameworks, which in turn affect economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis also supports this view, arguing that the paths of institutional evolution are dynamic and influenced by cultural responses to environmental changes (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.
