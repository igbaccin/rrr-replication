The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societal norms and values interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic trajectories. One live disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors directly influence institutional quality and economic growth, or whether institutions and growth are primarily shaped by other factors such as geography, technology, or political structures. Some scholars argue that cultural norms can either facilitate or hinder the development of institutions that support economic growth. Others contend that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or technology, and that culture plays a secondary role. Still others posit that culture itself is shaped by historical experiences such as colonialism and the slave trade, which in turn influence institutions and growth. The interplay between culture and institutions is complex, and understanding this relationship is crucial for developing effective policies aimed at fostering economic growth. The question of whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional quality and economic growth remains unanswered. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic development. They and Nunn and Wantchekon share the claim that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the primary driver of economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies determined whether they could settle and establish institutions, which in turn affected current economic performance. This relationship is strong and statistically significant, demonstrating that institutions are a key factor in long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) suggest that the type of institutions established during colonialism can significantly impact long-run economic development. Therefore, the institutional framework set by colonial powers can either foster or hinder economic growth, depending on whether it is extractive or inclusive.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in this process. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional framework was instrumental in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. The financial revolution in England provided a stable market for public debt and fostered a parallel market for private debt, creating a large and positive externality. This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The institutions governing public choice in England thus played a necessary role in making possible both economic growth and political freedom. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. This underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was a response to economic needs and a catalyst for broader economic and political transformations.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27) show that the slave trade had profound and lasting effects on African societies, shaping cultural norms and institutions in ways that continue to influence economic development. This mistrust was a transient response to the slave trade and became deeply ingrained in the social fabric, affecting how individuals and communities interact and cooperate. The slave trade disrupted social structures, leading to a pervasive sense of distrust that hindered collective action and weakened institutions. This mistrust, in turn, affected state centralization and governance, creating a cycle of political instability and economic underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability further impeded economic growth by undermining the effectiveness of public institutions and reducing the capacity for coordinated economic development. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic impacts, perpetuating a culture of mistrust that continues to shape Africa's development trajectory.

The new economic history of Africa underscores the significance of historical institutional variables in shaping contemporary development trajectories, (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) contends that institutions are the primary drivers of development, and that their gradual evolution necessitates a historical investigation that extends beyond the colonial period. This approach highlights that current institutions are deeply rooted in precolonial history and prehistory, shaping the effectiveness of governance and economic policies. This perspective is echoed by Pierre Englebert, who argues that congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions enhances government legitimacy and fosters development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This interdependence suggests that the historical evolution of institutions, shaped by both endogenous and exogenous factors, has played a crucial role in determining Africa's economic trajectory. Moreover, the historical context of African economies reveals that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly, underscoring the importance of understanding the specific institutional and economic conditions of each country. The legacy of these historical institutions continues to influence contemporary economic policies and development strategies, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's institutional history.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) both show that women's roles in economic development are deeply intertwined with cultural and institutional factors. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the enduring impact of gender-based colonial constructs on production systems and gender relations in Africa, noting that these constructs may be as enduring as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. Goldin, meanwhile, underscores the transformative shifts in women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., driven by changes in social norms and institutional barriers. The role of women in economic development is a function of individual agency and is significantly shaped by broader cultural and institutional contexts. This interplay is evident in Akan state formation, where women's indispensable role in community reproduction was intrinsically linked to matrilineal social structures. This cultural context, however, does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Goldin's analysis of the quiet revolution in women's roles in the U.S. economy further illustrates this point, as the shifts were due to various exogenous changes including increased relative demand for female office workers and growth of educational institutions, which made "nice" jobs available and greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. These changes in household production technology and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women underscore the complex interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks in shaping economic development.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia illustrate different historical paths and institutional evolutions, highlighting that economic growth is contingent on a range of factors that are not fixed over time (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This thesis is further complicated by the fact that institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, which is a critical point that economic historians must consider. This nuanced view of economic development is further supported by the fact that in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity. However, this higher agricultural labour productivity did not lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices, because labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This pattern of investment and labour movement suggests that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This historical perspective on economic development is crucial because it highlights the dynamic nature of economic growth and the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which it occurs.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Institutions, as the fundamental cause of economic growth, are deeply rooted in cultural norms and historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which in turn shaped long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin further elucidates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic development, emphasizing the need to consider dynamic historical paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interaction highlights how cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, are integral to the evolution of institutions that foster economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was crucial for economic growth, illustrating how cultural attitudes towards property and governance can drive institutional change (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Nunn and Wantchekon reveal that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists and affects contemporary economic development, underscoring the enduring impact of cultural factors on institutions and growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4) underscores the importance of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement involves the extent to which historical institutions can be measured and compared across different contexts. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of contemporary trust levels, which provides a direct link between historical cultural factors and current economic outcomes.
