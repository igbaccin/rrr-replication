The role of culture in shaping institutions is a contentious area of inquiry, particularly when considering its impact on long-run economic growth. Institutions are widely acknowledged as critical drivers of economic performance, with secure property rights and effective governance fostering growth by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. The degree to which cultural factors influence the development and functioning of these institutions is still debated. On one hand, some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values significantly shape institutional frameworks, thereby influencing economic outcomes. These scholars posit that cultural norms can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of growth-promoting institutions. On the other hand, other researchers contend that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and mortality rates, with culture playing a secondary role. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing policies aimed at fostering growth in developing economies. A central question emerges: to what extent does culture, through its influence on institutions, drive long-run economic growth? The answer to this question has the potential to reshape our understanding of economic development and inform policy decisions. This question has been explored through various lenses, including the impact of cultural beliefs on institutional efficiency and the role of historical events in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They demonstrate that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This institutional change reflected increased security of property rights, which was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The institutions that facilitated this growth provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This positive externality is a key mechanism through which institutions foster economic growth.

Therefore, the presence of positive transaction costs necessitates the role of institutions, as they are essential for managing these costs and facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework, which secures property rights and reduces transaction costs, is fundamental to the economic success of nations. The role of institutions in economic growth has qualifications. For instance, while institutions are crucial, their impact can be contingent on other factors, such as the broader economic and political context. This suggests that institutions are a key driver of economic growth, and they operate in conjunction with other factors.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective underscores the transformative power of cultural shifts, where new ideologies and ideas fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. McCloskey posits that the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" like the Netherlands and Britain were able to harness new rhetoric to create new circumstances, and vice versa, leading to the Great Enrichment (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr extends this idea by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. Mokyr also points out that much of the knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and practices that were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge, while not easily codified, played a crucial role in the economic development of Europe. The implication is that cultural factors, including the dissemination of ideas and knowledge, were fundamental in shaping the economic landscape of Europe. This perspective suggests that culture, through the spread of ideas and knowledge, can significantly influence long-run economic growth, offering a nuanced view of the interplay between culture and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that high European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which have persisted and significantly impacted current economic performance. This institutional framework was designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, and it did not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions is crucial; they were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) demonstrate the strong relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies. This institutional legacy has profoundly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and development. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) both focus on the role of economic institutions in European development, highlighting distributional conflicts and urbanization patterns as key factors in shaping economic outcomes. This cluster of scholarship does not directly address how culture influences long-run growth, but it does provide insight into the institutional frameworks that supported economic development in Europe. This perspective is supported by van Zanden (1999, p.2), who contends that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that economic growth was not solely driven by cultural beliefs or values. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework is a recurring theme in this cluster. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the European family system, community-based welfare systems, and labour markets were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency suggests that the institutional framework as a whole played a crucial role in economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) qualify this perspective by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, indicating that institutional frameworks alone cannot fully explain the differences in economic development between these regions. This implies that while economic institutions were important, other factors, such as cultural differences, may have also played a role in shaping long-run growth.

The persistent cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, shaped by the slave trade, highlight the role of historical exploitation in long-term economic development, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's legacy includes a deep mistrust of others, which hinders economic growth. This mistrust arises from the historical exploitation and violence inflicted on African societies during the slave trade. The slave trade's legacy is evident in the fragmented political institutions and weakened states that persist in Africa today. This is because the slave trade caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. Recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. This effect is partly due to the slave trade's impact on state centralization, which in turn affects economic development. Nunn (2008, p.27) qualifies this perspective by arguing that the slave trade is not the sole cause of Africa's underdevelopment. The slave trade's impact on economic development is complex and multifaceted, involving various historical, cultural, and institutional factors. The historical exploitation and violence inflicted on African societies during the slave trade have shaped long-term cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which persist in Africa today and affect economic development.

The 'reversal of fortune' literature, as shared by Austin and Sokoloff and Engerman, emphasizes the role of institutions and historical paths in shaping economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The mechanism through which these institutions influence development is by shaping the distribution of political power and economic resources, which in turn affects long-term growth trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can perpetuate socio-economic disparities, influencing long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this perspective by noting that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that the impact of institutions on growth can vary over time (Austin 2008, p.25). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional influence on economic development, indicating that while institutions play a crucial role, their effects are not uniform and can change depending on the historical context.

Culture, as a fundamental driver of institutions, plays a crucial role in shaping long-run growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action. For example, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to the rules of guilds, while the Protestant Reformation's shift in beliefs led to the decline of these guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that cultural factors, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of cultural values in shaping institutions that support long-term economic development. Cultural influences are not uniform across different historical periods. Austin highlights that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, indicating that cultural factors' impact on growth can vary over time (Austin 2008, p.25). Nunn and Wantchekon reveal that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists today and affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This enduring cultural impact illustrates how historical events can shape long-term institutional frameworks and economic performance. Culture, therefore, is a dynamic force that continually shapes institutions and influences economic growth over extended periods.

The institutional frameworks shaped by cultural values are not static. North argues that institutions are critical for economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide the security necessary for long-term economic activity (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments during this period dramatically reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth and political freedom (North 1989, p.9). Cultural values, by shaping these institutional frameworks, play a pivotal role in determining long-run growth. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe reveals that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were influenced by cultural beliefs and values, which in turn affected economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of these institutions within a broader framework suggests that cultural values create a complex web of institutional influences that drive economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interplay between culture and institutions highlights the multifaceted nature of long-run growth, where cultural values continuously shape and reshape institutional frameworks over time. The specific measurement of transaction costs and property rights security shows that cultural influences are embedded in the very structures that govern economic activity.
