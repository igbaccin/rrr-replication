The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional theory. At stake is the extent to which cultural values and beliefs shape the institutional frameworks that underpin economic development. The central disagreement revolves around whether culture is a primary driver of institutional change and growth, or whether other factors, such as geographical determinants or economic incentives, play a more significant role. One position emphasizes the direct influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, arguing that these beliefs can motivate individuals to adopt efficient rules and structures. Another position suggests that cultural factors are less influential, and that economic growth is primarily driven by institutional frameworks that emerge from broader social and political dynamics. The debate is further complicated by the interplay between cultural beliefs and the economic incentives that shape institutional development. For instance, the persistence of certain institutions can be attributed to the cultural norms that reinforce them, even if these norms are not directly related to economic efficiency. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the long-term trajectory of economic growth and development. The role of culture in shaping institutional frameworks is a critical area of inquiry, as it sheds light on the complex interplay between social norms, economic incentives, and institutional change. This interplay is evident in the historical evolution of economic institutions, where cultural beliefs have both facilitated and hindered the adoption of efficient rules and structures. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They argue that the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution played a pivotal role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would have led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast's analysis highlights the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth and political freedom. This is further supported by the development of a stable market for public debt, which provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England, initially formed to intermediate public debt, soon expanded into private operations (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North (1989, p.1) argues that the neoclassical model, which highlights population and savings as key growth determinants, is valid only with the limiting assumption of zero transaction costs. In a world with positive transaction costs, institutions matter significantly, underscoring the importance of secure property rights in economic development. The role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security is central to understanding long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) asserts that colonialism's institutional changes, not cultural factors, are the primary cause of long-run growth. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies influenced the types of institutions established, which in turn shaped current economic performance. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions that persisted over time, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) state that these institutions were created to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-term economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal mechanisms by condensing different historical periods and paths. This critique highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions influenced economic development. Austin's view is that colonial institutions' effect on economic growth is not uniform; it changes with different historical contexts and institutional evolution paths. This implies that the long-run effects of colonial institutions on economic growth are contingent on the specific historical and institutional contexts in which they operate (Austin 2008, p.25).

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by distributional conflicts, not by efficiency or cultural factors. They demonstrate that these conflicts influenced the structure of economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, which in turn affected economic outcomes. Ogilvie shows that cultural explanations, such as those proposed by Max Weber and Putnam, fail to account for the complexities of institutional frameworks. She points out that the interdependencies between institutions within a broader framework are crucial for understanding economic growth. For example, the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England were interconnected with various markets and social structures, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency suggests that economic institutions cannot be studied in isolation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further qualify this by highlighting that the persistence of inequality in New World societies was influenced by government policies that advantaged elites. These policies restricted competition and shaped the evolution of institutions, contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective underscores that the fundamental cause of long-run growth is not solely determined by cultural factors but is also influenced by the dynamics of distributional conflicts and institutional interdependencies.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a cultural factor that has had long-lasting effects on the development of institutions and economic growth, as it has undermined the ability of societies to form cooperative relationships and develop strong, effective institutions. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are not limited to the direct economic costs of the trade itself. It also led to long-term political instability and weakened states, which in turn hindered economic growth. This is consistent with historical accounts and recent empirical research (Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's impact on state centralization and political instability is a key mechanism through which it has affected economic development. This instability has made it difficult for African countries to develop strong, effective institutions that can support economic growth. This long-term political instability and weakened states have been exacerbated by the legacy of mistrust, which has made it difficult for societies to form cooperative relationships and develop strong, effective institutions. This legacy of mistrust has had a profound impact on economic development in Africa, as it has undermined the ability of societies to form cooperative relationships and develop strong, effective institutions. This mistrust has also made it difficult for African countries to attract foreign investment, as investors may be hesitant to invest in countries where they perceive a high level of risk. The impact of the slave trade on economic development in Africa is complex and multifaceted. The slave trade has significantly affected Africa's economic development, with cultural and institutional factors mediating this impact. The legacy of mistrust and political instability left by the slave trade continues to hinder economic development in Africa today.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) both contend that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, driven by factors such as urban development and wage gaps. The early modern great divergence was marked by significant institutional changes in Europe, particularly in north-western regions, which led to high levels of investment in agriculture and subsequent increases in agricultural labour productivity. This productivity did not result in low food prices, as labour shifted from agriculture to industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This institutional change was a key driver of economic development in Europe, as it facilitated investment and technological innovation. These developments highlight how institutional changes and structural shifts can drive economic growth, even in the absence of cultural factors. The role of culture in these processes is somewhat unclear. van Zanden (1999, p.6) notes that the development of silver wages reflected economic development across Europe, suggesting that cultural factors may have influenced economic outcomes, but this relationship is complex and multifaceted. The impact of culture on economic growth is not straightforward, as it interacts with institutional changes and structural shifts in ways that are difficult to isolate.

Broadberry and Gardner identify that African economies exhibit a complex interplay between historical economic patterns and institutional reforms. Broadberry and Gardner and Hopkins, both find that the economic performance of African economies is deeply rooted in historical institutional variables, which often extend beyond the colonial period (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). One specific mechanism is the congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions. Hopkins suggests that governments promoting growth are more effective when they align with indigenous institutions, enhancing legitimacy and fostering development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that institutional reforms in Africa, to be effective, must consider the historical context and indigenous structures. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) observe that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied greatly, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability suggests that while historical institutional congruence is beneficial, it is not a panacea. The success of institutional reforms in Africa depends on a nuanced understanding of both historical contexts and contemporary economic realities.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) assert that women's roles in economic development have been significant but often overlooked. They argue that African women contributed substantially to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that challenges views which marginalize their role. During the pre-colonial period in Asante, women were essential for community reproduction, as their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth in this matrilineal society. One queen mother's statement, "If my sex die in the clan then that very clan becomes extinct," underscores the critical role of women in maintaining the social fabric and economic continuity of their communities. The colonial period introduced gender-based constructs and codified gender roles, which had long-term effects on gender production and economic dynamics. These colonial constructs may have been as persistent as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs, influencing the sexual division of labor and economic dependency models. Goldin (2006, p.3) shows that shifts in women's employment and education in the U.S. were driven by exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. These changes altered female labor supply and made part-time work more available, dispelling barriers to the employment of married women and transforming women's roles in the economy. The persistence of colonial gender norms in Africa and the transformative shifts in women's employment in the U.S. highlight the complex interplay between cultural norms, institutional changes, and economic development.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions and fostering long-run economic growth. This role is evident in both the persistence of colonial gender norms in Africa and the transformative shifts in women's employment in the U.S., where cultural norms influenced institutional changes and economic development. Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions, with Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivating medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Austin highlights the dynamic, partly endogenous nature of African responses to their environments, which ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). These findings underscore the importance of cultural norms in shaping institutions that facilitate economic growth.

The interplay between cultural norms and institutional changes is further illustrated by the role of women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This contribution was shaped by cultural norms and gender roles that defined the production system and economic activities. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring influence of cultural norms on economic outcomes.

The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in fostering economic growth and the dynamic nature of African responses to their environments show that cultural norms and institutional changes are intertwined in shaping long-run economic growth. The measurement of institutional frameworks and the analysis of historical evidence, as used in the cited sources, settle the disagreement by showing that cultural norms and institutional changes are fundamental to long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Austin 2008, p.25).
