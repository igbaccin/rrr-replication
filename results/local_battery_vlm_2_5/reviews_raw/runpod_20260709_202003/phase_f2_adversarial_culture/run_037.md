The role of culture in dominating institutions is a contentious issue in the study of long-run economic growth. At stake is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values influence the establishment and persistence of institutions that foster or hinder economic development. One position argues that culture shapes institutions by motivating collective action and determining the structure of economic interactions. Another position posits that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and mortality rates, with culture playing a secondary role. A third position suggests that the relationship between culture and institutions is dynamic and endogenous, with both factors influencing each other over time. The debate hinges on whether culture is a fundamental cause of long-run growth or merely a contextual variable that interacts with other determinants. Ogilvie criticizes the notion that economic institutions are shaped by efficiency considerations, and instead posits that distributional conflicts are the primary driver of institutional development. This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are designed to maximize economic efficiency, and instead suggests that they are the result of power struggles and political negotiations. Austin highlights the importance of understanding the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutions, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin's argument complements Ogilvie's critique of efficiency-based explanations of institutions, as both scholars emphasize the need to consider the complex and interconnected nature of institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. The institutions that emerged during this period, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was crucial for the growth of a stable market for public debt, which provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional development was not merely a byproduct of other forces but a necessary condition for economic growth and political freedom. The implications of these institutional changes reach beyond economic growth. The secure property rights and reduced transaction costs facilitated by these institutions also supported the parallel development of a market for private debt, further reinforcing economic stability and growth. Nonetheless, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated and their long-term effects on economic growth remain subjects of ongoing debate.

The dominant role of ideas and ideologies in driving economic growth in Europe from 1600 to 1848 is a central theme in the scholarship of McCloskey (2015, p.10) (Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey argues that the cultural struggle in Europe was not merely a byproduct of institutional changes, but a fundamental driver of economic change. For instance, the rhetoric of economic growth, which emphasized the importance of individualism and innovation, created a new set of circumstances that fostered economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr further supports this perspective, highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This knowledge was not merely codified in technical drawings, but also embodied in tacit forms such as skills and expertise, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of this knowledge was not uniform across Europe. The implicit forms of knowledge, such as skills and expertise, were more easily transmitted within societies that valued individualism and innovation, further reinforcing the role of culture in driving economic growth. The transmission of tacit knowledge was also a function of the technology and costs of codification, as well as the payoff to efforts to do so. This highlights the importance of cultural factors in facilitating the transmission of useful knowledge and driving economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run growth. They propose that European mortality rates in colonies determined the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that persisted and affected current income per capita. This mechanism is supported by evidence showing a strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions. In addition, the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to have played a significant role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among formerly poor colonies. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is further emphasized by the fact that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to maximize long-run growth. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonialism had a lasting effect on the economic development of these regions. Austin (2008, p.25) qualifies this by noting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This implies that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run growth may vary depending on the historical context and the specific institutions in place. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are therefore a key factor in understanding the long-run growth of former colonies, but their effects must be considered within the broader historical and institutional context.

This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who find that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This pattern is shared with the finding that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). One mechanism through which inequality and mistrust can affect economic development is by shaping institutions in ways that hinder growth. For instance, high levels of inequality can lead to institutions that protect the interests of the elite at the expense of broader economic development. This is evident in the persistence of inequality in New World societies, where institutions were designed to maintain the power and wealth of the elite. The persistence of inequality can be further exacerbated by a culture of mistrust, which can undermine social cohesion and reduce the effectiveness of collective action. This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of Africa, where the legacy of the slave trade has left a lasting impact on social and economic institutions. The relationship between inequality, mistrust, and economic development is complex and multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon note that the malaria transmission index provides a close approximation to historical conditions, suggesting that environmental factors may also play a role in shaping institutions and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This highlights the importance of considering multiple factors when analyzing the long-run effects of inequality and mistrust on economic development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping pre-industrial European economic institutions, a perspective shared by Broadberry and Gupta (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This framework emphasizes that the institutional structures of serfdom, the community, and guilds were driven by power struggles rather than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie's analysis suggests that these institutions emerged as a result of conflicts over the distribution of resources and power, which in turn influenced economic development. Specifically, Ogilvie's analysis of guilds highlights how distributional conflicts led to the establishment of institutions that regulated economic activities, such as craft and merchant guilds. Broadberry and Gupta extend this perspective by demonstrating that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, with Europe's institutional changes driving agricultural investment and productivity. This early divergence underscores the significance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic development. The interdependencies within these institutional frameworks, as noted by Ogilvie, complicate the direct attribution of economic outcomes to specific institutions. These interdependencies suggest that the overall institutional framework, rather than individual institutions, is crucial for understanding economic development. This nuanced view of institutional development underscores the need for a holistic approach to studying the long-run effects of distributional conflicts on economic growth.

Nunn (2008, p.27) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) share a focus on Africa's economic development and institutional reforms. They argue that historical factors, such as slave trades and colonial rule, have significantly impacted Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn contends that the slave trades led to long-term political instability and weakened states, which in turn hindered economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27). This historical instability is a direct result of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. Hopkins adds that institutional variables are more significant than other variables in explaining development, and that current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This historical depth underscores the importance of understanding Africa's precolonial and colonial past to grasp the current institutional landscape. However, Hopkins also notes that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective in promoting growth if they are congruent with indigenous institutions. High degrees of congruence underline the legitimacy of governments and enhance their ability to foster institutions that induce development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This congruence is crucial for creating conditions that favor stable governance and economic growth, rather than regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. The historical legacy of the slave trades and colonial rule thus shapes Africa's institutional framework, impacting its long-run economic development.

The role of culture in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally intertwined with the institutions that govern societies. This relationship is evident in the historical context of Africa, where the legacy of the slave trade and colonial rule has significantly influenced the institutional framework and, consequently, economic development. The slave trade, for instance, has been shown to have caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, impacting the development of institutions that foster growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent force that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework underscores the need to study entire institutional systems rather than individual components in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This holistic approach is crucial for understanding how cultural beliefs and values, such as those related to trust and reciprocity, can influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical legacy of institutions is also evident in the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, played a critical role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in fostering economic growth and political freedom, demonstrating the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The historical evidence shows that the institutions governing public choice in England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The historical evidence from Africa and England highlights the enduring impact of cultural and institutional legacies on long-run economic growth. Both regions show that the evolution of institutions is deeply rooted in cultural beliefs and historical experiences. The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
