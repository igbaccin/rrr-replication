The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars. At stake is the intellectual debate over whether cultural factors fundamentally influence economic outcomes by shaping institutions that govern economic activity. The disagreement hinges on whether culture operates as a dominant force in establishing institutions that foster growth, or if other factors, such as geographic determinants, are more influential. Some scholars posit that cultural norms and beliefs directly influence the institutional frameworks that support economic growth. These institutions, in turn, determine the allocation of resources and the incentives for economic activity. Others argue that geographical factors, such as climate and disease environments, shape institutional development and economic outcomes more directly. The debate also extends to whether cultural factors are endogenous to institutional development or if they operate independently to influence economic growth. This review will explore how cultural factors shape institutions that drive long-run economic growth, and how these institutions interact with other determinants of economic outcomes.

The cultural perspective on institutional development emphasizes the role of historical and social norms in shaping economic institutions. North and Weingast highlight that institutions play a critical role in economic growth by providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs. They argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This, in turn, reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt. This dynamic underscores how cultural and institutional factors interact to support economic growth. North and Weingast's analysis complements Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits that unchanging environmental obstacles determine economic development. Austin argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what is called the 'sophisticated geography' approach. Africans' responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. This perspective supports the idea that cultural factors, alongside geographic determinants, significantly influence institutions that foster economic growth. Acemoglu et al. North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutional evolution, particularly in seventeenth-century England, was pivotal for securing property rights and fostering economic growth. They underscore the significance of the financial revolution, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional development was a critical enabler of long-term economic growth and political stability, responding to economic pressures. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and bolster property rights, creating a stable environment for economic activity. This institutional framework was instrumental in facilitating both public and private debt markets, thereby fostering a broader economic ecosystem. The implications of these institutional changes extend beyond economic growth; they also underpin the development of political freedom and long-term capital markets. Other factors, including cultural and technological advancements, also play significant roles. Despite this, the institutional framework set in motion during the seventeenth century remains a cornerstone of England's economic success.

This influence of mortality rates on institutional establishment, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48), underscores the enduring impact of historical events on current economic conditions. The mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies directly impacted the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to the creation of extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for European colonists, promoting long-term economic development. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their rents, often at the expense of long-term growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this by noting that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This mechanism underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time underscores the challenge of institutional change. The extractive institutions established during the colonial period have had enduring effects on the economic performance of former colonies, even as the broader institutional framework has evolved. This persistence underscores the difficulty of reversing the effects of extractive institutions once they are in place, as they become entrenched and resistant to change.

This persistence of historical institutional variables, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), is crucial for understanding the economic development of Africa. They argue that institutional variables are more significant than other variables in explaining development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) posits that institutional change is gradual and calls for historical investigation because current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory. This historical perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who note that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. The field of African economic history has experienced a 'renaissance' due to newly digitized sources of data and the application of innovative techniques. These methods reveal that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This suggests that the economic performance of Africa is deeply rooted in historical institutional variables, which have shaped the continent's economic trajectory over centuries. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further emphasizes that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective in promoting growth if they are congruent with indigenous institutions. High degrees of congruence underline the legitimacy of governments and enhance their ability to foster institutions that induce development. Low levels of congruence have the opposite effect and create conditions that favour patrimonial politics, which lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. Understanding these historical institutional variables can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This underscores the importance of considering the historical context when designing institutional reforms in Africa.

This shaping of cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, due to the impact of the slave trade on African development, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) (Nunn 2008, p.27), highlights the long-term economic outcomes influenced by historical events. This is because a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. The slave trade thus may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. This mistrust and institutional weakness can create an environment where economic development is hindered. This variability suggests that other factors, such as colonial boundaries and malaria transmission, may also play a role in shaping African development.

African women's economic contributions are vital to understanding the continent's growth, with Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8) highlighting significant roles in pre-colonial and colonial periods. The role of women in economic development is illustrated through their involvement in community reproduction and trade networks. For instance, in Asante society, women were essential to the transmission of office and wealth, underscoring their indispensable role in society (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. also underscores the broader implications of women's economic roles. These changes involved significant shifts in educational institutions and household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). The implications of these transformations are significant. They suggest that understanding the role of women in economic growth requires considering both historical contributions and contemporary changes in social norms and institutions. The impact of women on economic development is multifaceted and extends beyond immediate contributions to include long-term effects on social structures and economic institutions.

They share a view that the divergence between Europe and Asia was driven by distinct institutional trajectories, with Europe's path being more conducive to economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which can obscure the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. This oversimplification can lead to an incomplete understanding of how institutions evolve and their impact on long-term economic growth. This early divergence underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes over the long run. This qualification highlights the nuanced relationship between institutions and economic development, where institutional changes can have varied and complex effects on different sectors of the economy. The interplay between institutions and economic development is multifaceted, requiring a deeper understanding of how institutional changes can lead to different economic outcomes in various contexts.
