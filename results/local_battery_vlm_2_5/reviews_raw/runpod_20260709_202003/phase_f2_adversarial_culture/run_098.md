The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in the field of economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether cultural factors are a primary driver of institutional development or whether institutions evolve independently of cultural influences. On one side, scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values significantly shape economic institutions, influencing long-term economic performance. On the other side, some researchers posit that institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors such as geography and mortality rates, with culture playing a secondary role. This debate is crucial because it determines how policymakers should approach economic development: whether they should focus on cultural reforms or on institutional changes. The resolution of this debate could significantly impact the strategies employed to foster economic growth, especially in regions that have historically lagged behind. For example, the role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions has been highlighted by scholars who contend that certain cultural values, such as trust and reciprocity, facilitate coordinated actions that improve economic efficiency. However, other researchers emphasize the importance of institutional frameworks that support secure property rights and economic growth, suggesting that cultural factors are less influential. This tension underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the interplay between culture and institutions in driving long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The role of institutions in economic growth is underscored by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and significantly enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). By providing a stable market for public debt, the Bank of England also facilitated the development of a market for private debt, offering a large and positive externality (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions operated and their long-run effects are subjects of ongoing debate.

The legacy of colonial institutions, particularly extractive ones, underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic development, as seen in the long-term effects on growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) find that the presence of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the long-lasting economic impacts of institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices. This institutional framework had profound implications for economic development, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) demonstrate a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as the significant impact of these institutions on income per capita. The extractive nature of these institutions meant that they were not designed to encourage economic development but rather to maximize the benefits for the colonizing power. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their control and extract rents, rather than fostering economic growth. The legacy of these institutions persists, as they continue to influence economic performance in former colonies.

The distributional conflicts, as analyzed by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), reveal that the economic outcomes are deeply influenced by the institutional arrangements that arise from these conflicts. These authors demonstrate that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over the distribution of resources and power. This distributional conflict, rather than cultural factors, is a primary driver of institutional arrangements. The institutional frameworks that emerged from these conflicts, such as the community and the guild system, were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the powerful. These institutions were not necessarily efficient or growth-oriented, but rather served to entrench the existing power dynamics. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with various markets and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. The persistence of inequality in New World societies suggests that the institutional frameworks that emerged from these distributional conflicts could also hinder economic growth and development. The qualitative differences in factor endowments, such as land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These inequalities were reinforced by institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This highlights that while distributional conflicts shape institutions, the resulting frameworks can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The pivotal role of ideas and ideologies in economic change, as argued by McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56), highlights the importance of cultural factors in driving economic growth during the period from 1600 to 1848. Ideas shaped economic outcomes by influencing societal attitudes towards innovation and entrepreneurship. For example, the cultural shifts in the Netherlands and Britain fostered a new rhetoric that encouraged economic growth. This rhetoric created new circumstances, which in turn generated more innovative ideas. This process was not merely about the accumulation of information but involved a complex interplay of cultural and ideological factors. The growth of useful knowledge was not confined to written texts but also included tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge was crucial for economic development, as it embodied skills and expertise that were not always codified. The diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, highlighting the importance of a shared intellectual movement. The role of tacit knowledge complicates the idea that economic growth can be solely attributed to the dissemination of codified information. The transmission of tacit knowledge required social structures that enabled personal interactions and training, underscoring the importance of cultural and institutional frameworks in facilitating economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia, as addressed by Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), illustrate the varied historical paths and institutional evolutions that have shaped economic outcomes. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'simple geography' thesis, suggesting that the African story indicates a more dynamic and partly endogenous approach. This dynamic approach acknowledges Africans' own responses to their environments, which shaped economic development. They argue that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 were more similar to the stagnating parts of Europe rather than the developing north-western regions. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the early modern period as a critical phase in the great divergence, where institutional changes and investment patterns played pivotal roles.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that the slave trade, by causing a culture of mistrust, has long-lasting effects on African development. They demonstrate that historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn influence long-term economic outcomes. The slave trade weakened state centralization, leading to political instability and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, has adverse effects on economic development. This mechanism is further supported by Nunn, who argues that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, indicating that the legacy of the slave trade extends beyond immediate economic impacts to include institutional development and long-run growth. The relationship between the slave trade and economic development is complex. While the slave trade's impact on state centralization and political instability is clear, the extent to which these factors influence economic development varies across different historical contexts. Nunn (2008, p.27) notes that recent empirical studies suggest that Africa's history can explain part of its current underdevelopment, focusing on the link between countries' colonial experience and current economic development. This implies that the impact of the slave trade on African development is not isolated but intertwined with other historical factors, such as colonialism.

Institutions are fundamentally shaped by cultural beliefs and values, which in turn influence economic growth. Ogilvie highlights the role of cultural beliefs in shaping economic institutions, noting that distributional conflicts rather than efficiency often drive the formation of these institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that cultural beliefs such as trust and norms of reciprocity can facilitate coordinated actions and improve economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these cultural beliefs over time underscores their enduring impact on institutional frameworks and economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework further complicates the role of culture in economic growth. Ogilvie argues that institutions are interconnected and must be studied as part of a wider framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency is evident in the European family system, which was linked to labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, all of which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with various markets and family systems, potentially fostering economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is further illustrated by the impact of the slave trade on African development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy has had profound implications for economic development, highlighting the long-term effects of historical cultural influences (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The endurance of these cultural beliefs and values over centuries underscores their role in shaping institutions and economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they provide the framework within which economic activities occur. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further illustrate this point by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England played a critical role in the country's long-run success, demonstrating the necessity of institutions in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the enduring impact of cultural beliefs and values on institutional frameworks. The shared finding is that institutions matter for long-run growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). There is a disagreement over the extent to which cultural beliefs and values drive the formation of institutions. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12), while Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on African development settles this disagreement, as it provides a clear example of how cultural beliefs and values shaped by historical events can have long-lasting effects on institutions and economic outcomes.
