The role of culture in shaping institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether cultural factors are primary drivers of economic institutions or if they are secondary to other determinants. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby affecting economic growth. Others propose that institutions are primarily shaped by factors such as geography, disease environments, and extractive colonial practices, with culture playing a secondary role. The stakes are high, as understanding the causal mechanisms behind long-run growth can inform policies aimed at fostering economic development. This debate centres around the relative importance of cultural factors versus other determinants in shaping institutions that drive economic growth. The live disagreement hinges on whether cultural beliefs and values are the primary drivers of economic institutions or if they are overshadowed by other factors such as geography and colonial practices. This question is pivotal because it determines whether policies aimed at fostering economic development should prioritize cultural changes or focus on other institutional reforms. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They demonstrate how the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was a critical factor in enabling long-term economic growth, responding to economic forces. The implications of this institutional framework are far-reaching. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) underscore that even if other forces had ultimately led England to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without the institutional changes. The secure property rights facilitated by these institutions provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework supported economic growth and fostered political freedom, highlighting the interconnected nature of economic and political institutions. However, the stability of these institutions is contingent on broader political and social conditions, as North (1989, p.9) notes that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War played a pivotal role in the original trade of certain rights to Parliament.

Colonial institutions significantly affected long-run growth in former colonies, challenging the role of culture as the fundamental cause of growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. show that high European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism operates through the political power of colonists, who designed institutions to maximize their rents. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutions are directly linked to the political and economic strategies of colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, played a crucial role in determining the economic trajectories of former colonies, illustrating how exogenous shocks can lead to institutional paths that affect long-run growth.

This historical context underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development, where cultural and institutional factors intertwine. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a persistent institutional outcome. In turn, this mistrust has impeded the development of robust institutions necessary for economic growth. Nunn shows that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability has had lasting effects on state centralization, which is a critical factor in economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27). However, Nunn also notes that the impact of slavery on current economic development is not straightforward, and recent empirical studies suggest that Africa's history can explain part of its current underdevelopment. These studies focus on the link between countries' colonial experience and current economic development (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.1). This suggests that while the slave trades have had a significant impact on African development, other historical factors, such as colonialism, also play a role in shaping current economic outcomes.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that economic growth is primarily driven by the growth of useful knowledge and ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective foregrounds the role of ideologies and cultural shifts in economic development. For McCloskey, the "Great Enrichment" in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by a new rhetoric that fostered economic growth. This rhetoric, which emphasized bourgeois values and dishonorable aristocratic attitudes, created new circumstances that, in turn, generated new rhetoric. Mokyr extends this idea by emphasizing the importance of useful knowledge, which was diffused widely within Europe. This knowledge was codified in technical drawings or periodicals and also embodied in tacit forms such as skills and personal training. The transmission of this tacit knowledge was crucial for economic growth. Mokyr suggests that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by personal communications and international conferences. This cultural exchange allowed for the rapid dissemination of useful knowledge, which in turn drove economic growth. Mokyr qualifies the ease of knowledge diffusion, noting that access to knowledge depended on written records and also on personal transmission and training. The implication of Mokyr's qualification is that the effectiveness of cultural exchange in driving economic growth is contingent on the mechanisms of knowledge transmission.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) re-evaluate historical economic theories by examining the dynamics of institutional evolution and distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective offers insights into how these factors shaped economic development. This is evident in the way pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts and by efficiency. These institutions were deeply rooted in the power dynamics and conflicts of the time. Austin (2008, p.25) shows that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. (2002) call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach emphasizes the role of endogenous factors, including cultural responses to environmental challenges, in shaping economic development. The implication of this dynamic is that cultural factors, while not the sole determinant, play a significant role in shaping institutions and, by extension, economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) qualifies the role of cultural factors by highlighting the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. This suggests that while cultural factors are important, they are part of a complex web of interactions that also include economic, political, and social elements.

The early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia is evident by 1800, with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15); (Pamuk 2007, p.24) arguing that this divergence was shaped by differences in urban development, labour productivity, and institutional changes. Broadberry and Gupta show that in the developing regions of north-western Europe, institutional change drove investment in agriculture, leading to high labour productivity and economic development. This investment did not result in lower food prices because labour shifted out of agriculture into industry and services. In the advanced parts of India and China, grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower. This discrepancy suggests that cultural and institutional factors, such as investment patterns and labour mobility, played a crucial role in shaping economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.5) argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes, including greater labour mobility and institutional changes in the guilds, which contributed to the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations. The mechanisms driving these changes are complex, but the evidence supports the idea that institutional changes and cultural factors, such as labour mobility and technological innovation, were significant drivers of economic divergence. The implication is that cultural factors, while not the sole determinant, played a significant role in shaping institutions and, by extension, economic growth. The role of cultural factors is part of a complex web of interactions that also include economic, political, and social elements.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and de Vries (1994, p.18) focus on specific technological and institutional changes in agriculture, highlighting how these developments influenced agricultural practices and economic behavior. This technological innovation, which facilitated more effective land management, is a key mechanism through which agricultural development was spurred. De Vries (1994, p.18) similarly identifies the Industrious Revolution as a distinct phenomenon preceding and facilitating the Industrial Revolution, driven by increased labor input and new consumption patterns. This revolution in labor practices and consumption norms highlights how institutional changes in agriculture can drive broader economic shifts. The Industrious Revolution's impact on economic behavior suggests that cultural factors, such as changes in norms around labor and consumption, can be a critical component of institutional change. The relationship between these cultural factors and long-run growth remains complex. De Vries notes that while Europe's urban network established a framework for regional economic development, it also required industrial growth to fully materialize. This underscores the necessity of broader economic and technological developments for the full realization of institutional changes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Cultural factors, such as norms around labor and consumption, can significantly influence institutional change and economic behavior. This highlights how cultural shifts, such as changes in trust and reciprocity norms, can underpin the development of institutions that support long-run growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that cultural factors can shape the institutional frameworks that drive economic development (North 1989, p.1). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is recognized by studies of European economic institutions, suggesting that cultural beliefs and values can encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cultural factors also play a role in shaping long-run growth through their impact on trust and social capital. Putnam's theory of 'social capital' highlights how features of social organization, such as trust, norms, and networks, can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This indicates that cultural factors can have long-lasting effects on economic development by shaping the institutional frameworks that support growth.
