The role of culture in shaping institutions and driving long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic development: is it the cultural beliefs and values that shape institutions, or are institutions shaped by other factors, with culture playing a secondary role? One position argues that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby impacting economic growth. This perspective posits that cultural norms can motivate individuals to adhere to certain institutional rules, which in turn affect economic outcomes. For example, the Protestant Reformation is said to have motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, which in turn influenced economic efficiency. Another position contends that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and mortality rates, with culture playing a lesser role. This view suggests that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the importance of institutions in economic development. The live disagreement hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional formation and economic growth, or whether it is secondary to other factors such as geography and mortality rates. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the underlying mechanisms of economic development and the role of culture in shaping institutions. North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They contend that the evolution of institutions in England, particularly those related to public finance and property rights, played a pivotal role in the country's economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework allowed for the growth of a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant of long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established. This was because the mortality rates determined the feasibility of European settlement, which in turn shaped the institutions that were introduced. These institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic performance of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further explain that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize benefits for the colonizers, not to encourage economic development. They argue that the equilibrium institutions in colonies were shaped by the politically powerful groups, who were often the European colonists. These groups had an interest in maintaining institutions that would maximize their rents, even if it meant stifling long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implications of this argument are significant, as it suggests that the long-run economic outcomes of former colonies are deeply rooted in the institutional legacies of colonialism. This challenges the idea that cultural factors alone determine economic growth, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) agree that distributional conflicts fundamentally shape economic institutions, not efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that the institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe were driven by struggles over wealth and power. She critiques the efficiency-based explanations, which often attribute these institutions to cultural factors. For example, the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They highlight that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of these societies led to institutional differences that impacted long-term development. The interplay between economic institutions and distributional conflicts suggests that cultural factors, while influential, are not the sole determinants of economic growth. The persistence of inequality in New World societies, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue, underscores the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic performance.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Both studies point to a complex interplay of institutions and economic development. A key mechanism in this process is the role of institutional change in driving investment and productivity. This suggests that institutional frameworks that facilitate investment and productivity growth are crucial for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) clarifies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that institutional effectiveness varies over time. This temporal variability indicates that the impact of institutions on economic growth evolves with historical contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) highlight that women have played a pivotal role in economic growth and development, both historically and contemporaneously. In pre-colonial Africa, women were crucial to the reproduction of communities, with their roles in matrilineal societies defining the transmission of office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores the importance of women's contributions to economic productivity and social stability. In the United States, a quiet revolution transformed women's employment and education, driven by changes in social norms and increased opportunities for women in the workplace (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts were catalyzed by exogenous factors such as the growth of educational institutions and changes in household production technology. Goldin (2006, p.8) observes that these transformations were not uniform and varied by cohort, suggesting that the impact of these changes on economic growth was not immediate or uniform across all segments of society. This suggests that while women's roles in economic development are significant, the mechanisms through which these contributions translate into long-run growth are complex and multifaceted. This complexity is further illustrated by the enduring effects of colonial constructs on gender relations, which continue to influence economic outcomes in post-colonial contexts.
