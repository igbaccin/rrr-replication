The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography and institutional theory. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural beliefs and values are the primary drivers of institutional change and, consequently, economic growth. On one side, scholars argue that cultural factors, such as norms, values, and beliefs, significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. On the other side, scholars propose that institutions are shaped by more material factors, such as geographical determinants, mortality rates, and political dynamics. These competing views highlight the need to understand the interplay between cultural factors and institutional frameworks in fostering long-run growth. The debate centers on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional change or merely a secondary factor influenced by other determinants. The literature on this topic reveals a complex interplay between culture and institutions, with some scholars emphasizing the role of cultural values in shaping economic institutions and others focusing on material factors such as geography and political dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions, particularly those governing public choice, were instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period dramatically lowered transaction costs, reflecting an increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was pivotal in enabling long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate that the financial revolution in England was crucial for the country’s long-run success, suggesting that even if other forces might have eventually led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further enhancing economic stability and growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutional changes have qualified implications. While the financial revolution was indeed a turning point, the specific conditions that allowed these institutions to flourish in England may not be universally applicable. This underscores the importance of contextual factors in the effectiveness of institutions, suggesting that the success of institutional reforms in fostering economic growth depends on the unique historical and political circumstances of each society.

Cultural factors influence economic institutions, but distributional conflicts fundamentally shape power dynamics and resource allocation within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts, not efficiency or cultural beliefs. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of powerful groups, such as landowners and guilds, at the expense of others. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) analysis of New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The mechanism by which distributional conflicts shape economic institutions is through the control of political power. Powerful groups use their influence to design institutions that protect their interests and maintain their dominance. This process is not deterministic. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) warns that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that institutional change is complex and unpredictable. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a key driver of institutional change, the specific outcomes depend on the unique historical and political circumstances of each society. The implication is that institutional reforms aimed at promoting economic growth must address the underlying distributional conflicts.

This perspective is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48), who argue that the institutional legacies of colonialism are a primary determinant of economic outcomes in former colonies. They argue that the mortality rates faced by European colonizers determined the type of institutions established in colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework persisted over time, significantly affecting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) demonstrate a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the robustness of this institutional legacy. The implication is that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectories of former colonies, shaping their long-run growth patterns.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). He notes that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, faces challenges in explaining why favourable institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others. Austin (2008, p.2) also shares the rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Instead, he suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic highlights the role of endogenous factors in shaping economic development, suggesting that cultural responses to environmental challenges can significantly influence institutional outcomes. This divergence underscores the importance of institutional changes in driving economic development, as higher agricultural labour productivity in Europe led to investment in industry and services, whereas in Asia, similar productivity levels did not translate into comparable economic growth. This pattern suggests that cultural and institutional factors played a crucial role in shaping the economic trajectories of these regions.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) (Hopkins 2009, p.8) highlight the enduring influence of historical institutional variables on Africa's economic development. This perspective underscores the significance of long-term institutional changes, which are often gradual and deeply rooted in precolonial and pre-historic contexts. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that current African institutions are products of a complex historical process that extends well beyond the colonial period. This historical continuity suggests that understanding contemporary economic challenges requires delving into Africa's deep historical institutional context. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that African economies were more integrated into global markets than previously thought, with export production playing a pivotal role in economic growth patterns. This integration was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Englebert's work, as cited by Hopkins (2009, p.18), adds that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth is enhanced when they align with indigenous institutions. This congruence fosters governmental legitimacy and reduces the likelihood of patrimonial politics, regionalism, centelism, and corruption. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) caution that the historical data used to study African growth often begins too late to capture the full scope of economic changes. A nuanced understanding of Africa's economic past requires detailed historical data and a sensitivity to the enduring impact of precolonial institutions on contemporary economic development.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) demonstrates that the Black Death catalyzed long-term structural changes in Europe, driving economic development through increased labor costs and technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24; van Zanden 1999, p.2). The Black Death's impact on Europe was profound, leading to a significant wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence was deeply rooted in the structural changes initiated by the plague. The plague's impact on labor markets was particularly noteworthy, as it led to a scarcity of labor, which in turn drove up wages. The high cost of labor also incentivized the adoption of new technologies that reduced the need for human labor, further accelerating economic development. This period saw a decline in interest rates and the skill premium, reflecting the broader economic transformations underway. The economic development of Europe during this period was influenced by a variety of factors, including the development of silver wages and the role of cities like London in driving economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.6). This complexity suggests that while the Black Death was a pivotal event, its effects on long-run growth were mediated by a range of economic and institutional factors.

The Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge are distinct phenomena that precede and facilitate economic growth but do not directly address culture as the fundamental cause (de Vries 1994, p.18; Mokyr 2005, p.56). de Vries (1994, p.18) argues that the Industrious Revolution, which involved increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a crucial precursor to the Industrial Revolution. This period saw a significant shift in how labor was utilized, with households releasing their labor reserves to the market and relying more on market-supplied services (de Vries 1994, p.18). This transformation involved technological advancements and changes in labor practices and consumption behaviors. This knowledge was often embodied in tacit forms, such as skills and implicit understanding, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). Mokyr (2005, p.56) further notes that the diffusion of this knowledge across Europe was facilitated by the Enlightenment, which promoted the exchange of ideas through periodicals, translations, and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This dissemination of knowledge was crucial for economic development, as it allowed for the widespread adoption of new technologies and practices. The role of culture in facilitating this growth remains under-explored. While the Industrious Revolution and the growth of useful knowledge laid the groundwork for economic development, the specific cultural factors that drove these phenomena are not fully elucidated in these studies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) highlight the significant role of women in economic growth and development, challenging the marginalization of their contributions. In pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women were indispensable to community reproduction, particularly in matrilineal societies where their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth. This dynamic is evident in the Akan state formation, where women's roles were crucial despite the intensifying competition over resources and trade routes. This economic significance was not limited to Africa; in the U.S., a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics. Goldin (2006, p.3) notes that exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions, altered female labor supply and made "nice" jobs available. These shifts highlight how cultural and institutional changes can empower women economically. The long-term consequences of colonial constructs and codified gender roles, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) note, may persist. This suggests that while women's contributions are significant, the enduring impact of historical gender constructs can shape contemporary economic dynamics.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa created a culture of mistrust that has persisted over centuries, significantly impacting contemporary levels of trust. This mistrust is a legacy of the historical political instability and weakened states caused by the slave trade. The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to long-term political instability and fragmented states, which in turn hindered economic development. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability. This instability resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political fragmentation has enduring effects on economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.1) explains that slavery may be seen as one source of precolonial origins for modern corruption. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4; 2011, p.15) also note that the malaria transmission index provides a close approximation to historical conditions, indicating that the environmental factors contributing to the slave trade's impact have remained relatively stable. This suggests that the cultural legacy of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, continues to influence contemporary African societies, potentially complicating efforts to foster economic growth through institutional reforms. The mistrust and political instability caused by the slave trade are thus deeply intertwined with the broader cultural and institutional dynamics that shape long-run economic growth.

The significance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions is further highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate the gains from trade that are key to the wealth of nations. The creation of secure property rights, as discussed by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), played a critical role in England's long-run success, demonstrating how institutions can foster economic growth and political freedom. The financial revolution in England, with the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

This contribution challenges views that marginalize their role and highlights the dynamic interplay between cultural beliefs and economic outcomes.
