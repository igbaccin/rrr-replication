The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography and institutional economics. At stake is the fundamental question of what causes sustained economic development. One perspective posits that cultural values and beliefs directly influence the formation of institutions, which in turn govern economic outcomes. This view suggests that cultural norms, such as trust and reciprocity, foster institutions that enhance economic efficiency and growth. A different viewpoint posits that the formation of institutions is mainly driven by exogenous factors, such as geographical conditions and mortality rates, alongside cultural influences. This debate is crucial because it determines whether policy interventions should focus on cultural transformation or institutional design. The resolution of this debate hinges on understanding the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural values are the primary drivers of institutional development or if they are merely complementary factors. The rival explanations propose that distributional conflicts and mortality rates are the fundamental causes of institutional development. The resolution of this debate will determine the role of culture in fostering institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They contend that the evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in establishing a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the growth of a market for private debt. North (1989, p.1) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional framework, which included the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, was a product of fiscal crises and laid the groundwork for long-term economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further emphasize that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, suggesting that even if other forces would have ultimately led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England, shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt, began private operations, which further solidified the economic framework. The implication is that the institutional framework was necessary for making possible economic growth and political freedom.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies, as well as the growth of useful knowledge, were pivotal in driving economic change. The cultural struggle for new rhetoric and ideas, which in turn shaped new circumstances, was a key mechanism by which economic growth was fostered. The Great Enrichment was a product of institutional changes, intertwined with the evolution of ideas and ideologies, which enabled new circumstances. Mokyr (2005, p.56) adds that the diffusion of useful knowledge across borders was critical. This diffusion was facilitated by the dissemination of knowledge through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which underscored the importance of a shared intellectual movement. This shared movement was a global phenomenon that transcended specific institutional frameworks, not confined to national boundaries. The implication is that the growth of useful knowledge, a global process, fostered economic growth, not limited by national institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.14) also notes that much of the useful knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills and practices, which were transmitted through personal training and apprenticeship. This tacit knowledge, while crucial, was not easily codified and required personal interaction for its dissemination. Therefore, the growth of useful knowledge, while global in its reach, was also deeply rooted in local, personal networks.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which are the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current economic performance. High mortality rates in colonies led to the creation of extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, established during the colonial period, persists and continues to affect economic outcomes today. The simplicity of this thesis is challenged by the diverse historical paths and institutional evolutions of different colonies, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific and context-dependent (Austin 2008, p.25).

The slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma and exploitation experienced during the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and fostered a deep-seated skepticism toward others. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states, further support this argument (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability is a key mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy continues to affect economic development. This institutional framework, shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade, persists and continues to affect economic outcomes today. The legacy of the slave trade extends to the social fabric of African societies, creating a culture of mistrust that hinders collective action and economic cooperation, not limited to economic underdevelopment.

Distributional conflicts are a fundamental driver in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality, offering a compelling alternative to cultural determinants of long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, were actively maintained to preserve the status quo, not merely a byproduct of cultural factors. This interdependency means that the institutions created to address distributional conflicts are likely to be deeply entrenched and resistant to change, further reinforcing the economic and social structures that favor elites. This dynamic of distributional conflicts shaping institutions and perpetuating inequality underscores the importance of considering the political economy of institutional change in understanding long-run growth.

The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is multifaceted, with some scholars emphasizing the direct influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, can motivate economic behaviors and shape institutions like guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further illustrate this by demonstrating how the financial revolution in England, driven by institutional changes, led to secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Cultural beliefs and institutions are thus intertwined in fostering economic development. The specific mechanisms by which culture influences institutions remain a subject of scholarly debate. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more nuanced view of how institutions evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.2). This view is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement concerns whether cultural factors or institutional frameworks have a more direct impact on long-run growth. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores the enduring impact of historical cultural factors on contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that cultural beliefs can shape institutions over the long run. The historical evidence of the financial revolution in England and the enduring cultural mistrust in Africa settles the disagreement by showing that both cultural factors and institutional frameworks play crucial roles in long-run growth, with cultural beliefs often shaping the institutions that drive economic development.
