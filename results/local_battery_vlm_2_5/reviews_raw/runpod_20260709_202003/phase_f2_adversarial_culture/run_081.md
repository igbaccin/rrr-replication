The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is understanding the fundamental forces that propel economic development over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional change or secondary to other, more material determinants. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. They contend that cultural factors, such as trust, norms, and social networks, can facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic growth. Others posit that cultural factors are less influential and that institutional frameworks are shaped primarily by exogenous factors such as geography or economic conditions. The debate hinges on whether cultural elements are integral to the institutional frameworks that underpin long-term economic success or whether they are merely epiphenomena of deeper structural forces. In this debate, the role of culture in institutional evolution is a critical battleground. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North contends that institutions, rather than population or savings, are fundamental to economic growth (North 1989, p.1). Acemoglu et al. share this institutional focus, arguing that extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North illustrates this with the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the impact of institutions on economic growth is shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize maximizing their own rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional perspective challenges cultural explanations by highlighting that the design of institutions is influenced by the interests of those in power. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional view suggests that the long-run economic growth of a society is fundamentally determined by the quality of its institutions, which can either facilitate or hinder economic development.

This institutional perspective, however, does not account for the influence of cultural and social norms on the effectiveness of property rights enforcement. These scholars demonstrate that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, which led to the growth of a stable market for public debt, played a critical role in the nation's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This financial revolution provided a significant positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, enabling secure property rights and facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework is a deliberate outcome of public choice institutions that prioritize secure property rights. Hornbeck's analysis of agricultural development on the American Plains further illustrates this point, showing that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced property protection and reduced externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This finding underscores the importance of institutional innovations in protecting property rights and fostering economic development. Hornbeck also notes that in some settings, social norms can play a complementary role in encouraging property protection, as seen in modern California where ranchers control cattle through social norms (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This suggests that while institutions are fundamental, cultural and social norms can also influence the effectiveness of property rights enforcement.

Ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, as McCloskey and Mokyr share (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective challenges the institutional focus of Hornbeck. The process of economic growth was propelled by the dissemination of useful knowledge and the cultivation of a rhetoric that embraced innovation and progress. This knowledge was codified in texts and embodied in tacit skills and know-how, which were transmitted through personal interactions and apprenticeships (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The diffusion of this knowledge was facilitated by international conferences, periodicals, and translations, which created a shared intellectual movement across Europe (Mokyr 2005, p.56). Mokyr notes that the effectiveness of these mechanisms depended on the technology and costs of codification, as well as the payoff to efforts to do so (Mokyr 2005, p.14).

This focus on distributional conflicts shifts the emphasis away from cultural factors and towards the political and economic dynamics that shape institutions. They contend that the persistence of institutions such as serfdom and craft guilds can be attributed to the political struggles between different social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality had institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These policies were maintained because they served the interests of powerful groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the effectiveness of these institutions is contingent upon the broader political and economic context. This interdependency highlights the complexity of institutional change and the importance of considering the entire institutional framework. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies the downstream implications of this perspective by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions may vary over time. The significance of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the political dynamics that underpin institutional change.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade was a catalyst for a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has had lasting effects on economic development. The slave trade disrupted social structures and created an environment where trust was undermined, leading to long-term political instability and weakened state institutions. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, hindered economic development by creating an environment where institutions were unable to function effectively. The historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability are consistent with the empirical research showing that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. This suggests that the slave trade's impact on African economic development was a result of immediate disruptions and the long-term institutional weaknesses it created.

Goldin (2006, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that women's roles in economic growth and development are significant, though not directly addressing how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. The role of women in economic development is multifaceted, involving both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as changes in gender relations and production systems. For instance, Akan state formation in the 17th century highlights the indispensable role of women in community reproduction, where their bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth in a matrilineal society (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores the economic and social contributions of women, which have long-term implications for gender production and the broader economic landscape. Goldin notes that the transformation of women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. was driven by exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts illustrate how societal norms and economic structures interact to shape women's roles in development, offering a nuanced perspective on the broader theme of culture's influence on institutions.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how cultural and institutional factors interact to shape economic outcomes over the long term. The interplay between culture and institutions is further underscored by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and low transaction costs are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast illustrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical evidence shows that cultural and institutional frameworks are integral to economic development.

Cultural beliefs and values are active forces that shape economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, and the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic interaction between culture and institutions is also evident in the work of North, who shows how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This approach acknowledges the complex interplay between cultural beliefs, institutional frameworks, and economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the historical and empirical evidence presented. All scholars agree that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The remaining disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs are exogenous or endogenous to the institutional framework. Ogilvie argues that exogenous cultural theories provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin shows that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic interaction between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks in the context of African state formation, where women's indispensable role in the reproduction of communities highlights the endogenous nature of cultural beliefs within the institutional framework (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).
