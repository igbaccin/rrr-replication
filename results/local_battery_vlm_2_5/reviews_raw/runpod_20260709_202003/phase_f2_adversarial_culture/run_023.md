The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the explanation of persistent economic disparities between regions and nations. Two positions exist. One is that culture influences institutions and, through them, long-run economic growth. The other is that institutions, shaped by other factors, are the primary drivers of growth. The former camp argues that culture, by shaping institutions, determines the efficiency of economic systems. For example, Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The latter camp, however, posits that institutions, shaped by exogenous factors, drive growth directly. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The disagreement hinges on whether culture is a critical antecedent to institutions that shape growth, or if institutions, shaped by other forces, are the primary drivers of growth. The stakes are high because the answer determines the focus of policy interventions aimed at fostering economic growth.

North and Weingast maintain that institutions, not cultural factors, are the primary drivers of economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694 led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, further reinforcing the role of institutions in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective is supported by Jeffrey Williamson's work, which provides further evidence for the thesis that institutions are crucial for economic development. The role of institutions in economic growth has qualifications. The financial revolution in England, while critical, does not negate the potential influence of other forces that could have led to success under different circumstances.

The 'reversal of fortune' and economic divergence theories primarily discuss the role of institutions and geographical factors in economic development, rather than focusing on culture as the fundamental driver of long-run growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis suggests that the institutional framework and geographical factors are the primary drivers of economic divergence. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further support this view by highlighting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This implies that the institutional and geographical factors played a significant role in economic development long before the industrial revolution. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what could be called the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This qualification underscores that while institutions and geography are crucial, they are not the sole determinants of economic growth. The dynamic responses of Africans to their environments, including the use of various technologies when available, highlight the importance of endogenous factors that go beyond mere institutional and geographical determinants. This perspective opens the door to considering how cultural factors, such as technological adaptation and endogenous responses, might also play a role in long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman jointly argue that distributional conflicts are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These conflicts involve power and resource allocation. Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts shaped European institutions such as serfdom and guilds, which were not necessarily efficient but served to maintain elite control. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman show how New World societies with greater inequality were characterized by institutions that favored elites, such as policies that restricted competition. These institutions, while not efficient, were instrumental in maintaining the status quo and the power of the elite. The persistence of these institutions underscores how distributional conflicts can lead to enduring economic structures that prioritize the interests of the powerful, even at the cost of overall economic efficiency. This perspective suggests that cultural factors, while influential, are secondary to the structural power dynamics that shape economic institutions.

McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective challenges the view that institutions or interests alone drive economic growth. McCloskey argues that the shift in rhetoric and ideology was the catalyst for the Great Enrichment. Mokyr (2005, p.56) supports this by noting that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. The diffusion of knowledge through periodicals, translations, and personal communications facilitated the spread of ideas and ideologies that drove economic growth. This diffusion was a broader intellectual movement that transcended borders. However, Mokyr also highlights that much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practices that were not easily codified. This tacit knowledge required personal transmission and training, which could slow the pace of diffusion and implementation. The implications of this are significant, as the spread of ideas and ideologies, while powerful, was not uniform or instantaneous. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus complex, involving both the rapid spread of ideas and the slower transmission of tacit knowledge.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This extractive institutional setup was reinforced by the political power dynamics of the time, where powerful groups resisted institutional changes that would not directly benefit them. The persistence of these extractive institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on long-term economic performance. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasizes that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even if they do not encourage economic development. This implies that the long-run growth effects of colonial institutions are complex and multifaceted, involving not just the initial extractive setup but also the subsequent resistance to institutional change. The institutional legacy of colonialism thus plays a crucial role in shaping long-term economic trajectories, with the persistence of extractive institutions being a key factor in understanding current economic disparities.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is seen as a rival explanation to broader cultural factors influencing growth. The mechanism by which the slave trade influenced economic development involves the long-term political instability and weakened states it caused. The slave trade disrupted state centralization, leading to political fragmentation (Nunn 2008, p.27). This fragmentation, in turn, hindered the establishment of effective institutions necessary for economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade thus persists through its impact on institutional development, which in turn affects economic outcomes. The slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are not limited to its immediate impacts but also include its long-term consequences on political stability and institutional quality, which can explain part of Africa's current underdevelopment.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted. Culture influences economic institutions, which in turn shape economic outcomes. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional frameworks established during pre-industrial and colonial periods have enduring effects on economic performance. Ogilvie demonstrates how distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, which had significant impacts on economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. reveal that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of historical institutional frameworks in determining long-run economic growth.

The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks is crucial for understanding economic development. Ogilvie and North highlight the interconnected nature of institutions, showing that various economic and social systems worked together to foster growth. Ogilvie discusses how the European family system, labour markets, and credit markets were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North emphasizes that secure property rights and the financial revolution in England were critical for economic growth, illustrating how institutions governing public choice played a necessary role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This interdependence suggests that studying individual institutions in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered.

The legacy of the slave trade and its impact on African institutions provides a stark example of how historical events shape long-run economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon, and Acemoglu et al. show how the slave trade's adverse effects on economic development include its long-term consequences on political stability and institutional quality. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Acemoglu et al. reveal that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings highlight the enduring impact of historical events on institutional development and economic outcomes.

The specific historical evidence of the English financial revolution and its role in fostering economic growth settles the disagreement between the enduring impact of cultural beliefs and the role of institutions in economic development. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Nunn and Wantchekon's demonstration that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), provides the empirical comparison that settles the disagreement. The English financial revolution and the culture of mistrust in Africa are specific historical events that illustrate the precise mechanisms by which culture and institutions interact to shape long-run economic growth.
