The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, are primary drivers of institutional quality and economic performance, or whether institutions themselves shape cultural outcomes. On one side, scholars argue that cultural beliefs and norms significantly influence the development and effectiveness of institutions, which in turn drive economic growth. On the other side, some contend that institutional frameworks, once established, can override cultural influences and dictate economic trajectories. Additionally, there are those who propose that the relationship between culture and institutions is more complex, with cultural factors and institutions mutually reinforcing each other over time. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. The central question is whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional quality and economic performance, or whether institutions themselves shape cultural outcomes. This question is crucial because it directly impacts how policymakers approach economic development, particularly in regions where cultural norms and institutional frameworks are deeply intertwined. The role of women and gender dynamics in economic growth highlights how cultural norms and gender relations significantly influence long-run economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). They point out that the colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant long-term consequences for gender production. This influence extends to the economic dependency models, where breadwinner wives continue to play a crucial role. Goldin provides a complementary perspective, arguing that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, such as the increased relative demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes in household production technology and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women altered female labor supply (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin (2006, p.8) also notes that expectations regarding future work, social norms concerning women's family and career, and factors accounting for women's life satisfaction began to change in the late 1960s and 1970s (Goldin 2006, p.8). This transformation in social norms and gender roles underscores the broader impact of cultural shifts on economic development.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice, particularly those fostering secure property rights, are crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The English financial revolution of the late seventeenth century, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth, as secure property rights incentivized investment and economic activity. The parallel development of markets for public and private debt further underscores the positive externalities generated by stable institutional frameworks (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutional changes have implications that go beyond economic growth. Institutions shape economic outcomes through both direct economic mechanisms and broader societal developments.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. These institutions are shaped by the mortality rates experienced by European colonizers, which in turn influenced the type of institutions established in the colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions persist over time and significantly impact current economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are profound, suggesting that the historical institutional changes introduced by colonialism have long-lasting effects on economic development.

The reversal of fortune thesis and the role of inequality in economic growth are discussed without explicitly linking these phenomena to cultural factors, as shared by Austin and Sokoloff and Engerman (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Austin critiques the simplicity of the reversal of fortune thesis, arguing that it overlooks the dynamic and endogenous nature of African responses to their environments. This perspective emphasizes that African societies actively engaged with their environments, utilizing available technologies to adapt and change their circumstances. Austin's argument suggests that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and how they interact with endogenous factors such as cultural adaptations. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea by showing that the New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional framework, shaped by the initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality, influenced the long-term development paths of these societies. The persistence of inequality in these societies highlights the importance of understanding the historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs. Austin (2008, p.25) notes that Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories, suggesting that the institutional legacies of colonialism are not the sole determinant of economic development. This dynamic view of institutional evolution challenges the notion that institutions are static and unchanging, instead emphasizing the role of endogenous factors in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

The new economic history of Africa focuses on institutional reforms as a means to eliminate poverty, but does not directly address the role of culture in long-run growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Hopkins (2009, p.8) posits that institutional variables, shaped by historical contexts extending into precolonial and even prehistorical periods, are crucial in explaining development. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who highlight the significance of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in influencing growth patterns. The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions is pivotal in promoting growth; high levels of congruence enhance government legitimacy and foster development-inducing institutions, while low levels create conditions favoring patrimonial politics, regionalism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This dynamic interplay between institutions and cultural congruence suggests that the effectiveness of institutional reforms in Africa may hinge on their alignment with pre-existing cultural and historical contexts. However, the extent to which these historical and cultural factors can be harnessed for growth remains an open question, as the new economic history of Africa has yet to fully explore the role of cultural elements in long-run economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society. (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered a climate of mistrust, which in turn hindered the development of strong, centralized states. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that this political instability resulting from the slave trade had lasting effects, weakening state institutions and fragmenting governance structures. This fragmentation impeded economic growth by creating conditions unfavorable to the establishment of effective, growth-promoting institutions. This reduced social capital, in turn, affects economic development by diminishing the cooperative behavior necessary for collective action and economic growth. This institutional degradation, characterized by weak state centralization and high levels of corruption, further compounds the challenges to economic development. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa thus creates a complex interplay of cultural and institutional factors that together shape the continent's economic trajectory.
