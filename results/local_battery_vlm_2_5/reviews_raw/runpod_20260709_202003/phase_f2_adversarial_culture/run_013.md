The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the ability to explain the divergent economic trajectories of nations, with implications for understanding the origins of prosperity and poverty. One side of the debate posits that cultural beliefs and values are crucial in shaping the institutions that govern economic activity, thus driving long-run growth. This perspective emphasizes the endogenous nature of institutions, suggesting that they emerge from the cultural fabric of societies. In this view, cultural factors such as trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that enhance economic efficiency and growth. The other side argues that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geographic conditions or historical events, and that cultural influences are secondary or even spurious. This perspective suggests that institutions are the result of rational responses to external constraints, rather than internal cultural dynamics. The disagreement centers on whether cultural beliefs and values are a fundamental cause of long-run growth or merely a secondary factor. The debate is pivotal for understanding the mechanisms underlying economic development and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. The question of whether cultural factors are a primary driver of institutional change and economic growth remains unresolved. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade shaped African cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which persist to this day and influence long-term outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This instability, in turn, has been linked to contemporary economic performance, with historical state development playing a crucial role (Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn further contends that the legacy of slavery includes modern corruption, suggesting that the slave trade's effects are not limited to political instability but extend to institutional development as well (Nunn 2008, p.1). While the transmission of malaria is mentioned as a potential historical factor, the evidence suggests that the indicators used to measure it have remained consistent over time, implying that the cultural and institutional impacts of the slave trade are more enduring than transient factors such as disease prevalence (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The historical context of the slave trade thus not only shaped immediate economic outcomes but also laid the groundwork for enduring cultural and institutional patterns that continue to affect African development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that the development of secure property rights is a fundamental driver of economic growth, as evidenced by the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and the evolution of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments played a critical role in fostering economic growth and political freedom, indicating that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal for long-term economic development. The financial revolution not only facilitated economic growth but also provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework ensured that economic actors could confidently invest in long-term ventures, knowing that their property rights were secure. The impact of these institutional changes varied across different sectors. Thus, while secure property rights are essential for economic growth, the benefits of institutional changes can be unevenly distributed, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800. This divergence was marked by significant differences in wages and economic development, with northwest Europe experiencing higher agricultural productivity and institutional changes that facilitated investment and economic growth. The institutional changes in northwest Europe, such as those that led to higher agricultural labour productivity, were not merely geographical but also driven by investment and institutional reforms (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). These reforms, in turn, led to higher productivity in agriculture and a shift of labour into industry and services. This divergence varied across Europe and Asia. This discrepancy suggests that while there were similarities in certain economic indicators, the overall economic development pathways differed significantly. The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 was also influenced by factors such as the Black Death, which initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These structural changes included declining interest rates, increased mobility, and labour-saving technological innovations. The impact of the Black Death on economic development varied across Europe. While it contributed to the rise of rural industries and greater institutional flexibility in some regions, it also led to significant demographic shifts and economic disruptions. These factors collectively highlight the complex interplay between economic development, institutional changes, and structural shifts in Europe and Asia before 1800.

The new economic history of Africa highlights the significance of institutional variables in explaining development, and this perspective is shared by Hopkins and Broadberry and Gardner (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa assumes that institutional change is gradual and calls for historical investigation because current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory. This historical lens is crucial for understanding how contemporary institutions, particularly those that foster economic growth, can be congruent with indigenous institutions. Broadberry and Gardner assert that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. The implication of this congruence is that high degrees of congruence underline the legitimacy of governments and enhance their ability to foster institutions that induce development. The role of culture in shaping these institutions and their effectiveness in promoting long-run growth remains a crucial area for further investigation.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Bryant, examine how institutions in New World societies, which evolved to advantage elites, contributed to the persistence of inequality through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Bryant 2006, p.35). These institutions, by restricting competition and concentrating political power, shaped development paths that were distinct from those of European societies. The differentials in paths of development across societies have long been of central concern to scholars, with explanations often focusing on institutions such as the security of property rights, prevalence of corruption, and investment in public infrastructure (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The cultural underpinnings of these institutions and their influence on long-run growth remain largely unexplored. Bryant argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, suggesting that the cultural norms and social relations that enabled industrial modes of production in Europe are crucial factors that cannot be overlooked (Bryant 2006, p.35). The implication is that the cultural context in which institutions operate is a critical factor in determining their effectiveness in promoting long-run growth, a theme that carries into the next section.

Technological and agricultural advancements, such as the Industrious Revolution and the introduction of barbed wire fencing, significantly influenced economic growth by enhancing productivity and enabling better management of resources (de Vries 1994, p.18; Hornbeck 2010, p.6). De Vries argues that the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, laid the groundwork for industrial growth by fostering a framework of regional economic development. This shift was not merely a prelude to industrialization but a distinct phenomenon that reshaped societal structures and consumption habits. Hornbeck shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains improved agricultural development by protecting property rights and mitigating externalities from livestock damage. This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, leading to increased agricultural productivity. The effectiveness of these technological advancements depended on the social norms and institutions that supported their implementation. Ellickson's analysis of a modern California county illustrates that social norms often play a crucial role in enforcing property rights and managing resources, even in the absence of strict legal responsibilities (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This suggests that the cultural context in which these technological advancements are introduced can significantly influence their impact on economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Goldin, illustrate that women's roles in economic growth and development have been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as in the U.S., during the 20th century (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). One mechanism linking women's roles to economic growth is the transmission of wealth and social status. In matrilineal societies, women's bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth, making them indispensable to the reproduction of communities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This dynamic underscores the economic value of women's roles in society, highlighting their contribution to economic growth. However, the transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. was not merely evolutionary; it was driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation suggests that cultural shifts and institutional changes can significantly alter women's economic roles, potentially leading to increased productivity and growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe. For McCloskey (2015, p.10), the "bourgeois virtues" of honesty and hard work were crucial to the Great Enrichment. This cultural shift fostered an environment where economic growth could flourish, as opposed to the institutional changes that were the focus of interest-only theories. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further elucidates this idea by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was a critical factor in sustaining economic development. This diffusion was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which ensured that useful knowledge spread rapidly across borders. This tacit knowledge was transmitted through personal training and apprenticeships, underscoring the importance of social and cultural networks in the dissemination of economic ideas. The role of tacit knowledge in economic growth suggests that cultural factors, such as the willingness to share and learn from one another, were essential for the sustained economic development observed in Northwestern Europe. This cultural environment, characterized by the free exchange of ideas and the value placed on useful knowledge, laid the groundwork for the Industrial Revolution and the subsequent economic growth that transformed the region. The shared finding that ideas and ideologies drove economic change suggests that cultural beliefs and social norms can significantly influence economic development (McCloskey 2015, p.9; Mokyr 2005, p.14).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional and geographic explanations for economic development, arguing against simplistic views of causation and emphasizing dynamic, endogenous processes (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). They propose that economic development is better understood through the lens of distributional conflicts and the interdependencies of institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the African story supports a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic perspective is echoed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who contends that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights the importance of endogenous factors in shaping economic institutions. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further qualifies that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework must be considered, as ignoring this can lead to failure in understanding economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Thus, the dynamic and endogenous processes that shape economic institutions are complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced approach that considers both distributional conflicts and the interdependencies of institutions.

The complexities of economic growth and environmental impacts are influenced by technological advances and shifts in industrial output, as well as by social changes that alter pollution intensities (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Stern 2004, p.3). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) further notes that the erratic indicators of productivity levels reflect the gaps between existing and best-practice technology, and that social competence for exploiting advanced methods is still limited, particularly in the earlier years and in the more recent latecomers. This dynamic is crucial because it underscores how the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This implies that the relationship between economic growth and environmental impacts is not straightforward, and that simple convergence hypotheses may not adequately capture the complexities involved. The implication of the EKC's potential demise is that economic growth does not necessarily lead to environmental improvement, and that environmental policies must be carefully designed to mitigate the negative impacts of industrial shifts and technological advances.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that colonial institutions are a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, rather than cultural factors, are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions, despite their extractive nature, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further show that these institutional legacies played a pivotal role in the economic growth and industrialization of formerly poor colonies, contributing significantly to current income differences. The implications of this perspective are profound: it suggests that the economic trajectories of former colonies are deeply rooted in the institutions imposed during colonial rule, rather than being solely determined by cultural factors. As such, efforts to foster economic development in these regions must account for the institutional frameworks inherited from colonial times.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency by altering the structure and equilibrium of collective action games (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while the Protestant Reformation's disappearance of these beliefs led to guild decline. This shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can shape economic institutions and efficiency. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by cultural and historical factors, have long-lasting effects on economic performance.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system, interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. Austin supports this view, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity underscores the need to consider the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional evolution.

Institutions matter in economic growth, as shown by North's argument that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, holds only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further illustrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and economic growth. This historical evidence shows that institutions play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

The role of women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods is significant. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impacts on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production. The historical evidence from Akan state formation in the 17th century shows that women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities, with their bloodlines defining the transmission of both office and wealth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions, shaped by cultural and historical factors, play a crucial role in long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural beliefs directly influence economic institutions and efficiency, or if the impact is mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions. The historical evidence from the European family system and the Akan state formation in the 17th century shows that cultural beliefs and gender roles significantly influence economic institutions and growth. This evidence settles the disagreement by illustrating the direct and indirect ways in which culture shapes institutions and economic performance.
