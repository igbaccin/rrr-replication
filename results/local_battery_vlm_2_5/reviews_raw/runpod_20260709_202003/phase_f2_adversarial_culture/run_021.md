The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve and why some achieve sustained economic growth while others stagnate. The live disagreement centers on whether cultural factors, such as trust and social norms, or institutional frameworks, such as property rights and governance structures, are the primary drivers of economic development. Some scholars argue that cultural factors, shaped by historical events, persist over time and significantly influence economic outcomes. Others contend that institutions, by providing the rules and incentives that govern economic activity, are the key determinants of growth. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs whether interventions should focus on changing cultural attitudes or reforming institutional structures. Institutions shape the economic environment, but culture can influence how these institutions are perceived and utilized. Understanding the interplay between culture and institutions is essential for designing effective policies that promote long-term economic growth. The debate over the role of culture in dominating institutions highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical and cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.” North (1989, p.1) reports, “Coase pointed out that the neoclassical model, which has served as the basis of economic reasoning for most scholars in the Western world, holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs; but that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter.”

The pervasive culture of mistrust, rooted in the slave trade, shaped institutional frameworks and influenced social dynamics, thereby impacting economic development in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists today, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is a deeply ingrained social dynamic that influences contemporary economic outcomes. The historical accounts of political instability align with recent empirical research, which finds that a country's history of state development is a key determinant of current economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's legacy of mistrust and political instability thus creates a vicious cycle where underdeveloped institutions hinder economic growth, perpetuating the cycle of underdevelopment.

The historical parallels between Italy's city-states and Africa's experience with mistrust underscore the broader significance of institutional governance in fostering economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast's analysis of the English financial revolution highlights the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as crucial factors that dramatically reduced transaction costs (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional evolution reflects an increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. North elaborates on this by noting that the gains from trade, specialization, and division of labor are key underlying features of modern economies, emphasizing the role of institutions in facilitating these processes (North 1989, p.1). The evolution of these institutions thus stands as a significant factor in the long-run growth of England, illustrating the enduring impact of institutional development on economic performance.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe. The role of intellectual shifts is exemplified by the rise of useful knowledge as a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective is supported by the diffusion of knowledge across Europe, facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. The spread of ideas and knowledge fostered a common intellectual movement across the West. The growth of tacit knowledge, which includes skills and other forms of implicit knowledge, also played a crucial role. This tacit knowledge was often transmitted through personal interactions and training, highlighting the importance of social networks and educational systems in disseminating useful knowledge. Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that the payoff to efforts to codify knowledge was a function of the technology and costs of codification. The diffusion of knowledge thus depended on both written records and personal transmission, underscoring the multifaceted nature of knowledge dissemination in driving economic growth. This multifaceted transmission of knowledge, both codified and tacit, highlights the importance of cultural factors in facilitating the spread of ideas and knowledge, which in turn drove economic growth.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800, and the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century, highlight the complex interplay of cultural factors and regional variations in economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This pattern was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly, underscoring the complex interplay of factors driving economic development in these regions. The historical national accounting insights provided by Broadberry (2021) reveal that African economic history has experienced a renaissance, driven by newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques. This renaissance has offered new perspectives on the patterns of growth and development in Africa, rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). The economic performance of these regions was determined by a multitude of influences, including institutional changes, investment patterns, and market dynamics.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) both highlight that cultural and gender-based constructs can shape economic development by influencing institutional frameworks. These authors argue that the economic development of societies is fundamentally shaped by the institutions that emerge from their unique historical and social contexts. In the case of African societies, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) demonstrate that women's roles in pre-colonial and colonial periods were indispensable to community reproduction, particularly in matrilineal societies like Asante. The sexual division of labor established during these periods had long-term consequences for gender production and economic development. These gendered dynamics are not isolated to Africa. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions often advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The persistence of these inequalities suggests that the institutional frameworks that emerge from cultural and historical contexts can have profound and lasting effects on economic development. The role of gender in shaping these institutions is a critical factor that must be considered in any comprehensive analysis of long-run growth.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates was a pivotal factor in shaping current economic performance. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, creating a persistent institutional framework that hindered economic development. This institutional bias was deeply rooted in the political and economic strategies of the colonial powers. The impact of these institutions is evident in the significant correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as the relationship between early institutions and those that persist today. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism affected the economic performance of these colonies and shaped their long-term development trajectories. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to benefit the colonizers, created a legacy of underdevelopment that continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, highlights the enduring effects of historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth.

The role of culture in shaping institutions is profound, as institutions themselves are the product of cultural evolution. North argues that institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and these rules are shaped by cultural beliefs and norms (North 1989, p.1). This perspective is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, influencing the development of institutions and economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The long-term effects of these cultural norms are evident in the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth.

The extractive nature of colonial institutions, designed to benefit the colonizers, created a legacy of underdevelopment that continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies significantly affected the types of institutions established, which in turn shaped long-term development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, highlights the enduring effects of historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth. North and Weingast further illustrate how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The role of culture in this process is evident in how societal norms and beliefs influenced the development of these institutions.

The persistent impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is further underscored by the work of Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective is complemented by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The enduring influence of these historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth is evident in the legacy of underdevelopment that continues to affect former colonies today.
