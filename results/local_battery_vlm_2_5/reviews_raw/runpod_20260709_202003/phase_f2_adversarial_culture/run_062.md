The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is whether cultural factors, such as beliefs, values, and norms, can shape institutions in ways that either promote or hinder economic growth over the long term. The live disagreement centers around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development or if other factors, such as economic efficiency, geographical determinism, or historical legacies, are more decisive. One position emphasizes the direct impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, arguing that cultural values can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Another position focuses on the role of economic efficiency in shaping institutions, suggesting that institutions are designed to maximize economic outcomes rather than being driven by cultural factors. A third position highlights the importance of historical legacies, such as colonial institutions and the slave trade, in shaping current economic outcomes. The interplay between these factors is complex, and understanding their relative importance is crucial for developing effective policies to promote economic growth. The cultural values of societies can influence the development of institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. McCloskey and Mokyr (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56) agree that the growth of useful knowledge and ideas were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey argues that the economic change was driven by a new rhetoric, which made new circumstances, and these circumstances then again made new rhetoric. This rhetoric was a cultural struggle that shaped the economic growth. Mokyr builds on this argument by highlighting that the diffusion of knowledge was not confined to national boundaries but was readily shared within the Western world. This diffusion was facilitated through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which underscored the importance of a shared intellectual movement. The idea that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth is further supported by Mokyr's observation that much of the knowledge was tacit and embodied in skills, which moved about through personal transmission and training. This tacit knowledge, along with codified knowledge, played a crucial role in economic growth. However, Mokyr also notes that the codification of knowledge was a function of technology and costs, and the payoff to efforts to do so varied. This qualification suggests that while the growth of useful knowledge was a significant driver of economic growth, the mechanisms through which this knowledge was diffused and utilized were complex and multifaceted. The interplay between tacit and codified knowledge, and the conditions under which they were shared, highlights the nuanced role of ideas and ideologies in driving economic change.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 1690s marked a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was not merely a response to economic forces but a deliberate political choice that transformed England's economic trajectory. It was the institutions of public choice that enabled the English to overcome their fiscal crises and build a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework provided a large and positive externality, supporting the growth of a stable market for private debt. The Bank of England, initially formed to intermediate public debt, quickly expanded into private operations, underscoring the interconnectedness of public and private financial systems. He argues that with positive transaction costs, institutions matter significantly, as they facilitate the gains from trade that are key to the wealth of nations. This perspective contrasts with views that attribute economic development primarily to cultural factors, emphasizing instead the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental to long-run economic development. The colonial experience led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, which have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This relationship is supported by the strong first-stage correlation between settler mortality and current institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). One mechanism through which these institutions operate is by maximizing the rents to European colonists, rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, the persistence of these extractive institutions is not solely due to economic factors; political power dynamics also play a crucial role. Powerful groups may resist institutional change if they fear losing their political influence or if they do not directly benefit from the economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This political dimension underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run development, suggesting that the role of culture in dominating institutions is not straightforward but is intertwined with political and economic factors.

Institutional frameworks in New World societies, characterized by greater inequality, restricted competition and perpetuated disparities (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these unequal institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics at play, where elites resisted changes that might threaten their privileged positions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on the interconnectedness of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that the interdependency of various institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on how these institutions interact with one another. This interdependency underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run development, highlighting that the role of culture in dominating institutions is not straightforward but is intertwined with political and economic factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Economic growth dynamics are complex, driven by both exogenous factors and the interplay of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the process of catching up in economic growth is intricate, influenced by social competence and institutional changes that affect productivity levels. This social competence for exploiting advanced methods is limited, particularly in earlier years and in more recent latecomers. This productivity did not result in an abundance of grain and low food prices, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. The implication is that cultural factors, such as social competence and institutional changes, play a significant role in shaping economic growth dynamics. This suggests that while cultural factors are important, their influence may wane over time as other factors come into play.

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects economic development, offering an alternative explanation to cultural factors alone (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is likely rooted in the historical experience of exploitation and betrayal, where communities were often divided and pitted against each other during the slave trade era. This instability led to weakened and fragmented states, which are less capable of providing the necessary infrastructure and institutions for economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, is not just cultural but also institutional, affecting the very fabric of African societies. The implications of mistrust and political instability are far-reaching, affecting not only economic development but also social cohesion and governance.

Culture fundamentally shapes institutions by influencing their formation and persistence, thereby driving long-run growth. This is evident in the legacy of the slave trade, which not only left cultural marks but also institutional ones, affecting the very fabric of African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The impact of culture on institutions is further illustrated by the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions. For example, serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by these conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, in turn, influenced economic growth by facilitating or hindering coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework is crucial, as seen in the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, both of which contributed to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The remaining disagreement is whether cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, explain economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
