The fundamental cause of long-run growth has long been a contentious issue in economic literature, with scholars debating the role of culture in shaping institutions that drive economic development. One position argues that culture, through its influence on institutions, is a critical factor in long-run growth. Institutions, in this view, are structural arrangements deeply rooted in cultural values and norms that guide economic behaviour and political decision-making. This perspective emphasizes the interdependency of cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks, suggesting that cultural factors can either facilitate or hinder economic growth by shaping the efficiency and effectiveness of institutions. Another position contends that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and mortality rates, which determine the type of institutions established in a region. This view posits that the persistence of extractive institutions in certain areas can be attributed to historical factors that are independent of cultural influences. The tension between these positions lies in the extent to which culture, through its impact on institutions, can be considered a fundamental cause of long-run growth. This debate is significant because it addresses the underlying mechanisms that drive economic development, offering insights into how policies and interventions can be designed to foster sustainable growth. The role of culture in institutions, therefore, is a pivotal question that intersects with broader discussions on economic development and institutional design. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They show that the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional change was pivotal in enabling long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution thus created a stable environment for economic activities, fostering both political freedom and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in this regard, as they reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The implications of these institutional changes are profound, as they highlight the necessity of institutions in making possible economic growth and political freedom.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was pivotal in fostering economic growth. This perspective highlights the role of cultural shifts in shaping economic outcomes. McCloskey notes that the cultural struggle in France, for instance, was a significant factor in economic growth, with the motto "Pour la Patrie, les Sciences et la Gloire" reflecting a broader cultural ethos that influenced economic development (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Mokyr further elaborates that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was crucial, as it transcended national boundaries and was facilitated through various means such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences (Mokyr 2005, p.56). This diffusion involved codified knowledge and tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that the spread of ideas and knowledge, both explicit and tacit, was a fundamental driver of economic growth, challenging the notion that institutional changes alone were sufficient.

Acemoglu et al. assert that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis holds that European colonialism introduced institutions that fundamentally shaped long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, were not conducive to economic development. The extractive nature of these institutions was evident in their design to benefit European colonists directly, and to foster long-term economic gains for the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, introduced by colonial powers, created a lasting impact on the economic trajectory of these regions, highlighting the deep-seated influence of colonial institutions on economic performance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, and cultural factors. These distributional conflicts led to the development of institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which were designed to benefit elites and maintain their power. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had a lasting impact on economic performance. Economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were designed to benefit elites, and to foster long-term economic gains for the broader population. This framework was interdependent with other institutions, such as labor markets and credit markets, and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The interdependency of institutions highlights the complexity of economic development and the need to study entire institutional frameworks, not individual institutions in isolation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this perspective to the New World, arguing that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies.

The literature on economic growth and technological advance highlights various factors influencing long-term economic performance, including institutional changes and social dynamics (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who observe that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, tied closely to foreign trade and the market for exports. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth extends beyond formal structures to include broader social and political dynamics. This is not to say that economic growth is purely a function of institutional changes and social dynamics. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, and social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited, particularly in the earlier years and in the more recent latecomers. This suggests that while institutional changes and social dynamics are crucial, technological factors and social competence also play a significant role in economic growth. The interplay between these factors underscores the need for a holistic approach to understanding economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade has had a profound and lasting impact on African economic development, with culture being a crucial mediating variable. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, and this mistrust significantly impedes economic growth. Trust is a critical factor in economic development, as it facilitates cooperation and reduces transaction costs. The historical trauma of the slave trade has left deep-seated cultural attitudes that hinder the formation of social capital and collective action, both of which are essential for sustained economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability further exacerbated the economic underdevelopment by creating an environment of uncertainty and lack of effective governance. The legacy of the slave trade encompasses economic factors, social and political dimensions, all of which are interconnected and reinforce each other. Nunn (2008, p.1) also highlights the enduring impact of slavery on institutional development, suggesting that the precolonial origins of modern corruption can be traced back to the slave trades. The persistence of these institutional weaknesses, rooted in the historical experience of slavery, continues to hamper Africa's economic development.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex, with cultural beliefs and values influencing institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. Ogilvie highlights the significance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions, noting that exogenous cultural theories explain how non-economic beliefs can affect institutional structures and economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, are more likely to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial, as demonstrated by the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, both of which contributed to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional evolution, emphasizing that African responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately shaped economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is further supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights and facilitate economic transactions (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, illustrating how financial innovations like the Bank of England provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). In the context of Africa, Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural mistrust, rooted in historical experiences, continues to influence institutional development and economic outcomes in Africa. The legacy of the slave trade, encompassing economic, social, and political dimensions, reinforces institutional weaknesses that hinder Africa's economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the enduring impact of slavery on institutional development, which can be traced back to the precolonial origins of modern corruption (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
