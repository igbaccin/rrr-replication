The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature. At stake is the fundamental question of what underpins economic development over the long term. One position holds that culture, through its influence on institutions, is a primary driver of economic growth. This view posits that cultural beliefs and values shape the institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder economic performance. The opposing position argues that institutions are shaped by other factors, such as geographical conditions or historical events, and that culture plays a secondary or even negligible role. These competing views highlight the complexity of understanding the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. The debate is crucial for comprehending the enduring economic disparities observed across different regions and historical periods. The resolution of this debate could provide insights into the mechanisms that sustain economic growth and inform policies aimed at fostering development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were critical in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, with North (1989, p.1) emphasizing the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs. This institutional evolution was pivotal in England's long-run economic success, as it facilitated a stable market for public debt and provided a positive externality for private debt markets. The financial revolution in England thus played a necessary role in economic growth and political freedom, as it ensured that property rights were secure over time. The institutional framework established during this period was not merely a byproduct of other forces but a fundamental driver of economic development. North notes that the neoclassical model's assumption of zero transaction costs overlooks the critical role of institutions, which are essential for economic growth in a world with positive transaction costs. Other factors, such as technological change and resource allocation, also play a role in driving economic development.

Ideas and ideologies, not institutions, were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). The French struggle during this period showcases the cultural dimensions of this phenomenon, where economic growth was stifled by conservative ideologies until new rhetoric emerged, fostering a virtuous cycle of new circumstances and rhetoric. This process was evident in countries like the Netherlands and France, where the interplay between rhetoric and circumstances led to economic enrichment. Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. The diffusion of knowledge across Europe, facilitated by periodicals, translations, and personal communications, underscores the role of ideas in driving economic development. Mokyr's emphasis on the diffusion of tacit knowledge, which moved about through personal transmission and training, highlights the importance of social networks and cultural exchange in the spread of useful knowledge.

In contrast, the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, rather than cultural explanations. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-term economic performance. Specifically, the mortality rates faced by European colonizers determined the institutional framework: high mortality led to extractive institutions, which persist today and have a profound impact on current economic outcomes. This mechanism is supported by the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as the significant impact of these institutions on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, even when they are extractive and do not encourage economic development, is driven by the political and economic interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on economic development, which can be traced back to the initial conditions set by European colonizers. The impact of these institutions is not merely historical but has tangible effects on contemporary economic performance.

This perspective is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions and perpetuating inequality, offering an alternative explanation to cultural determinants of long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share this posture. Distributional conflicts, rooted in the unequal distribution of power and resources, drive the formation of institutions that favor elites and maintain existing power structures. This is evident in the persistence of institutions like serfdom and craft guilds in pre-industrial Europe. These institutions, rather than being efficient, were shaped by the conflicts between different social groups vying for control over resources and power. For example, serfdom in Europe was maintained not because it was efficient but because it served the interests of the nobility who controlled the land. The same dynamic is seen in the New World societies, where institutions were shaped by the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality led to the formation of institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. However, the impact of these institutions on long-run growth is qualified by the interplay with other factors, such as the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This underscores the complexity of the relationship between distributional conflicts, institutions, and long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa engendered a pervasive culture of mistrust, which continues to impede economic growth. This cultural legacy is a result of the long-term political instability and weakened state institutions caused by the slave trades. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades led to fragmented states, which in turn hindered economic development. This is consistent with the broader finding that state centralization is a crucial determinant of economic performance. The relationship between slave exports and economic development is complex and multifaceted. While the slave trades had a direct adverse effect on economic development, they also influenced cultural attitudes, such as trust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic outcomes by reducing social capital and impeding cooperation. This cultural legacy of mistrust, therefore, complements the institutional legacy of weakened states in shaping Africa's long-run growth. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Austin (2008, p.2) highlight that the complexity in economic growth processes involves a dynamic interplay of technological advances and social changes. Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the intricate dynamics of technological and social changes, challenging simple convergence hypotheses. This complexity is evident in the varied responses of countries to their environments and the adoption of institutions that foster economic growth. Austin echoes this by noting that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' responses to their environments, utilizing various technologies, ultimately changed the course of their economic development. This dynamic interplay highlights how cultural attitudes and institutional responses are intertwined, shaping long-run growth trajectories. This multifaceted nature of growth processes does not directly address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Instead, it provides a rich context in which cultural factors can be seen to interact with institutional frameworks in complex ways, influencing economic outcomes over the long term.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn influence long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). The English financial revolution, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, both critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores how cultural attitudes toward institutions can drive economic development.

Cultural beliefs and values also influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that exogenous cultural theories can explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of 'social capital' further illustrates how cultural beliefs about trust, norms, and networks can improve societal efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, explain the superior economic performance of certain regions over others. For example, the 'civic' cultures of northern Italy, characterized by high social capital, encouraged institutions that favored economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions highlights how cultural attitudes can shape economic outcomes over the long term.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is further illustrated by the impact of the slave trade on African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This historical trauma highlights how cultural attitudes can be shaped by external forces and how these attitudes, in turn, can dominate institutions and influence long-run economic growth. The measurement of trust levels across different regions provides empirical evidence of the enduring impact of historical cultural trauma on contemporary economic development.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that cultural attitudes and institutions are intertwined and influence long-run economic growth trajectories (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which exogenous cultural theories can fully explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's analysis of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis provides the specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement by showing that Africans' own responses to their environments, utilizing various technologies, ultimately changed the course of their economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between cultural attitudes and institutional responses highlights how cultural factors can interact with institutional frameworks in complex ways, influencing economic outcomes over the long term.
