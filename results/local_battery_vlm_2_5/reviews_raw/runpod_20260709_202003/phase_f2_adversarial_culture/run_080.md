The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue, with significant implications for understanding the dynamics of economic development. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement: does culture shape institutions, which in turn drive economic growth, or are there other, more primary factors at play? Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby impacting economic growth. Others contend that institutions are shaped by a variety of factors, including geographical and historical contingencies, and that culture plays a more limited role. Additionally, there is a competing perspective that emphasizes the role of extractive institutions, which are established in response to specific historical conditions and persist over time. The question of how culture interacts with institutions to influence long-run growth is thus a critical one, with far-reaching implications for policy and theory. The debate hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or a secondary factor influenced by other determinants. Understanding this relationship is essential for developing effective strategies to promote economic growth and development. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Ideas and ideologies, as primary drivers of economic change, are specific manifestations of culture that shape long-run growth (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey contends that the Great Enrichment was driven by a shift in rhetoric that valued innovation and entrepreneurship, which in turn created new circumstances that fostered economic growth. Mokyr adds that the growth of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was instrumental in this process. Mokyr's analysis shows that the diffusion of knowledge across Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, and international conferences, which highlights the role of cultural exchange in spreading useful knowledge. This cultural exchange enabled the dissemination of ideas and ideologies that drove economic growth. Mokyr's discussion of tacit knowledge further underscores the importance of cultural transmission, as much of the useful knowledge was embedded in skills and practices that were passed down through personal training and apprenticeships. However, Mokyr also notes that the codification of knowledge was a function of technology and costs, suggesting that the economic payoff to efforts to codify knowledge was a crucial factor in the diffusion of useful knowledge. This implies that the economic incentives to share and utilize knowledge were shaped by the broader cultural context, which included not only ideas and ideologies but also the economic and technological conditions that facilitated the spread of knowledge.

The literature on complexity in economic growth processes and social change highlights the multifaceted nature of growth dynamics, influenced by technological advances and social changes, without explicitly linking these to cultural factors as the primary driver of long-run growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Goldin 2006, p.8). Goldin (2006, p.8) further illustrates this point by examining how shifts in social norms and technological changes in household production altered female labor supply and occupational patterns (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes were not merely economic but also deeply cultural, involving shifts in expectations and norms regarding women's roles in the workforce. The complexity of these interactions means that the relationship between cultural factors and growth is intricate. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) acknowledges that while institutional changes can limit growth, they can also facilitate it by enhancing social competence for exploiting advanced technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This highlights the dual role of cultural factors, which can both enable and constrain economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective shifts the focus from cultural factors to the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional changes in England, particularly the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution in England not only provided a stable market for public debt but also created a positive externality for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework ensured that economic growth was more decisive and rapid, underscoring the necessity of institutions in fostering economic development. The impact of these institutions on economic growth varies across different contexts. North and Weingast acknowledge that while institutions played a necessary role, other forces could have led to success, albeit more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, suggesting that the role of institutions in economic growth is contingent on specific historical and political conditions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World. The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds, were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply influenced by power struggles between different social groups. In pre-industrial Europe, these institutions were shaped by conflicts over the distribution of resources and power, with elites often using their influence to maintain or enhance their positions. This dynamic is exemplified by the persistence of inequality in New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The role of culture in this context is a backdrop against which distributional conflicts played out. Ogilvie's critique of cultural explanations highlights that the persistence of certain institutions can be better understood through the lens of power and conflict. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies was a direct result of institutional frameworks that favored elites, thereby shaping economic development paths. This focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the importance of cultural factors entirely. Ogilvie acknowledges that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions, but only as part of a broader framework where power dynamics are the primary driver. This nuanced view suggests that while cultural factors may play a role, they are often secondary to the more fundamental forces of distributional conflict and institutional power.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers were pivotal in shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. Their work shows that European mortality rates in colonies influenced the types of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that prioritized the interests of the colonizers over the economic development of the colonies. This perspective is further supported by the finding that institutions designed to maximize colonial rents rather than long-run growth were prevalent in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of economic outcomes in former colonies. This suggests that the role of culture in economic development is secondary to the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule. The colonial legacy of extractive institutions continues to shape economic performance in many regions today.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust that persists and significantly impacts trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This culture of mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has had profound effects on social capital and economic development. The slave trade not only resulted in the loss of human capital but also created political instability, which weakened state structures (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability, in turn, hindered the development of strong institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. The historical trauma of the slave trade has left a lasting impact on African societies, affecting their ability to build trust and develop strong institutions. This legacy of mistrust and institutional weakness continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa today.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework. Ogilvie highlights that the European family system, for instance, was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, all contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered. North and Weingast further emphasize the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a crucial role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. The financial revolution in England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9).

The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The disagreement lies in the specific role of culture in shaping these institutions. While Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of trust levels in societies with different historical experiences of the slave trade. This empirical comparison shows that the legacy of mistrust, as a result of the slave trade, has a direct and measurable impact on the development of strong institutions and, consequently, on economic growth.
