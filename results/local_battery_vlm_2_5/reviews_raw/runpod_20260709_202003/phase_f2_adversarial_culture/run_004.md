The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and historical scholarship. At stake is the understanding of the deep determinants of economic development, and whether cultural factors, operating through institutions, are a primary driver of economic growth. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run growth. They contend that cultural factors such as trust, norms, and social networks can facilitate coordinated actions and foster institutions that promote economic growth. Some scholars argue that extractive institutions, influenced by factors such as mortality rates and colonial legacies, are key drivers of economic performance. These extractive institutions, they argue, persist over time and significantly affect current income per capita. The live disagreement centers around whether cultural factors, through their influence on institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run growth or if other factors, such as extractive institutions, play a more decisive role. The debate is further complicated by the interplay between cultural beliefs and the institutional frameworks that govern economic activities.

The relationship between culture and institutions is complex, with cultural beliefs and values potentially influencing the structure and efficiency of economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that cultural theories, particularly those emphasizing distributional conflicts, provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, and the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline. This perspective highlights how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. Similarly, Austin notes that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe. This suggests that cultural factors, operating through institutions, have a significant impact on long-run economic growth. Austin also rejects the idea that unchanging environmental obstacles are the primary determinants of economic development, emphasizing instead the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of economic growth. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks underscores the importance of understanding how cultural factors influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They identify the financial revolution and the creation of the Bank of England as pivotal events that dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional development fostered a stable market for public debt, which in turn provided a positive externality for the growth of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). He argues that the gains from trade, specialization, and division of labor are key underlying features of modern economies, and that these gains are facilitated by institutions that reduce transaction costs. The financial revolution, including the creation of the Bank of England, was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, reflecting the increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). The institutional changes in England were driven by both economic pressures and political negotiations and compromises. This indicates that the path to economic growth is complex, shaped by interactions between economic, political, and institutional factors.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the initial conditions set by European settlers in colonies, which were shaped by their cultural and institutional frameworks, had a profound impact on the long-run growth trajectories of these regions. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies with high mortality led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persist today and affect economic performance. This correlation supports the idea that the initial conditions set by European settlers significantly shaped the institutions in these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) reinforce this by demonstrating a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of these historical factors on economic outcomes. This finding is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutions played a crucial role in the process of economic growth and industrialization among former colonies. Austin (2008, p.25) notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments also played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This nuanced perspective underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including endogenous responses, in understanding long-run growth.

The role of women in economic development intersects with cultural and institutional factors, offering a nuanced perspective on long-run growth dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that women have been instrumental in economic growth and development, both historically and contemporarily. African women's contributions have been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) note that women's bloodlines in matrilineal societies, such as Asante, defined the transmission of both office and wealth, underscoring their indispensable role in community reproduction. This cultural dimension, where women's roles are deeply embedded in societal structures, highlights how gender dynamics can influence economic outcomes. Goldin (2006, p.3) complements this view by showing how shifts in household production technology and institutional changes, such as the availability of part-time work and the removal of barriers to married women's employment, altered female labor supply in the U.S. Goldin (2006, p.8) notes that the turning point for women's earnings relative to men occurred around 1980, suggesting that while women's economic contributions are significant, the path to parity is complex and influenced by multiple factors. This highlights how cultural and institutional factors can both facilitate and constrain women's economic roles, thereby influencing long-run growth.

Distributional conflicts and inequality are examined as key factors shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies, highlighting how these conflicts can lead to persistent inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions often reflected the interests of powerful elites, who used their influence to maintain control and preserve their advantages. This dynamic is evident in the institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies, where elites used their power to shape policies that maintained their dominance. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. The European family system, for instance, was interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, all of which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. This interdependency underscores the complexity of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective acknowledges that cultural beliefs and values can also influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. Richardson, for example, contends that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efficient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline. This shows that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by changing the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game. The interplay between distributional conflicts, inequality, and cultural beliefs thus shapes the institutional landscape in ways that can either facilitate or hinder long-run growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.24) shows that the Black Death was a pivotal event that triggered long-term structural changes in Europe. He and Broadberry and Gupta (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15)  argue that these changes led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death initiated a series of economic transformations, including declining interest rates, increased labour mobility, and institutional changes in guilds. These changes fostered the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations. The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with parts of Asia experiencing similar economic conditions to those in Europe. These findings highlight the importance of historical events in shaping long-term economic trajectories, but do not directly address cultural factors as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.

The slave trade played a pivotal role in shaping African cultural attitudes, particularly in fostering mistrust, which has had enduring economic and social repercussions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) show that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust in Africa. This mistrust is a dynamic outcome of historical events that continue to influence contemporary economic development. The slave trade, by causing long-term political instability, weakened state centralization and fragmented political structures (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political fragmentation, in turn, impeded the development of strong, centralized institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. The historical impact of the slave trade on political instability and state fragmentation has been shown to correlate with current economic performance. The history of state development, as measured by the depth of experience with state-level institutions, is positively correlated with real per capita GDP (Nunn 2008, p.27). This finding underscores the importance of historical events in shaping long-term economic trajectories, highlighting how cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, can be a lasting legacy of historical events.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, can persist as a legacy of historical events. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency. For instance, cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating how non-economic cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate the gains from trade and technological change (North 1989, p.1). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, such as the creation of the Bank of England, was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Acemoglu et al. show that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional change. The historical impact of the slave trade on political instability and state fragmentation, as measured by the depth of experience with state-level institutions, is positively correlated with real per capita GDP (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding highlights how cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, can be a lasting legacy of historical events. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in securing property rights and fostering economic growth, as well as the impact of European mortality rates on institutional development in colonies, settles the disagreement by showing that cultural attitudes and historical events significantly influence long-term economic trajectories.
