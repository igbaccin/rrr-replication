The role of culture in shaping institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic and institutional theory. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically, and whether cultural factors can override or reinforce institutional frameworks. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby impacting long-run growth. Others contend that institutional frameworks, shaped by factors such as secure property rights and effective governance, are the primary drivers of economic development, with culture playing a secondary role. A third perspective posits that the interaction between culture and institutions is more complex, involving a dynamic interplay where each influences the other in a feedback loop. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors can independently drive institutional change and economic growth, or if they are merely epiphenomena of deeper institutional dynamics. This literature review explores these competing views, focusing on how culture interacts with institutions to shape long-run growth trajectories. One stream of research emphasizes the direct impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, illustrating how these beliefs can structure collective action and influence economic efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The development of institutions that ensured secure property rights significantly reduced transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. He argues that institutions matter because they address positive transaction costs, which are absent in the neoclassical framework. This institutional framework was pivotal in fostering a stable market for both public and private debt, which provided a large and positive externality for economic growth. This institutional framework was crucial for the long-term capital market and economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further underscore that the financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, implying that even if other forces would have ultimately led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. However, North also notes that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, suggesting that the institutional changes were not solely driven by economic factors but also by political necessities. The development of institutions that ensured secure property rights and reduced transaction costs was a necessary condition for economic growth in seventeenth-century England.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) contend that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices have long-lasting economic impacts. They propose that high mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn affected long-term economic performance. This claim is supported by a high correlation between mortality rates faced by Europeans in the colonies and the subsequent establishment of European settlements, early measures of institutions, and current institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) show that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to maximize long-run growth. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism underscores the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic development, as these extractive institutions persist and continue to affect economic outcomes long after the colonial period. The persistence of these institutions suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have a lasting impact on the economic trajectory of former colonies, shaping their institutional frameworks and economic performance in profound ways.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) share a view that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping core economic institutions, a perspective that challenges efficiency-based explanations. The institutions that emerged from these conflicts often restricted competition and maintained inequality, as seen in the policies that favored elites in societies with greater inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). For instance, in pre-industrial Europe, the guilds were not primarily about efficiency but about controlling access to resources and markets, thereby maintaining the status quo for those in power. This dynamic is also evident in the New World, where institutions were designed to preserve the wealth and power of the elite classes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that the study of institutions requires an understanding of the entire institutional framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency implies that the persistence of inequality and the maintenance of power structures are deeply embedded within the broader institutional landscape.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the divergence in economic development between Europe and Asia was already significant by 1800, a perspective shared by Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This early modern divergence was driven by factors such as the Black Death and subsequent institutional changes, which facilitated economic growth in Europe. The Black Death led to labor scarcity and high wages, which in turn drove technological innovations and increased mobility (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Pamuk further elaborates that the Black Death contributed to the rise of rural industries and greater institutional flexibility, particularly within guilds (Pamuk 2007, p.24). This institutional adaptability was crucial for the long-term economic development of northwestern Europe, setting it apart from other regions. The role of culture in shaping these institutions and driving long-run growth remains an open question. Pamuk posits that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450, highlighting the importance of early institutional responses to exogenous shocks (Pamuk 2007, p.5). These institutional changes were pivotal in shaping the economic landscape of Europe, but the cultural underpinnings of these institutions require further investigation to fully understand their role in long-run growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a key driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.56); (McCloskey 2015, p.10) highlights. This perspective is supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9), who emphasizes the role of ideas and ideologies in driving economic change. McCloskey (2015, p.9) illustrates this point with the example of France, where the cultural struggle and the adoption of new rhetoric led to economic growth. This process was a complex interplay of ideas and cultural factors. Mokyr (2005, p.14) further elaborates on the importance of tacit knowledge, which was often transmitted through personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge was a crucial component of the useful knowledge that drove economic growth. However, McCloskey (2015, p.10) notes that the diffusion of knowledge was not always straightforward, as it could be influenced by cultural and institutional factors. The growth of useful knowledge is deeply intertwined with cultural and institutional dynamics.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists and affects contemporary economic outcomes by reducing trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust manifests in various forms, including reduced levels of social capital and lower levels of interpersonal trust, which are crucial for economic development. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust creates a barrier to the formation of cooperative relationships necessary for economic growth. This mistrust can hinder the development of institutions that support economic activity, such as effective governance and legal systems, by undermining the trust required for their functioning. However, Austin qualifies the impact of this mistrust by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. This dynamic response highlights the agency of African societies in shaping their economic outcomes despite the historical legacy of mistrust.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Cultural beliefs and values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. Ogilvie notes that cultural beliefs can change the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game, thereby affecting economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's work on Africa demonstrates that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic response highlights the agency of societies in shaping their economic outcomes despite historical legacies. The interdependence of institutions within a wider framework underscores the importance of studying entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast emphasize that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth shows that cultural factors are integral to the functioning of institutions that support long-run growth. The historical evidence of African societies adapting to their environments and the institutional changes in England highlight the importance of cultural responses in shaping economic trajectories. This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.25; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) shows that culture plays a crucial role in dominating institutions, ultimately influencing long-run growth.
