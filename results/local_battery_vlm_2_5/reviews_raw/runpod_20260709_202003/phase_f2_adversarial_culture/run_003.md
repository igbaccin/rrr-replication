The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding whether cultural beliefs and values fundamentally influence the trajectory of economic development. One side of the debate posits that cultural factors, such as trust and social capital, are crucial in fostering institutions that promote growth. For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital suggests that societies with high levels of trust and reciprocity are more likely to develop institutions that support economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). On the other hand, some scholars argue that institutions are primarily shaped by economic and political factors, with culture playing a secondary role. This perspective is evident in Acemoglu et al.'s work, which highlights the impact of European mortality rates on the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies, thereby influencing current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The disagreement revolves around whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or a peripheral influence. This tension underscores the need to explore how cultural factors interact with economic and political determinants in shaping institutions that support long-run growth. Understanding this interaction is crucial for developing policies that can enhance economic development in diverse cultural contexts.

The secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30), underscore the importance of institutions in fostering economic development. This financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly lowered the costs of transacting, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutions that emerged during this period provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of a market for private debt. This institutional framework was essential for long-term economic growth and political freedom, as it ensured that property rights were secure over time (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are a necessary condition for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own; cultural and political factors also play a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) both address the role of institutions and geographical factors in economic development, rather than focusing on culture as a fundamental driver of long-run growth. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. They note that African responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. This dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach suggests that African economic development was influenced by complex interactions between geography, technology, and local responses. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 looked similar to the stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe rather than the developing north-western parts, and that grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower. This implies that economic development in Asia was constrained by factors other than institutional differences, such as technological limitations and the nature of economic activities. Austin (2008, p.2) also notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape. This dynamic interaction between geography, technology, and local responses highlights the complexity of economic development and the need to consider multiple factors, including cultural and political factors, in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) assert that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey argues that the economic growth in Northwestern Europe was driven by a cultural shift that valued trade-tested betterment, which in turn was enabled by new rhetoric that fostered a new cultural environment. This rhetoric was not just a product of elite interests but a broader cultural movement that shaped economic outcomes. Mokyr complements this view by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He highlights that the diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by the spread of the Enlightenment, which promoted the sharing of ideas through periodicals, translations, and personal communications. This diffusion of knowledge was not merely a function of national differences but a broader intellectual movement that transcended borders. The spread of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, was crucial to economic growth. Mokyr further notes that tacit knowledge, which includes skills and expertise, was often transmitted through personal interactions and training. This form of knowledge was particularly important in the eighteenth century, as it moved about through various channels. The interplay between codified and tacit knowledge underscores the complexity of economic growth. McCloskey's emphasis on the cultural struggle in France and Spain illustrates how the dissemination of ideas and ideologies can shape economic development. The cultural environment in these countries was influenced by a rhetoric that valued trade-tested betterment, which in turn drove economic growth. Mokyr's focus on the diffusion of useful knowledge adds a layer of complexity. He notes that the spread of knowledge was not just a matter of codified information but also involved tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions. This highlights the importance of social and cultural factors in the dissemination of knowledge. The cultural environment in Europe during this period was characterized by a dynamic interplay between codified and tacit knowledge. This interplay was crucial to the economic growth of the region, as it facilitated the spread of useful knowledge. The spread of useful knowledge was not just a function of national differences but a broader intellectual movement that transcended borders.

The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which endures today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is a rival explanation to the broader claim that culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. This mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but a living influence on modern African societies. It is a critical factor in understanding the reduced levels of trust observed in Africa today, which in turn affects economic growth. Trust is a vital component of economic transactions, facilitating cooperation and reducing transaction costs. The historical legacy of the slave trade has thus left an indelible mark on African societies, creating a cultural environment that is less conducive to economic growth. The persistence of this mistrust, as Nunn notes, is also linked to the political instability and weakened state structures that resulted from the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability has further hindered economic development by creating an environment of uncertainty and lack of institutional robustness. The broader implication is that the cultural impact of the slave trade, through its effect on trust and political stability, provides a significant antecedent to the broader claim of culture dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between historical events, cultural norms, and economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) contend that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. They establish a mechanism where high European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The institutional framework introduced by colonial powers, shaped by factors such as settler mortality, determined the trajectory of economic development in these regions. This institutional legacy is evident in the correlation between early colonial institutions and current economic performance. The persistence of these institutions, despite their extractive nature, highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonialism has had a profound and lasting effect on the economic trajectory of these regions, shaping their development paths in ways that continue to influence current economic performance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency or cultural factors, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies. Ogilvie highlights that the persistence of inefficient institutions in pre-industrial Europe can be explained by the power of elites to maintain structures that served their interests, even if these structures hindered overall economic efficiency. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who demonstrate that New World societies with greater inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These policies, driven by the interests of the powerful, often resulted in institutions that were not conducive to long-term economic growth. For example, Ogilvie notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered. This interdependency can lead to a system where inefficient institutions persist because they are part of a broader structure that benefits those in power, even if it hinders overall economic efficiency. However, Ogilvie acknowledges that the interdependency of institutions means that changes in one area can have ripple effects throughout the system, potentially leading to unintended consequences. This highlights the complexity of institutional change and the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutions interact.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Institutions, including economic and political frameworks, are deeply influenced by cultural norms and beliefs, which in turn shape the broader institutional landscape. Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs played a significant role in the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. For instance, Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, while the Protestant Reformation led to their decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This demonstrates that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the collective action game (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. provide empirical evidence that institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates, influenced by cultural and environmental factors, persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the enduring impact of cultural and institutional legacies on long-run growth.

The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding their persistence and impact. Ogilvie argues that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that changes in one area can have ripple effects throughout the system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This is supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that favourable contracting institutions are partially interdependent with favourable property rights institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further facilitating economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy has significant implications for long-run growth and development.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are crucial for economic growth, a finding shared across multiple studies. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.1). The precise role of culture in dominating institutions remains a subject of debate. This is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the measurement of the impact of institutions on income per capita, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).
