Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn are fundamental to long-run economic growth. The debate centers on whether cultural factors dominate institutions or if other variables, such as geographic conditions or political structures, exert a more significant influence. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic performance. Some scholars posit that geographic factors, such as mortality rates, and political structures, such as secure property rights, are more critical in determining institutional quality and, consequently, economic growth. These positions are not mutually exclusive; they highlight the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic outcomes. The extent to which cultural factors dominate institutions in driving long-run growth remains a contentious issue. The debate hinges on whether cultural values, such as trust and reciprocity, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability, or if these institutions are more influenced by geographic and political factors. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for understanding the historical roots of economic development and for designing policies that promote growth. Culture's role in shaping institutions is particularly evident in the context of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They trace this evolution to the "English financial revolution," which saw the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments. These innovations dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework was instrumental in making England's economic success more decisive and rapid. The financial revolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further enhancing economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). North (1989, p.1) elaborates on this by highlighting how the institutional changes in England fostered gains from trade, which are key to the wealth of nations. The secure property rights and efficient markets enabled by these institutions allowed for specialization and division of labor, leading to improved productivity and technological change. The institutional framework in England thus played a necessary role in fostering economic growth and political freedom. These institutional changes presented challenges. This highlights the importance of considering the institutional framework when examining long-run economic development. The secure property rights and efficient markets enabled by these institutions allowed for specialization and division of labor, leading to improved productivity and technological change. The long-term effects of these institutional changes are evident in the enduring economic success of England.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. They contend that the dissemination of useful knowledge, both codified and tacit, underpinned the economic changes of this period. This knowledge circulated widely within Europe, facilitated by periodicals, translations, and personal communications. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates that tacit knowledge, which includes skills and know-how, was crucial and often transmitted through personal training and apprenticeship. While this tacit knowledge was essential, the codification of knowledge also played a significant role, albeit with varying levels of detail and sophistication. McCloskey (2015, p.10) highlights that the cultural and ideological shifts in societies like the Netherlands and France fostered a new rhetoric that, in turn, created new economic circumstances. This dynamic interplay between ideas, ideologies, and the dissemination of knowledge suggests that economic growth was deeply influenced by the broader cultural and intellectual environment. This perspective underscores the importance of ideas in shaping economic development, providing a nuanced understanding of the factors that drive long-run growth.

The enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth underscores the significance of historical legacies in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. They jointly find that European mortality rates in colonies shaped the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn had a significant impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). High mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. These institutions persist over time and significantly affect economic performance. The institutional framework established during colonial times has a lasting effect on economic development, as seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This effect is deeply rooted in the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. underscore that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who had little incentive to promote long-run growth, leading to institutions that maximized short-term rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that it creates a path dependency where extractive institutions hinder economic growth and development over the long run. Acemoglu et al. also note that the impact of these institutions can be mitigated by subsequent institutional reforms that promote property rights and economic development. This suggests that while colonial institutions have a lasting effect, they are not immutable and can be changed through deliberate policy interventions.

Distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that culture dominates as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, restricting competition and maintaining inequality. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges that these institutions are interdependent, and studying one in isolation may overlook the broader framework that sustains them. This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts are a key driver, the complex interplay of institutions must also be considered.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade disrupted social structures and created a pervasive mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, in turn, hindered the development of social capital, which is crucial for economic growth. The detrimental effects of the slave trade on trust levels are evident in contemporary African societies, where low trust levels persist. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates on this by showing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability further exacerbated the economic underdevelopment of Africa. The impact of the slave trade on trust levels varies across Africa. This variation suggests that while the slave trade had a profound impact on trust levels, other factors also play a role in shaping contemporary trust levels in Africa. The persistence of mistrust in African societies highlights the enduring legacy of the slave trade and its complex interplay with economic development.

The interplay between technological advancement and social changes drives economic growth, a relationship that Abramovitz (1986, p.21); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) both support. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) points to the uneven distribution of social competence in exploiting advanced technologies, which creates a bottleneck in the catch-up process. This unevenness suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the ability to effectively implement and utilise these technologies varies significantly across societies. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.1) highlight that the prosperity of parts of Asia and Europe before 1800 diverged due to differences in institutional changes and investment in agriculture. This implies that the social and institutional frameworks that facilitate technological adoption are critical in determining economic outcomes. These differences in social competence and institutional frameworks highlight the importance of contextual factors in shaping economic growth trajectories. This raises the question of how cultural factors, which shape social competence and institutional frameworks, influence long-run growth.

Cultural factors, deeply embedded in social structures, significantly influence the institutional frameworks that govern economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, can shape economic institutions like guilds, thereby affecting economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Putnam's theory of social capital, which includes trust, norms, and networks, demonstrates that civic cultures fostering these elements encourage institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural influences are interdependent with broader institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how the European family system, labour markets, and credit markets were interconnected, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence underscores that cultural factors are intricately woven into the fabric of institutional development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by North, who argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These secure property rights, in turn, facilitated the development of financial markets and the growth of a stable market for public debt, providing a positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical evidence shows that cultural factors, by shaping institutional frameworks, play a fundamental role in long-run economic growth.

Cultural factors also influence economic outcomes through their impact on social capital and trust. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for reduced trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical trauma highlights how cultural factors can have long-lasting effects on economic development by shaping social norms and behaviors. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This shows that cultural factors, by influencing institutional development, can perpetuate economic disparities over time.

Cultural factors shape the institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder technological adoption and economic growth. A shared finding among the sources is the critical role of institutions in long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural factors, which shape social competence and institutional frameworks, can be traced to specific historical events, such as the slave trade, or are more broadly influenced by the evolution of institutions over time. The historical evidence of the financial revolution in England and the impact of the slave trade on African cultures settles this disagreement. The financial revolution in England, which led to the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically declined the cost of transacting and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists today and accounts for reduced trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This empirical comparison shows that specific historical events can have long-lasting effects on cultural factors and, consequently, on economic development.
