The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic growth over the long term. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic performance. Proponents of this view posit that cultural factors can motivate individuals to adhere to rules that enhance economic efficiency and growth. Another position contends that the primary determinant of long-run growth is the establishment of secure property rights and stable political institutions, which facilitate economic activity and investment. This view emphasizes the importance of institutional frameworks that reduce transaction costs and provide a secure environment for economic transactions. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are autonomous drivers of institutional change or whether they operate through the intermediary of political and economic institutions. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the mechanisms that underpin economic growth and development. The role of culture in this process remains a critical area of inquiry, as it sheds light on the complex interplay between cultural beliefs, institutional frameworks, and economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth and industrialization. The argument is that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality was a key determinant of current economic performance. The European settler mortality rates in colonies were a significant factor in shaping the institutions that would later affect economic outcomes. The European settlers tended to establish institutions that maximized their own rents, promoting long-run growth in the colonies only incidentally. This was because the institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who were reluctant to initiate changes that would not benefit them directly. Austin (2008, p.25) qualifies the implication of this mechanism by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic and partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This means that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic development trajectories. This highlights that while institutions play a crucial role, the interaction between institutions and local responses to the environment is also important in shaping economic outcomes. Therefore, the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward and is influenced by various factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to cultural factors, emphasizing instead the role of institutional evolution in shaping economic outcomes. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth, as secure property rights encouraged investment and economic activity. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England, particularly the establishment of stable markets for public and private debt, provided a positive externality that supported economic development. Furthermore, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) suggest that the financial revolution in England played a critical role in the country's long-run success, implying that even if other forces had led to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. This underscores the necessity of institutional change in achieving economic growth and political freedom. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated are intricate. For instance, the institutions that led to the growth of a stable market for public debt also facilitated the development of a market for private debt, illustrating the interconnected nature of institutional evolution and economic growth.

McCloskey and Mokyr share a view that economic growth is fundamentally driven by ideas and ideologies, not merely by interests or institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). McCloskey emphasizes that the cultural and ideological shifts in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were pivotal in fostering economic change. This perspective is supported by Mokyr, who argues that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting the diffusion of knowledge as a critical factor in the process. The shared finding that ideas and ideologies are key drivers of economic growth implies a mechanism where cultural and intellectual shifts create an environment conducive to innovation and economic development. Specifically, McCloskey illustrates this through the example of France, where the "Franco-French War" reflects a cultural struggle that hindered economic growth until ideological shifts occurred (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This mechanism is qualified by Mokyr, who notes that the diffusion of knowledge was not merely a function of written records but also relied heavily on tacit knowledge and personal transmission (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The role of culture in economic growth involves the spread of written ideas and the embodied knowledge that facilitates innovation. This underscores the importance of cultural and intellectual environments in driving long-run economic growth, suggesting that the permeation of ideas and ideologies through both explicit and tacit channels is essential for sustained economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) propose that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe were primarily shaped by distributional conflicts and cultural factors. They argue that these conflicts led to the development of institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and communities, which were not necessarily efficient but served to maintain the power of elites. This perspective challenges the notion that cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic institutions and, by extension, economic growth. The institutional arrangements that emerged from these conflicts often favored the elite and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, a pattern that Ogilvie (2007, p.27) suggests can be observed in the interdependencies between various institutions within a broader framework. This interdependency means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered. The implication is that the role of culture in shaping economic institutions is less direct than previously thought, and that the complex interplay of distributional conflicts and institutional interdependencies is a more accurate explanation for long-run economic growth.

This nuanced view suggests that the role of culture in economic development is multifaceted, extending beyond direct institutional influences. Goldin (2006, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that women's contributions to economic growth are significant, both historically and contemporaneously. This is evident in the historical context of African societies, where women played indispensable roles in community reproduction and economic activities. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth, highlighting their central role in economic and social structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This historical perspective underscores the importance of women's economic contributions, which are often overlooked in broader discussions of economic growth. The quiet revolution in women's employment and education in the U.S. further illustrates this point, as shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career dynamics began to change in the late 1960s and 1970s (Goldin 2006, p.8). These changes were driven by various exogenous factors, including increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). The long-term consequences of these changes are complex and multifaceted, as evidenced by the enduring impact of colonial constructs on gender relations in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This suggests that while women's economic contributions are significant, the interplay between cultural norms, institutional frameworks, and economic growth is far more intricate, extending beyond direct causal relationships.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence in economic development between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, a finding shared by Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.5). This divergence was driven by institutional changes and the impact of historical events, such as the Black Death. Pamuk asserts that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These changes included greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to the guilds, as well as an era of labor-saving technological innovations. Van Zanden notes that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was complex (van Zanden 1999, p.2). This complexity suggests that while institutional changes and historical events played a significant role in economic divergence, the interplay between these factors and cultural norms requires further exploration.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Hopkins, argue that institutional variables play a pivotal role in explaining the development trajectories of African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The influence of these variables extends beyond colonial rule, necessitating a historical investigation into precolonial and even prehistorical periods to understand the current state of African institutions. The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions is crucial for promoting growth. High congruence legitimizes governments and fosters institutions that induce development. This relationship is further nuanced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, which collectively shape the timing and scale of growth in export production (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins notes that low levels of congruence create conditions that favour patrimonial politics, which can lead to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutional variables and economic performance in Africa, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of historical contexts to inform institutional reforms.

Bryant asserts that the revisionist position, which dismisses Eurocentric views on modernization, is empirically suspect and analytically incoherent (Bryant 2006, p.35). Bryant (2006, p.35) and Austin (2008, p.2) both emphasize that the transition to industrial modes of production and imperial hegemony is contingent upon prior developments in technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms. This causal connectedness means that modernization is an emergent process building on accumulated resources and opportunities. The revisionist approach, which often focuses on shorter-term and immediate processes, risks explanatory distortions by overlooking these deeper, long-term influences. Bryant (2006, p.35) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the development of legal-juridical systems extending rights and protections, along with advancements in military organization and weaponry, were critical factors in Europe's trajectory towards modernity. These developments were part of a broader, interconnected process that included civic urbanization, agricultural and commercial innovations, and the rise of bureaucratized polities. The secularizing dynamic within Latin Christendom, facilitated by the massive fissure within the Church, further refashioned both the institutional orders of Europe and the mentalities of its inhabitants. This dynamic underscores the importance of cultural and social structures in shaping the European passage to modernity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the institutional legacies of colonialism, which often involved the imposition of extractive institutions, can have lasting impacts on economic development in formerly colonized regions. This qualification highlights the complex and often contradictory role of institutional legacies in shaping long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa has had profound and enduring effects on cultural attitudes, particularly in fostering a pervasive culture of mistrust. This cultural shift, in turn, has significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes. The legacy of the slave trade has left deep-seated impacts on social capital, with trust being a critical factor in economic development. The slave trade disrupted social structures and weakened state institutions, leading to long-term political instability. As Nunn (2008, p.27) points out, this historical context of state fragility is closely linked to current economic underperformance. The fragmentation of African states due to the slave trade has resulted in weakened governance and institutional environments that are less conducive to economic growth. This institutional weakness, characterized by corruption and political instability, has hindered Africa's development trajectory. The impact of the slave trade on cultural attitudes, particularly mistrust, is one factor among many in Africa's economic challenges. Nunn (2008, p.1) acknowledges that while the slave trade is a significant factor, it is part of a broader historical context that includes other determinants of economic development. This nuanced understanding highlights the complexity of Africa's economic performance, where the legacy of the slave trade interacts with other historical and contemporary factors to shape current outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted, with cultural beliefs and values significantly shaping economic institutions and outcomes. Ogilvie highlights how cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, demonstrating that non-economic cultural beliefs can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural approach is further exemplified by Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that features such as trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam argues that civic cultures, characterized by trust and reciprocity, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast underscore the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as seen in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The interplay between culture and institutions is further complicated by the historical context of African development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the dynamic role of gender in economic production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This cultural and gender-based perspective is crucial for understanding the broader historical context of African development, where the legacy of the slave trade interacts with other historical and contemporary factors to shape current outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a more dynamic, partly endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.2). This approach acknowledges Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, and ultimately changing the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). The shared finding is that culture shapes institutions, which in turn influence economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9).

The precise remaining disagreement is whether the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. Austin and Johnson show that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). Austin's rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development, underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth and the need for a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).
