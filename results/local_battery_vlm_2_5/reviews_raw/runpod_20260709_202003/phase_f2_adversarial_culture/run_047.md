The role of culture in shaping institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors influence the creation and maintenance of institutions that foster economic growth. One position posits that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, thereby influencing economic performance. Another position argues that the impact of culture is mediated through other factors, such as the historical development of institutions or the specific economic conditions of a region. A third position, however, suggests that culture itself is shaped by historical events, such as the slave trade, and that these cultural changes have long-lasting effects on economic institutions and growth. This debate highlights the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth, and underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how cultural factors contribute to the development of institutions that support long-run economic growth. The debate hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or a secondary factor influenced by other historical and economic forces. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) contend that the institutional frameworks governing economic activity are pivotal for long-run growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was a direct consequence of enhanced security in property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on the role of institutions by showing that the creation of the Bank of England was a pivotal point in the English financial revolution. This institution provided a stable market for public debt, which had a positive externality on the development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England's operations in private markets, which began shortly after its formation, further underscored the importance of secure property rights in economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) highlight that the institutions governing public debt markets were instrumental in creating a favorable environment for economic growth, as they provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt was crucial, but its private operations also played a significant part in fostering economic growth. This interplay between public and private debt markets underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in economic development. The evolution of these institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can drive long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) share the view that economic divergence theories primarily focus on institutions and geographical factors, rather than cultural elements, as the drivers of long-run growth. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and argues that the 'sophisticated geography' approach allows for a more dynamic understanding of economic development. This approach acknowledges that Africans' responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) similarly highlight that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture and high agricultural labour productivity. However, this higher productivity did not necessarily lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This nuanced view of economic development suggests that while institutions and geography play crucial roles, cultural factors and endogenous responses to environmental challenges also shape long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both posit that economic institutions are fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts within societies, rather than cultural factors. They argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies were shaped by the interests of elites seeking to maintain their power and wealth. For example, Ogilvie contends that serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions, but rather institutions that served the interests of powerful groups. This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms by which institutions shape long-run growth are complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie highlights the interdependencies between different institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that institutions must be studied as part of an interconnected system. This idea is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who emphasize the role of institutions in shaping the distribution of wealth and power in society, which in turn affects long-run growth. The role of culture in shaping these institutions remains an open question. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework may resist the influence of exogenous cultural factors, suggesting that cultural factors may play a more limited role in shaping long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism's institutional changes are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. They show that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This mechanism is supported by the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, indicating that institutions shaped by colonial experiences persisted and significantly impacted economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The institutional framework established during colonial rule was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, as these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, the persistence of these extractive institutions does not imply that cultural factors are irrelevant; instead, it suggests that the interplay between institutions and culture is complex and multifaceted. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, while significant, do not operate in isolation from cultural influences, which may also shape the long-run growth trajectory of societies.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the growth of useful knowledge, driven by ideas and ideologies, was a fundamental cause of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). This perspective underscores the role of cultural factors in shaping economic outcomes, as the dissemination of useful knowledge was facilitated by cultural and intellectual movements that transcended national boundaries. Mokyr (2005, p.56) emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge was not merely a function of codified information but also relied heavily on tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This highlights the importance of cultural factors in the diffusion of knowledge, as the transmission of tacit knowledge required social networks and cultural institutions that supported the exchange of ideas. McCloskey (2015, p.10) further illustrates this point by noting that the cultural struggle in France, known as the "Franco-French War," was a key factor in the country's economic development, as the new rhetoric that emerged from this struggle facilitated the adoption of new economic practices (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This suggests that while cultural factors played a significant role in the diffusion of useful knowledge, the global nature of this intellectual movement also highlights the importance of international exchange and cooperation in driving economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade is a historical antecedent to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade's impact on African economic development is not merely a matter of lost labor but also a cultural shift that undermines social capital. This mistrust has long-term economic consequences. Nunn notes that the slave trade caused political instability and weakened African states (Nunn 2008, p.27), which in turn hindered economic development. This political instability is a direct result of the mistrust fostered by the slave trade, as it made collective action and cooperation more difficult. Nunn and Wantchekon further emphasize that this mistrust is a significant factor in Africa's current economic underdevelopment. Yet, Nunn qualifies the extent of this cultural shift by noting that the slave trade is just one of many factors that have contributed to Africa's underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.1). This suggests that while the slave trade's impact on African economic development is significant, it is not the sole cause of Africa's current economic challenges.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted and deeply rooted in historical and economic contexts. Culture influences the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action. Ogilvie explains that cultural beliefs can shape institutional frameworks that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. For instance, the Protestant Reformation's impact on guilds illustrates how cultural shifts can transform economic institutions. This dynamic is further supported by Putnam's theory of social capital, which highlights that cultures characterized by high levels of trust and norms of reciprocity foster institutions conducive to economic growth. Putnam's work on Italy demonstrates that civic cultures, which emphasize horizontal social networks and altruistic values, lead to stronger guilds and communes, thereby explaining the economic performance disparities between northern and southern Italy over centuries. This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27) underscores the enduring impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions and growth.

The historical evolution of institutions governing public choice, particularly in seventeenth-century England, further underscores the role of culture in economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. These institutional changes, driven by cultural and political factors, were crucial for England's long-run economic success. The creation of the Bank of England and the subsequent decline in transaction costs reflect the increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This institutional framework provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, highlighting how cultural and institutional changes can synergistically drive economic growth. The financial revolution's role in England's economic success shows that institutions played a necessary role in fostering economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9).

The impact of culture on economic institutions is also evident in the context of Africa's economic underdevelopment. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and significantly affects collective action and cooperation. This mistrust is a critical factor in Africa's current economic underdevelopment, as it hinders the formation of institutions that support economic growth. The slave trade's cultural impact is just one of many factors contributing to Africa's underdevelopment. Nunn qualifies the extent of this cultural shift by noting that while the slave trade's impact is significant, it is not the sole cause of Africa's current economic challenges. This perspective is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin's work suggests that African economic development is influenced by a dynamic, partly endogenous version of geography, where Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories. The shared finding (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Austin 2008, p.25) demonstrates that cultural factors, such as trust and responses to environmental conditions, play a crucial role in shaping economic institutions and growth.
