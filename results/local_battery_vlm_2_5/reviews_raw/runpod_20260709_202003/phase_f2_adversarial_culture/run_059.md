The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is whether cultural factors can fundamentally alter institutional frameworks, thereby influencing economic performance over the long term. One position posits that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, fostering efficiency and growth. Another position argues that the impact of culture is indirect, mediated through other factors such as geography or mortality rates. A third position contends that cultural factors, such as mistrust, can persist over centuries and influence economic outcomes. The disagreement centers on whether cultural factors are a primary driver of institutional change or a secondary influence. The answer to this question has profound implications for understanding the dynamics of economic growth and the potential for policy interventions to foster development. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and understanding their origins and evolution is crucial for grasping long-term economic performance. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argue that the development of public choice institutions in England during the 17th century was pivotal in securing property rights and fostering long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This institutional framework enabled the English financial revolution, which dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments bolstered the security of property rights, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted the exchange of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, laying the groundwork for these institutional developments. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England during the 17th century was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These institutional changes have implications that go beyond economic growth; they also bolster political freedom. This institutional framework provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, as the Bank of England began private operations shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). These institutional developments in England facilitated economic growth and created an environment that supported financial innovation, which in turn reinforced economic stability. The financial revolution in England, driven by institutional changes, had profound implications for the country's economic trajectory and the broader development of capital markets.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts are the key driver behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Distributional conflicts, which arise from the unequal distribution of resources and power, shape the institutions that govern economic interactions. This perspective challenges the notion that cultural factors or efficiency considerations are the primary determinants of institutional development. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influence institutions is the control that elites exert over economic policies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) illustrate this mechanism by showing that in societies with greater inequality, elites implement policies that advantage themselves, such as restricting competition and maintaining their dominant positions. This dynamic leads to the persistence of inequality and the entrenchment of institutions that favor the elite class. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further qualifies this by noting the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework. This interdependency means that institutions cannot be studied in isolation; instead, the entire institutional framework must be considered. This implies that distributional conflicts influence institutions across the entire institutional structure, not just a single policy or practice. The persistence of inequality results from economic policies and the interlocking nature of institutions that reinforce each other. This view suggests that addressing inequality requires a comprehensive approach that considers the broader institutional framework.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) share a view that ideas and knowledge were pivotal in driving economic growth in Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.10; Mokyr 2005, p.56). For McCloskey, the change in rhetoric was a critical factor. The new rhetoric, which emerged in countries like the Netherlands and Britain, fostered a cultural environment conducive to economic growth. This rhetoric actively shaped economic interests, creating a feedback loop where new ideas generated new circumstances, which in turn spawned more innovative ideas. Mokyr, similarly, emphasizes the role of useful knowledge, arguing that the diffusion of knowledge across Europe was a key driver of economic growth. The spread of knowledge through various means, such as periodicals, translations, and international conferences, facilitated the sharing of ideas and innovations. This diffusion was a global phenomenon that transcended individual countries. McCloskey's focus on rhetoric and Mokyr's on knowledge diffusion together highlight how intellectual shifts can catalyze economic change. However, Mokyr also notes that the dissemination of knowledge was not solely reliant on written texts but also on tacit knowledge, skills and expertise passed down through personal training and interactions (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This aspect underscores the importance of social and cultural contexts in the transmission of knowledge, suggesting that economic growth is about the availability of information and the social mechanisms that facilitate its spread.

Technological advancement and social change are both crucial for economic growth, and their interplay is complex; Abramovitz and Broadberry and Gardner (1986; 2022) highlight that technological progress is not just a matter of adoption but also of social competence and institutional adaptation. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that productivity levels were erratic indicators of gaps between existing and best-practice technology, suggesting that the ability to exploit advanced methods is not uniform across countries. This social competence is influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This underscores the importance of institutional changes in facilitating technological advance, as countries must adapt their institutions to exploit new technologies effectively. The process of catch-up is not straightforward. As the pre-World War I decades wore on, however, both these qualifying circumstances became less important. This implies that while institutional changes are necessary, they are not sufficient on their own; countries must also build social competence over time to fully exploit technological advancements.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These changes were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies significantly influenced the type of institutions established. High mortality rates led to the implementation of extractive institutions, which persisted over time and had a lasting impact on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their rents and political power, fostering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions explains a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies. This institutional legacy highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic growth, underscoring the importance of understanding historical institutional changes in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)(Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust emerged as a result of the slave trade's disruption of social structures and institutions, leading to a pervasive lack of trust in both interpersonal and institutional relationships. The slave trade decimated populations and undermined the development of strong, centralized states capable of providing public goods and enforcing property rights. Nunn demonstrates that the slave trade's adverse effect on state centralization is a key mechanism through which it hindered economic development. The historical accounts reveal that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, resulting in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, prevented the establishment of effective institutions necessary for economic growth. The relationship between slave exports and state centralization underscores how the slave trade's impact on political institutions persists into the present day, shaping current economic performance. Nunn's findings are bolstered by empirical studies that link a country's history of state development to its current economic performance, indicating that the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence Africa's economic trajectory. The historical disruption of social trust by the slave trade has significant implications for contemporary economic development, as trust is a critical component of social capital that facilitates economic exchange and cooperation.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the historical development of economic institutions. Institutions, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. The English financial revolution, for instance, created a stable market for public debt, which provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This financial revolution, as North shows, led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and increased security of the time dimension of property rights, both critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is also recognized by studies of European economic institutions, which suggest that favorable contracting institutions may be partially interdependent with favorable property rights institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of culture in shaping these institutions is further illuminated by the impact of the slave trade on social trust. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical disruption of social trust by the slave trade has significant implications for contemporary economic development, as trust is a critical component of social capital that facilitates economic exchange and cooperation. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of institutions on economic growth is primarily due to their efficiency or their distributional effects. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the European financial revolution, which shows that the creation of institutions that fostered secure property rights was crucial for economic growth.
