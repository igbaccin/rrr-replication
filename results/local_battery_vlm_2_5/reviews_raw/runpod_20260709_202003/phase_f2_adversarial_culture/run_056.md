The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and institutional theory. The stakes are high because the answer shapes our understanding of economic development, policy design, and the influence of historical factors on contemporary economic outcomes. The debate revolves around whether cultural factors, including beliefs, norms, and values, fundamentally shape institutions that drive economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geographic conditions or political structures, play a more decisive role. One position holds that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting long-run economic performance. Another position argues that cultural factors are less decisive and that other forces, such as mortality rates or political institutions, dominate in shaping economic outcomes. Yet another position contends that the relationship between culture and institutions is complex and multifaceted, with cultural factors interacting with other determinants in shaping economic growth. This interplay suggests that a comprehensive understanding of long-run growth requires considering how cultural beliefs and values interact with other institutional and environmental factors. The debate is not merely academic; it has practical implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The resolution of this question could inform policies aimed at fostering economic growth by identifying the most effective levers for institutional change. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, as the Bank of England began private operations shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutions that emerged in seventeenth-century England thus fostered a secure environment for economic activities, which in turn drove long-term growth. The role of institutions in economic development presents complexities. With positive transaction costs, institutions matter, as they facilitate the gains from trade that are key to the wealth of nations (North 1989, p.1). This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic growth, even in the absence of cultural factors.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the growth of useful knowledge and ideas drove economic growth. They assert that the diffusion of knowledge, both codified and tacit, was the primary driver of economic growth in Europe. This knowledge, he states, was codified in texts and embodied in skills and tacit knowledge, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training. The movement of tacit knowledge, in particular, was facilitated by the technological and economic conditions of the time. This perspective highlights the importance of ideas and ideologies in driving economic growth, as they facilitated the dissemination of knowledge and skills that were essential for economic development. Mokyr also notes that the diffusion of knowledge varied across Europe. For instance, the "intellectual movement" that Mokyr discusses was more pronounced in some countries than others, suggesting that cultural factors may have played a role in the differential adoption of new ideas and technologies. This implies that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, their impact was mediated by cultural factors that influenced their reception and implementation.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) highlights that the type of institutions established during the colonial period, particularly extractive ones, have a profound impact on long-run economic growth. This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run growth is mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions, shaped by European colonists, were designed to maximize rents and promote long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional change adds complexity to this view. For instance, the African story suggests that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This implies that while colonial institutions played a significant role, the long-run impact on economic growth was also influenced by local adaptations and responses to these institutions. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is thus nuanced, with local factors playing a crucial role in shaping outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) focus on how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They argue that inequalities in land and natural resources led to the development of different institutions that either promoted or hindered economic growth. The institutional frameworks that evolved in response to these inequalities were shaped by the interests of the powerful elites. For instance, in societies with greater inequality, elites could influence government policies to restrict competition and maintain their advantages. This perspective highlights how the power dynamics within a society can shape the institutional landscape, leading to enduring patterns of inequality and economic stagnation. The interdependencies among institutions mean that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. The entire institutional framework must be considered, as institutions are interlinked and influence each other (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependence suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a web of institutional relationships that reinforce or mitigate the effects of inequality.

The early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia, evident before 1800, challenges the notion that this gap is solely attributable to later developments such as industrialization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; van Zanden 1999, p.2). This divergence is marked by distinct economic trajectories: while parts of Asia remained stagnant, the prosperous regions of Europe, particularly in the northwest, experienced significant economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) attribute this divergence to the role of institutional change, which facilitated investment in agriculture and led to high agricultural labor productivity. This institutional framework in Europe was conducive to economic growth, as labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services. Van Zanden (1999, p.6) notes that the development of silver wages in Europe reflected broader economic trends, indicating that the economic development of various parts of Europe was interconnected. This interconnectedness suggests that the economic divergence was part of a broader economic transformation. The role of culture in shaping these institutions is evident in the way economic development was influenced by cultural factors such as investment patterns and labor mobility.

The impact of the slave trade on African economic development highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development because it undermines social capital and reduces cooperation, which are essential for economic growth. This instability is a direct consequence of the slave trade's impact on state centralization, which in turn affects economic performance. The link between the slave trade and political instability is evident in the historical accounts of long-term political instability resulting from the slave trades. This instability led to weakened and fragmented states, which are less capable of promoting economic development. The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with recent empirical research showing that a country's history of state development is an important determinant of current economic performance. This highlights the long-term effects of the slave trade on African economic development, which include the cultural and political legacies that continue to affect Africa today.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, with various scholars highlighting different aspects of this relationship. Cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. These beliefs can alter the collective action game, affecting how institutions function and their impact on economic growth. For example, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' posits that societies with high levels of trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks are more likely to develop institutions that foster economic growth and political accountability. This is evident in the economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times, where cultural beliefs and values have shaped institutional frameworks that support economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Institutional frameworks are interdependent, and studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. The European family system, for instance, was interlinked with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interconnected with various markets and the nuclear family system, potentially furthering economic growth. These interdependencies highlight the importance of examining entire institutional frameworks to understand their impact on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2).

The historical determinants of cultural norms, such as trust, are crucial for understanding long-run economic performance. The slave trade in Africa, for example, led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural legacy has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a key component of social capital that facilitates coordinated actions and improves societal efficiency (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice is also critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This institutional change reflected increased security of property rights, which is essential for long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions that emerged from the English financial revolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further supporting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks interact to influence economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of trust levels in contemporary Africa and their correlation with historical slave trade data. This empirical comparison highlights the enduring impact of cultural legacies on economic development.
