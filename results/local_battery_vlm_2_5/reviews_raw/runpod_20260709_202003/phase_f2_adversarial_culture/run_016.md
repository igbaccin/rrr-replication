The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the explanatory power of cultural factors, which include beliefs, values, and norms, in shaping economic institutions and, by extension, economic outcomes over the long run. Scholars disagree on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional development or a secondary factor influenced by other determinants. Some argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic growth. Others contend that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography, technology, and political dynamics, with culture playing a lesser role. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic development, and for informing policies aimed at fostering growth in diverse cultural contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.27) and North (1989, p.1; 1989, p.9) both offer perspectives on how institutions shape economic growth, but they diverge on the role of cultural factors in this process. While Ogilvie emphasizes the influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, North focuses on the importance of secure property rights and transaction costs, suggesting that cultural factors may be less decisive. This tension sets the stage for a deeper exploration of how culture interacts with institutions to drive long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that extractive institutions established by colonial powers have lasting effects on economic performance. This argument is grounded in the idea that European mortality rates influenced the type of institutions established in colonies. High mortality rates led to extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic growth. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial practices, persists and significantly affects current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions is not merely a historical artifact but a critical factor in contemporary economic disparities. This correlation underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The mechanisms through which these institutions operate are complex, involving political power dynamics and the reluctance of colonizers to initiate institutional changes that would not directly benefit them. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for the colonizers rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective highlights how colonial institutions, rooted in extractive practices, continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies. While the establishment of extractive institutions had immediate economic consequences, their long-term effects are evident in the current economic performance of these regions. The influence of these institutions extends beyond economic metrics, affecting social and political structures as well.

Building on this, Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Hopkins, identify the slave trade as a critical factor in shaping contemporary African societies, particularly through its enduring impact on trust levels. The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists and significantly impacts contemporary trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent cultural norm that continues to influence social and economic interactions. Nunn and Wantchekon's work highlights that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, underscoring the profound and lasting impact of historical events on contemporary social capital. The mechanism through which the slave trade influenced trust levels involves the disruption of social networks and the erosion of communal bonds, which are essential for building trust. This disruption has had a ripple effect, affecting not only interpersonal relationships but also broader institutional frameworks. The legacy of mistrust is evident in the challenges African societies face in developing strong, cohesive institutions that can drive long-term economic growth. Hopkins builds on this by suggesting that understanding the historical determinants of institutional change is crucial for effective institutional reforms. This historical perspective is essential for addressing the root causes of Africa's economic challenges, which are deeply embedded in its institutional and cultural fabric. However, while the importance of historical context is clear, the path to institutional reform is complex and requires a nuanced approach that considers both historical legacies and contemporary realities.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). They show that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution was pivotal in driving economic growth and political freedom. The secure property rights facilitated by these institutions created a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the development of a market for private debt. The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt provided a substantial positive externality for private debt markets, further stimulating economic activity. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, contrasting with views that emphasize cultural factors. The role of institutions in economic growth is complex. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were responses to fiscal crises, indicating that institutional change is often driven by specific historical contexts and crises. This suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their evolution is contingent on historical and political factors, which can either accelerate or impede their development.

Distributional conflicts drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were not merely efficient responses to economic needs but were deeply rooted in the struggle for power and resources among different social classes. These institutions were shaped by the distributional conflicts that arose from the unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power. The persistence of these institutions can be seen as a result of the elite's ability to maintain their power through various government policies that restricted competition. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the development of institutions that advantaged the elite. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the institutions that evolved to maintain the status quo. However, this perspective is qualified by the interdependence of individual institutions within a broader framework, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12). She argues that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient, as institutions are interdependent and form part of a wider framework. This interdependency suggests that the evolution of economic institutions is a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including distributional conflicts and the broader institutional framework.

This complexity is reflected in the reversal of fortune thesis, which, while insightful, may oversimplify the causal relationship between historical institutions and current economic status. Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.27) argue that this approach compresses different historical periods and paths, leading to an incomplete understanding of long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the thesis struggles to account for why institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This gap highlights the need for a more dynamic understanding of institutional evolution, one that considers endogenous factors and the period-specific nature of institutions. Nunn (2008, p.27; 2008, p.1) extends this critique by showing that Africa's slave trades had adverse effects on economic development, illustrating how precolonial factors can shape long-term economic outcomes.

Acknowledging this complexity, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) explore specific economic contributions and development in Africa, focusing on the roles of African women and the divergence between Europe and Asia. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women were indispensable to the reproduction of communities in Asante, contributing significantly to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The role of women in matrilineal societies, such as the Akan, underscores how gender dynamics and cultural norms shaped economic outcomes. This perspective is complemented by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the great divergence, which shows that the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 had economic conditions similar to stagnating parts of Europe. The divergence was not solely due to geographical factors but also involved high levels of investment and institutional change. Akyeampong and Fofack note how colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, suggesting that these constructs may be as persistent as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. This implies that cultural factors, including gender roles and social norms, played a crucial role in shaping economic development. However, Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the early modern great divergence indicates that the impact of cultural factors may be more nuanced, as institutional change and investment also played significant roles. The interplay between cultural norms and institutional factors highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a more holistic understanding of the factors at play.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs significantly influence economic institutions, noting that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules of guilds, while the Protestant Reformation led to their decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence extends to social capital, where trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions that improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that cultural factors, such as those influencing mortality rates, have lasting effects on institutional development and economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks highlights the complexity of economic development. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both show that the impact of cultural factors is nuanced, involving multiple dimensions of institutional change and investment (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Cultural norms and institutions are interdependent, creating a framework that supports economic growth. Ogilvie discusses how the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interlinked with labour, land, and credit markets, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin agrees, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This interdependency suggests that cultural factors and institutions co-evolve, with each reinforcing the other. Austin's and Ogilvie's work shows that the dynamic interplay between cultural norms and institutional frameworks is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The role of cultural factors in shaping institutions is further supported by the work of North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast show that the financial revolution in England, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional change was a product of cultural and political factors, including the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Akyeampong and Fofack both show that cultural factors, including gender roles and social norms, played a crucial role in shaping economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The historical evidence from England and Africa suggests that cultural factors are as persistent as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs, and that they continue to influence economic development today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
