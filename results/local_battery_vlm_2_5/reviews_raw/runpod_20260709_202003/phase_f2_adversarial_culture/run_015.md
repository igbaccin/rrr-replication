The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. This debate hinges on the extent to which cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions, which in turn drive growth. One position posits that cultural beliefs directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. Another position argues that cultural factors are less influential than other determinants, such as institutional frameworks and economic incentives. The disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are antecedents that trigger institutional changes or whether they are part of a broader institutional framework that shapes economic outcomes. This tension is evident in the literature, where some scholars emphasize the direct impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, while others see culture as an element within a broader institutional system. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for comprehending the long-term economic growth trajectories of societies. The relationship between culture and institutions is complex, with cultural beliefs potentially influencing economic outcomes through their impact on institutional frameworks, which in turn affect economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.10); (Mokyr 2005, p.56) contend that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. The diffusion of useful knowledge, particularly tacit knowledge, was a key mechanism in this process. Mokyr (2005, p.14) elaborates that much of the useful knowledge in eighteenth-century Europe was tacit, embodied in skills and practical know-how that could not be easily codified. This tacit knowledge was transmitted through personal interactions, apprenticeships, and practical training, which facilitated its spread and application in economic activities. The cultural milieu of Northwestern Europe, with its emphasis on innovation and practical application of knowledge, fostered an environment where this tacit knowledge could thrive and drive economic growth. The role of culture in this process varied across Europe. While countries like the Netherlands and Britain embraced the new rhetoric and circumstances that fostered economic growth, others like Spain and France initially resisted these changes due to conservative cultural attitudes. This resistance highlights the complex interplay between culture and economic growth, where cultural attitudes can either facilitate or hinder the adoption and application of useful knowledge. The spread of useful knowledge, particularly tacit knowledge, was a crucial driver of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848.

North (1989, p.1) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the institutional framework governing public choice was instrumental in securing property rights and driving economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution was not merely a response to economic needs but a critical factor in enabling long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth. The institutions governing public debt also provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further integrating the financial system and fostering economic activity (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This integration highlights how institutional changes can create feedback loops that amplify economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. and shared findings (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) demonstrate that differences in European mortality rates led to divergent institutional paths in colonies, which continue to influence current economic outcomes. The mortality rates of European settlers were a significant determinant in shaping colonial institutions. These extractive institutions were maintained by politically powerful groups who benefited from the status quo, even if it hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting impact on economic performance, with former colonies that had extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). However, the relationship between mortality rates and current institutions is mediated by the persistence of these early institutions over time, suggesting that the initial conditions set by European settlers had a profound and lasting effect on the institutional framework of the colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Hornbeck (2010, p.6) find that institutions in New World societies significantly influenced the dynamics of inequality and agricultural development. These institutions, shaped by the unique environmental and historical contexts of the colonies, created conditions where wealth, human capital, and political power were concentrated among a few (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The role of property rights and social norms in agricultural development is highlighted by Hornbeck's work, which shows that the introduction of barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the ability to secure property rights and manage resources effectively was crucial for agricultural development. However, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operated varied across different regions, influenced by local conditions and historical legacies.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) argue that distributional conflicts and economic institutions played a pivotal role in shaping pre-industrial Europe's economy. This cluster focuses on how these conflicts and institutions influenced economic outcomes, rather than directly addressing the role of culture in long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Pamuk (2007, p.24), who argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These structural changes, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, contributed to the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations. The Black Death's impact on the economic structure of Europe highlights the significance of exogenous shocks in reshaping institutions and economic outcomes. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that institutions are interdependent and must be studied within their broader frameworks. This interdependency suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts and economic institutions on long-run growth is complex and multifaceted, involving various institutional frameworks and external factors. The interplay between these factors and the role of culture in long-run growth remains an area for further exploration.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) argue that the great divergence was already under way before 1800, challenging the views of 'world historians' who often situate the divergence later. This divergence is marked by significant differences in economic development, with north-western Europe experiencing institutional changes that drove agricultural investment and productivity, while Asia saw comparable grain wages but lower silver wages. Van Zanden (1999, p.2; 1999, p.6) supports this view by highlighting the complex relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe, noting that continuous growth in GDP per capita was not a universal experience. The development of silver wages in Europe reflected broader economic trends, with England and London playing pivotal roles in the eighteenth century. This economic divergence is not merely a matter of timing but also involves distinct institutional and economic trajectories. The implications of these findings extend beyond the timing of the great divergence, suggesting that the institutional frameworks and economic conditions in Europe and Asia were fundamentally different, with Europe's institutional changes fostering higher productivity and economic growth. This institutional divergence underscores the importance of understanding the role of culture in shaping long-run growth, as cultural factors likely influenced the institutional frameworks that drove economic development in both regions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade had profound and enduring effects on African economic development, shaping cultural attitudes that persist to this day. The slave trade instilled a deep-rooted mistrust within African societies, which has had lasting implications for economic outcomes. This mistrust is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent cultural factor that continues to influence contemporary economic behavior. The mechanism through which the slave trade fostered mistrust involves the disruption of social cohesion and the erosion of trust within communities. The legacy of the slave trade, including its impact on state development and political stability, highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes that influence long-term economic outcomes. This historical perspective complements the broader understanding of how cultural attitudes, shaped by historical events, can dominate institutions and fundamentally influence long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Goldin (2006, p.8) argue that women have played a pivotal role in economic growth and development, both in Africa and the United States. They highlight that African women were indispensable to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, contributing significantly to the reproduction of communities. In matrilineal societies like Asante, women's roles were central to the transmission of wealth and office, underscoring their economic importance (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This perspective is complemented by Goldin's analysis of the U.S., which shows that transformations in women's employment and education have been driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and shifts in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes altered female labor supply and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women, illustrating how cultural and institutional factors can facilitate economic growth. Goldin notes that these transformations involved a quiet revolution in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career (Goldin 2006, p.8). This underscores the complex interplay between cultural attitudes and institutional changes in driving long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views oversimplify causation, compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This dynamic involves the technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms that mediate these responses (Bryant 2006, p.4). These factors are mediated inheritances from the past, and without them, there can be no phased conversion to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony. The revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent (Bryant 2006, p.35). Bryant (2006, p.35) notes that the grand comparativists and synthesizers have identified a list of key determinants in the European trajectory, including civic urbanization, legal-juridical developments, productive innovations, and more. These factors are part of a causal connectedness that governs every transition from past to present, emphasizing the importance of cultural norms and social relations in economic development. This compression can obscure the complex interplay between cultural attitudes and institutional changes, which are crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The Industrious Revolution, marked by heightened labor input and novel consumption habits, played a pivotal role in catalyzing economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.18) and Abramovitz (de Vries 1994, p.18; Abramovitz 1986, p.21) highlight that this period was characterized by significant changes in household behavior and technological advancements, which laid the groundwork for subsequent industrial development. de Vries (1994, p.5) emphasizes that European urban networks, far from being stagnant, were dynamic systems of commercial interaction that facilitated regional economic development. This urban framework was crucial in supporting the industrious revolution, as it provided the necessary infrastructure for increased labor input and new consumption patterns. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) further elaborates on the complex dynamics of technological advance and social change during this period, noting that productivity levels were erratic and that social competence for exploiting advanced methods was still limited in the earlier years. This suggests that the Industrious Revolution was not just about technological progress but also about the social and institutional changes that enabled broader participation in the economy. However, Abramovitz (1986, p.21) also points out that institutional changes could limit economic growth, particularly in affluent and well-established economies. This implies that while the Industrious Revolution created conditions for economic growth, the long-term sustainability of this growth depended on continuous institutional adaptation and social competence.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth. Ogilvie highlights that cultural beliefs and values, such as those around trust and reciprocity, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. reinforce this idea, showing that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, underlining the enduring impact of institutional frameworks shaped by cultural and historical contexts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings illustrate how cultural norms can influence institutional development and economic outcomes over the long term.

Moreover, the interdependence of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding economic growth. Ogilvie notes that the European family system, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance were interconnected and contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was essential for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This shared finding shows that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their integration within a coherent framework that supports economic activities and secure property rights.

The role of culture in institutional development is further underscored by the impact of social capital and civic cultures. Ogilvie discusses Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need to consider the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional development (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic view aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights (North 1989, p.1). The underlying point of agreement is that institutions shaped by cultural norms and social capital are fundamental to long-run growth. The remaining disagreement is whether the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is necessary for economic growth. Austin's analysis shows that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of institutional development, which ultimately changed the environment (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional development, as shown by the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
