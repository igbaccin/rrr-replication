The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history. The stakes are high because the answer shapes our understanding of how societies develop and why some prosper while others stagnate. At the heart of the debate lies a disagreement between those who view culture as a primary driver of institutional change and economic growth, and those who see institutions as shaped by more exogenous factors like geography, technology, or political structures. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting efficiency and growth. Others contend that institutional frameworks evolve independently of cultural factors, driven by more immediate economic or political considerations. Still others propose that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including culture, but that culture alone cannot explain long-term economic growth. This interplay is evident in the evolution of institutions like serfdom, guilds, and credit markets, which have been variously attributed to cultural norms, economic efficiency, and political dynamics. The question of whether culture dominates institutions in driving long-run growth is thus deeply intertwined with how we understand the broader forces shaping economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast, together with North (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was pivotal for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This financial stability and the secure property rights it underpinned were essential for fostering economic growth. These institutional changes had implications that went beyond economic growth; they also played a critical role in the development of political freedom. North and Weingast contend that even if other forces might have eventually led England to success under the Stuarts, the institutional changes would have made the process more rapid and decisive (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

Pamuk and others argue that the Black Death initiated a series of long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15; Pamuk 2007, p.24). These changes included a decline in interest rates, increased labour mobility, and institutional reforms in guilds, all of which contributed to the rise of rural industries. This structural shift was driven by the high cost of labour, which in turn encouraged labour-saving technological innovations. The resulting economic divergence was due to the structural changes initiated by the Black Death. This pattern of economic development underscores the significance of early structural changes in shaping long-term economic trajectories. These structural changes had implications that went beyond economic growth alone. The high cost of labour and the resulting technological innovations also had profound effects on the development of political institutions. The increased mobility and flexibility brought about by the Black Death contributed to institutional changes that facilitated the rise of rural industries and the decline of interest rates. These institutional changes, in turn, played a critical role in the development of political freedom, as they created conditions that favoured the emergence of more democratic political systems.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that economic institutions are fundamentally shaped by distributional conflicts, which can persist over long periods and influence the trajectory of economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective challenges the notion that efficiency is the primary driver of institutional change, instead highlighting the role of power dynamics and elite interests. For instance, Ogilvie explains that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and the craft guild, were shaped by the interests of elites who sought to maintain control over resources and labor. This approach offers a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve, emphasizing that efficiency may not always be the primary goal. The distributional conflicts that underpin these institutions can have lasting effects on economic outcomes, as seen in the persistence of inequality in societies where elites have successfully maintained their control over resources. The interdependencies between different institutions within a broader framework can complicate this dynamic. Ogilvie acknowledges that institutions are interconnected, and studying them in isolation may not fully capture their impact on economic development. This interdependency suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they operate within a complex web of institutional relationships that can either reinforce or mitigate their effects.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are fundamental drivers of long-run economic outcomes. They posit that European mortality rates in colonies influenced the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn shaped current economic performance. The high correlation between settler mortality and current institutions underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. This relationship is further supported by the finding that early colonial institutions have persisted and significantly affected contemporary economic outcomes. This persistence is due to European colonies often being designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The nature of these institutions was a product of political power dynamics as well as economic efficiency. The colonial powers often implemented extractive institutions to maintain their control and extract resources, which had lasting effects on the economic trajectory of these regions. This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how the initial conditions set by colonialism can lock countries into paths of underdevelopment.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10); (Hopkins 2009, p.8) focus on the significance of institutional variables in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, highlighting that current institutions are deeply rooted in historical contexts that predate colonial rule. This historical depth is crucial because it underscores how institutional changes, though gradual, can profoundly influence economic growth. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding is significant because it reveals that economic growth in Africa was shaped by precolonial and indigenous factors in addition to colonial institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) elaborates on this by noting that contemporary institutions are more likely to promote growth when they align with indigenous institutions. This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and their ability to foster development-oriented institutions. The legacy of colonialism often resulted in institutions that were not congruent with indigenous systems, leading to regionalism, clientelism, and corruption. This dynamic is crucial because it highlights how the initial conditions set by colonialism can lock countries into paths of underdevelopment, even when institutional reforms are implemented. Furthermore, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) note that the 'renaissance' in African economic history has provided new insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries, rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence. This suggests that a deeper understanding of Africa's economic history can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

The role of women in economic development intersects with cultural and institutional factors, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Goldin 2006, p.8) document that women's contributions to economic growth were significant both pre-colonially and during colonial periods. In the case of African societies, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) demonstrate that women were vital to the reproduction of communities, with their bloodlines defining the transmission of office and wealth in matrilineal societies. This is a dynamic that can be seen in other societies as well. For example, Goldin (2006, p.8) finds that women's role in the economy was transformed by a series of exogenous changes. These included the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, the growth of educational institutions, and changes in household production technology. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.6) show that in pre-colonial Akan state formation, women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities. This suggests that the role of women in economic development has deep historical roots. Goldin (2006, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the shifts in women's employment and education were due to various exogenous changes, including increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions. This implies that while women's contributions to economic development are significant, they are also contingent on broader structural changes.

The slave trade in Africa engendered a culture of mistrust, a phenomenon that has persisted and has significantly impacted economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust has been identified as a key factor in the reduced levels of interpersonal trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon provide empirical evidence that this mistrust accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This persistent mistrust has created an environment where cooperation and collective action are hindered, thereby impeding economic growth. Austin qualifies the impact of the slave trade by highlighting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. (2002b) call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This means that Africans' own responses to their environments, including the technologies available to them, ultimately shaped their economic trajectories. This dynamic response to environmental challenges indicates that the impact of the slave trade on economic development is interwoven with other historical and environmental factors.

This contribution was shaped by cultural beliefs and social norms, which defined gender roles and production systems. The colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had long-term consequences for gender production, reflecting the stickiness of pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).
