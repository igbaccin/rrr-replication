The relationship between culture and economic growth has long been a contentious issue in the social sciences. At stake is the explanatory power of culture in shaping long-run economic development. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence economic institutions, which in turn drive growth. Others posit that culture is merely a proxy for other underlying factors, such as geographic determinants or institutional frameworks, which are the true drivers of economic performance. A third group identifies cultural beliefs as antecedents to institutional frameworks. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is crucial for designing effective interventions that can foster sustainable economic development. The core disagreement revolves around whether culture is a primary driver of economic growth or a secondary factor that operates through other mechanisms. The resolution of this debate could significantly impact how economists and policymakers approach development challenges. This literature review will explore the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth, focusing on the interdependencies between cultural beliefs, economic institutions, and long-term economic performance. Institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England, led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional development was not merely a response to economic pressures but a critical factor in enabling long-term economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the impact of institutions on economic performance is highly significant, indicating that the quality of institutions today is strongly influenced by historical factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). This suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent on historical and contextual factors that shape their development and persistence. The role of institutions in economic growth is thus not just about their current form but also about their historical evolution and the specific mechanisms through which they operate.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the "behaviour of the older industrial economies today" can be understood through "patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe" (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic view suggests that economic development is not merely a product of static institutions but is influenced by how institutions evolve over time and the specific contexts in which they operate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further illustrate this point by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights that the effectiveness of institutions in driving economic growth is contingent on their historical and political contexts, which can shape their design and implementation. However, Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this dynamic view by noting that the African story suggests a more endogenous version of economic development, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This implies that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is mediated by endogenous factors specific to each region.

Distributional conflicts, rather than cultural factors, are argued to be the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) similarly highlight that inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power were central to the development of institutions in New World societies. These inequalities, they argue, were exacerbated by government policies that advantaged elites and restricted competition, leading to the persistence of inequality and the entrenchment of institutions that reinforced it. This dynamic suggests that the distribution of power and resources, rather than cultural factors, is a key determinant of institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further qualifies that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that we cannot study one institution in isolation. This interdependency implies that the impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development may be more complex and multifaceted than initially suggested, with various institutions reinforcing and shaping each other in ways that are not always predictable.

Bryant (2006, p.35) and McCloskey (2015, p.10) argue that the economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by ideas and ideologies, not just by interests or institutions. This perspective underscores the role of cultural factors in shaping economic growth. Ideas and ideologies can fundamentally alter the trajectory of economic development by influencing the social norms, legal frameworks, and technological advancements that underpin growth. This interplay of ideas and ideologies with economic change suggests that cultural factors are not merely incidental but are integral to the fundamental causes of long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Goldin, demonstrate that the role of women in economic development is a critical factor that intersects with cultural and institutional dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). The role of women in economic development is evident in both pre-colonial and colonial Africa, where women's contributions were indispensable to economic growth and community reproduction. For instance, in matrilineal societies like Asante, women's bloodlines defined the transmission of office and wealth, highlighting their economic and social significance (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). These changes were driven by exogenous factors such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin 2006, p.3). The role of women in economic development, therefore, is a complex interplay of cultural norms, institutional factors, and economic change, which can both hinder and drive economic development over extended periods.

Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has persisted and influenced long-term economic and social outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust, rooted in historical exploitation, has eroded social capital and hindered collective action, which are crucial for economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the legacy of the slave trade is evident in contemporary African societies, where mistrust impedes the formation of social networks and inhibits economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a cultural artifact but a deeply ingrained response to historical trauma. This political instability, a direct consequence of the slave trade, has created an environment where corruption thrives, further stymieing economic development. The adverse effects of the slave trade on state centralization and political stability are profound, creating a vicious cycle where weak institutions and political instability reinforce each other, perpetuating underdevelopment. The historical determinants of mistrust and political instability highlight the enduring impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy by altering the dynamics of collective action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural influences can manifest in various forms, such as trust, norms, and networks, which collectively constitute social capital (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of social capital highlights how civic cultures, characterized by high levels of trust and reciprocity, foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is further supported by North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they facilitate secure property rights and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.1). The development of institutions like the Bank of England, which provided secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, played a crucial role in England's economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast also emphasize the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth, noting that the financial revolution in England was pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The interdependence of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie points out that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework has been recognized in studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights the need to study entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation. Austin shares this perspective, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin argues that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed the course of economic development (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic view of institutional evolution underscores the importance of cultural and historical contexts in shaping economic outcomes.
