The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth has sparked a significant debate among scholars. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The central disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional change, or if other factors, such as geographic and economic conditions, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values shape institutions in ways that foster economic growth, while others contend that institutions are shaped by exogenous factors like mortality rates and geographic conditions. This debate is crucial because it informs policies aimed at fostering economic development by highlighting the importance of cultural versus institutional determinants. Additionally, the debate underscores the need to understand the historical and contextual factors that influence long-run growth. The question of whether culture is the fundamental cause of long-run growth through its influence on institutions remains unresolved, with scholars divided on the relative importance of cultural versus other factors. A closer examination of the arguments reveals a nuanced understanding of how cultural beliefs and values interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast argued that the establishment of institutions governing public choice in 17th-century England fundamentally shaped secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of these institutions was pivotal in England's economic success. In this context, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework fostered both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The evolution of public debt markets provided a positive externality for private debt markets, further bolstering economic stability and growth. The institutions governing public choice thus played a crucial role in England's economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

Distributional conflicts, not cultural factors, are the more fundamental driver of economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that the evolution of economic institutions is primarily shaped by the struggles over resources and power among different social groups. In pre-industrial Europe, for instance, the persistence of serfdom and the rise of guilds can be attributed to the efforts of elites to maintain their dominance and control over economic activities. This is exemplified by the emergence of guilds, which were not necessarily efficient but served as tools for elites to regulate markets and suppress competition. Similarly, in the New World, the institutions that evolved in societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks are often shaped by the distributional conflicts that arise from inequality, rather than cultural beliefs or values. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework indicates that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic growth is complex. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that different institutions are interconnected, and changes in one area can have ripple effects across the entire framework. This interdependency means that the influence of distributional conflicts on economic institutions and, consequently, on long-run growth, is complex and multifaceted.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. The mortality rates faced by European colonizers influenced the type of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality rates leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents rather than promote long-run growth. This extractive institutional framework persists over time, significantly affecting current economic performance. These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, who designed them to maximize their own rents rather than foster economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive institutions is not solely due to the initial conditions set by colonialism. The political dynamics within these colonies also play a crucial role in maintaining these institutions.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone shape economic growth, instead focusing on the role of intellectual shifts. This highlights how ideas and ideologies can shape economic growth through cultural shifts that influence institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further supports this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.56) also notes that much of the knowledge was tacit, embedded in skills and personal transmission, underscoring the importance of cultural factors in disseminating useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.14). The implication is that the cultural environment, which facilitates the spread of ideas and knowledge, plays a crucial role in economic growth. However, the role of culture in this process is not merely supportive; it can also act as a barrier, as seen in the case of Spain, where conservative cultural attitudes throttled economic growth despite the examples set by other European countries (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This suggests that while culture can drive economic growth through the dissemination of ideas, it can also hinder growth by resisting institutional changes that might threaten established power structures.

This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional paths that different regions have taken. Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) share a perspective that historical institutional paths and economic divergences have been shaped by the interplay of various factors over time. This divergence was influenced by the institutional changes and investment patterns in agriculture, which varied significantly across regions. For instance, in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change led to investment in agriculture, resulting in high agricultural labour productivity. However, this higher productivity did not necessarily translate into abundant grain and low food prices, as labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. This pattern highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutional changes and economic growth, as the same institutional changes can have different outcomes in different regions. The dynamic nature of these institutional paths underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and regional contexts in which economic growth occurs. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth suggests that the role of culture in shaping these institutions can be just as complex.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, identify a significant mechanism through which the slave trade has exerted a long-lasting influence on African economic development: a deep-seated culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is not merely a residue of historical events but a persistent cultural trait that continues to shape contemporary social and economic interactions. The slave trade, by its very nature, involved the betrayal of trust, as communities were often divided and turned against each other. This historical trauma has left a lasting imprint on African societies, fostering an environment where trust is scarce and social capital is low. The culture of mistrust has, in turn, hindered the development of strong, cohesive institutions, as people are less likely to cooperate and invest in collective endeavours. Nunn elaborates on this by highlighting that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states (Nunn 2008, p.27). This political instability further exacerbated the challenges in building robust institutions, as the constant power struggles and lack of central authority made it difficult to establish and enforce rules that could foster economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, is not just economic but also deeply institutional, shaping the very fabric of African societies in ways that continue to affect long-run growth.

Culture plays a crucial role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values, such as those embedded in 'civic' cultures, can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has had lasting institutional effects that hinder growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings highlight how cultural norms can either facilitate or obstruct the development of institutions conducive to economic prosperity. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, as noted by Ogilvie, further underscores the importance of cultural influences (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency means that cultural factors can shape not just individual institutions but entire institutional frameworks, which in turn influence economic outcomes. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, is not just economic but also deeply institutional, shaping the very fabric of African societies in ways that continue to affect long-run growth. This shows that culture, through its impact on institutions, is a fundamental cause of long-run growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This is consistent with North's broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights (North 1989, p.1). Acemoglu et al. provide empirical evidence that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the initial conditions, influenced by cultural and institutional factors, can have long-lasting effects on economic development. Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and context-dependent, with cultural factors playing a significant role in shaping this relationship. The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This mistrust, in turn, affects the development of institutions and economic growth. The specific historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on trust and institutions in Africa settles the disagreement by showing that cultural factors, shaped by historical events, have a direct and measurable effect on long-run growth.
