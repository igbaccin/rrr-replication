The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the explanatory power of culture as a causal variable in shaping institutional frameworks that foster economic growth. The live disagreement centres around whether culture, through its influence on institutions, is a primary driver of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, institutions, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions in ways that either promote or hinder growth. Others contend that the impact of culture is secondary to other factors, such as the extractive nature of institutions or the historical legacies of colonial rule. The interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth is complex, with some studies highlighting the enduring effects of cultural norms on economic performance, while others emphasize the importance of institutional frameworks in mediating the impact of culture on growth. The debate ultimately hinges on whether cultural factors are autonomous drivers of institutional change or if they are conditioned by broader historical and geographical contexts. To understand the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth, it is crucial to examine how cultural beliefs and values influence the development of institutions that support or impede economic growth. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.1) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. They assert that the financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional framework was crucial for the long-term capital market and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by highlighting how the financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth. This institutional evolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) further illustrate this by noting that the institutions facilitating public debt also enabled private operations, underscoring the interconnectedness of public and private financial markets. These institutions had profound implications, fostering economic growth and supporting political freedom. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions are crucial, they are part of a complex web of factors influencing long-run growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the driver of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was the proliferation of ideas and ideologies, not the institutions or interests that dominated the region. McCloskey contends that the "better" institutions that emerged were defined by the interests of the elite, who acquired information about improved institutions and compelled others to adopt them. This process involved a cultural struggle, encompassing rhetoric and new circumstances. Mokyr adds that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth, with tacit knowledge playing a crucial role. This knowledge was written down and embodied in skills, transmitted through personal interactions. The diffusion of knowledge within Europe was facilitated by periodicals, translations, and international conferences, which allowed ideas to spread rapidly. The growth of economic knowledge was a broader European phenomenon, not confined to one nation. Mokyr notes that the diffusion of knowledge varied across Europe. The spread of tacit knowledge, in particular, depended on personal transmission and training, which could vary significantly between regions. This variability in the diffusion of tacit knowledge suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial drivers of economic growth, their impact was not uniform across Europe. The implication is that while ideas and knowledge were fundamental to economic growth, their effectiveness was mediated by local conditions and the mechanisms of knowledge transmission.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. This perspective challenges the primacy of culture as the key determinant of economic growth. The mechanism through which this occurs is the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. In such colonies, the high mortality rates faced by European settlers led to the creation of institutions that were extractive and did not encourage economic development. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic performance. The extractive nature of these institutions is further underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who note that equilibrium institutions may be designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if this does not encourage economic development. This pattern underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. The downstream implication of this mechanism is that the institutional framework set up during colonial times continues to influence economic performance, even long after the colonial period has ended. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) show that the impact of institutions on income per capita is highly significant and larger than the OLS estimates, suggesting that institutional changes are crucial and their effectiveness is mediated by local conditions and the mechanisms of institutional transmission.

This highlights that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader cultural and social context in which they operate. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by distributional conflicts. The institutional framework of serfdom is a case in point. This system concentrated power and resources in the hands of a few, reinforcing existing inequalities. The downstream implication is that these institutional frameworks, once established, create a self-reinforcing cycle where distributional conflicts are perpetuated through institutions that favor the elite, thereby hindering long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies this by noting that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. This interdependency suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is complex and multifaceted, requiring a holistic approach to understand their full effects.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade's impact on Africa's development is rooted in a culture of mistrust, which is an antecedent to the long-run growth that the topic investigates. The slave trade is suggested to have caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, is linked to political instability and state centralization. This instability, in turn, is linked to long-run economic underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the slave trade may have also caused precolonial origins for modern corruption, which in turn is linked to long-run economic underdevelopment.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The thesis suggests that the African story indicates a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach considers Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changing their environments. The thesis also notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. They note that in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change usually brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity. This higher agricultural labour productivity did not lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices because labour moved out of agriculture into industry and services. One downstream implication is that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. Austin qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run growth. Institutions are defined by the rules that structure political, social, and economic interactions. As North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further elaborates on this by highlighting the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the entire institutional framework must be considered to understand economic growth. This interdependency is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The financial revolution in England facilitated economic growth and provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This shows that cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, can explain the superior economic performance of certain regions. For example, Putnam's theory of 'social capital' suggests that societies characterized by high social capital, trust, norms, and networks can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These 'civic' cultures encourage institutions that favour economic growth and political accountability. The historical evidence from northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times supports this argument, as these cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern Italy (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is further supported by the fact that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific (Austin 2008, p.25). This period-specificity is crucial for understanding the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic growth. In conclusion, the historical evidence and empirical comparisons show that culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run growth. The precise remaining disagreement is whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, which shows that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25).
