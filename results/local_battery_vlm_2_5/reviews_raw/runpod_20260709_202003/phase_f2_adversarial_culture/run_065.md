The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development studies. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors, distinct from purely economic or institutional determinants, can fundamentally influence economic trajectories. One position emphasizes the primacy of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions, arguing that these cultural elements can either facilitate or hinder growth. This perspective is often framed within broader debates about social capital and the efficiency of collective action. On the other hand, rival explanations focus on exogenous factors such as mortality rates or geographic conditions, which are seen as more directly influencing the establishment of economic institutions. These rival explanations often downplay the role of culture, instead highlighting the impact of environmental and demographic factors. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are antecedents that trigger institutional changes or whether they are merely epiphenomena of deeper structural forces. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policymakers and economists seeking to promote sustainable economic growth. This question is particularly salient in the context of pre-industrial Europe, where the interplay between cultural beliefs and economic institutions is evident in the development of guilds and communal structures. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The slave trade is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and affects long-term development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the historical experience of the slave trade has left an enduring legacy of mistrust, which impedes economic development. The slave trade's adverse effect on state centralization is a key mechanism through which this cultural legacy manifests. Nunn (2008, p.27) shows that the slave trades caused political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. This political instability, in turn, hindered the development of effective institutions. The negative effects of the slave trade on political stability and institutional development are further compounded by the persistence of mistrust. The historical experience of the slave trade has left a legacy of mistrust that persists to this day, affecting long-term development. This perspective challenges the notion that culture alone dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.

This perspective shifts the focus from cultural determinants to the underlying power dynamics that shape economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies. The persistence of inequality is a key mechanism through which distributional conflicts affect long-run growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate that the differences in institutions are deeply rooted in the historical and social contexts that shaped these societies, and are not merely a result of conventional economic factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial for understanding economic growth, suggesting that the institutional framework as a whole must be studied to comprehend long-run growth. The qualitative nature of these differences in institutions highlights the importance of understanding the historical and social contexts that shaped these societies, providing a more nuanced view of the factors driving economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic change. This stance is evident in the cultural shifts that facilitated the Great Enrichment. The cultural struggle in France, for instance, was a prolonged battle over the very nature of society. This battle was about the underlying values and ideologies that shaped economic behavior (McCloskey 2015, p.9). The diffusion of useful knowledge, a key component of economic growth, spread widely within the Western world, and was not confined to national borders. This diffusion was facilitated by various means, including periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications (Mokyr 2005, p.56). The tacit knowledge, which was often embodied in skills and practices, played a crucial role in this process. The transmission of this knowledge depended on both written records and personal training, highlighting the importance of social and cultural factors in the dissemination of knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This perspective suggests that the economic growth observed in Northwestern Europe was deeply intertwined with the cultural and ideological shifts of the time, and was not merely a result of institutional changes.

North and his colleagues have argued that institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The role of institutions in economic growth is underpinned by their ability to reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic activity. North highlights that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, fails to account for the role of institutions in economic growth. North and Weingast provide a specific example of this mechanism through the creation of the Bank of England, which significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. further support this view by showing that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which can lead to the establishment of extractive institutions that do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The implication is that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact can be complex and multifaceted, depending on the specific historical and political context. This complexity suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is not straightforward, and that further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms by which institutions shape economic outcomes.

This cluster critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and Eurocentric views, arguing that these perspectives oversimplify causation and are empirically suspect (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35). This critique is grounded in the observation that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, failing to account for the dynamic and endogenous factors that have influenced economic growth. The revisionists' claim that major Eurasian societies were progressing along a comparable modernisation path is challenged by the intricate interplay of technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms that underpin economic development. This interconnectedness means that transitions to industrial modes of production or imperial forms of hegemony are deeply rooted in the accumulation of resources and opportunities from the past, and are not merely the result of immediate processes. Austin notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach acknowledges that Africans' own responses to their environments, utilising various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with cultural and historical contexts to shape economic outcomes. This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is not straightforward and requires a more dynamic and context-sensitive approach.

Van Zanden and others (van Zanden 1999, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) examine the economic development and divergence in Europe, focusing on how specific historical events and institutional changes influenced regional economic outcomes. The Black Death initiated a series of developments, including greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional changes to guilds, which collectively contributed to the rise of rural industries and labour-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). The Black Death also led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.5). This wage gap, in turn, influenced the economic development trajectories of different regions within Europe. This divergence was driven by cultural and institutional contexts, and was not solely driven by economic factors. This dynamic interplay between economic, institutional, and cultural factors underscores the complexity of Europe's economic development and divergence, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how these elements interact to shape economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) focus on institutional reforms as a means to eliminate poverty in Africa, while not directly addressing the role of culture in long-run growth. This literature highlights the significance of institutional variables in explaining development, emphasizing that current institutions are shaped by a complex history that includes precolonial and colonial periods. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions in promoting growth is enhanced when they align with indigenous institutions, which underlines the legitimacy of governments and fosters development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This suggests that institutional changes, even if gradual, can have significant impacts on economic development. This temporal limitation underscores the need for a more comprehensive historical analysis that includes precolonial and colonial periods, as well as the role of culture in shaping long-run growth.

Technological and agricultural advancements significantly influenced economic development by enhancing productivity and enabling better resource management. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and de Vries (1994, p.18) share a focus on how these advancements (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; de Vries 1994, p.18) drove economic growth. Hornbeck (2010, p.6) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains enabled farmers to protect their property rights more effectively, which in turn reduced externalities from livestock damage and enhanced agricultural development. This technological innovation allowed for more efficient land use and increased agricultural productivity, illustrating how technological advancements can directly impact economic growth. The role of social norms in this context highlights that technological progress alone is not sufficient for economic development, and social and institutional factors also play a crucial role. De Vries (1994, p.18) argues that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a distinct phenomenon that preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This period saw significant changes in urban networks and commercial interactions, establishing a framework for regional economic development. The Industrious Revolution's impact on economic growth underscores the importance of increased labor input and new consumption patterns in driving economic development. de Vries (1994, p.18) also notes that the Industrious Revolution was not a uniform process and varied across different regions, suggesting that local conditions and institutions influenced how technological and agricultural advancements were adopted and implemented. This variability in adoption patterns indicates that while technological and agricultural advancements are crucial for economic development, their impact is moderated by local contexts and institutions, which in turn are shaped by cultural factors.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women have been a driving force in economic development, a perspective shared with Goldin (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). This perspective is supported by Goldin, who highlights the transformative impact of women's changing roles in the U.S. economy, noting that shifts in women's employment, education, and family dynamics were preconditions for broader economic changes (Goldin 2006, p.8). Goldin identifies several exogenous factors, including increased demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology, that facilitated these shifts (Goldin 2006, p.3). These changes were not uniform across different regions or cultures, suggesting that local conditions and institutions, shaped by cultural factors, influenced how women's roles evolved and their impact on economic development.

Culture shapes institutions, and institutions, in turn, drive economic growth. This relationship is evident in the historical evolution of economic institutions, where cultural factors have influenced the development of property rights and other key institutions. For instance, the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial, as seen in the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These systems, characterized by specific cultural beliefs and values, facilitated economic growth by fostering trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England also highlight how institutional changes, driven by cultural and political factors, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution, in turn, supported secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England further illustrates how cultural and institutional factors interplay to foster economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The role of culture in shaping institutions is also evident in the long-term consequences of colonial constructs and codification of gender roles. These constructs have had significant impacts on the dynamics of gender relations, with long-term consequences for gender production and economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The sexual division of labor established during the colonial period continues to influence contemporary economic structures, highlighting the enduring impact of cultural and institutional factors (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The persistence of these colonial constructs suggests that cultural beliefs and values, once established, can be as sticky as pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).

The persistence of cultural and institutional factors in shaping economic development is further supported by the role of social capital in facilitating coordinated actions. Social capital, characterized by features such as trust, norms, and networks, improves the efficiency of society by fostering economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This is evident in the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times, where cultural beliefs and values have influenced the development of institutions that favor economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
