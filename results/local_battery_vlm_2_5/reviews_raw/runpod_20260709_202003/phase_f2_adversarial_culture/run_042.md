The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. Two main positions exist: one emphasizes the primacy of cultural values and beliefs in shaping economic institutions, which in turn drive growth; the other focuses on exogenous factors, such as geographical conditions or institutional frameworks, as the primary drivers. The disagreement revolves around whether cultural factors are endogenous to institutional development or whether they are exogenous influences that shape institutions independently. A third position identifies the slave trade as a significant antecedent that shapes cultural norms, which then influence institutional development. The debate highlights the need to understand the interplay between culture, institutions, and long-term economic performance, as these factors collectively determine a society's economic trajectory. The question of whether cultural values and beliefs fundamentally cause long-run growth through their influence on institutions remains unresolved. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The evolution of institutions in England, as detailed by North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1), was instrumental in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, building on the cultural and religious factors that influenced these institutional changes. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically decreased transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn fostered a parallel market for private debt, creating a positive externality that facilitated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The secure property rights enabled by these institutions were critical for long-term capital markets and overall economic development. However, North acknowledges that while institutions play a necessary role, other forces, such as technological change and resource allocation, also contribute to economic growth.

The role of distributional conflicts and urbanization patterns in shaping economic outcomes, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), underscores the importance of institutional changes in pre-industrial Europe. Distributional conflicts, in this context, refer to the struggles over the allocation of resources and power among different social groups. This mechanism highlights how institutional frameworks, shaped by distributional conflicts, could facilitate economic growth by improving resource allocation and productivity. However, van Zanden (1999, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living was not straightforward; economic growth did not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards. This suggests that while institutional changes driven by distributional conflicts could stimulate economic growth, the benefits of this growth were not uniformly distributed across society.

The examination of economic institutions and inequality by Austin (2008, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) reveals that cultural factors, while influential, were not the sole drivers of long-run growth. They jointly establish that the evolution of economic institutions is closely tied to the distribution of power and resources within societies, with elites often shaping institutions to maintain their advantages. This dynamic is evident in how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need to consider the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that different historical periods and paths of institutional evolution need to be carefully considered, as the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing these different historical periods. This nuanced view suggests that the benefits of institutional changes are not uniformly distributed across society, aligning with van Zanden (1999, p.2)'s observation that economic growth does not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards. The implication is that while institutional changes driven by distributional conflicts could stimulate economic growth, the benefits of this growth were not uniformly distributed across society.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the influence of ideas and ideologies on economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 highlights the broader intellectual movements that transcended national boundaries. This perspective highlights a cultural shift where the dissemination of useful knowledge played a pivotal role. Mokyr (2005, p.14) elaborates that useful knowledge was not merely codified in written form but also embodied in tacit skills and practical expertise. This tacit knowledge was transmitted through personal interactions and training, facilitating its spread across Europe. The cultural environment of the time, characterized by the "bourgeois and aristocratically dishonorable countries" like the Netherlands and Britain, fostered a new rhetoric that encouraged innovation and economic growth. This rhetoric, in turn, created circumstances that further stimulated the spread of useful knowledge, leading to a virtuous cycle of economic enrichment. This suggests that while cultural factors were influential, they operated in conjunction with broader intellectual movements that transcended national boundaries.

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), underscores the enduring impact of colonialism on long-run economic outcomes. This mechanism underscores how initial institutional structures, shaped by colonial experiences, have enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, highlighting the extractive nature of these colonial institutions. This perspective is crucial as it suggests that the legacy of colonialism, through the imposition of extractive institutions, continues to influence economic outcomes.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa, as identified by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4); (Nunn 2008, p.27), has fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to impact trust levels within African societies. They argue that this mistrust is a direct result of the historical experience of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and eroded interpersonal trust. Nunn (2008, p.27) elaborates on this by showing that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. This political instability further exacerbated the mistrust within societies, as it undermined the development of strong, centralized institutions capable of enforcing rules and protecting citizens. The fragmented political landscape created by the slave trades thus reinforced the culture of mistrust, making it difficult for societies to rebuild trust and establish effective governance structures. This persistent mistrust has had profound implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action, which are essential for growth. This finding underscores the enduring influence of the slave trade on African societies, highlighting how historical events can shape long-term cultural attitudes and economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with the evolution of economic institutions and their impact on societal development. Ogilvie and Putnam both show that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of economies. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs, such as those about the afterlife, can motivate economic behaviors and shape institutions like guilds. Putnam's theory of social capital further illustrates that trust, norms, and networks, features of social organization, can improve societal efficiency by facilitating coordinated actions. These cultural elements foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. This is evident in the superior economic performance of northern Italy compared to southern Italy since medieval times, where 'civic' cultures encouraged strong guilds and communes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cultural attitudes also play a crucial role in shaping institutional frameworks that support long-term economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework was essential for the long-term capital market and economic growth itself, as it provided a positive externality for the development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North and Weingast 1989, p.24).

The historical impact of the slave trade further underscores the enduring influence of cultural attitudes on economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon reveal that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This persistent mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action, which are essential for growth. The slave trade's impact on trust is significant, highlighting how historical events can shape long-term cultural attitudes and economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The shared finding across these studies is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The disagreement lies in whether the causal link between culture and institutions is direct or mediated by other factors. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, while Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Austin 2008, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparative analysis of colonial settlements and their institutional legacies, which shows that the impact of institutions on economic performance is highly significant.
