The role of culture in shaping institutions and thereby influencing long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural factors are merely epiphenomenal, or whether they play a causal role in the establishment and persistence of institutions that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. One side of the debate argues that cultural beliefs and values directly shape economic institutions, with some cultures fostering growth-promoting institutions while others do not. The opposing view posits that institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or political dynamics, with culture playing at most a secondary role. The disagreement hinges on whether culture is a causal force in its own right or merely a reflection of deeper structural factors. This debate is crucial because it bears on the efficacy of policies aimed at fostering economic growth through institutional reform. If culture is a primary driver, then policies must address cultural factors directly; if not, then other levers must be pulled. The question of whether culture is a causal force in long-run growth is thus central to understanding the mechanisms of economic development. A key point of contention is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values can shape institutions and thereby influence economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The institutions that emerged during this period were essential for the growth of a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated long-term economic growth. The impact of these institutions varied across sectors. While the financial revolution undoubtedly spurred economic growth, the benefits were not immediately realized in all areas due to the complex interplay of political and economic factors. The evolution of these institutions highlights how secure property rights and efficient financial markets can foster long-term economic growth, even in the face of political and economic challenges.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) propose that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. They assert that cultural shifts, including changes in rhetoric and ideologies, played a crucial role in fostering economic growth, which was not merely a result of institutional improvements. McCloskey highlights that the "cultural struggle" within France and Spain demonstrates how conservative ideologies can hinder economic growth, even in the presence of institutional changes. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing the diffusion of useful knowledge across nations, asserting that the growth of knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr further explains that the transfer of knowledge, both codified and tacit, was facilitated through personal transmission and training, which underscores the importance of cultural and social networks in disseminating economic ideas. Mokyr also points out that the codification of knowledge was influenced by the technology and costs of codification, suggesting that the economic benefits of ideas depend on the efficiency of knowledge transfer. This implies that while ideas and ideologies are crucial, their economic impact is mediated by the efficiency of knowledge dissemination and the cultural context in which they are embedded. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex, involving the generation of new ideas and their effective dissemination and integration into the economic fabric.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight distributional conflicts as a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World, showing that inequality shaped institutions that favoured elites and persisted over time. This cultural dimension of distributional conflicts is crucial because it reveals how power dynamics can be embedded in cultural norms and beliefs, which in turn shape institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elucidate this mechanism by showing how differentials in paths of development can be attributed to the institutions that evolved in response to these power dynamics. The persistence of inequality and the institutions that support it is a downstream implication of this mechanism. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) qualifies this implication by noting that institutions are interdependent and studying one in isolation may not capture the full picture. This suggests that while distributional conflicts and the resulting inequality are significant factors, they are part of a complex web of institutional interdependencies that also include cultural norms and beliefs.

This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, highlighting the long-term effects of historical institutional changes. They and Nunn and Wantchekon (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) establish that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which in turn affected current income per capita. The mechanism operates through the mortality rates faced by European settlers, which correlated strongly with the establishment of European settlements. These settlements then shaped early institutions, which persisted and influenced current institutions. The persistence of these institutions is a key factor in determining long-run economic outcomes. In particular, the institutions established during colonial times were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17) show that the impact of these institutions on income per capita is highly significant, with a strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions. This suggests that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had a profound and lasting effect on economic development. The persistence of these institutions, however, is subject to the qualifications of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who note that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and may not always encourage economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, shared by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) in highlighting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This thesis suggests that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences of Europe and its overseas 'off-shoots' were pivotal in shaping long-term economic growth. The African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). This pattern of economic development is contrasted with the stagnating southern, central, and eastern parts of Europe, where grain wages were comparable to those in north-western Europe, but silver wages were substantially lower. This indicates that the economic divergence was influenced by a complex interplay of institutional changes, investment patterns, and policy experiences (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). The persistence of these institutions, however, is subject to the qualifications of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who note that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and may not always encourage economic development. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic development highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the factors that drive long-term growth.

The slave trade profoundly shaped African development by fostering a culture of mistrust, a pattern that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This mistrust is rooted in the historical experience of exploitation and betrayal, which has left a lasting imprint on social norms. Nunn (2008, p.27) elaborates on this, showing that the slave trades led to long-term political instability and weakened state structures. This instability, in turn, hindered economic development by creating an environment where corruption could thrive. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic impacts to shape the broader cultural and institutional landscape of Africa, influencing long-term growth trajectories.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, as recognized by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The European family system, for instance, was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). The community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, furthering economic growth in the centuries before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency highlights the importance of cultural factors in shaping economic institutions and growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, showing how institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
