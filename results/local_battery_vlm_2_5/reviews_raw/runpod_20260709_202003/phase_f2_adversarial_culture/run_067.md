The question of how culture influences institutions as a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth is central to understanding the persistent disparities in economic development across regions. The stakes are high, as identifying the causal mechanisms at play can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable growth. One live disagreement centers around whether cultural factors, such as social capital and trust, directly shape institutions that promote economic growth, or whether other factors, like institutional frameworks and historical events, are the primary drivers. Another key debate revolves around the role of distributional conflicts and the efficiency of economic institutions. While some scholars emphasize the impact of cultural beliefs on institutional structures, others focus on the interplay between institutions and economic performance, suggesting that the efficiency of institutions is not the sole determinant of economic growth. Additionally, the role of historical events, such as the slave trade, in shaping cultural norms and their subsequent impact on economic institutions adds another layer of complexity to this debate. The extent to which cultural factors operate through institutions to drive long-run growth is a contentious issue, with some scholars arguing for a direct causal link while others propose more indirect or complementary relationships. The interplay between culture and institutions in driving economic growth is a multifaceted issue that requires a nuanced understanding of historical contexts and institutional frameworks. Colonialism's institutional legacy is a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth, as Acemoglu et al. and Nunn and Wantchekon (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) argue. The institutional changes introduced by colonial powers, particularly the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, have persisted and significantly impacted current economic performance. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, were shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from maintaining the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the lack of incentives for European colonists to initiate institutional change, as they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. This institutional path dependency has led to a significant fraction of current income differences, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. This framework, however, does not account for the role of cultural factors in shaping institutional outcomes, leaving open the question of how cultural transmission might interact with institutional changes introduced by colonialism.

Ogilvie shows that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds in pre-industrial Europe can be better explained by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. This perspective is reinforced by Sokoloff and Engerman, who demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. Ogilvie further elaborates that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is crucial, as institutions like the European family system and community-based welfare systems were interconnected with labor markets, credit markets, and insurance provision. This interdependency indicates that institutions are part of a complex framework that influences economic growth and inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the persistence of inequality is due to institutions, underlying factor endowments, and the initial conditions that shaped these institutions. This implies that while distributional conflicts are significant, the initial conditions and factor endowments also play a crucial role in the long-run persistence of inequality. The complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth underscores the multifaceted nature of factors influencing productivity levels and technological advancement, with Abramovitz and Austin both emphasizing that simple convergence hypotheses are insufficient to explain long-run growth dynamics (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). Abramovitz posits that the catch-up process is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. This perspective highlights that productivity levels are shaped by the social competence to exploit advanced methods. Austin further elaborates on this complexity by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This dynamic view suggests that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience of countries are crucial in understanding their economic trajectories. Austin also shares the rejection of the 'simple geography' thesis, which posits unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Instead, Austin advocates for a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach considers Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available. This dynamic interaction between technology and social competence is critical in shaping long-run growth. The implication of this complexity is that institutional change and social competence are active agents in the growth process. Austin notes that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific. This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions and social competence interact over time to drive economic growth.
