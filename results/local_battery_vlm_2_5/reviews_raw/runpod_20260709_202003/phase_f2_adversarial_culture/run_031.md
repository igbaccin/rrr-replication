The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development, with scholars debating whether cultural factors are primary drivers or secondary influences. On one side, some argue that cultural beliefs and values can shape institutions in ways that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. On the other side, others propose that the impact of culture is overstated, and that institutions are primarily shaped by economic or political factors. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of culture can inform policies aimed at fostering economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether cultural factors are antecedents that trigger institutional changes, or whether they are epiphenomena that arise from underlying economic or political structures. The debate is further complicated by the fact that cultural factors can interact with institutions in complex ways, making it difficult to disentangle cause and effect. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. In their seminal work, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1) emphasize that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which they describe as the English financial revolution, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction in transaction costs was crucial for enhancing the security of property rights over time, a dimension vital for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this, noting that the financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The institutions that emerged during this period provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further bolstering economic activity. The implications of these institutional changes are complex. While North and Weingast (1989, p.30) highlight the necessity of these institutions for economic growth and political freedom, North (1989, p.9) underscores that even if other forces might have led to England's success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively. This suggests that while cultural factors and exogenous influences might play a role, the institutional framework was indispensable for the rapid and decisive economic growth observed in England.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) propose that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates that this useful knowledge was codified in texts and embodied in tacit forms, such as skills and expertise, which were transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This tacit knowledge, while difficult to codify, was instrumental in driving economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.10) underscores that cultural shifts, including changes in rhetoric and ideology, played a pivotal role in creating new circumstances that facilitated economic growth (McCloskey 2015, p.9). These cultural shifts were drivers of economic transformation. Mokyr (2005, p.56) qualifies that the diffusion of knowledge was not uniformly rapid or straightforward; it depended on various factors, including the technology and costs of codification and the payoff to efforts to do so (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This implies that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, their impact on economic growth was mediated by practical considerations and the efficiency of knowledge transmission.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. They show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The mechanism linking colonial institutions to economic outcomes involves the persistence of these institutions over time, which in turn affects economic performance. This persistence is rooted in the institutional legacies of colonial rule. The impact of these institutions on economic performance is mediated by the specific historical and political contexts in which they were implemented. This implies that while institutional changes due to colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes, their effects are contingent on various factors, including the efficiency of knowledge transmission and the payoff to efforts to do so (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The slave trade's impact on African development is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust, which persists and affects long-term development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn jointly establish that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is rooted in the historical political instability and state fragmentation it caused. This instability is a key mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy of mistrust persists, as it hindered the development of strong, centralized institutions that could foster trust and cooperation. Nunn and Wantchekon further show that contemporary African countries' economic development is influenced by the historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as trust. The slave trade's impact on state centralization is significant, as it directly affects the development of institutions that promote economic growth. This historical legacy of mistrust and political instability continues to shape African development, as it hinders the formation of strong, centralized institutions that are crucial for economic growth. The persistence of this culture of mistrust, therefore, is a critical factor in understanding the long-run effects of the slave trade on African development.

Distributional conflicts and inequality significantly shape economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, influenced the course of development by affecting policies that governed interactions between private citizens and constrained the behavior of political and economic elites. This institutional framework, interdependent with various markets and social systems, furthered economic growth in specific contexts. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes, means that studying one institution in isolation is insufficient. This implies that the long-run effects of distributional conflicts on economic institutions are complex and multifaceted, requiring a holistic approach to understand their impact on growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are discussed, highlighting different historical paths and institutional evolutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This dynamic view posits that Africans' responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately shaped their economic trajectory. This divergence was driven by institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture and high agricultural labour productivity. The implications of this institutional evolution extend to the broader framework of economic growth, as the higher agricultural labour productivity did not necessarily lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices. This period-specificity underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run growth.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping the institutions that govern economic growth, with its influence extending across various historical contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that cultural beliefs and values can directly impact the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, shaping the collective action game in ways that influence economic outcomes. For instance, Putnam's theory of social capital emphasizes that cultural traits such as trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions, thereby enhancing economic efficiency. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that historical cultural developments, such as the legacy of the slave trade, have lasting effects on contemporary social norms, including trust, which in turn affect economic performance. The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run growth. This interdependency suggests that the cultural underpinnings of institutions are part of a wider system that shapes economic outcomes. For example, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England was interdependent with labour markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system, contributing to economic growth before industrialization. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Austin (2008, p.2) further contends that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, indicating that the institutional framework governing economic growth varies across different historical contexts. This period-specificity underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the historical determinants of differences in cultural norms of behavior, such as the independence of cities during the eleventh to fourteenth centuries (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
