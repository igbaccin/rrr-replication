The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth has been a contentious issue in economic scholarship. At stake is understanding the deep determinants of economic growth and development, which can inform policy and economic theory. The central disagreement revolves around whether culture shapes institutions in ways that drive long-term economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography or extractive institutions, are more decisive. Some scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby impacting economic growth. Others contend that the impact of culture on institutions is overstated, and that institutions themselves, shaped by factors like mortality rates and colonial settlements, are the primary drivers of economic performance. This debate is crucial for comprehending the historical trajectories of different regions and for designing effective policies to foster economic growth. A critical examination of these perspectives reveals the complexities involved in understanding the interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. Culture shapes institutions through the direct influence of beliefs and values, as well as through the interaction of these cultural elements with other factors to create institutional frameworks that support or hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines social capital and hampers cooperation. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight that the colonial period intensified gender-based constructs and codified gender roles, which had significant long-term consequences for gender production. These constructs may explain counterfactual evidence from economic dependency models, which have found that breadwinner wives continue to contribute significantly to economic growth. The persistence of mistrust and the enduring impact of colonial gender constructs suggest that cultural factors, rooted in historical experiences, continue to shape economic outcomes in Africa. The legacy of the slave trade and colonial rule has left a lasting imprint on African societies, influencing contemporary economic development through the lens of mistrust and gender dynamics.

The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices have long-lasting economic impacts (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. demonstrated that high mortality rates faced by European colonizers led to the implementation of extractive institutions, which in turn hindered long-term economic growth. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than to foster economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions can be attributed to the political power dynamics at play, where dominant groups may resist institutional changes that could undermine their political influence or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of African colonies, where the legacy of colonial rule has left a lasting imprint on economic development. The enduring impact of these institutions suggests that the historical experiences of colonialism continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Bryant (2006, p.35) both assert that institutions play a pivotal role in economic development, with the former emphasizing that institutional variables are more significant than other factors in explaining development. This perspective is evident in the new economic history of Africa, which posits that institutional change is gradual and deeply rooted in historical contexts that extend beyond the colonial era. This gradual institutional change is particularly relevant when considering the influence of indigenous institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The legitimacy and effectiveness of governments are enhanced when their institutions align with pre-existing indigenous structures, a finding that is congruent with the idea that institutional congruence can promote growth and development. Englebert's work, cited by Hopkins, supports this notion by demonstrating that high degrees of congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions can enhance a government's ability to foster development-inducing institutions. Bryant (2006, p.35) argues that the effectiveness of institutions is also contingent upon the broader social, cultural, and technological contexts in which they operate. This broader context can either facilitate or hinder the impact of institutions on development, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is complex and multifaceted. The interplay between institutions and cultural norms highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that drive long-run growth in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are not as simple as they may seem. The great divergence was well underway before 1800, which suggests that the differences in economic development between Europe and Asia were not solely due to the industrial revolution. This divergence was driven by complex factors, including institutional change, investment in agriculture, and high agricultural labour productivity. These factors were not uniform across regions, and the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth was period-specific. This perspective highlights the importance of considering the broader social, cultural, and technological contexts in which institutions operate. Austin (2008, p.25) posits that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic trajectory. This dynamic, partly endogenous, version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. The interplay between institutions and cultural norms highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that drive long-run growth in Africa.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The evolution of institutions in England during this period was pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, as these institutions facilitated the development of a stable market for public debt. This, in turn, provided a significant positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were key drivers of this process. These institutions not only reduced transaction costs but also enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, especially in contexts where cultural factors are often cited as primary drivers. However, North and Weingast's work suggests that the role of institutions in fostering economic growth is more nuanced than simply providing a stable environment for economic activity. The institutions that emerged in seventeenth-century England were not just passive facilitators but active shapers of economic outcomes, influencing the trajectory of economic development through their impact on property rights and financial markets.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.4) analysis of New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the distributional conflicts that favored the powerful, leading to enduring institutional structures that reinforced existing power dynamics. The interdependencies within these institutional frameworks, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes, further entrenched these structures, making them resistant to change. However, the dynamic nature of these conflicts suggests that over time, shifts in power dynamics could lead to institutional changes, albeit within a constrained framework that still favors the dominant groups. This dynamic interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional evolution underscores the complexities of long-run economic growth, where cultural factors and power struggles are inextricably linked to the formation and persistence of institutions.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe highlights how cultural beliefs and values underpin the formation of institutions. She argues that Catholic beliefs motivated medieval craftsmen to adhere to guild rules, demonstrating how cultural factors can influence economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This shows that cultural factors are deeply embedded in the institutional frameworks that support economic development. They illustrate how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutions and consequent falling transaction costs are critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This dynamic interplay between cultural factors and institutional evolution underscores the complexities of long-run economic growth, where cultural factors and power struggles are inextricably linked to the formation and persistence of institutions. Austin's work further supports this by noting that economic historians see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin also contends that the African story suggests a dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach, where Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic fortunes (Austin 2008, p.25). This shows that cultural factors evolve in response to economic and environmental changes. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This demonstrates how cultural factors can influence the establishment of institutions that have long-run economic effects. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show that the gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant impact on the dynamic of gender relations with long-term consequences for gender production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). They also show that Akan state formation intensified from the 17th century, as Akan groups, drawn into the dual international trading networks of the trans-Sahara and the trans-Atlantic, competed over mineral resources, trade routes, land and subjects (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This demonstrates how cultural factors can influence economic development and growth. The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions, which in turn drive long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement is whether cultural factors are static or dynamic. Austin shows that cultural factors are dynamic and evolve in response to economic and environmental changes (Austin 2008, p.25). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of the African story, which shows that Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed their economic fortunes.
