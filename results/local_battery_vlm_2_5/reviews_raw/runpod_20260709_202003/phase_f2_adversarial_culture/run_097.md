The substantive question at stake is the role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. The debate is fierce between those who argue that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, and those who contend that institutions are shaped by more material factors, such as geographic endowments or distributional conflicts. The former view aligns with those who emphasize the importance of social capital and civic cultures in fostering growth, while the latter includes scholars who focus on the extractive nature of institutions imposed by colonizers or the impact of mortality rates on institutional development. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing policies aimed at fostering growth in developing economies. The stakes are high, as different explanations lead to different policy prescriptions. For instance, if culture is the primary driver, then policies aimed at cultural change might be more effective. If material factors exert more influence, policies targeting these factors would be more prudent. The debate centers on the extent to which cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions and their efficiency, and whether this shaping is more influential than material factors. This question is crucial for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing effective policies aimed at fostering growth in developing economies. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North (1989, p.1) posits that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt, as the Bank of England began private operations shortly after its formation for intermediating public debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth is contingent on the broader economic and social context.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both argue that economic growth in Europe was fundamentally driven by the diffusion of ideas and knowledge. McCloskey posits that the economic changes in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were primarily driven by the rhetoric that fostered a new ideology, which in turn shaped new circumstances. Mokyr elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr explains that the diffusion of knowledge was facilitated by the ready transfer of ideas within Europe through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications. These channels enabled the rapid dissemination of useful knowledge, which was crucial for economic growth. The implication is that the economic growth in Europe was not just a result of institutional changes or interests but was deeply rooted in the cultural and ideological shifts that facilitated the spread of knowledge. This perspective suggests that culture, through the lens of ideas and ideologies, played a pivotal role in driving long-run growth. Mokyr also highlights the importance of tacit knowledge, which was often transmitted through personal interactions and training (Mokyr 2005, p.14). This qualification underscores the multifaceted nature of knowledge transfer, indicating that while formal institutions may not have been the primary drivers, the informal transmission of knowledge was equally critical. This dynamic interplay between formal and informal knowledge systems highlights the complexity of economic growth, suggesting that culture, through the dissemination of ideas and knowledge, was a fundamental driver of long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They demonstrate that high mortality rates in European colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. Specifically, they exploit differences in European mortality rates as a source of exogenous variation to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding underscores the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

They show that the institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists were not geared towards maximizing long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups that may have feared losing their political power if there was institutional development. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between political power and economic institutions, suggesting that the extractive nature of colonial institutions was a significant barrier to economic growth. These findings are consistent with the broader argument that the institutional legacy of colonialism has had a profound and lasting impact on economic development in former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) and Bryant (2006, p.35) show that New World societies with greater economic inequality developed institutions that advantaged elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is evident in the institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies, which were shaped by the unique factor endowments of the colonies. This institutional framework, which restricted competition and limited opportunities for social mobility, had long-lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions, as Bryant (2006, p.4) notes, is tied to the broader cultural and social structures that underpinned European modernity. Bryant (2006, p.4) argues that the European trajectory to modernity was facilitated by a range of factors, including legal-juridical developments, productive innovations, and the rise of bureaucratized polities. These factors collectively contributed to the institutional frameworks that supported economic growth in Europe. Bryant (2006, p.4) also points out that the European passage to modernity was marked by a secularizing dynamic that refashioned the institutional orders of Europe and the mentalities of its inhabitants. This secularizing dynamic, along with the broader cultural and social structures, played a crucial role in shaping the institutions that supported economic growth in Europe. The implications of these findings suggest that the role of culture in shaping institutions is not limited to the New World but extends to the broader context of European modernity, where cultural norms and social structures played a significant role in the development of institutions that supported economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts and endogenous factors rather than exogenous cultural influences. This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency concerns or cultural beliefs. Ogilvie highlights that distributional conflicts, which arise from the struggle over resources and power, played a crucial role in the development of these institutions. For instance, serfdom and guilds can be seen as mechanisms that redistributed resources and power among different social groups, thereby shaping the institutional landscape of Europe. Austin extends this argument by emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the role of endogenous factors, such as technological change and institutional evolution, was more complex and dynamic than previously thought. However, Austin's critique does not directly address the role of culture in shaping institutions, leaving open the question of how cultural norms and social structures might have influenced long-run growth in Europe. Moreover, Austin's emphasis on the dynamic nature of institutional evolution suggests that cultural factors, while not the primary drivers, could still have played a significant role in shaping the institutional landscape of Europe over time. This dynamic interplay between endogenous factors and cultural norms highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of culture in shaping institutions and supporting long-run growth.

The impact of the slave trade on African development highlights how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which in turn affect long-term economic and social outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade left a legacy of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and significantly impedes economic development. This mistrust is a persistent cultural norm that influences contemporary social capital. This political instability, rooted in the legacy of the slave trade, has weakened state institutions and fragmented governance structures, creating an environment where corruption thrives. Nunn (2008, p.1) identifies that slavery is a source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, which in turn undermines economic growth. This historical context of mistrust and political instability, fostered by the slave trade, has created a challenging environment for the development of strong, effective institutions that are crucial for sustained economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic impacts to shape the broader cultural and institutional landscape of Africa, with lasting implications for long-term development.

The role of culture in shaping institutions, and thereby influencing long-run economic growth, is a complex interplay that extends beyond immediate economic impacts. Culture influences the development of institutions through its impact on trust and social capital. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe highlights that cultural beliefs and values, such as those promoting trust and reciprocity, foster institutions that support economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This cultural influence is not limited to Europe; Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists and undermines the development of strong institutions crucial for sustained economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further complicates this relationship. Ogilvie acknowledges that institutions are interdependent and must be studied within their broader framework (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency is evident in North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The persistence of cultural influences on institutions over time is a critical factor in long-run economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s finding that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond immediate economic impacts to shape the broader cultural and institutional landscape of Africa, with lasting implications for long-term development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The disagreement is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values, associated with institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain superior economic performance (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the English financial revolution, which shows that institutions played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).
