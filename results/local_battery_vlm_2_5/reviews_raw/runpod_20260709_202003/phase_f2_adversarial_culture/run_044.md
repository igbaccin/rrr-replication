The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure and efficiency of economic institutions, thereby fostering growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of cultural norms on institutional frameworks, arguing that certain cultural beliefs can lead to more efficient institutions that promote economic growth. This position aligns with the idea that cultural factors, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency. Another position, however, posits that the relationship between culture and economic growth is more complex and indirect, mediated through other factors such as institutions and policy. This perspective suggests that while culture may influence institutions, it is the quality of these institutions that ultimately determines economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether culture is a primary driver of institutional efficiency and economic growth, or if it is merely one of many factors that contribute to a broader institutional framework. Understanding the role of culture in this context is crucial for grasping the mechanisms behind long-run economic growth and the potential for policy interventions to foster development. The live disagreement concerns the extent to which cultural beliefs directly shape institutions and economic outcomes, versus the extent to which these beliefs are one of many factors in a complex interplay of influences. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further elucidated by North and Weingast, who, along with North, highlight the significance of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England. The evolution of these institutions, particularly the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This institutional framework provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn facilitated the growth of a market for private debt. This financial revolution, as North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.1) describes, was critical to England's long-run economic success. The Bank of England's role in intermediating public debt created a large positive externality for private debt markets. As North and Weingast (1989, p.24) note, this institution allowed for the secure investment of capital, which was previously hoarded or hidden due to insecurity. The financial revolution thus played a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. The impact of these institutions varied. Jeffrey Williamson's work, cited by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), suggests that while these institutions were crucial, other forces might have eventually led to similar outcomes, albeit more slowly and less decisively. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in driving long-run economic growth.

Building on this, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. They establish that the mortality rates faced by Europeans in colonies influenced the type of institutions established. High mortality rates in colonies led to the creation of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This pattern is evident in the strong correlation between settler mortality and current institutions, as well as between early institutions and those in place today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions significantly affects current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era policies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further emphasize that these extractive institutions were designed to serve the interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework shaped by colonial powers has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, illustrating how institutional changes can override other factors in determining long-run economic outcomes. The institutional legacy of colonialism thus provides a compelling alternative explanation for the fundamental causes of economic growth, challenging the primacy of cultural determinants.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) contend that the influence of ideas and ideologies should be considered in driving economic change during the early modern period. McCloskey (2015, p.10) posits that intellectual shifts were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. Mokyr (2005, p.56) complements this view by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as a key factor in economic growth. This intellectual movement was not confined to a single nation but diffused widely across Europe, with personal communications and international conferences facilitating the spread of knowledge. The diffusion of ideas and knowledge across Europe underscores the importance of intellectual shifts in driving economic growth. Furthermore, the transmission of tacit knowledge, which is not easily codified, played a crucial role in this process. Mokyr (2005, p.56) notes that much of the useful knowledge in eighteenth-century Europe was tacit, relying on personal transmission and training. This highlights the significance of social networks and cultural factors in disseminating knowledge and fostering economic growth. The interplay between ideas, knowledge, and cultural factors thus emerges as a critical mechanism in understanding long-run economic growth.

This perspective is echoed by Austin, who re-evaluates historical economic theories to show that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, were pivotal in shaping pre-industrial European institutions. This perspective is shared by Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12), who also emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts in explaining the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds. Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the importance of dynamic, endogenous factors in institutional development. This dynamic is evident in Austin's analysis, which shows that African responses to their environments, rather than static geographical obstacles, drove economic changes. This focus on endogenous factors aligns with Ogilvie's emphasis on the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that institutions are not isolated entities but are part of a complex system. Ogilvie further elaborates on the interdependency of institutions, noting that the European family system and the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England were integral to economic growth. This interdependency implies that cultural factors, which influence the structure and equilibrium of collective action, play a crucial role in shaping institutions and, by extension, economic growth. Ogilvie's analysis of social capital and civic cultures illustrates how cultural beliefs and values can foster institutions that promote economic growth and political accountability. However, Ogilvie cautions that the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework may resist external influences, suggesting that cultural factors are deeply embedded and resistant to change. This resistance highlights the enduring influence of cultural factors on institutions and economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Broadberry and Gupta, build on this by examining how institutional factors and economic inequality shaped development paths in different regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). They argue that differences in institutions are crucial for understanding divergent economic growth trajectories. Specifically, Sokoloff and Engerman posit that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This mechanism is closely tied to the distribution of political power and human capital, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape. Broadberry and Gupta further illustrate this point by highlighting the early modern great divergence, where the prosperous parts of Asia between 1500 and 1800 resembled the stagnating parts of Europe rather than the developing northwestern regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.1). This divergence was driven by institutional factors that influenced investment in agriculture and labor productivity. The relationship between institutional factors and economic inequality is complex. Broadberry and Gupta note that higher agricultural labor productivity did not necessarily lead to an abundance of grain and low food prices, as labor moved out of agriculture into industry and services (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This qualification underscores that the effects of institutional factors on economic growth are not straightforward and depend on various contextual elements. The nuanced interplay between institutions and economic inequality highlights the enduring influence of these factors on long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, further illustrate this by arguing that the slave trade had profound and lasting effects on African development by shaping cultural attitudes, notably mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to a pervasive culture of mistrust that continues to influence contemporary social and economic outcomes. The historical trauma of the slave trade resulted in weakened states and political instability, which are linked to reduced economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). The negative relationship between slave exports and state centralization is consistent with the historical accounts of the slave trades causing long-term political instability, which resulted in weakened and fragmented states. The long-term effects of the slave trade are not limited to economic underdevelopment but also include a persistent culture of mistrust and political instability. This mistrust, in turn, affects broader social capital and interpersonal relationships, which are crucial for economic growth. However, Nunn's broader claim that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development is qualified by the understanding that the impact of the slave trade on contemporary development is multifaceted and not solely attributable to mistrust (Nunn 2008, p.1).

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both demonstrate that cultural beliefs and values can influence economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow guild rules, while Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These cultural factors are not just historical artifacts but have persistent effects on contemporary economic performance. The cultural approach, as exemplified by Putnam's theory of social capital, highlights that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, tend to develop institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the impact of institutions on economic performance is significant and measurable (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework further underscores the importance of culture in economic growth. Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. both note that institutions are interdependent and must be studied within their broader frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17).

The role of culture in dominating institutions is also evident in the context of Africa's slave trades. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural mistrust affects broader social capital and interpersonal relationships, which are crucial for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The long-term effects of the slave trade are not limited to economic underdevelopment but also include a persistent culture of mistrust and political instability. This mistrust, in turn, affects broader social capital and interpersonal relationships, which are crucial for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The impact of the slave trade on contemporary development is multifaceted and not solely attributable to mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). Austin supports this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the dynamic and partly endogenous nature of economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). The historical evidence of the slave trade and its effects on contemporary development provides a clear example of how culture shapes institutions and economic growth.

The specific historical evidence of the slave trade and its effects on contemporary development settles the disagreement between the multifaceted impact of the slave trade and the role of culture in shaping institutions. The measurement of contemporary trust levels and the historical analysis of the slave trade's impact on African societies show that culture plays a critical role in dominating institutions and long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Austin 2008, p.25).
