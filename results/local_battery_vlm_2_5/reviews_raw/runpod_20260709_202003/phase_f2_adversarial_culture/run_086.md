The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in the literature. The stakes are high in this debate, as it directly impacts how we understand the drivers of economic development and the potential for policy intervention. The disagreement centers around whether cultural factors shape institutions in ways that promote or hinder growth, or whether institutions are primarily shaped by other factors, such as geography or economic efficiency. One position argues that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure of economic institutions, thereby affecting economic efficiency and growth. This perspective is often associated with theories that emphasize the role of social capital and civic cultures. Another position contends that institutions are shaped by more fundamental factors, such as the need for secure property rights and the reduction of transaction costs. This view is often aligned with institutional theories that focus on the political and economic determinants of institutional change. The tension between these positions is evident in the debate over whether cultural factors or institutional efficiency are the primary drivers of long-run growth. A closer look at the literature reveals that the role of culture in dominating institutions is not straightforward, and that the relationship between culture, institutions, and economic growth is complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Nunn and Wantchekon establish that the slave trade in Africa led to the development of a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Akyeampong and Fofack concur that this culture of mistrust has persisted and significantly impacted trust levels (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The slave trade's historical impact on African societies is both economic and deeply cultural. The mistrust that developed due to the slave trade has had long-term effects on social cohesion and economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack document how colonial constructs and gender roles have also shaped African societies. The gendered division of labor, established during the colonial period, continues to influence economic production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The slave trade's impact on trust is particularly pronounced in regions that were heavily affected by it, and this mistrust has become deeply ingrained in the social fabric. These cultural attitudes, shaped by historical events, have lasting implications for economic development and social relations. This enduring mistrust and its implications for social capital are particularly salient in regions where the slave trade was most intense.

North (1989, p.1) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) agreed that ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. North (1989, p.1) argued that institutions matter in economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. One specific mechanism through which this occurs is the role of cultural beliefs in shaping collective action and social norms. This pattern of cultural influence on institutions underscores how ideologies can drive economic change. The impact of cultural beliefs on economic institutions varies across societies. McCloskey (2015, p.10) noted that the cultural struggle in France, characterized by the "Franco-French War," highlights how deeply ingrained cultural attitudes can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. This shows that the effectiveness of cultural beliefs in driving economic change depends on the specific historical and social context in which they operate.

North and Weingast, along with Acemoglu et al., argue that institutions governing public choice are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They propose that the evolution of institutions in England during the 17th century played a pivotal role in ensuring the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast note that the financial revolution in England provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, highlighting the interdependence between public and private financial markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework secured property rights and created an environment conducive to economic growth by fostering a stable market for public debt. Acemoglu et al. extend this argument by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This pattern underscores how initial institutional conditions, shaped by exogenous factors, can have long-lasting effects on economic performance. Sokoloff and Engerman qualify this view by emphasizing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This implies that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be mediated by the socio-economic context in which they operate.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Bryant (Austin 2008, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.35) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical paths of economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the institutionalist approach for its difficulty in quantitatively testing causal mechanisms and its inability to explain why some countries adopted growth-favorable institutions while others did not (Austin 2008, p.2). This criticism highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic development paths that consider the complex interplay of institutions and exogenous factors. Bryant (2006, p.35) supports this view by emphasizing the importance of cultural norms and social relations in shaping economic transitions (Bryant 2006, p.35). This perspective underscores the role of endogenous factors, such as technological advancements and institutional changes, in driving economic growth. This nuanced understanding is crucial for comprehending the long-term impact of institutions on economic growth, as it recognizes the multifaceted nature of development paths. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by noting that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) call the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic view challenges the idea of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development. Bryant (2006, p.35) qualifies this by arguing that any attempt to overturn the reigning comprehension of the key determinants in the European trajectory would appear to be an audacious, unpromising venture (Bryant 2006, p.4). This qualification suggests that while endogenous factors are important, they must be considered within the broader context of historical and cultural determinants.

van Zanden (1999, p.2); (Pamuk 2007, p.24; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15) examines economic divergence and historical development, focusing on factors such as the impact of the Black Death and institutional changes. These scholars find that the economic performance of Europe and Asia during the early modern period was more complex than previously thought. Van Zanden (1999, p.2) argues that the economic development of Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not follow a linear path of continuous growth, as suggested by some studies. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15), who contend that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. They highlight that the prosperous parts of Asia during this period resembled the stagnating regions of Europe. This divergence resulted from high levels of investment in agriculture and institutional changes that led to increased agricultural labor productivity. These changes also ushered in an era of labor-saving technological innovations. This pattern suggests that institutional changes and structural shifts, such as those initiated by the Black Death, played a crucial role in the economic divergence between Europe and Asia. The role of culture in these long-term structural changes remains underexplored, leaving open the question of how cultural factors might have influenced these institutional and technological developments.

Goldin and other scholars (Goldin 2006, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) examine regional economic and social changes, focusing on how these transformations influence long-run growth. Goldin (2006, p.8) shows that exogenous changes, such as shifts in demand for female office workers and advancements in educational institutions, significantly altered female labor supply and occupational structures. These changes were driven by specific historical events and technological advancements. Hopkins (2009, p.8) extends this analysis by highlighting the importance of institutional variables in economic development. He argues that current institutions are shaped by a long history, including precolonial and prehistory periods, and that effective institutions are those congruent with indigenous ones. This congruence enhances the legitimacy of governments and fosters developmental institutions, which in turn promote growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) provide a nuanced view by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. They attribute this growth to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The downstream implication of these findings is that regional economic and social transformations, driven by exogenous changes and institutional congruence, can significantly influence long-run growth. This variability suggests that while regional changes can drive growth, their effectiveness depends on specific local conditions and institutional frameworks.

Stern (2004, p.3) and van Zanden (2009, p.24) jointly assert that environmental and technological factors in economic growth do not directly address the role of culture in shaping long-run growth (Stern 2004, p.3; van Zanden 2009, p.24). They both explore the impacts of technological change and population growth on economic growth, but they do not explicitly examine how cultural factors might influence these dynamics. This focus on human capital and technological change underscores the importance of economic and environmental factors but does not delve into the cultural underpinnings that might shape these factors. This omission is significant because cultural norms and values can influence how societies invest in human capital and adopt new technologies. For instance, Hornbeck (2010, p.6) notes that social norms can encourage behaviors that support economic development, such as ranchers controlling cattle to protect property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.6). This highlights the potential for cultural factors to play a role in economic growth, even if the literature on environmental and technological factors does not explicitly address this role. The variability in the effectiveness of these norms suggests that cultural influences on economic growth are complex and context-dependent.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) highlight that the growth of useful knowledge is pivotal for economic growth, emphasizing the role of technological advancement and social competence. Mokyr (2005, p.56) argues that the diffusion of knowledge, both codified and tacit, facilitates the spread of innovations. This diffusion is essential for fostering economic development, as it allows nations to adopt and implement new technologies effectively. Mokyr (2005, p.14) elaborates on this by noting that tacit knowledge, which is often embodied in skills and personal transmission, plays a crucial role in technological advancement. This tacit knowledge can be more challenging to codify and disseminate, yet it remains essential for economic progress. Historical accounts indicate that the slave trades in Africa led to long-term political instability, which weakened state structures and hindered economic performance (Nunn 2008, p.27). This historical context illustrates how social and institutional factors can impede the diffusion of useful knowledge, underscoring the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth. Furthermore, Abramovitz (1986, p.21) points out that social competence for exploiting advanced technologies was limited in earlier periods. This limitation suggests that the effectiveness of technological adoption is influenced by the social and institutional context. The diffusion of useful knowledge is deeply intertwined with cultural and institutional factors that facilitate or hinder economic growth.

This revolution was characterized by a significant increase in labor input and the emergence of new consumption patterns, which drove economic growth by fostering a more productive and integrated market. The increased labor input during this period was driven by cultural and social factors that encouraged greater participation in the labor market. This heightened productivity facilitated the accumulation of capital, which in turn supported industrial growth. The new consumption patterns that emerged during this period also played a crucial role in stimulating economic activity. As people began to consume more goods and services, demand increased, driving further production and economic growth. This dynamic highlights the interconnectedness of cultural, social, and economic factors in shaping long-run growth. The Industrious Revolution's impact on industrialization underscores the importance of considering cultural and social factors in understanding economic development. The shift in consumption patterns and increased labor input during this period were deeply intertwined with the broader economic and social transformations that characterized the era. This interplay between cultural, social, and economic factors illustrates the complex nature of economic growth and the need for a holistic approach to understanding its drivers.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence economic institutions and efficiency. She argues that "cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations of strong guilds and communes, explain the superior economic performance of northern compared to southern Italy since medieval times." (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect the security of property rights and the cost of transacting (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further emphasize the role of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the financial revolution in England as a critical factor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These arguments collectively underscore the importance of cultural and institutional frameworks in driving economic development. The evolution of these frameworks, shaped by cultural beliefs and institutional designs, is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The interplay between culture and institutions is further illustrated by the role of social capital. Ogilvie discusses Putnam's theory of social capital, which posits that features like trust, norms, and networks facilitate coordinated actions and improve societal efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work suggests that the dynamic, partly endogenous responses to environments, making use of various technologies when available, ultimately changed the economic landscape (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay between cultural beliefs, institutional frameworks, and economic responses highlights the complex nature of economic growth and the need for a holistic approach. The historical evidence shows that cultural and institutional frameworks evolve over time, shaping economic development in profound ways. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the role of the financial revolution in England, which provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This historical case illustrates how institutions, shaped by cultural beliefs and values, can drive economic growth and development.
