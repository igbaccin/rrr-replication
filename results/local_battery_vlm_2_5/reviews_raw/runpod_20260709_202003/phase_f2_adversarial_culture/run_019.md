The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the extent to which cultural factors, such as norms, values, and beliefs, can influence the formation and evolution of institutions that promote economic growth. One key debate centers on whether cultural factors are exogenous or endogenous to the institutional development process. On one side, scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values can shape economic institutions and efficiency, thereby influencing long-run growth. On the other side, scholars posit that institutions themselves are the primary drivers of economic growth, with culture playing a secondary or even negligible role. This disagreement underscores the need to understand how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to drive economic development. The interplay between culture and institutions is crucial for comprehending the mechanisms that underpin long-run growth. This is because institutions that facilitate economic growth are often the product of complex interactions between cultural norms and historical contexts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs, which is critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights, which is a key dimension of long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This financial revolution underscores how institutions can play a necessary role in making possible economic growth and political freedom. The evolution of these institutions was not straightforward. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) acknowledge that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in driving economic growth, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, their impact is contingent on broader historical and economic contexts.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with a key mechanism being the differential mortality rates faced by European colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These mortality rates influenced the types of institutions established in colonies, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonizers rather than promoting long-run economic growth. This institutional framework, shaped by the extractive interests of the colonizers, persisted over time and significantly impacted current economic performance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. show that there is a strong correlation between settler mortality and the current institutional framework, which in turn affects income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). The persistence of these institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that the design of these institutions was not aimed at maximizing long-run growth but rather at securing the interests of the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy, shaped by colonial experiences, continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. The persistence of these institutions, however, does not imply that they are immutable; rather, they are subject to change driven by shifts in power dynamics and economic conditions, as evidenced by the industrialization and economic growth experienced by some former colonies.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting long-term economic development by reducing trust within society (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade was a significant factor in the development of a culture of mistrust, which continues to affect economic outcomes. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and eroded trust among communities. Nunn elaborates on this by highlighting that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. This political instability further exacerbated the economic challenges faced by African societies, as it hindered the development of strong, centralized institutions that could foster economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade is evident in the current economic underdevelopment of many African countries, where the historical trauma of slavery continues to influence contemporary economic and political structures. The persistence of this mistrust and political instability underscores the profound and enduring impact of the slave trade on African economic development, shaping the institutional landscape in ways that continue to hinder growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) assert that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that the "Great Enrichment" was driven by a new rhetoric that fostered economic growth, highlighting that the cultural struggle in France and Spain, for instance, hindered economic growth due to conservative reasons. Mokyr (2005, p.56) elaborates on this point by noting that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth, and that this knowledge was readily diffused within the world in which the Enlightenment took root. Mokyr (2005, p.56) explains that much of the knowledge that counted was embodied in implicit forms such as skills and tacit knowledge. This tacit knowledge moved about through personal transmission and training, underscoring the importance of cultural and intellectual shifts in driving economic growth. This qualification suggests that while ideas and ideologies were crucial, the institutional and cultural context in which they operated also played a significant role in shaping economic growth. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between ideas, culture, and institutions in driving long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both explore how distributional conflicts and institutional arrangements shape economic outcomes, but they do not directly address how culture influences long-run growth. These authors focus on the role of institutions in mediating conflicts over resource distribution, with Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlighting how institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by these conflicts rather than purely by efficiency considerations. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and economic dynamics that underpin institutional development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate on this by showing how inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) qualifies this by noting the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic outcomes is not straightforward but rather part of a complex system of interlinked institutions. This complexity indicates that while distributional conflicts are crucial in shaping institutions, their effect on long-run growth is mediated through a web of institutional interactions.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis and historical paths complicate causal explanations by suggesting that different periods and paths are at play (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This divergence is not merely a result of geographical factors but also reflects dynamic, endogenous responses to environments and technological availability. These responses ultimately changed the economic landscape, highlighting the role of institutional evolution and investment in shaping economic growth. This implies that while institutions play a crucial role in economic development, their effects are mediated through complex interactions with other factors, including cultural and technological responses.

Culture plays a significant role in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie argues that cultural beliefs and values, such as those espoused by Putnam's theory of 'social capital', can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. This perspective is supported by the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework, as highlighted by Ogilvie. This interdependency is crucial because it shows that institutions are not isolated entities but are part of a complex system that includes cultural and technological responses (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). North and Weingast underscore this point by demonstrating how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). Moreover, North's work emphasizes the critical role of institutions in economic growth, particularly in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which are essential for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). These findings collectively show that cultural factors, by shaping institutional frameworks, are fundamental to long-run economic growth.

The role of culture in institutional development is further illuminated by the impact of cultural beliefs on economic outcomes. Ogilvie discusses how Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow efficient rules, while the Protestant Reformation led to the decline of guilds, highlighting the influence of cultural beliefs on institutional structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural mistrust has profound implications for institutional development and economic growth, as it affects the ability of societies to coordinate and cooperate effectively. The shared finding is that cultural beliefs and values are deeply embedded in institutional frameworks and significantly influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The historical and empirical evidence shows that institutions matter in economic growth, and cultural factors are a critical component of this relationship. North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England illustrates how institutional changes, driven by cultural and political factors, led to secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This is further supported by North's work, which emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, both of which are essential for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.1; North 1989, p.9). Austin qualifies this by noting that the relationship between a particular set of institutions and economic growth is period-specific, suggesting that the impact of institutions on long-run growth is not uniform across different historical contexts (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.25). The specific historical evidence and empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the financial revolution in England, which played a critical role in long-run economic success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical case demonstrates the crucial role of cultural and institutional factors in shaping economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between culture, institutions, and economic development.
