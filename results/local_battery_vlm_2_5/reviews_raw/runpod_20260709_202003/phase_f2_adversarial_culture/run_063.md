The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the extent to which cultural beliefs and values shape the institutions that drive economic growth. Two competing positions emerge from the literature. The first position argues that cultural beliefs and values can directly influence the structure of economic institutions and thereby affect economic efficiency. The second position posits that the institutions governing public choice and secure property rights are the primary drivers of economic growth. These positions conflict over whether cultural factors are integral to the institutional frameworks that foster long-run growth, or whether institutions operate independently of cultural influences. Understanding this debate is crucial for grasping how cultural factors interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic development over the long term. This interplay is pivotal in determining whether cultural beliefs and values can be harnessed to promote economic growth or if they are merely epiphenomenal to the institutional structures that truly matter. The interplay between culture and institutions has been a subject of interest, with scholars investigating how cultural beliefs and values might influence economic institutions and, consequently, economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) argue that gender-based colonial codifications of economic roles significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). In the pre-colonial Akan society, women were crucial to the reproduction of communities, as their bloodlines defined the transmission of both office and wealth in this matrilineal society. This pattern was disrupted by colonial codifications that imposed new gender roles and altered production dynamics, which may explain why some economic dependency models, such as those that find breadwinner wives, continue to be relevant. One such model is Goldin's (2006) "quiet revolution" which describes how women's employment, education, and family dynamics transformed in the U.S. The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, which made "nice" jobs available, and the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. These changes in women's roles, however, were not uniform across societies. For example, in the Akan society, women's roles were deeply entrenched in the cultural fabric, and their economic contributions were significant. The role of women in economic growth and development highlights how cultural constructs, such as gender-based colonial codifications, significantly influence long-term economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and van Zanden (1999, p.2) share a common focus on the complexities of economic growth, highlighting that historical patterns of economic development are influenced by a variety of factors, including institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience, which can be shaped by cultural norms. They argue that economic growth is not merely a result of secure private property rights, but rather a dynamic process shaped by endogenous responses to environmental and technological changes. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) qualify this understanding by noting that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, indicating that institutional changes and investment in agriculture played a crucial role in shaping economic development. This highlights the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs.

The new economic history of Africa suggests that institutional variables play a crucial role in explaining development, but this perspective does not directly address how culture influences long-run growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that institutional change is gradual and calls for historical investigation because current institutions are the product of a past that may extend beyond the short period of colonial rule into precolonial history and even into prehistory. This approach highlights the importance of understanding precolonial institutions in Africa. However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) also notes that other social scientists have looked further into the past, with political scientist Pierre Englebert arguing that contemporary institutions are more likely to be effective in promoting growth if they are congruent with indigenous institutions. This congruence underlines the legitimacy of governments and enhances their ability to foster institutions that induce development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth is not only about their congruence with indigenous institutions but also about their alignment with broader economic factors. Understanding this interplay is crucial for designing effective institutional reforms that can promote long-run growth in Africa.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental cause of long-run economic development, challenging the notion that culture dominates institutions in this context (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) posit that the differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions has a profound impact on economic development, independent of cultural factors. This dynamic is evident in the strong first-stage relationship between settler mortality and current institutions, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.17). However, the relationship between colonial institutions and long-run growth is not straightforward. This implies that the institutional framework established during colonial times may have been inherently designed to exploit resources rather than foster sustainable growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than cultural factors, are the primary drivers of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie asserts that distributional conflicts are a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These distributional conflicts are driven by the interests of powerful elites who shape institutions to maintain their dominant position. This dynamic is evident in the development of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft and merchant guilds. These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework, influenced by distributional conflicts, had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of inequality and the lack of competition meant that these societies were less dynamic and less innovative, hindering long-term economic growth. The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is recognized by some studies of European economic institutions. This interdependency suggests that the entire institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, played a crucial role in economic development. However, Ogilvie acknowledges the complexity of institutional frameworks, stating that "we cannot study one institution in isolation" (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This highlights the need for a holistic approach to understanding how institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, influence long-run growth.

North & Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The development of institutions such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution provided a stable market for public debt, which in turn supported the growth of a market for private debt, a large and positive externality (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework not only ensured economic growth but also laid the groundwork for political freedom. The implications of this institutional evolution are profound, as it underscores the necessity of secure property rights and efficient financial systems for long-term economic development. This perspective challenges views that attribute economic development primarily to cultural factors, highlighting the critical role of institutional evolution in shaping economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further complicated by the interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights that different institutional frameworks may resist cultural encroachment, suggesting an interdependency between individual institutions and broader cultural contexts (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency is also recognized in the context of European economic institutions, where the family system, labour markets, and credit markets were interlinked, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting a more dynamic and endogenous version of the 'sophisticated geography' approach (Austin 2008, p.2). This dynamic interplay between culture and institutions is evident in the African context, where African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The gender-based colonial constructs and codification of gender roles had significant long-term consequences for gender production and economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping institutional frameworks that support economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.1). However, the precise role of culture in dominating institutions remains a subject of disagreement.
