The role of culture in dominating institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding economic development. Central to this debate is the question of whether cultural factors drive the establishment and persistence of institutions that foster or hinder economic growth. On one side, scholars argue that cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions, influencing efficiency and growth. On the other side, scholars propose that institutions are primarily shaped by other factors, such as geographical conditions or extractive colonial practices, and that cultural influences are secondary or even irrelevant. The conflict arises from differing views on the primacy of cultural factors in institutional development and their impact on long-run economic growth. For instance, some scholars contend that cultural beliefs about the afterlife influenced the rules of guilds in medieval Europe, while others posit that the extractive institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. The debate hinges on whether cultural factors are a necessary component of institutional frameworks that drive economic growth or if they are mere epiphenomena of other more fundamental causes. The stakes are high, as resolving this debate could provide a clearer path for economic development policies. This discussion begins with the role of culture in shaping institutions and their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.1). This institutional development was driven by a series of fiscal crises that led to a reconfiguration of political and economic power. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional shift was a political maneuver that involved trading certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, responding to economic pressures (North 1989, p.9). The resulting financial revolution provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further solidifying the economic framework (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Jeffrey Williamson's work supports this thesis, highlighting that these institutional changes were necessary for England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). North and Weingast acknowledge that these institutions were critical, while other forces might have eventually led to success, albeit more slowly and less decisively (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This institutional perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and efficient financial systems in driving economic growth, suggesting that cultural factors, while influential, may not be the primary drivers of long-run economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlights that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds. These institutions were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts among different social groups, responding efficiently to economic conditions. For example, the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe can be attributed to the strength of landowning elites who used their political power to maintain a system that benefited them economically. This perspective challenges the notion that culture alone drove long-run growth, as it shows that institutional arrangements were often the result of power struggles and distributional conflicts. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this idea to New World societies, where initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality led to institutions that favored elites and restricted competition. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) cautions that these institutions were interdependent within a broader framework, suggesting that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions was complex and multifaceted. The interdependency of institutions means that studying one institution in isolation may not fully capture the dynamics at play.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Bryant (2006, p.35) share that ideas and ideologies were key drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, as the cultural norms and mentalities of inhabitants were reshaped by the secularizing dynamic that followed the fissure within Latin Christendom (Bryant 2006, p.35; McCloskey 2015, p.10). The shift in ideas facilitated a multi-faceted dynamic that refashioned the institutional orders of Europe and the mentalities of its inhabitants. This dynamic was a complex interplay of cultural and social factors that enabled the transition to industrial modes of production, not merely a matter of calculation or elite interests. Bryant (2006, p.35) warns that the revisionist preference for restricting effective or salient causality to shorter-term and immediate processes is prone to explanatory distortions, cautioning that effective causality is not limited to shorter-term processes. This caution underscores the importance of considering the long-term accumulation of resources and opportunities that enabled the phased conversion to industrial modes of production. The enabling technologies, institutions, social relations, and cultural norms that facilitated this transition were mediated inheritances from the past, highlighting the interconnectedness of cultural and institutional factors in driving economic change. These insights suggest that the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth involves a complex interplay of ideas, institutions, and long-term historical processes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies are a key determinant of the institutions established, which in turn shape current economic performance. This mechanism hinges on the fact that high mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions over time has significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that there is a strong correlation between settler mortality, early institutions, and current institutions, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies. The institutional framework established during colonial times thus plays a crucial role in determining long-run economic outcomes, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions is a significant factor in the economic performance of former colonies today.

The slave trade's impact on African development illuminates how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which persist and influence long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon identify the slave trade as a catalyst for developing a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect trust levels today. This cultural shift is a persistent factor that hinders economic development, not merely a historical artifact. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn both highlight how the slave trade led to political instability and weakened state institutions. Nunn further underscores that this institutional fragility is a direct consequence of the slave trade's legacy, creating a vicious cycle where mistrust and weak institutions reinforce each other. The downstream implication of this is significant: the legacy of the slave trade, through its impact on cultural attitudes and institutional development, continues to shape Africa's economic landscape. This historical trauma set in motion a chain of events that continues to influence contemporary development trajectories, affecting immediate economic outcomes.

The role of women in economic development intersects with cultural and institutional factors, contributing to the dynamics of long-run growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Goldin 2006, p.8). Akyeampong and Fofack note that African women played a crucial role in economic growth and development, both pre-colonially and during the colonial period. The gendered colonial constructs and codification of gender roles significantly impacted gender relations, with long-term consequences for gender production. This dynamic is evident in Akan state formation, where women's roles were indispensable to community reproduction, as "If my sex die in the clan then that very clan becomes extinct" (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). The sexual division of labor established during the colonial period reflects the enduring influence of pre-colonial social norms and cultural beliefs. This legacy of women's economic contributions suggests that cultural attitudes toward gender roles can shape institutional development and economic trajectories. Goldin's analysis of the U.S. reveals that exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and shifts in household production technology, altered female labor supply and occupational patterns. These changes were preconditions for broader shifts in social norms and expectations regarding women's family and career roles (Goldin 2006, p.8). The transformation in women's employment and education was not uniform across all sectors or regions, highlighting the complex interplay between cultural attitudes, institutional changes, and economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.3). This nuanced understanding of women's roles in economic development underscores the importance of considering cultural factors in long-run growth trajectories.

Hornbeck (2010, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.24) focus on the technological and structural changes that influenced economic growth, rather than directly addressing how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.6; Pamuk 2007, p.24). This mechanism underscores the importance of technological advancements in shaping economic outcomes, as they can alter the incentive structures that govern resource allocation and productivity. Pamuk (2007, p.24) highlights that the Black Death brought about greater mobility, flexibility, and institutional change to the guilds, and contributed to the rise of rural industries. Scarcity and the high cost of labour ushered in an era of labour-saving technological innovations (Pamuk 2007, p.24). These structural changes, driven by demographic shifts and technological adaptations, illustrate how exogenous shocks can catalyze economic transformations. The role of culture in these transformations remains less explicit. While Hornbeck (2010, p.6) acknowledges the influence of social norms in property management, the broader cultural context that shapes these norms is not fully explored. This gap suggests that while technological and structural changes are crucial for economic growth, the cultural underpinnings that influence these changes deserve closer examination.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) together show that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are rooted in institutional evolution, investment patterns, and policy experiences. The 'sophisticated geography' approach acknowledges that environmental factors interact with endogenous responses, shaping economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) contends that African responses to their environments, leveraging available technologies, ultimately altered economic trajectories. This dynamic interaction suggests that cultural factors, influencing how societies respond to their environments, play a crucial role in long-run economic growth. This pattern highlights how cultural attitudes towards investment and institutional frameworks can catalyze economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that high agricultural productivity did not necessarily lead to abundant grain and low food prices, as labour shifted into industry and services. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between cultural factors, institutional changes, and economic outcomes, suggesting that cultural attitudes towards labour and industry are pivotal in shaping long-run growth.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is multifaceted. Institutions that foster economic growth, such as secure property rights, are deeply intertwined with cultural attitudes and social norms. For instance, Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated economic institutions in medieval Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This relationship is further exemplified by Putnam's theory of social capital, which argues that societies with high social capital, characterized by trust and norms of reciprocity, tend to have institutions that favor economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These cultural beliefs and values, along with their associated institutional manifestations, explain the superior economic performance of certain regions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework underscores the complexity of this relationship. For example, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision systems, all of which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural attitudes towards investment, labor, and industry are pivotal in shaping long-run growth. Moreover, Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of cultural and institutional frameworks on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions over time underscores the profound influence of cultural factors on long-run growth.

The significance of cultural attitudes in shaping economic institutions is further supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contrary to neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast extend this argument by showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, both of which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of cultural attitudes and institutional frameworks in driving long-run economic development.

The role of culture in dominating institutions is further illustrated by the contributions of African women to economic growth and development. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender roles and cultural norms in shaping economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). This perspective is supported by the historical evidence of Akan state formation, where women remained indispensable to the reproduction of communities, underscoring the cultural and economic significance of gender roles (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.6). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further emphasizes the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic development, challenging simplistic causal explanations and highlighting the complex interplay between cultural factors, institutional changes, and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2).

In sum, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the enduring impact of cultural beliefs and values on economic institutions. The underlying point of agreement is that cultural attitudes and social norms significantly influence the establishment and evolution of institutions that foster economic growth (North 1989, p.1). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of cultural factors is primarily through the establishment of extractive institutions or through the creation of institutions that foster secure property rights and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the enduring impact of cultural beliefs and values on economic institutions, as exemplified by the superior economic performance of regions with high social capital and the significant contributions of African women to economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4).
