The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over the long term, and whether cultural factors are primary drivers or secondary influences. One position holds that culture shapes institutions, which in turn govern economic outcomes. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of cultural beliefs and values on institutional frameworks, suggesting that cultural factors can influence economic growth by altering the structure and efficiency of collective action. The opposing position argues that institutions are shaped by other factors, such as geographic conditions or political dynamics, and that culture plays a secondary role. This debate is crucial for policymakers and scholars aiming to understand the deep-seated factors that drive economic development. The disagreement hinges on whether cultural factors are the primary drivers of institutional change or whether they are influenced by other, more immediate causes. This question is central to the broader inquiry into the mechanisms of long-run economic growth and the role of institutions in facilitating or hindering development. The debate over the primacy of culture in shaping institutions is ongoing, with scholars presenting evidence from various historical and contemporary contexts to support their claims. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) argue that institutions introduced during colonialism have significantly affected current income levels per capita. European mortality rates in colonies, which were influenced by factors such as disease and climate, shaped the type of institutions established by European colonizers, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have persisted and continue to influence economic outcomes today (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions, even when they are not conducive to economic development, underscores their enduring impact on economic performance. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism is a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth, independent of cultural influences.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) argue that the fundamental cause of long-run growth is the growth of useful knowledge and ideas, not institutions or interests. McCloskey explains that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective is supported by Mokyr, who argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr demonstrates that the diffusion of knowledge was not constrained by national boundaries within the Western world. This suggests that the spread of ideas and knowledge facilitated economic growth across different nations. McCloskey adds that the cultural struggle in France, for example, led to a new rhetoric that made new circumstances, which then again made new rhetoric, and the Great Enrichment came. The implication is that the ideas themselves, not just the institutions or interests, played a pivotal role in driving economic change. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that much of the useful knowledge was tacit and embedded in skills, making it dependent on personal transmission and training. This highlights the importance of social and cultural factors in the dissemination of knowledge. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is thus significantly qualified by the need for social and cultural factors to facilitate the spread of ideas and knowledge.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These conflicts are driven by the struggle for control over resources and power among different social groups. The institutions that emerge from these conflicts often serve to entrench the interests of the dominant groups, even if they are not efficient. For instance, guilds in pre-industrial Europe were about controlling access to markets and resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, can persist over time, influencing long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions presents challenges. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework can make it difficult to change any single institution without affecting the entire system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interdependency suggests that cultural factors, which influence the broader institutional framework, play a crucial role in determining the long-run growth trajectory of an economy.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) highlight different historical paths and institutional evolutions, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia are complex phenomena that cannot be attributed to a single factor. This thesis suggests that the institutional frameworks of different regions evolved in response to unique historical contexts, with cultural factors playing a significant role in shaping these institutions. This divergence is evident in the differing economic development trajectories of Europe and Asia, with Europe experiencing significant institutional changes that led to investment in agriculture and industry. This investment, in turn, resulted in high agricultural labor productivity and economic growth. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, and the adoption of growth-enhancing institutions varies across countries. This variation suggests that cultural factors, which influence the broader institutional framework, play a crucial role in determining the long-run growth trajectory of an economy. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the African story suggests a more dynamic, partly endogenous, version of what Acemoglu et al. call the 'sophisticated geography' approach. This approach highlights the role of Africans' own responses to their environments, making use of various technologies when available, in shaping economic development. This dynamic interplay between culture, institutions, and economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the cultural factors that dominate institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. Their perspective highlights the critical role of institutional evolution in shaping long-term economic development. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note that the financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs. This reduction was pivotal in enhancing the security of property rights, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated economic growth and supported political freedom. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by explaining that the financial revolution led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional stability provided a large and positive externality for the development of a market for private debt, further bolstering economic growth. The implications of these institutional changes are nuanced. North and Weingast (1989, p.24) acknowledge that while the financial revolution played a critical role, other forces could have led to England's success, albeit more slowly and less decisively. This suggests that while institutions are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own; cultural and other factors may also play significant roles.

The slave trade's impact on African development illustrates how historical events can shape cultural attitudes, such as mistrust, which then influence long-term economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.27). This cultural shift is a critical mechanism through which the slave trade has had a lasting impact on African development. Nunn and Wantchekon's work shows that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This cultural mistrust can undermine social capital, which is essential for economic growth and development. The relationship between historical events and cultural attitudes is complex, and other factors may also contribute to the persistence of mistrust and its economic implications. For instance, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) acknowledge that while the slave trade had a significant impact on trust, other historical factors, such as colonial rule and malaria transmission, also played a role in shaping contemporary African development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). This suggests that while the slave trade and the resulting cultural mistrust are significant factors, they are not the sole determinants of long-term economic outcomes.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is deeply intertwined with the development of economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts are a better explanation for pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions than efficiency, showing how cultural beliefs and values influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Putnam's theory of 'social capital' emphasizes that cultural beliefs and values, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect the security of property rights and the cost of transacting, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further illustrate this by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). These studies collectively demonstrate that cultural attitudes and institutions are interconnected, with cultural beliefs shaping the institutional framework that underpins economic growth.
