The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether cultural factors are fundamental causes or merely epiphenomena. At the heart of this debate lie two competing views. One position posits that cultural beliefs and values directly influence the formation of institutions, which in turn shape economic outcomes. Proponents of this view argue that cultural factors, such as trust and norms of reciprocity, can enhance economic efficiency and growth by fostering institutions that support coordinated action and accountability. The opposing view contends that cultural factors are secondary to other determinants, such as geographic endowments or institutional frameworks, which are the primary drivers of economic growth. This perspective suggests that cultural beliefs may be endogenous to institutional and economic structures, and that they are causes. The stakes are high: if cultural factors are indeed fundamental causes, then policies aimed at fostering economic growth must address cultural dimensions. If cultural factors are incidental, then policy efforts should focus on other determinants. The debate over the role of culture in long-run economic growth hinges on whether cultural factors are exogenous and causally prior to institutions, or if they are endogenous and shaped by other factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “(Here Max Weber was right on.)'54 Exogenous cultural theories are also applied on the micro-level, with Richardson contending that Catholic beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the 'efﬁcient' rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline.” Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “Determinants of whether Europeans could go and settle in the colonies, therefore, have an important effect on institutions today.”

North and Weingast, alongside North, argue that institutions are pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.1)(North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They posit that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was instrumental in this process. The financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, played a critical role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional development was a driving force that catalyzed long-term economic growth, and it was a response to existing economic conditions. North highlights that the neoclassical model, which emphasizes population and savings, overlooks the significance of institutions when transaction costs are positive (North 1989, p.1). North and Weingast further elaborate that the institutions governing public choice, such as those facilitating the market for public debt, provided significant externalities for the development of private debt markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This institutional framework supported economic growth and underpinned political freedom, creating a virtuous cycle where secure property rights and economic prosperity reinforced each other. This nuanced view underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, suggesting that while institutions are crucial, they are part of a broader ecosystem of factors influencing long-run growth.

Distributional conflicts were a key driver of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, and cultural factors were not. Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The craft guilds and merchant guilds, for example, were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, and they were efficient organizations. Ogilvie contends that the rules and structures of these guilds often reflected the interests of elites who used them to maintain their status and control resources. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality and hindering economic growth. For instance, in societies with high inequality, elites often implemented policies that favored their interests, such as limiting access to education and political power for the lower classes. This focus on distributional conflicts does not negate the role of cultural factors entirely. Ogilvie acknowledges that cultural beliefs can influence economic institutions, but she argues that these beliefs are often shaped by underlying power dynamics. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are a primary driver, cultural factors can still play a significant role in shaping economic institutions and, consequently, long-run growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.10) and Mokyr (2005, p.56) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge and ideas, not institutions alone, were fundamental to economic growth. They jointly propose that the dissemination of ideas and ideologies drove the economic transformation in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, and institutional changes were not the cause. McCloskey contends that the shift in rhetoric and ideologies, particularly the rise of bourgeois values, was crucial in fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge, highlighting that the growth of such knowledge was widely diffused across Europe, and it was not confined to specific national boundaries. This diffusion was facilitated by periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which allowed ideas to spread rapidly and widely. The shared finding is that the growth of useful knowledge and new ideologies were critical in driving economic growth. Mokyr further elaborates that the dissemination of knowledge included tacit knowledge, which was transmitted through personal interactions and training, and it was not limited to codified forms. This tacit knowledge played a significant role in the economic development of Europe. This implies that the economic growth was deeply influenced by the cultural and ideological shifts that facilitated the spread of knowledge, and it was not merely a result of institutional changes. This perspective underscores the importance of cultural factors in shaping the economic landscape, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge and new ideologies were pivotal in driving long-run growth.

The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are argued to be a fundamental driver of long-run economic outcomes, challenging the notion that culture is the primary determinant. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) assert that European colonial powers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This institutional framework was shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, who designed institutions to maximize their rents and to promote long-run economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional changes in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that the establishment of extractive institutions was a critical factor in the economic development of colonies. These institutions were designed to extract resources and maintain political power, and they were not designed to encourage economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.17)  argue that the high correlation between mortality rates faced by European settlers and the establishment of early institutions highlights the exogenous variation in institutional quality. This exogenous variation, in turn, significantly influenced current income per capita, suggesting that institutional changes due to colonialism had a lasting impact on economic performance. While these findings emphasize the role of institutions in economic growth, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that the design of institutions was influenced by the interests of politically powerful groups, who may not have been directly benefiting from the economic gains. This qualification suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be more complex than initially thought, as the design and implementation of institutions are influenced by a variety of factors, including political power and economic interests.

The Industrious Revolution and the dynamics of technological advance and social change are important phenomena that precede and facilitate economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; de Vries 1994, p.18). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and de Vries (1994, p.18) both highlight the role of social change in economic growth, with Abramovitz (1986, p.21) discussing "social competence for exploiting the then most advanced methods" (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). De Vries (1994, p.18) describes this phenomenon as a distinct phase that facilitated the Industrial Revolution, emphasizing that it was a critical period of regional economic development and it was not merely a prelude to industrialization (de Vries 1994, p.5). The Industrious Revolution focuses on the economic and social transformations that paved the way for later industrial developments, and it does not explicitly link culture to long-run growth. This complexity suggests that while technological advance and social change are crucial, they are not sufficient on their own to drive long-run growth. The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth remains an open question, as these dynamics do not explicitly address cultural factors. This underscores the need to consider additional factors that might influence the design and implementation of institutions, including cultural dimensions that are not captured by technological advance and social change alone.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the slave trade has had a lasting impact on African development through its influence on cultural attitudes. By fostering a culture of mistrust, the slave trade has left a legacy that persists to this day, shaping economic and social outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon's work shows that this mistrust is a significant factor in the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, accounting for at least half of this effect. This mistrust is a contemporary force that continues to influence economic development, and it is not merely a historical artifact. Nunn (2008, p.27) further elaborates that the slave trades caused long-term political instability, leading to weakened and fragmented states. This political instability has had profound implications for economic performance, as recent empirical research shows that a country's history of state development is a crucial determinant of current economic performance. This political instability, in turn, has contributed to the persistence of a culture of mistrust. Nunn (2008, p.1) notes that slavery has been a source of precolonial origins for modern corruption, which further complicates the economic and social landscape. This corruption, rooted in historical injustices, continues to hinder development efforts. The legacy of the slave trade extends to cultural and political dimensions that continue to shape Africa's development trajectory, and it is not limited to economic factors.

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is complex and multifaceted. Shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.24; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) show that cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. This is because culture can change the structure and equilibrium of the collective action game, which in turn affects the institutional framework. For example, cultural beliefs about the afterlife motivated medieval craftsmen to follow the rules of guilds, while the disappearance of these beliefs with the Protestant Reformation caused guilds to decline (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Similarly, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). This shows that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). The institutions leading to the growth of a stable market for public debt provided a large and positive externality for the parallel development of a market for private debt (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). Therefore, the role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in the way cultural beliefs and values can influence the structure of economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy. This is particularly relevant in the context of Africa, where the legacy of the slave trade has contributed to a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.15). The persistence of a culture of mistrust in Africa is a direct result of the historical injustices of the slave trade, which continue to hinder development efforts. This cultural dimension is crucial in understanding the long-run growth trajectories of different regions, as it highlights the importance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions and the efficiency of the economy.
