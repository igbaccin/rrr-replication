The role of culture in shaping institutions that drive long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of whether cultural factors are primary drivers of institutional development or whether they are secondary to other forces. One position holds that cultural beliefs and values significantly influence the structure of economic institutions and their efficiency, thereby affecting economic growth. This perspective often emphasizes the role of social capital and norms of trust and reciprocity in fostering institutions that support economic development. A rival position contends that institutions are primarily shaped by economic and political factors, with culture playing a more limited role. This view often highlights the importance of secure property rights and the political delivery of these rights as key drivers of economic growth. The debate revolves around the extent to which cultural factors can independently shape institutions and economic outcomes, or whether they are merely epiphenomena of deeper economic and political forces. Ogilvie and North and Weingast both argue that institutions matter in economic growth, but they differ in their emphasis on the role of culture. While Ogilvie highlights the influence of cultural beliefs on economic institutions, North and Weingast focus on the evolution of institutions governing public choice and their role in fostering secure property rights. This tension sets the stage for a deeper exploration of how culture interacts with institutions to drive long-run economic growth. This impact of ideas and ideologies on economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 is argued by McCloskey and Mokyr. McCloskey (2015, p.10) argues that economic change was driven by ideas and ideologies, not by interests or institutions. The accumulation of information combined with interest was a mere matter of calculation. Mokyr (2005, p.56) further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge was the mainspring of economic growth. The diffusion of knowledge within the West was facilitated through periodicals, translations, international conferences, and personal communications, which highlights the interconnectedness of intellectual movements across borders. In the 18th century, much of the useful knowledge was tacit, embodied in skills and practical experience, and its transmission relied on personal interactions and training. This tacit knowledge was crucial for economic growth, as it complemented codified knowledge and was often more readily accessible through social networks. The role of tacit knowledge in economic development underscores the importance of cultural factors, as it was often transmitted through apprenticeships and social interactions, which were deeply embedded in the cultural fabric of societies. The dissemination of ideas and knowledge, both codified and tacit, was a key driver of economic growth in Northwestern Europe.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting how institutional frameworks shaped by colonial practices have long-lasting economic impacts. The mortality rates faced by European settlers in colonies influenced the type of institutions established, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to maximize short-term gains for colonists rather than promote long-run growth. These institutions, in turn, have persisted and shaped current economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to foster long-run economic development. This extractive nature of colonial institutions, therefore, had profound and enduring effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical roots of institutional frameworks and their role in shaping long-term economic growth.

The literature on complexity in economic growth processes highlights the multifaceted nature of technological advance and social change, and it does not specifically address how culture dominates institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Austin 2008, p.2). The process of economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including technological advancements and social dynamics. This complexity is further illustrated by the erratic indicators of productivity levels and the limited social competence for exploiting advanced methods, particularly in the earlier years and among the more recent latecomers (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis further complicates this picture by highlighting the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic development, where Africans' responses to their environments ultimately changed their economic trajectories (Austin 2008, p.25). This dynamic interplay suggests that the institutional frameworks supporting economic growth are not static but evolve in response to technological and social changes. Austin's analysis indicates that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is period-specific, meaning that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth can vary over time. This period-specificity implies that the role of institutions in economic growth is not uniform but rather contingent on the broader context of technological and social change. This nuanced view of economic growth processes underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of technological advance and social change in shaping long-term economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.1) claim that institutions governing public choice were essential for establishing secure property rights and fostering economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The institutions governing public debt also provided a positive externality for the development of private debt markets, further integrating public and private financial systems (North and Weingast 1989, p.24). This integration underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as they not only fostered secure property rights but also created a stable environment for financial transactions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4) argue that the distribution of power and resources among different groups within a society significantly influences the development of economic institutions. This power dynamic can lead to the creation of institutions that favor certain groups at the expense of others, which in turn affects long-run economic growth. For instance, in pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the rise of guilds can be attributed to the struggles between different social classes, with elites using their power to maintain institutions that secured their privileges. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this idea by showing how the initial inequality in New World societies shaped the institutions that evolved, with elites implementing policies that restricted competition and maintained their dominance. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) qualifies this by noting that the interdependency of individual institutions within a broader framework means that studying one institution in isolation can be misleading. This interdependency suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is not straightforward, as the interactions between various institutions can either amplify or mitigate the effects of these conflicts.

Culture plays a pivotal role in shaping the institutions that drive long-run economic growth. Ogilvie shows that cultural beliefs and values, such as those associated with 'civic' cultures, can encourage institutions that foster economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is echoed by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate that historical experiences, such as the slave trade, have shaped cultural norms of behavior, including trust, which in turn influence economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The interdependency of institutions within a broader framework means that cultural influences interact with other institutional elements (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This interaction can either amplify or mitigate the effects of distributional conflicts on economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Acemoglu et al. support this view by showing that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Furthermore, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The role of culture in dominating institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth is evident in how cultural beliefs and values shape economic institutions. The precise mechanisms by which culture influences these institutions are a subject of debate. The shared finding across sources is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The disagreement lies in whether cultural beliefs directly shape economic institutions or if they operate through intermediary institutions. Austin qualifies that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that the impact of culture on institutions is not straightforward and may vary across different historical contexts. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the financial revolution in England, which played a critical role in England's long-run success (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical case shows how institutions, shaped by cultural and political factors, can drive economic growth.
