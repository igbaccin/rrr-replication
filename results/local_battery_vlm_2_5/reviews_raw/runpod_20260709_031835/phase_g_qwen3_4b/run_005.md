Institutions serve as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim that demands rigorous examination of historical and institutional dynamics. This literature review examines the intellectual stakes of whether institutional structures, particularly those governing political and economic interactions, can consistently drive sustained economic development across diverse contexts. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions act as the primary causal mechanism or are merely correlated outcomes of economic processes, with scholars like North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) advancing complementary arguments that institutions shape growth trajectories through political commitments and institutional design. This review synthesizes evidence that institutional frameworks evolve in response to power dynamics, economic rents, and historical contingencies, thereby supporting the causal link between institutional quality and long-term prosperity.

North's analysis of the Bank of England's establishment reveals how secure property rights and institutional frameworks directly catalyze economic growth through reduced transaction costs and enhanced capital accumulation, a mechanism central to institutional quality's role in long-run prosperity (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further contextualize this by demonstrating that institutional features like property rights enforcement and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics, where institutions that maximize short-term rents for elites often hinder long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The colonial origins of inequality, as shown by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), illustrate how institutional disparities rooted in historical power structures directly influence growth trajectories, with extractive institutions perpetuating cycles of underdevelopment (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Evidence from Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) underscores that institutional quality is not a static trait but evolves through interactions between economic and political forces, particularly in contexts of high inequality where institutional design becomes a conduit for elite consolidation (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). These findings collectively establish that institutional quality, defined by its capacity to enforce property rights and maintain rule of law, directly shapes long-run economic growth by channeling political power toward sustainable development rather than short-term rents. This framework provides a causal pathway through which institutions act as primary drivers of prosperity, not mere correlates of economic processes.

Mokyr (2005, p.2) identifies the intellectual and institutional evolution of Europe as the engine of 'progress with a little p', a stable, incremental advancement that underpinned long-run economic growth divergence. This framework, supported by Kuznets (1973, p.4), Temin (2002, p.4), and Allen (2001, p.1), demonstrates that technological innovation and institutional adaptation emerged in tandem, with early industrialization requiring specific social structures to sustain productivity gains. Temin (2002, p.4) further clarifies that elastic labor supply, once a driver of growth, became constrained by institutional changes that shifted economic power toward skilled labor. Together, these sources reveal that Europe's growth trajectory depended on institutions that enabled technological adoption without disrupting the social fabric, a process that required balancing short-term rents with long-term development. This interplay between institutional design and technological progress created a self-reinforcing cycle of innovation and stability, ultimately distinguishing Europe's economic trajectory from other regions. The historical path of Europe thus exemplifies how institutions can channel intellectual and technological advancements into sustained growth, setting the stage for the next phase of institutional evolution.

Austin (2008, p.24) identifies that institutions function as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, yet African economic history reveals a more complex causal pattern where institutional evolution is endogenous to historical processes. This perspective is reinforced by Hopkins (2009, p.22), who notes that ethnic fragmentation and institutional congruence critically shape economic outcomes in Africa, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) on women's roles in agricultural production and trade. Nunn (2008, p.3) further demonstrates how the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding negatively impacted institutional development, creating conditions that hindered long-term growth. These findings collectively indicate that African economic trajectories were shaped by institutional evolution triggered by historical events, not isolated institutional factors. The interplay between historical context and institutional adaptation thus provides a pathway for understanding how institutions channel economic growth in Africa. This historical continuity of institutional change remains critical for interpreting contemporary African development challenges.

Stern (2004, p.3) identifies that economic development triggers environmental degradation through shifting industrial structures, with early industrialization increasing emissions while later transitions toward services reduce pollution intensity (Stern 2004, p.3; Stern 2004, p.6). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further note that African economies have historically experienced rapid growth phases but remain vulnerable to regression due to persistent institutional weaknesses (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.18). This environmental Kuznets curve mechanism, which channels economic growth through institutional adaptation, demonstrates how environmental degradation can reverse long-term development trajectories when institutions fail to manage industrial transitions effectively. The interplay between industrial restructuring and institutional resilience thus shapes whether African economies achieve sustainable growth or revert to stagnation.

Institutions serve as the primary driver of long-term economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.28). Both scholars emphasize that institutional structures, such as property rights enforcement and rule of law, are critical to sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further confirm that institutional features significantly influence economic growth trajectories, particularly in historically disadvantaged regions. North (1989, p.9) highlights the role of institutional innovations in reducing transaction costs and fostering capital markets, which are essential for long-run growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that technological advancement and economic growth are interconnected, with institutions adapting to support these transformations. The consensus across these studies underscores that robust institutions are indispensable for economic progress, with their design directly impacting growth outcomes. The extent to which institutions can be reformed without triggering regression remains contested. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may perpetuate extractive practices, hindering long-term growth, while North and Weingast (1989, p.2) stress that institutional commitment is necessary for effective economic governance. The historical evidence from colonial-era institutions, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1), reveals that institutional differences rooted in colonial policies have long-term consequences for economic development. Historical comparisons of institutional evolution across regions provide the clearest empirical resolution to this disagreement, demonstrating that institutional resilience, not economic growth, determines sustainable development outcomes.
