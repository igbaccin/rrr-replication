Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim that has been contested and refined across decades of scholarship. The intellectual stakes involve determining whether enduring economic structures, not temporary market forces, drive sustainable prosperity. A live disagreement centers on whether institutions act as exogenous constraints or endogenous outcomes that shape development trajectories. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, particularly those with multiple veto points and strong judicial oversight, are critical to economic growth by enabling stable property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.1) emphasize that institutions emerge from historical contexts and colonial legacies, directly influencing long-term growth potential through their impact on property rights and rule of law. Kuznets (1973, p.4; 1973, p.1) highlight how institutional evolution is intertwined with technological advancement, suggesting that institutions must adapt to new economic realities to sustain growth. These perspectives collectively underscore the centrality of institutions in economic development, though their specific mechanisms and historical roots remain contested. The debate over institutional causality continues to shape policy and theory, with implications for both historical understanding and future growth strategies.

Komlos (1998, p.2) reinforces this linkage by showing that structural economic shifts, including per-capita productivity changes, are deeply embedded in institutional evolution, particularly during periods of rapid industrialization. Bohannan (1959, p.10) adds a historical dimension by illustrating how colonial trade networks and market systems in West Africa facilitated economic integration while simultaneously altering local institutional structures. Together, these sources establish that resource institutions, through their role in mediating industrial transitions, urban dynamics, and economic integration, act as foundational drivers of economic development. The interplay between resource availability, institutional adaptation, and economic restructuring thus creates enduring pathways for growth, with colonial-era trade systems exemplifying how resource-based institutions can catalyze or constrain long-term development. These findings underscore that institutional quality is not merely a byproduct of economic activity but a core mechanism through which resource constraints shape economic outcomes over time.
