Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remains a contested intellectual frontier, where the stakes extend beyond empirical accuracy to the very foundations of economic explanation. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional structures constitute the primary driver of sustained prosperity or whether alternative factors, such as technological innovation or resource endowments, dominate the causal chain. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political commitment underpins economic growth, while Austin (2008, p.24) challenges the simplicity of institutional determinism by highlighting the complex interplay between historical context and institutional outcomes. This tension underscores the critical role of institutional design in shaping economic trajectories, yet also reveals the limitations of universal institutional explanations when confronted with heterogeneous historical experiences. The debate thus hinges on whether institutions act as the singular cause or as one of multiple interconnected variables in the growth process.

North (1989, p.9) identifies institutional quality as the specific manifestation of institutions that drives long-run economic growth, aligning with the fundamental role of institutions in economic development. This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), whose empirical analysis demonstrates that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law are critical determinants of economic outcomes. The evidence from North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.28) further underscores how institutional design, particularly the protection of political liberties, emerges as a key mechanism for fostering economic growth. For instance, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies how institutional innovations can reduce transaction costs and catalyze long-term capital accumulation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) complement this by showing that equilibrium institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritize rent extraction over sustainable growth, as seen in the colonial contexts where institutions were designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists. This pattern highlights a critical tension: while institutions are foundational to economic development, their effectiveness depends on the political dynamics that govern their evolution. Thus, institutional quality serves as the specific manifestation of institutions that drives long-run economic growth, ensuring that the mechanisms of institutional design are both robust and adaptive to the evolving needs of economic development.
