Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim with profound implications for understanding how societies develop and sustain prosperity. This assertion hinges on the interplay between political structures and economic outcomes, where institutional quality directly shapes growth trajectories. A live disagreement exists over whether institutions are primarily the result of historical accidents or deliberate choices, with scholars divided on the mechanisms through which they influence economic performance. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, such as explicit veto points and common law courts, are critical to economic growth by mitigating conflicts over power. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize that institutional features like property rights enforcement and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes, shaped by politically powerful groups that may resist change to protect their interests. These perspectives converge on institutions as pivotal, yet diverge on their causal role: North and Weingast (1989, p.2) frame institutions as the foundation for market development, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight how extractive institutions can perpetuate poverty by prioritizing short-term rents over long-term growth. The debate thus centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of economic outcomes or a secondary consequence of power dynamics. Understanding this tension is crucial for designing effective policies that foster sustainable development. The role of institutions in economic growth remains a contested yet vital question for scholars and policymakers alike.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth requires institutional transformations incompatible with pre-existing social structures, as technological advancements demand institutional shifts that replace family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery with more efficient systems. This observation aligns with Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Temin (2002, p.4), who emphasize how institutional evolution responds to technological change and demographic shifts, respectively. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate that institutional differences, such as the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption, create divergent paths of development across societies, where extractive institutions perpetuate inequality and hinder long-term growth. Hopkins (2009, p.22) adds that institutional congruence with indigenous practices enhances governance effectiveness and promotes inclusive economic outcomes, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) highlight how gendered institutional arrangements affect resource allocation and economic participation. Together, these sources demonstrate that historical institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that determine how resources are allocated and used across generations. The structural relationship between institutional design and economic outcomes reveals that societies with institutions aligned to technological progress and social dynamics achieve sustained growth, whereas those with misaligned institutions face persistent constraints.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson establish that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law are critical mechanisms for long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This structural relationship reveals how institutions function as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics, where extractive institutions persist due to the reluctance of dominant groups to cede control over resource distribution (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). These findings underscore that the misalignment between institutional design and economic needs leads to persistent underdevelopment, particularly when institutions fail to adapt to evolving technological and social conditions. Institutional quality thus becomes a pivotal determinant of growth trajectories, as societies with robust institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts can sustain higher levels of productivity and innovation over time. The colonial legacy of institutional misalignment provides a historical foundation for understanding why certain regions continue to experience economic stagnation despite technological advancements.

This mechanism, where constitutional design channels political power toward enforceable rules, allows societies to overcome short-term political cycles and sustain capital formation. The Bank of England’s creation in 1694 (North 1989, p.9) exemplifies how institutional innovation reduces transaction costs through secure time-based property rights, while North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political commitment to rule of law is critical for markets to function beyond immediate political pressures. Consequently, constitutional institutions that balance political accountability with economic stability become indispensable for long-run growth, as they provide the institutional discipline necessary for capital accumulation and technological adoption.
