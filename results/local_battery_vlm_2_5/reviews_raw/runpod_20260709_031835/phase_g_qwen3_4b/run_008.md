Institutions serve as the foundational driver of long-run economic growth, a claim central to understanding historical and cross-national development trajectories. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional structures can consistently channel resources toward sustainable prosperity or whether they are inherently prone to failure under shifting political and economic conditions. A live disagreement persists between scholars who view institutions as self-sustaining mechanisms for growth and those who emphasize their vulnerability to external shocks and internal path dependencies. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that political commitment to institutional rules is critical for economic development, while Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights how technological advancement requires institutional shifts incompatible with pre-industrial social structures. These perspectives converge on the necessity of institutional adaptability but diverge on the mechanisms that enable or impede this evolution. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutions significantly explain income disparities across former colonies, suggesting their role as causal factors in growth processes. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) further clarifies that even scholars rejecting institutional efficiency often acknowledge their capacity to enhance economic welfare through transactional improvements. These sources collectively indicate that institutions function as active causal agents in growth dynamics, though their stability and effectiveness remain contested by historical and political contingencies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that institutional features, such as expropriation risk and property rights enforcement, emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by politically powerful groups, thereby influencing long-run economic growth. This perspective, supported by North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), reveals how institutions function as causal mechanisms through which political power structures determine economic outcomes. Together, these sources indicate that institutional quality directly shapes economic development by determining the stability of property rights, rule of law, and political systems. The evidence shows that institutions act as a conduit between political power dynamics and economic outcomes, with their effectiveness varying based on the balance of power among different societal groups. This mechanism explains why certain societies experience higher growth rates while others stagnate, as institutional structures either facilitate or hinder the accumulation of capital and technological progress. Institutions thus serve as the primary driver of long-run economic growth by channeling political power into productive economic structures.

Mokyr (2005, p.2) identifies the intellectual evolution as a foundational process for economic development, with the Industrial Revolution emerging from a confluence of knowledge diffusion and institutional adaptation. This historical trajectory, as Mokyr (2005, p.2) emphasizes, challenges the assumption of pre-1800 economic stagnation by highlighting the role of intellectual activity in shaping growth trajectories. Complementing this view, van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that guilds historically facilitated skill transmission through formalized apprenticeship contracts, illustrating how early institutional frameworks enabled human capital accumulation. Similarly, Goldin (2006, p.3) demonstrates that shifts in educational access and labor market structures during the 20th century expanded the pool of skilled workers, thereby reinforcing the link between institutional evolution and human capital formation. McCloskey (2015, p.9) further underscores that ideas and ideologies, rather than institutional constraints, often drive economic transformation, as evidenced by the counterfactual analysis of France’s industrial stagnation without Anglo-Saxon examples. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.27) demonstrate that coal’s economic impact, though significant, was secondary to broader institutional and intellectual shifts in industrializing societies. Together, these sources reveal how intellectual and institutional evolution acts as the foundational process for economic development, channeling historical innovation into long-term growth. This historical pathway thus provides a critical antecedent for the institutional structures that ultimately drive sustained economic progress.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth requires institutions capable of accommodating technological advancements and industrial scale, which are incompatible with pre-existing social structures such as family enterprise or illiteracy. Temin (2002, p.4) complements this by demonstrating how labor market dynamics influence growth trajectories, with elastic labor supply historically sustaining industrial expansion through low wages and stable labor conditions (Temin 2002, p.4). Stern (2004, p.3) further contextualizes these patterns by showing how industrial structure evolves alongside economic development, shifting from resource-intensive sectors to services as countries mature (Stern 2004, p.3). Together, these sources reveal that institutional adaptability to technological and demographic changes acts as a prerequisite for sustained economic growth. The historical trajectory of institutional evolution thus provides the foundational framework through which economic development unfolds, channeling innovation into long-term prosperity.

Hopkins (2009, p.22) demonstrates that pre-industrial African institutional frameworks were deeply embedded in social structures, with group cohesion influencing economic outcomes through multiple loyalties and shared interests (Hopkins 2009, p.22). This interdependence between institutions and social dynamics is further reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19), who document how women's roles in subsistence economies evolved alongside colonial trade systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn (2008, p.3) complements this by showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding created institutional legacies that hindered economic development in sub-Saharan Africa (Nunn 2008, p.3). Together, these sources reveal that institutional legacies in Africa stem from complex interactions between historical conflicts, social structures, and economic practices, which directly shape long-run growth trajectories. The interplay between these factors underscores how early institutional choices, such as the centrality of ethnic and kinship networks, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Hopkins (2019, p.10) adds that the historiographical approaches to African economic history have evolved, yet the underlying institutional patterns remain critical for understanding development (Hopkins 2019, p.3).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) demonstrate that institutional shortcomings in African national accounts have constrained long-run economic growth, a finding that aligns with institutions as the fundamental cause of economic development. Their analysis reveals that historical data gaps have obscured the true trajectory of African economic performance, which has been particularly acute in regions where institutional frameworks were weak. Komlos (1998, p.2) corroborates this by showing that economic contractions in the 19th century led to persistent declines in percapita income, as institutions failed to mitigate the effects of these downturns. Allen (2001, p.1) further supports this by documenting how institutional weaknesses in early modern Europe contributed to prolonged income stagnation, with European cities experiencing divergent growth patterns from the 15th to 18th centuries. These findings collectively indicate that institutional deficiencies in historical and contemporary contexts have significantly hindered economic convergence, particularly in regions with weak governance structures. The persistent impact of institutional shortcomings on economic outcomes underscores the critical role institutions play in shaping long-run growth trajectories. Institutional frameworks that facilitate economic growth emerge as a consistent finding across multiple studies. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) identify the critical role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes, emphasizing that representative government structures with multiple veto points are essential for mitigating power imbalances. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features significantly influence economic development, particularly in contexts where historical institutions affect contemporary income disparities. These findings collectively underscore institutions as foundational to long-run economic growth. The persistent interdependence of institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), reveals that economic systems operate within complex institutional networks that cannot be isolated for analysis. This interdependence further supports the view that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even when they do not actively hinder economic development, suggesting that institutions are shaped by political dynamics rather than purely economic efficiency. Despite this, the evidence consistently points to institutions as the primary driver of economic outcomes. The colonial origins of comparative development, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), illustrate how institutional features like property rights enforcement directly impact long-term growth trajectories. Thus, institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.
