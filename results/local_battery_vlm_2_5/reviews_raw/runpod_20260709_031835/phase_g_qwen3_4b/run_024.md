Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remains a contested intellectual proposition, with scholars debating whether institutional structures alone can explain divergent economic trajectories across historical and cross-national contexts. The core tension lies in whether institutions function as primary drivers of growth or as contingent outcomes of other factors, such as technological advancement or ideological shifts. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political institutions underpin economic growth by enabling enforceable contracts and limiting rent-seeking, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions may be extractive even when they appear to foster development, depending on the political power dynamics that shape them. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights the interdependence of technological progress and institutional adaptation, suggesting that growth requires institutional frameworks capable of accommodating innovation. This debate underscores a critical divergence: whether institutions are the proximate cause of growth or a byproduct of broader economic transformations. The stakes extend beyond academic discourse, as resolving this question informs policy interventions aimed at fostering sustainable development in both developed and developing economies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutional features like expropriation risk, property rights enforcement, and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes of more fundamental institutions. This perspective aligns with North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.28), who emphasize how political institutions shape economic security and growth through mechanisms such as property rights and legal frameworks. The colonial context provides a critical lens for understanding these dynamics, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features significantly influenced economic development in formerly poor colonies, thereby contributing to persistent income disparities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further contextualize this by highlighting how institutional differences across societies impact growth trajectories, particularly through the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption. This pattern suggests that institutional evolution is deeply embedded in historical power dynamics, where the absence of robust property rights enforcement can perpetuate cycles of underdevelopment. Consequently, the colonial legacy of institutional design continues to shape contemporary economic performance, underscoring the necessity of addressing these structural roots to foster inclusive growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth necessitates institutional shifts incompatible with pre-industrial social structures, as evidenced by the incompatibility of steam and electric power with family enterprises, illiteracy, or slavery. This structural transformation, where technological advancement drives institutional adaptation, aligns with Stern (2004, p.3)'s observation that industrialization phases correlate with pollution intensity shifts, from heavy industry in early development to services in later stages. Komlos (1998, p.2) further contextualizes this by linking Kuznetsian growth to demographic consequences, noting how economic downturns can permanently affect child development through food insecurity. The interplay of these mechanisms demonstrates that institutional evolution is intrinsically tied to the pace of structural economic change, where the absence of adaptive institutions during industrial transitions perpetuates cycles of underdevelopment. Consequently, addressing the institutional barriers to technological adoption becomes critical for sustaining growth trajectories in developing economies.

Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that pre-colonial African societies experienced institutional trajectories shaped by slavery dynamics, where the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding directly undermined long-term economic development potential. This finding aligns with the broader historical pattern that slavery in pre-colonial Africa generated institutional structures less conducive to growth, as evidenced by the interplay between slave production and institutional evolution across multiple African regions (Nunn 2008, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19). The role of women in economic activity further complicates this narrative, as their contributions to food production and trade became increasingly constrained by colonial-era economic shifts, thereby reinforcing institutional barriers to growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Meanwhile, Ogilvie (2007, p.9) contextualizes the institutional evolution in pre-industrial Africa by highlighting how trade networks and social structures were historically interdependent, with institutions like the Kongo kingdom’s centralized governance enabling early trade integration despite their limited population size (Ogilvie 2007, p.9; Austin 2008, p.24). These dynamics underscore the historical roots of institutional development in Africa, where slavery and trade patterns co-evolved to shape economic trajectories. The evidence collectively reveals that pre-colonial African institutions were not static but were dynamically influenced by internal and external pressures, particularly the legacy of slavery and the emergence of trade networks that either facilitated or hindered growth pathways. Consequently, understanding the institutional legacy of slavery in Africa provides critical insights into the long-term economic development challenges faced by the continent.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, fundamentally shape economic trajectories, as demonstrated by the counterfactual analysis of France's historical development (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.8). This perspective challenges the institutionalist view by highlighting how the absence of Anglo-Saxon economic precedents in France stifled industrialization, despite the presence of capable thinkers like Vauban and Quesnay. Clark and Jacks further support this by showing that coal's role in the Industrial Revolution was constrained by transport costs, which limited the growth of energy-intensive industries such as iron making (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.27; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). Their evidence suggests that even with abundant coal resources, the structural inefficiencies in transportation networks hindered industrial progress, illustrating how resource endowments alone cannot drive sustained economic growth. Together, these sources reveal that historical economic development is shaped by a complex interplay of technological innovation and institutional frameworks, yet the critical factor remains the diffusion of ideas across regions. The absence of institutional catalysts, such as those seen in the Anglosphere, underscores how resource endowments and technological change must be embedded within broader ideological and institutional contexts to foster long-run growth.

Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth is a consensus among scholars, with North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasizing how political institutions directly shape economic outcomes by mitigating conflicts over resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate this by demonstrating that institutional features explain substantial income disparities across former colonies, reinforcing the role of institutions in developmental trajectories. The interdependence of institutional frameworks further underscores this perspective, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) illustrates how economic institutions function within broader systems that interact across markets and social structures to facilitate growth. Together, these sources confirm that institutional arrangements are pivotal in driving sustained economic development, particularly when they enable efficient transactional processes and long-term capital accumulation. However, the precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain contested, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlighting that equilibrium institutions may prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth, especially in contexts where politically powerful groups resist institutional change. This tension between institutional efficiency and growth outcomes is resolved through empirical evidence from the colonial era, where Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that institutions evolved in response to specific historical conditions, such as European colonization policies, thereby providing a clear historical lens for understanding institutional impacts on economic performance.
