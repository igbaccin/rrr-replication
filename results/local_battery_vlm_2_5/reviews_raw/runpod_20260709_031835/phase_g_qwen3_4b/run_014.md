Institutions are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, shaping how societies mobilize resources and sustain development over time. The intellectual stakes are profound: if institutions fail to evolve in response to economic change, societies risk stagnation, while effective institutional frameworks can unlock productivity and equity. A live disagreement centers on whether institutions act as a primary cause of growth or as a consequence of economic conditions, with scholars divided on the causal mechanisms that link institutional design to long-term prosperity. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, such as courts and veto points, mediate the struggle for economic control, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize institutions as critical conduits for growth across historically diverse economies. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights how technological advancement requires institutional shifts, underscoring the interplay between institutional evolution and economic modernization. This foundational tension shapes how we understand the role of institutions in sustaining growth, with implications for policy and historical analysis.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that institutional features such as expropriation risk and property rights enforcement are critical determinants of long-run economic growth, as they establish the equilibrium institutions that shape development outcomes. The analysis further shows that institutions evolve in response to political power dynamics, where extractive institutions may persist due to the reluctance of politically dominant groups to adopt reforms that could diminish their rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes is reinforced by North (1989, p.9), who identifies the Bank of England’s establishment as a pivotal moment where enhanced security of property rights catalyzed long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

This insight aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that institutional pathways significantly influence long-term growth trajectories by shaping access to economic opportunities, with extreme inequality perpetuating elite dominance and limiting broader participation in development. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.22) notes that institutional congruence with indigenous social structures enhances governance effectiveness, while its absence fosters corruption and regional fragmentation. These findings collectively underscore how historical institutions establish the rules for resource allocation and human interaction, thereby directing economic evolution. The role of institutions in mediating technological adoption and social transformation is further illuminated by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who shows that formal institutions, such as guilds, historically facilitated skill transmission but also created barriers to equitable participation. This historical trajectory reveals that institutions act as both catalysts and constraints in economic development, with their design determining whether societies can harness technological progress for inclusive growth. Consequently, the interplay between historical institutional structures and economic outcomes underscores institutions as the primary driver of long-run growth.

Temin (2002, p.4) demonstrates that institutional evolution, particularly the shift from agrarian labor to industrial employment, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth, contra the view that economic policies alone determine growth trajectories. (Temin 2002, p.4) and (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1) jointly establish that this institutional transformation, which reduces labor costs and enhances industrial stability, has historically underpinned sustained growth in European economies. The paper argues that this shift, as evidenced by the correlation between agricultural labor share and growth rates, creates a feedback loop where institutional maturity accelerates technological adoption and capital accumulation. This institutional evolution, therefore, remains the cornerstone of long-run economic development, with its quality and adaptability shaping the trajectory of prosperity.

Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that undifferentiated economic and social structures, as seen in pre-colonial African societies, impede trade and investment by limiting institutional capacity. (Nunn 2008, p.3) identifies that slavery's production and use hindered development through domestic warfare and raiding, while (Komlos 1998, p.2) links these constraints to Kuznetsian growth patterns. Vansina (1990) notes that small, undifferentiated societies lacked the infrastructure for sustained foreign trade, a condition that persisted until the Kongo kingdom's emergence with centralized governance. This institutional fragility, therefore, restricts long-run growth by preventing the development of trade networks essential for capital accumulation. These findings collectively underscore how undifferentiated institutions create persistent barriers to prosperity, directly supporting the role of institutional quality in economic development. Institutional constraints in development, such as undifferentiated economic and social structures, hinder long-run economic growth by limiting trade and investment opportunities.

Institutional quality directly shapes long-run economic growth through its role in enabling durable capital accumulation and market stability. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) establish that political institutions, particularly those with multiple veto points and courts that enforce economic rules, create the conditions for sustained investment by mitigating the risks of expropriation and enabling long-term contracts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that institutional features, such as property rights enforcement, are critical outcomes of political processes that balance competing interests, where institutions that maximize short-term rents may undermine long-term growth. These findings collectively show that institutional structures determine how economic activity is organized and sustained over time. The Bank of England’s creation in 1694 exemplifies how institutional innovation reduces transaction costs and fosters capital formation, while the colonial legacy of institutions in Africa reveals how historical power dynamics shape contemporary economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that institutions emerge from the interplay of political and economic forces, where secure property rights and effective governance are prerequisites for technological advancement and industrialization. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) confirm that institutions act as equilibrium outcomes that determine growth trajectories, with extractive institutions perpetuating poverty by limiting access to capital and markets. The colonial origins of economic disparities highlight how institutions designed to serve dominant groups can impede long-run prosperity. These perspectives converge on the view that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as their quality directly influences the capacity for capital accumulation and sustainable development.
