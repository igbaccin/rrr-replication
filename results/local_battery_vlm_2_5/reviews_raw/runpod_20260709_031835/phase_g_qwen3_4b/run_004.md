Institutions shape long-run economic growth through their role in enabling efficient transactions and fostering sustainable capital accumulation, a question central to understanding historical and cross-country development patterns. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutions act as contingent outcomes of economic processes or as fundamental drivers of prosperity, with implications for policy interventions and historical explanations. A live disagreement exists between scholars who view institutions as passive byproducts of economic activity and those who argue they actively structure growth trajectories through their enforcement mechanisms and adaptive capacities. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that political commitment to institutional rules is critical for economic development, while Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlight how institutional interdependencies create complex frameworks that cannot be isolated. These perspectives converge on the necessity of institutional coordination but diverge on the extent to which institutions are exogenous or endogenous to economic processes. The evidence suggests that institutions function as both outcomes and causes, with their efficacy depending on the context of political and economic interdependencies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) assert that institutional features such as expropriation risk and property rights enforcement emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political dynamics, directly influencing long-run economic growth trajectories. This perspective, anchored by (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; North and Weingast 1989, p.28; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.9), demonstrates that institutional equilibrium is a dynamic response to power struggles and resource distribution. The interplay between political power structures and institutional design creates conditions where property rights enforcement either facilitates or hinders economic development, as evidenced by the colonial legacy in former economies where extractive institutions maximized short-term rents for elites at the expense of long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, once established, become self-reinforcing, particularly when they align with the interests of politically dominant groups who resist reforms that might disrupt their existing power balances (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Such institutional configurations function as active determinants of contemporary economic outcomes, with their persistence often linked to the absence of robust accountability mechanisms and rule of law. The colonial origins of economic disparities, for instance, reveal how institutions that favored elite control over resources became entrenched, thereby constraining the potential for broad-based prosperity (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This mechanism underscores a critical pathway through which institutions become both outcomes and causes of economic performance, with their long-term effects rooted in the initial conditions of political and economic power distribution.

North (1989, p.9) identifies institutional capacity as a critical mechanism for long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs through secure property rights and financial innovation. The paper argues that institutional quality is a foundational driver of economic development, not a byproduct, challenging neoclassical assumptions that prioritize population and savings (North 1989, p.1). Institutional capacity thus emerges as a specific institutional type that channels growth through improved transactional reliability and reduced risk, a mechanism that underpins sustained prosperity. This capacity, therefore, provides a structural pathway for institutions to drive growth by mitigating the risks associated with long-term investments. North’s framework establishes that institutional capacity is pivotal in transforming economic potential into sustained growth through transactional efficiency and risk reduction. This mechanism directly links institutional design to long-run economic outcomes, reinforcing the role of institutions as the fundamental cause of growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) demonstrates that technological advancement fundamentally reshapes social and institutional structures, creating a structural barrier to growth in less developed regions. This mechanism operates through the incompatibility of modern industrial technologies with traditional social arrangements, as Kuznets argues that steam and electric power require institutional frameworks previously absent in developing economies (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Consequently, the institutional capacity to manage technological change becomes a critical determinant of economic development, directly challenging the notion that institutional design alone can drive growth without considering technological shifts. This finding underscores how technological adoption imposes structural constraints on institutional evolution, implying that growth trajectories are inherently tied to the synchronization of technological progress and institutional adaptation. The evidence reveals that the institutional capacity to manage technological change becomes a critical determinant of economic development, directly challenging the notion that institutional design alone can drive growth without considering technological shifts. Therefore, technological advancement necessitates institutional transformation to unlock sustained economic growth.

Institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth through their role in enabling technological adoption and adaptation, as demonstrated by Ogilvie (2007, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.28). Both scholars emphasize that institutional frameworks must evolve in response to technological shifts to sustain growth, with North and Weingast noting that political institutions directly influence economic outcomes by protecting property rights and enforcing contracts (North and Weingast 1989, p.28). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and (2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) establish that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law are critical determinants of economic development, with institutional quality accounting for significant income disparities across countries. These findings collectively underscore that institutional capacity to manage technological change is essential for long-run growth, as institutions must adapt to technological advancements to unlock economic potential. The evidence reveals that institutional design alone cannot drive growth without integrating technological progress, as technological adoption imposes structural constraints on institutional evolution (Ogilvie 2007, p.9). Therefore, institutions that effectively synchronize technological progress with adaptive institutional structures are the cornerstone of sustained economic growth.
