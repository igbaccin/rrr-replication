Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth through their capacity to enable credible commitments and institutional stability, a relationship central to economic theory. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions with multiple veto points and judicial primacy protect economic rights, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize institutions as drivers of industrialization and income disparities. Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies institutional shifts as necessary for technological adoption in modern economic growth. These streams collectively establish institutions as active causal factors in economic development, not mere byproducts. The intellectual stakes involve determining whether institutional reforms can address persistent inequality and underdevelopment through targeted policy interventions.

North's analysis of institutional quality as a determinant of economic growth reveals that colonial-era institutional structures directly shaped long-run development trajectories, with the Bank of England's establishment exemplifying how secure property rights catalyzed financial innovation and reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further demonstrate that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law emerge as equilibrium outcomes, shaped by historically contingent political power dynamics (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The colonial legacy, as emphasized by Sokoloff and Engerman, underscores how institutions that prioritize elite interests over broad economic participation perpetuate inequality and hinder development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This mechanism explains why some societies, despite similar levels of capital and labor, experience divergent growth paths due to institutional differences in property rights enforcement and political stability (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). Consequently, institutional quality serves as the critical causal mechanism linking historical institutional structures to contemporary economic outcomes, providing a robust foundation for policy interventions aimed at fostering inclusive growth.

This mechanism, where modern economic growth requires institutional shifts to accommodate technological advancement, directly channels the institutional causality framework into cross-regional development dynamics (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The paper establishes that the institutional barriers to growth, such as the need for large-scale industrial infrastructure and scientific literacy, arise from the historical divergence in institutional evolution between developed and developing regions (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Consequently, Kuznets's analysis underscores how institutional pathways to growth are not uniform but contingent on historical and policy-driven institutional transitions, reinforcing the centrality of institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) demonstrate that gender-specific institutions critically shape African women's economic contributions through historical patterns of resource control and production allocation. Their analysis reveals how pre-colonial agricultural systems established women's authority over food production while restricting their influence over cash-crop economies, a dynamic that persists in contemporary sub-Saharan Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional legacy creates a dual pathway where women retain significant roles in food security but face diminished participation in cash-based economic activities, directly linking institutional structures to gendered economic outcomes. By tracing the historical transition from subsistence to commercial agriculture, Akyeampong and Fofack show that institutional pathways to economic inclusion remain contingent on the historical allocation of production rights, reinforcing institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. The persistence of these gendered institutional constraints underscores how historical transitions in institutional design continue to influence contemporary economic performance across African regions, highlighting the critical role of gender-specific institutions in shaping growth trajectories.

Institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by embedding political and economic rules that enable sustained development. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) establish that political liberties and institutional design are deeply intertwined, with the success of propertied interests leading to institutional frameworks that mitigate economic risks. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features explain significant income disparities across former colonies, highlighting how institutions channel economic growth. Both streams converge on the idea that institutional quality directly influences economic outcomes, with North’s work emphasizing political commitment and Acemoglu’s research underscoring institutional equilibrium. The persistence of gendered institutional constraints, as noted in the prior section, reflects this broader pattern where historical institutional pathways continue to shape contemporary economic performance. North (1989, p.9) further illustrates that secure property rights and institutional innovation, such as the Bank of England, catalyze long-term economic growth by reducing transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that extractive institutions may persist even when economically inefficient, as politically powerful groups resist institutional change to maintain their rents. This tension between institutional stability and adaptability underscores the role of historical context in shaping institutional outcomes. The colonial origins of comparative development, as analyzed in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), reveal that institutional features emerge from equilibrium conditions shaped by colonial policies, directly linking historical institutional design to contemporary economic performance. Thus, institutions act as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with their historical evolution determining the trajectory of economic development across regions.
