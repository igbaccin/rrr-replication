Institutions fundamentally shape long-run economic growth by enabling efficient resource allocation and fostering sustainable development, a claim central to understanding why some economies thrive while others stagnate. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional structures are the primary driver of prosperity or merely correlated outcomes of broader economic forces. A live disagreement exists over whether institutions act as causal agents or are passive responses to pre-existing conditions, with scholars like North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasizing institutional enforcement mechanisms and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) arguing that institutions emerge from political power dynamics to maximize short-term rents rather than long-term growth. This tension underscores the critical role of institutional design in economic outcomes, where the interplay between political commitment and market efficiency determines growth trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutional features like expropriation risk and rule of law significantly influence long-run economic growth through historical pathways of colonial development. Their analysis of former colonies reveals that institutional arrangements, such as property rights enforcement, were shaped by politically dominant groups to maximize short-term rents rather than foster sustainable growth. This mechanism, where institutions emerge from power dynamics to serve immediate interests, is further contextualized by North (1989, p.9) through the Bank of England’s role in securing long-term capital markets by reducing transaction costs. The colonial origins of these institutional differences, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), underscore how expropriation risk and property rights enforcement directly affect economic outcomes. These findings align with Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8), who note that institutional features are complex outcomes of macroeconomic forces that shape both growth and inequality. The evidence collectively illustrates that institutional design, particularly the balance between political control and market efficiency, is a critical driver of long-term economic outcomes. Institutions that prioritize short-term political rents over long-term market stability hinder sustainable development, as observed in the historical context of European colonization and its lasting effects on economic performance. This dynamic explains why certain regions experience persistent underdevelopment despite similar starting conditions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) establish that institutional differences explain divergent growth paths across societies, emphasizing the role of property rights, corruption, and financial structures. This historical institutionalist perspective, which links institutional evolution to economic outcomes, is reinforced by Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Ogilvie (2007, p.9) in their analyses of how technological and institutional shifts interact. Kuznets notes modern economic growth requires institutions compatible with technological advancement, while Ogilvie demonstrates institutional frameworks such as guilds and credit systems operate interdependently to facilitate economic activity. Together, these sources underscore that institutions are not static but dynamically shape resource allocation and long-term growth trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.22) further contextualizes this by showing that institutional interdependencies can either foster or hinder economic progress, depending on the alignment with historical social structures.
