Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remain a pivotal intellectual debate, with scholars contesting whether institutional structures alone or interacting forces drive sustained prosperity. The tension centers on whether institutions function as the primary engine of development or whether other factors, such as technological innovation or political stability, exert greater influence. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasize that political institutions, particularly those with multiple veto points and strong judicial oversight, are critical for economic growth by enabling commitments to enforce rules. Their work suggests that the success of propertied interests in shaping institutions has historically been a catalyst for market development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate this by demonstrating that institutions significantly explain income disparities between regions, with extractive institutions often hindering long-term growth. These findings collectively underscore institutions' role in channeling economic activity and fostering sustainable development. The debate intensifies when considering whether institutions can be effectively reformed without destabilizing existing power structures, a challenge highlighted by Kuznets (1973, p.4) in his analysis of technological adoption and institutional constraints. This stream reveals that institutions are dynamically shaped by historical contexts and political choices, directly impacting economic trajectories. The stakes are high, as misidentifying the causal mechanisms could lead to policies that either perpetuate or alleviate poverty through institutional misalignment. Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth requires careful consideration of their interplay with other developmental forces to avoid unintended consequences.

This perspective aligns with North (1989, p.9) who identifies the Bank of England’s creation as a pivotal moment where secure property rights catalyzed capital markets and economic expansion. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) complement this by highlighting institutional disparities that explain divergent development paths, particularly in contexts where property rights and market access were unevenly distributed. These findings collectively demonstrate that institutions function as upstream drivers of economic growth, with their historical configurations directly influencing contemporary development outcomes. The colonial legacy of institutions, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), reveals how extractive institutions, even when seemingly stable, can perpetuate inequality by limiting opportunities for broad-based economic participation. This mechanism underscores the critical role of institutional quality in determining long-term growth prospects, as institutions that prioritize short-term political rents over long-term economic development often hinder sustainable progress. Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth thus require attention to their historical roots and adaptive capacity to ensure inclusive development.

Hopkins (2009, p.22) emphasizes that historical institutional configurations, particularly those rooted in ethnic fragmentation, can enable or hinder effective governance mechanisms. The interplay between these factors underscores how early institutional choices shape economic trajectories, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) document that women’s economic roles were historically constrained by informal institutions that limited their participation in cash-crop economies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further contextualize this by highlighting that African economies have experienced periods of rapid growth but remain vulnerable to institutional fragility, which can reverse progress. Nunn (2008, p.3) provides evidence that the production of slaves through conflict entrenched extractive institutions and disrupted trade networks essential for sustained growth. van Zanden (2009, p.19) shows that institutional adaptability, such as the emergence of guilds in Europe, was crucial for long-term human capital formation. Together, these sources reveal that historical institutions establish the foundational structures that determine economic development paths, with their evolution directly influencing the capacity for inclusive growth. The persistence of extractive institutions, even in contexts of technological advancement, thus remains a critical barrier to long-run economic prosperity.

Stern (2004, p.3) contends that resource constraints and environmental factors provide alternative explanations for long-run economic growth that compete with institutional quality as the primary driver (Stern 2004, p.3; Stern 2004, p.6). McCloskey (2015, p.9) further emphasizes that ideas and ideologies, rather than institutions, can significantly influence economic outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.8). Together, these sources illustrate how the interplay of resource availability, environmental conditions, and ideological frameworks shapes developmental trajectories, challenging the institutional primacy narrative. The evidence from Clark and Jacks (2007, p.27) underscores that coal's role in industrialization was constrained by transportation costs, suggesting that even critical energy inputs may not have been decisive for the Industrial Revolution's pace (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.27; Clark and Jacks 2007, p.1). This implies that the historical path of economic development could be more contingent on resource accessibility and technological adaptation than on institutional structures alone. Resource constraints and environmental factors thus provide alternative explanations for long-run economic growth that compete with institutional quality as the primary driver.

Institutional structures critically shape long-run economic growth through their role in enforcing property rights and facilitating capital accumulation, as North and Weingast (1989, p.28) demonstrate by linking political institutions to economic outcomes. Their analysis shows that the success of propertied interests in establishing institutional frameworks that mitigate political conflicts directly influences long-term economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) complement this by arguing that institutional features, such as expropriation risk and rule of law, emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political dynamics, thereby affecting economic development trajectories. Both scholars emphasize that institutions evolve in response to power struggles, with implications for sustaining economic growth over time. The colonial origins of comparative development further illustrate this dynamic, where institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for colonizers constrained long-term growth potential in formerly poor regions. This historical evidence underscores that institutional quality is a pivotal factor in economic development, as institutions that protect property rights and reduce transaction costs foster sustainable growth. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) converge on the idea that institutional arrangements must align with economic needs to enable technological adoption and capital formation, with the Bank of England’s creation exemplifying how secure property rights reduced transaction costs and spurred industrialization. The role of ethnic fragmentation in African institutions presents a counterpoint, where Hopkins (2009, p.22) argues that institutional congruence with indigenous structures is essential for effective governance and growth, suggesting that institutional design must account for local social dynamics. This divergence highlights the tension between universal institutional frameworks and context-specific adaptations, with the colonial legacy providing empirical evidence that institutions shaped by historical power dynamics can either enable or hinder economic progress. The historical comparison of colonial institutions reveals that institutions designed for short-term political gains often impede long-term growth, while those that prioritize inclusive property rights and rule of law foster sustainable development. Thus, institutions remain the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as their capacity to balance political power with economic needs determines the trajectory of development.
