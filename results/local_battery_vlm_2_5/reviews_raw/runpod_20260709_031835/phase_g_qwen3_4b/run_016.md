How institutions shape long-run economic growth remains a contested intellectual frontier, with profound implications for understanding how societies achieve sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions act as the primary driver of growth or whether other factors, such as ideas, political stability, or historical accidents, dominate this process. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political institutions, particularly those enabling enforceable contracts and property rights, are foundational to economic development, while Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasize institutions as critical mediators of growth, especially in contexts of historical inequality and colonial legacies. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights how technological progress requires institutional shifts to overcome constraints of traditional social structures, suggesting that institutions evolve in response to economic transformation. These perspectives collectively underscore institutions as pivotal yet context-dependent forces, with their role varying across historical and geographical settings.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) identify institutional features such as expropriation risk and property rights enforcement as equilibrium outcomes that shape long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes how secure property rights, evidenced by the Bank of England’s establishment, reduce transaction costs and foster economic expansion, directly supporting Acemoglu et al.’s framework. This convergence highlights that institutional quality, particularly the rule of law and property rights enforcement, is the specific mechanism through which institutions drive growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contextualize this by arguing that institutional paths vary across societies due to historical inequality, where elites maintain power through extractive institutions that limit broad-based economic opportunities. (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) underscores that these institutional differences, rooted in colonial legacies, explain divergent growth trajectories. The evidence collectively establishes that institutional features like expropriation risk and rule of law are not merely historical artifacts but active determinants of economic outcomes, with their evolution directly tied to the political and economic power dynamics of societies. Institutions, therefore, function as the critical conduit for sustained economic development through their capacity to enforce property rights and mitigate expropriation risks.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) demonstrates that institutional frameworks, particularly property rights, serve as the critical mechanism for long-run economic growth through their role in enabling entrepreneurial activity and reducing transaction costs. This claim is supported by the analysis of historical contexts where institutional evolution directly influenced economic outcomes, such as the Anglosphere's trajectory compared to France's constrained development (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.8). The paper's argument emphasizes that the interplay between ideas, institutions, and historical contingencies shapes economic trajectories, with McCloskey noting that the absence of influential bourgeois economic actors in France hindered its growth despite advanced technological capabilities. This perspective aligns with the broader literature on institutions as the fundamental cause of economic development, underscoring how specific institutional configurations can catalyze or impede sustained prosperity. The evidence collectively reveals that institutional quality is not static but dynamically responsive to the interplay of political and economic forces, thereby reinforcing the causal link between institutional structures and long-run economic outcomes. Institutions, therefore, remain indispensable for fostering environments where innovation and capital accumulation can thrive.

Kuznets contends that developed nations' policies create institutional barriers that hinder long-run economic growth in less developed regions, a claim that resonates with subsequent scholarship. (Kuznets 1973, p.4) and (Stern 2004, p.3) identify how technological advancements, such as steam power, require institutional shifts incompatible with traditional practices like family enterprise or slavery, thereby obstructing growth trajectories in regions where such transformations remain unaddressed. This dynamic is further contextualized by (Clark and Jacks 2007, p.27), which demonstrates that the costs of coal, critical for industrialization, would have significantly constrained economic expansion in pre-industrial England without institutional adaptations to facilitate trade. Similarly, (Allen 2001, p.1) reveals that historical divergences in European wage growth were partly shaped by institutional inertia, with economic leaders maintaining income stability while others experienced declines. These patterns collectively underscore a mechanism where institutional rigidity, perpetuated by policy choices of advanced economies, impedes the adoption of growth-enhancing technologies in less developed contexts. Consequently, the interplay between institutional inertia and policy-driven obstacles represents a critical pathway through which developed nations' actions can either catalyze or stifle the economic development of less developed regions.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) identify that economic policy in sub-Saharan Africa historically operated through rudimentary crop-based bartering systems, a structural condition that constrained women’s economic agency and technological adoption. This gendered institutional framework, as documented in Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), limited women’s control over cash-crop production through shifting labor allocation patterns, while men increasingly monopolized decision-making in export-oriented sectors. Nunn (2008, p.3) corroborates that slavery-induced institutional fragmentation in African societies hindered long-term development, with domestic warfare and raiding disrupting the emergence of formal economic structures. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further illustrates how institutional fragmentation in early modern Europe similarly impeded skill transmission, a mechanism that Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) link to the persistence of gendered labor division in agricultural economies. Goldin (2006, p.3) demonstrates that shifts in female labor participation, driven by educational access and institutional reforms, directly contributed to economic growth in industrializing societies, contrasting with the gendered institutional constraints observed in sub-Saharan Africa. Hopkins (2019, p.10) contextualizes these dynamics within broader historical shifts, noting that postmodern historiographical frameworks have often obscured the role of gendered institutions in economic development. These findings collectively establish that gender dynamics and institutional structures operate through parallel pathways to economic growth without one being a downstream consequence of the other. The interplay between gendered labor patterns and institutional design thus presents a distinct causal mechanism that complements rather than subsumes institutional explanations for long-run growth.

Institutional structures fundamentally drive long-run economic growth through their role in shaping political and economic equilibrium, as demonstrated by North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). These scholars identify institutional configurations as critical mechanisms that influence economic outcomes by establishing constraints on political power and enabling sustainable capital markets, thereby facilitating growth. North (1989, p.9) emphasizes that institutions such as the Bank of England reduced transaction costs by securing property rights, directly linking institutional design to economic development (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further argue that institutional features like property rights enforcement emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political dynamics, which in turn affect long-term growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The colonial origins of economic disparities highlight how institutions, particularly those established during European colonization, have historically constrained growth in less developed regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.4) notes that technological advancements require institutions compatible with industrialization, underscoring the interdependence of institutional frameworks and economic progress (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Hopkins (2009, p.22) adds that institutional congruence with indigenous social structures enhances governance effectiveness and promotes growth, while misalignment leads to corruption and regional instability (Hopkins 2009, p.18). These findings collectively establish that institutions are the primary driver of long-run economic growth, with their design and evolution directly impacting economic outcomes through political and market mechanisms. The divergence between North and Weingast's focus on political commitment and Acemoglu et al.'s emphasis on equilibrium institutions reveals a nuanced understanding of institutional causality, but the colonial evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1) provides a clear empirical foundation for institutional differences as the root cause of growth disparities.
