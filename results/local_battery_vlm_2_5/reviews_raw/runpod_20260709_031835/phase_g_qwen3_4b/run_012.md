Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remain a pivotal intellectual battleground, with scholars debating whether institutional structures consistently drive sustained prosperity or whether alternative factors such as historical accidents or resource endowments play a more decisive role. The tension between institutional efficiency and contextual complexity defines this discourse, as evidenced by Austin (2008, p.24) and Ogilvie (2007, p.9). Austin (2008, p.24) identifies institutions as 'the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth' in a logically coherent framework, yet cautions that re-examining the 'Reversal of Fortune' thesis reveals a more intricate causality when applied to African economic history. Similarly, Ogilvie (2007, p.9) highlights how institutions like craft guilds are often described as 'adequate and even progressive' rather than strictly efficient, emphasizing that their enduring presence stems from overcoming transactional obstacles to benefit the entire economy. These perspectives converge on the centrality of institutions while underscoring their contextual dependencies. The scholarly debate thus hinges on whether institutions function as universal engines of growth or as contingent configurations shaped by historical and social conditions. This tension between institutional universality and contextual specificity sets the stage for evaluating how institutional frameworks interact with broader economic dynamics.

North (1989, p.9) identifies institutional quality as a direct driver of long-run economic growth through the Bank of England's historical role in securing long-term property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001) and Acemoglu et al. (2002) establish that institutional features like property rights enforcement emerge as equilibrium outcomes that shape economic performance across historical contexts. Ogilvie complements this by demonstrating that institutions function as 'adequate and even progressive' systems that overcome transactional obstacles without requiring strict efficiency. These findings collectively establish that institutional quality directly influences long-run economic growth through its role in facilitating transactions and reducing uncertainty. The interdependence of institutional frameworks, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.9), underscores that institutions cannot be isolated but must be analyzed within broader economic and political contexts. This dynamic illustrates how institutional quality evolves in response to political dynamics, often prioritizing short-term rents over long-term growth. Consequently, institutional quality serves as a critical pathway for sustained economic development by enabling societies to overcome transactional barriers and foster productive engagement.

The divergence lies in how institutions are measured and contextualized: while North (1989, p.9) identifies financial institutions as catalysts for reducing transaction costs, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that institutional features explain significant income differences across former colonies through historical pathways. The empirical evidence from cross-colonial comparisons in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutions, particularly those regulating economic interactions, drive long-term growth patterns through historical legacies. Thus, institutions function as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with their historical evolution and political constraints shaping economic outcomes.
