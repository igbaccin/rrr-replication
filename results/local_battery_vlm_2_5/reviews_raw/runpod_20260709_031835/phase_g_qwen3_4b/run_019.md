Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth remain a pivotal intellectual battleground, with scholars debating whether institutional frameworks can consistently drive sustained prosperity across diverse historical contexts. The tension between institutional efficiency and path dependence underscores a critical divergence: some argue institutions must be inherently efficient to foster growth, while others emphasize their role as adaptive responses to power dynamics and historical contingencies. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) establish that political commitment to institutional rules is foundational, yet Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) caution that equilibrium institutions may prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth, reflecting a deep conflict over institutional design priorities. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights how technological advancement and institutional evolution are interdependent, suggesting that growth requires institutional transformations compatible with modern economic demands. This literature reveals a live disagreement over whether institutions act as autonomous drivers of growth or as contingent outcomes of political and economic power struggles.

This historical pattern, documented by Allen (2001, p.1) and Hopkins (2009, p.22), reveals how institutional evolution has been a prerequisite for sustained economic progress. The interdependence between technological change and institutional adaptation, as Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Stern (2004, p.3) identify, underscores the necessity of institutional frameworks that can accommodate evolving economic structures. For instance, the Kongo kingdom’s ability to foster trade through centralized governance and well-developed markets, as Nunn (2008, p.3) illustrates, exemplifies institutions that enable technological integration and long-term growth. Fragmented institutions persist in regions with weak ethnic cohesion, as Hopkins (2009, p.22) notes, and this can hinder the adoption of growth-oriented policies. These dynamics highlight how institutions serve as the conduit through which technological and economic transformations are realized, thereby shaping long-run growth trajectories. The historical evidence indicates that institutions that evolve in tandem with technological and economic shifts are fundamental to sustained economic advancement.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) establish that institutional features like property rights enforcement and rule of law critically shape long-run economic growth by enabling industrialization and income disparities across regions. Their analysis of colonial legacies reveals how extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for dominant groups, constrain development opportunities for marginalized populations. This mechanism operates through institutional features such as expropriation risk, which Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) identify as an equilibrium outcome that directly influences economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) further demonstrates that secure property rights, exemplified by the Bank of England’s creation, catalyze economic growth by reducing transaction costs and fostering capital accumulation. These institutional dynamics, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note, are deeply rooted in historical contexts where colonial policies shaped the initial distribution of institutional power. The evidence collectively underscores that institutions function as upstream determinants of growth, with their evolution directly impacting the capacity for sustained economic advancement. Institutions that prioritize long-term capital markets and secure property rights, as North (1989, p.1) argues, are essential for overcoming the constraints of extractive systems and fostering inclusive development. Thus, the institutional environment acts as a conduit for technological and economic transformation, fundamentally shaping long-run growth trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.24) demonstrates that institutional inequality directly shapes long-run economic growth through resource allocation mechanisms, as evidenced by the reversal of fortune thesis (Austin 2008, p.24); Ogilvie (2007, p.9) complements this by highlighting institutional interdependencies that facilitate market access (Ogilvie 2007, p.9). These findings collectively reveal that institutional inequality operates as a critical conduit for growth, with its impact on resource distribution determining economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19) further illustrate this by documenting how women’s economic roles in sub-Saharan Africa are constrained by institutional inequalities, leading to diminished resource allocation capabilities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.19; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Thus, institutional inequality functions as a pivotal factor in economic development, where the interplay between resource allocation and market access underpins sustained growth trajectories.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) demonstrate that institutional policy directly shapes long-run economic growth trajectories in Africa, where the interplay between policy interventions and institutional evolution determines development outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). Their analysis reveals that historical policy choices have significantly influenced the institutional foundations of African economies, with implications for sustained growth pathways (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.18). This stream of scholarship underscores how institutional policy acts as a critical antecedent to institutional development, thereby influencing economic performance through the mechanisms of resource allocation and market access. Thus, institutional policy serves as a foundational driver of economic growth, with its impact on institutional development determining long-term prosperity.

Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth is a consensus among scholars, with North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasizing that political institutions shape economic outcomes through rule enforcement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) corroborate this by demonstrating how institutional features like property rights and rule of law influence growth trajectories. The centrality of institutions in economic development is further reinforced by Ogilvie (2007, p.9), which shows that even when institutions appear inefficient, they often maximize economic welfare through collective benefits. Austin (2008, p.24) emphasizes that institutions influence growth and are shaped by historical and contextual factors. Despite this shared understanding, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, prioritizing short-term rents over long-term growth, while North (1989, p.9) stresses that institutional innovation, such as the Bank of England, directly reduces transaction costs and fosters economic expansion. The historical evidence from colonial contexts, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.1), provides a clear resolution: institutions evolved in response to specific historical conditions, with European colonization policies creating divergent institutional frameworks that explain persistent income disparities. Thus, institutions remain the foundational driver of economic growth, with their historical evolution and adaptive capacity determining long-term prosperity.
