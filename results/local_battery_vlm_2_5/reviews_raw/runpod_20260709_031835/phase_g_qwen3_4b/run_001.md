Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim that has profound implications for understanding how societies develop and sustain prosperity. The intellectual stakes involve assessing whether institutional structures such as property rights, political accountability, and rule of law primarily drive economic advancement or whether factors like technological innovation or human capital dominate the growth process. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize institutions as the root cause and those who argue that institutions are outcomes of economic development rather than its cause. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) argue that political liberties and representative government institutions are deeply intertwined with economic growth, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that institutional features emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics. This tension underscores whether institutions are the starting point for growth or a consequence of it.

North and Weingast (1989, p.28) identify political liberties as foundational to economic growth through constitutional mechanisms, while North (1989, p.9) demonstrates how the Bank of England reduced transaction costs by securing long-term property rights. These institutions, by embedding political commitment to rule of law, enable capital accumulation and sustained market development. The Bank of England's 1694 establishment exemplifies how institutional design transforms economic activity by mitigating the time dimension of property insecurity, a critical factor for long-run growth. This mechanism, where political institutions enforce rule of law, directly channels resources into productive investment rather than short-term political maneuvering. As North (1989, p.1) emphasizes, institutions that stabilize economic activity are distinct from neoclassical assumptions about population and savings, underscoring their role in shaping growth trajectories. The interplay between political commitment and institutional design thus creates a feedback loop where secure property rights attract capital, fostering economic expansion. Consequently, constitutional institutions serve as the cornerstone of growth by anchoring economic activity in durable political commitments.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson establish that institutional features, including property rights enforcement and rule of law, directly channel institutional quality into long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their empirical analysis of colonial origins reveals how institutions shaped economic outcomes through historical paths of institutional development, with expropriation risk and rule of law acting as critical determinants of growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). This framework demonstrates that institutions emerge as equilibrium outcomes, reflecting power dynamics between politically dominant groups and economic actors, thereby influencing the distribution of economic gains across societies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Consequently, institutions that prioritize long-term economic development over short-term political rents become pivotal in fostering sustainable growth, as evidenced by the differential impacts of colonial policies on contemporary income levels.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth requires institutional shifts incompatible with pre-industrial social structures, as technological advancements demand new property rights frameworks and contract enforcement mechanisms. This historical institutionalist perspective, which traces institutional evolution through technological adoption, is exemplified by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8), both of whom demonstrate how institutional pathways influence long-run development trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.22) further contextualizes this by showing that institutional congruence with preexisting social structures, such as ethnic and religious affiliations, can either facilitate or hinder growth, depending on the degree of alignment between contemporary institutions and historical social patterns. These findings collectively underscore how historical institutions act as the mechanism through which societies transition from pre-industrial to industrial economies, enabling the capital accumulation and technological progress necessary for sustained growth. The colonial legacy, as noted in prior sections, illustrates this mechanism through the differential impacts of institutional design on contemporary income levels.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) identify that African economic performance has been markedly worse than other regions, yet their empirical analysis reveals that many African countries have experienced periods of rapid growth comparable to historical precedents. This stream of scholarship highlights how gaps in national accounting data have obscured the potential for institutional development to drive long-run growth, rather than focusing on the causes of slow economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.18). The evidence underscores that understanding the risks of regression in economic progress remains critical for assessing whether recent transformations can sustain growth trajectories, even as current data limitations hinder precise causal attribution. Despite the challenges in measuring institutional quality across African economies, the findings suggest that institutional design and historical legacies continue to shape growth outcomes. This insight aligns with the broader literature on institutional quality, emphasizing that the capacity for sustained growth requires overcoming historical constraints through robust institutional frameworks.

Institutional design fundamentally shapes long-run economic growth, as evidenced by North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). Their work demonstrates that political institutions, particularly those governing the enforcement of economic rights, directly influence growth trajectories through mechanisms like property rights and rule of law. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) emphasize that institutional frameworks must balance political control with economic development, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics. These perspectives converge on the necessity of robust institutions for sustained economic progress, with North (1989, p.9) highlighting how specific institutional innovations, such as the Bank of England, reduced transaction costs and catalyzed long-term growth. The colonial legacy, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), reveals that institutions designed to maximize short-term rents for dominant groups often hinder long-run development. This divergence underscores a critical tension: institutions that prioritize immediate political control over long-term economic stability can impede growth, as seen in the extractive equilibrium institutions that emerged from colonial policies. The historical evidence from the colonial era, where institutions were shaped by European powers to serve specific interests, provides a clear case study of this conflict. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and North (1989, p.9) jointly illustrate that institutions must evolve to balance political and economic imperatives, ensuring that growth is not constrained by short-term political gains. This synthesis confirms that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as their structure directly determines the capacity for sustainable development.
