Institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth represent a pivotal intellectual claim with profound implications for understanding historical and contemporary economic development. The debate focuses on whether institutional structures such as property rights, governance mechanisms, and rule of law serve as primary catalysts for sustained prosperity or whether factors like geography, technology, or human capital dominate the causal chain. This literature reveals a live disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the indispensable foundation for growth, while others emphasize the role of exogenous shocks or alternative drivers. Evidence from North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscores the political dimension of institutional causality, noting that regimes must be committed to enforcing rules to facilitate economic activity. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how institutional features emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by political power dynamics. These perspectives collectively suggest that institutions are not merely correlative but constitutive of growth pathways. The question of institutional causality remains unresolved, yet the scholarly consensus leans toward institutions as the critical determinant of long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.9) demonstrates that historical institutions exert a causal influence on long-term economic outcomes through their role in shaping institutional frameworks. This perspective, supported by (Ogilvie 2007, p.9) and (Austin 2008, p.24), explains how institutional interdependencies such as the interplay between labor markets, credit systems, and social structures create conditions that either facilitate or hinder economic development. For instance, (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) emphasizes that pre-industrial institutional systems in Europe, like the community-based welfare structures of England, functioned as interlinked networks that collectively advanced economic growth. Similarly, (Hopkins 2009, p.18) highlights that ethnic fragmentation can undermine institutional effectiveness unless contemporary institutions align with indigenous structures, thereby reinforcing growth-oriented policies. These findings collectively underscore that historical institutions are not static but dynamically influence economic trajectories through their embeddedness in broader institutional ecosystems. The interplay between institutional design and historical context, as documented by (Hopkins 2019, p.10) and (Hopkins 2019, p.3), further illustrates how shifts in historiographical approaches have shaped our understanding of institutional causality in African economies. When institutional frameworks are robustly integrated with historical realities, they become more likely to sustain long-term growth. This historical continuity in institutional causality provides a foundation for understanding how institutions fundamentally drive economic outcomes across time.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson establish that institutional features such as property rights enforcement and rule of law critically shape long-run economic growth through equilibrium mechanisms (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Mokyr 2005, p.2). Their analysis demonstrates that institutions emerge as equilibrium outcomes shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritizing short-term rents over sustainable development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic explains why certain colonial institutions, which maximized European colonists' extraction, hindered long-term growth in formerly poor colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). The colonial origins of economic disparities, therefore, reflect institutional legacies that continue to influence development trajectories. Modern economic historians corroborate this view, noting that pre-Industrial Revolution economic activity was far more complex than the 'no growth before 1800' assumption suggests (Mokyr 2005, p.2). Institutional quality, as defined by these equilibrium features, becomes a pivotal determinant of economic outcomes across historical contexts. When institutions are structured to maximize long-term growth potential, economies achieve sustained development. This institutional legacy underscores the causal role of institutions in economic outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that modern economic growth requires institutional transformation, as technological advancements are incompatible with pre-industrial social structures such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery. This institutional shift, which Kuznets (1973, p.4) emphasizes, is foundational to sustained economic development. The cluster of evidence (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8) collectively demonstrates that institutional evolution directly shapes long-run growth by enabling the adoption of complex technologies and market mechanisms. Together, these sources establish that institutional evolution is not merely a byproduct of growth but an active driver that structures long-term economic trajectories. The evidence thus underscores that institutions, through their capacity to facilitate technological adoption and market integration, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.28) establish that political institutions directly shape long-term economic growth by enabling the enforcement of economic rights through constitutional structures. Their analysis of England's institutional evolution demonstrates how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of financial markets reduced transaction costs while securing property rights across generations. This institutional commitment to rule of law and market stability, as documented in North (1989, p.9), catalyzed the English financial revolution, which transformed economic activity through secure property rights and long-term capital allocation. The evidence reveals that political institutions, by mitigating the risk of expropriation and fostering trust in market transactions, become the cornerstone of sustained economic development. Political stability thus emerges as a critical prerequisite for institutional evolution, as institutions that protect economic rights generate the conditions necessary for capital accumulation and technological innovation. This mechanism underscores how constitutional frameworks directly influence the trajectory of economic growth through their role in securing property rights and enabling market integration.

Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that the historical production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding undermined institutional development in pre-colonial Africa, creating conditions where economic integration with external markets remained constrained. This finding aligns with van Zanden (2009, p.19), who identifies that formal institutions such as guilds in early modern Europe facilitated skill transmission across generations, thereby enabling sustained human capital accumulation. Together, these sources highlight how institutional frameworks shape long-run economic outcomes by mediating the relationship between social structures and market integration. Goldin (2006, p.3) further clarifies that institutional shifts, including the expansion of educational access and labor market reforms, catalyzed significant improvements in human capital formation, particularly for women, demonstrating that institutional evolution directly influences productivity trajectories. McCloskey (2015, p.9) underscores the role of institutional change in reconfiguring economic incentives, noting how the absence of institutional frameworks in certain regions stifled technological adoption and capital accumulation. These mechanisms collectively reveal that institutional change functions as a critical antecedent to economic growth by establishing the structural prerequisites for capital formation and technological advancement. The evidence underscores that institutional evolution, rather than being a byproduct of economic development, actively drives long-run growth through its capacity to facilitate market integration and human capital development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) demonstrate that institutional factors in African economies critically shape long-run growth trajectories, with their analysis revealing how historical institutional structures influence contemporary economic performance. This perspective aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.19), which highlights the persistent role of women in agricultural production despite evolving economic structures, showing how institutional frameworks affect labor allocation and resource control. Clark and Jacks (2007, p.27) further illustrate that institutional choices, such as the adoption of coal for industrialization, have long-term consequences for economic development, emphasizing the role of technological pathways in shaping growth. Allen (2001, p.1) provides evidence that institutional divergence in European cities during the early modern period contributed to long-term economic disparities, underscoring the role of institutional evolution in setting development trajectories. Stern (2004, p.3) complements this by noting how shifts in industrial composition and urbanization patterns reflect institutional adaptations to economic development, with implications for growth sustainability. These findings collectively indicate that institutional factors are not merely outcomes but active drivers of economic development, with their historical configurations determining the pathways of long-run growth. The evidence underscores that institutional evolution, rather than being a byproduct of economic development, actively drives long-run growth through its capacity to facilitate market integration and human capital development.

Institutional evolution remains the cornerstone of long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by North and Weingast (1989, p.28) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48). These findings collectively affirm that institutional configurations directly shape economic trajectories through their capacity to facilitate market integration and human capital development. The persistence of institutional frameworks, however, reveals a nuanced tension between their role as drivers and constraints of growth, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlighting how equilibrium institutions may prioritize rent extraction over long-term development. This divergence underscores the critical interplay between institutional stability and economic outcomes, as evidenced by the colonial origins of comparative development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.1). The historical comparison of European colonization policies demonstrates that institutions emerge from specific political and economic contexts, thereby resolving the tension through their adaptive capacity within evolving economic systems. This synthesis confirms that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with their historical configurations determining the pathways of sustainable development.
