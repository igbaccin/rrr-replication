The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can profoundly influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, enforcing property rights, and facilitating market exchanges. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations, such as cultural factors or geographical determinants. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are central to economic growth by influencing the incentives for investment and productivity. North and Weingast also emphasize the critical role of institutions, particularly those governing public choice, in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The debate is further complicated by scholars like Ogilvie who suggest that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, might better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This debate is not just academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable development and reducing poverty. The central question remains: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth, and what are the key factors that determine the effectiveness of these institutions? Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, alongside Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North identifies the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement as key factors in reducing transaction costs, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework, by ensuring the security of property rights, fosters a long-term capital market essential for sustained economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by highlighting how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutional frameworks in colonies. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, by promoting secure property rights and effective governance, laid the groundwork for long-term economic growth. Conversely, in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which hindered economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are shaped by politically powerful groups and may not encourage economic development. This implies that the design of institutions to maximize rents for colonial powers, rather than long-run growth, can have lasting negative effects on economic development. Therefore, the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to influence the economic trajectories of former colonies today.

The development of institutions that ensured credible commitment and secure property rights was a key factor in this growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the political commitment to these rules was essential for economic development, as it provided a stable framework for economic activity. This institutional framework allowed for the growth of capital markets and the development of economic exchange, which were critical for long-run economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by highlighting that the initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality in New World societies influenced the evolution of institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, were key to understanding pre-industrial Europe's economic institutions. The authors suggest that the development of economic institutions was deeply rooted in the conflicts over resource distribution, which shaped the institutional frameworks that governed economic activities. This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations, instead highlighting the role of power dynamics and social conflicts in institutional development. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions. This adjustment indicates that the evolution of institutions was influenced by the complex interplay of local economic and political forces. For example, colonial governments in Africa, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, often found it in their interest to encourage the expansion of indigenous commodity production. This shows that the institutional frameworks were responsive to the specific economic and political contexts in which they operated. The flexibility of these institutional frameworks in response to local conditions underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and contextual factors that shaped economic institutions. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions highlights the need to consider the broader social and political dynamics at play in economic development.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly influenced economic development through the interplay of exogenous factors and local initiatives, shaping institutional change. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share a finding that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development was not uniform. The British, for instance, were more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for economic advancement. This institutional shift, driven by colonial policies, created incentives for investment in education, which was essential for development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide a nuanced view of this dynamic. They show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, demonstrating that colonial policies could foster economic growth under certain conditions. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This variation underscores the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change. This interplay highlights the importance of understanding the specific conditions under which colonial policies could promote economic development, as well as the role of local initiatives in mitigating the negative impacts of colonial rule.

This perspective posits that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to fully harness the potential of technological innovations. This interplay between technological and institutional progress is crucial for long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, underscoring the role of the sovereign state in peacefully managing such conflicts.
