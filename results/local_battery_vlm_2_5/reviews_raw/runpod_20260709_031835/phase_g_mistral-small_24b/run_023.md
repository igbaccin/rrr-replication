The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate. The live disagreement revolves around the primacy of institutions versus other factors such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influence current economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks. Others contend that the focus on institutions overlooks the complex interplay of historical periods and paths, suggesting that institutional theories need to account for the dynamic nature of economic development. Additionally, there are rival explanations that emphasize distributional conflicts and the role of beliefs in shaping economic institutions. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive economic growth, and how these interactions evolve over time. The causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth are complex and multifaceted, requiring a detailed examination of historical and contemporary evidence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not encourage long-run economic development. Where Europeans settled in large numbers, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutional differences are crucial for understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the persistence of these institutions is due to the political and economic interests of the ruling elites, who may resist changes that could undermine their power. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic trajectories, even long after the colonial period has ended.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) re-evaluate historical economic institutions. They challenge the simplistic views of causation in economic development by arguing that shared beliefs and institutions are not always the outcome of efficiency or economic growth. This is particularly evident in colonial contexts, where the interests of colonial governments and the responses of indigenous populations played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further complicated by distributional conflicts, which provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by these conflicts rather than by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and economic interests at play, which can lead to the adoption of institutions that may not always align with economic efficiency. This nuanced view of institutional development suggests that long-run economic growth is influenced by a complex interplay of historical, political, and economic factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlight that the efficient use of innovations is essential for long-run economic growth. This is because technological progress spurs economic growth, but this growth is not sustainable without complementary institutional progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the source of technological progress and the pace at which it advances differed over centuries and among regions of the world. This variation is crucial because it highlights that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; rather, it must be complemented by institutional adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this mechanism by recognizing the possibility of coordination failures, which can hinder the efficient use of technological progress. He suggests that policies to rectify these failures are necessary for sustaining economic improvement. This implies that the institutional environment must be conducive to technological progress and must minimize coordination failures to ensure that economic growth is sustained over the long run. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also underscores the importance of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that political stability is a necessary condition for sustained economic growth. This political stability, in turn, is crucial for the institutional adjustments that complement technological progress.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered more extensive formal education systems than other colonial powers. This positive impact was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African leaders and families. This educational development was crucial for long-run economic growth, as it contributed to the accumulation of human capital and the dissemination of knowledge. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the educational investments made during the colonial period had tangible economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of economic growth varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, which Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue influenced the evolution of institutions that impacted long-term development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, alongside North's analysis of the role of transaction costs (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England in 1694, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional development was a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated trading certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. This trade-off underscores the critical role of political institutions in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth by fostering a long-term capital market. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the success of these institutions was about the political factors that ensured their enforcement and adaptation. This institutional framework was crucial for the development of markets and economic growth. The evidence from capital markets highlights that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary, contrasting with the bureaucratic structures in France that did not hinder economic growth to the same extent (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The implications of these institutional changes extend beyond economic performance, as they also shaped the broader political and social landscape of seventeenth-century England. This institutional evolution underscores the profound impact of political and economic institutions on long-run economic growth.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This adverse effect is mediated through a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. The slave trade affected intergroup trust by shaping external environments like the rule of law, and through internal norms of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development is a historical artifact that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes through the enduring effects of mistrust.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of institutions established during colonial periods, such as those in Africa, demonstrates how initial conditions can have lasting effects on economic development. For instance, the extractive institutions set up by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, continue to influence current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The historical legacy of institutions is further evident in the case of Africa, where the slave trade's impact on trust and economic development persists despite the end of colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). These findings highlight how initial institutional frameworks can have profound and enduring effects on economic trajectories. This shared finding is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
