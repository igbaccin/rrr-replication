The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. The stakes are high as scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of economic growth or if they merely facilitate growth driven by other factors. The live disagreement centres around whether institutions are the key determinant of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or population density, play a more significant role. One camp argues that institutions fundamentally shape economic outcomes by influencing investment, property rights, and market exchanges. Another camp posits that institutions are important but not the primary determinant, suggesting that factors such as population density and geographic endowments significantly influence economic growth. The debate is further complicated by the historical context, with scholars examining how colonial policies and institutional changes have shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. This review will explore these competing views, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. A critical theme is how institutions are shaped by and shape economic growth, and how this dynamic has unfolded over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established varied institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions focused on resource extraction and low mortality encouraging investment-friendly institutions. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development. For instance, colonies with extractive institutions, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, experienced stagnation, while those with inclusive institutions, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw robust economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their profound impact on economic trajectories, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33). This institutional design, driven by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, created a legacy that continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today. The institutional framework set by European colonizers remains a significant factor in determining the long-run economic growth of these regions.

This qualification highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes contribute to long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). The interaction between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and that technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where the resolution of growth-induced conflicts through effective institutional frameworks was pivotal. Kuznets illustrates this with examples from early industrialization periods, where the sovereign state played a crucial role in peacefully resolving conflicts that arose from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr further qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible, economic reasonableness, an aspect of institutional progress, is less so. This suggests that while technological advancements drive growth, the institutional environment must adapt dynamically to support and sustain this growth. Kuznets' observation that the pace of technological progress and economic growth varied across centuries and regions underscores the importance of contextual factors in this dynamic. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is essential for sustained economic growth, and the historical record shows that effective institutional frameworks can mitigate the costs of growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating long-term economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) focus on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the great divergence between Europe and Asia, examining historical periods and paths of institutional evolution rather than directly addressing institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This critique highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions adapt over time. Colonial administrations, for instance, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which shaped the outcomes of commodity production and fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15). This divergence was reinforced by the role of the service sector in economic growth, where merchant distribution and finance played crucial roles. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was not solely due to geographical factors but also resulted from significant investment in agriculture during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This investment, in turn, led to both high levels of economic development and low silver wages. The implications of these findings suggest that the institutional frameworks in Europe and Asia evolved differently, with Europe's institutional changes fostering economic growth more effectively. This divergence underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs, providing a more comprehensive view of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both argue that the colonial impact on African economic development highlights how institutional change was influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors during colonial rule. These scholars emphasize that African societies were not passive recipients of colonial rule; instead, local actors actively engaged with and shaped the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers. This agency is crucial because it shows that African societies actively participated in the institutional changes that occurred during this period. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered, suggesting that while African agency is important, the specific economic conditions and historical context are also critical factors in understanding long-term economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic divergence across colonial Africa, which can help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

The African slave trade, by fostering a culture of mistrust, has had enduring effects on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional theories of long-run growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, they argue, persists today and significantly impacts economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trade affected trust is multifaceted; it influenced both the external environment, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust. This internal mistrust can hinder economic development by reducing cooperation and social capital, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this by highlighting that the African slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period significantly longer than colonial rule. This long duration indicates that the effects of the slave trade on economic development are likely to be deep and pervasive. Other factors, such as colonial policies and recent economic events, also play significant roles. The enduring effects of the slave trade on trust and economic development underscore the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic conditions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in historical contexts and the evolution of property rights. This critique highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and the need to consider multiple factors, including indigenous economic and political action, which influenced colonial policies and institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15).

The enduring effects of historical events, such as the slave trade and colonial policies, on contemporary economic conditions underscore the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. The persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth, illustrates how institutions can hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is further supported by the observation that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The new economic history of Africa shows that good institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is evidenced by the fact that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The new economic history of Africa shows that the scope of development policy widened to incorporate what became known as 'governance', and policy recommendations allowed institutions and organizations to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is supported by the observation that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets is evident in the fact that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is further supported by the observation that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
