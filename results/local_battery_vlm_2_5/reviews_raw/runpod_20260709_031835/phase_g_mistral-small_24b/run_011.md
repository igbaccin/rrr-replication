The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. This question examines how institutional frameworks, such as property rights, legal systems, and governance structures, influence economic performance over long periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth, emphasizing how secure property rights and effective governance can foster investment and innovation. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and cultural beliefs also shaping economic outcomes. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development, and whether they evolve organically or are imposed through external forces such as colonialism. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complexity of economic growth and the multifaceted role of institutions. This complexity is evident in the interplay between the arguments that institutions drive growth directly and those that posit institutions as part of a broader set of factors influencing economic outcomes. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run economic development. In regions with low European mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions has had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies. The interaction between these colonial institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions. The influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic growth.

Distributional conflicts were a primary force shaping core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share this view, arguing that institutions were not merely efficiency-driven but were also deeply influenced by the struggles between different social groups. For instance, the persistence of serfdom in Europe and the craft guilds in New World societies can be attributed to the elite's efforts to maintain control and extract rents. This dynamic is evident in the New World, where the initial inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutions that favored the elite, thereby restricting competition and maintaining inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutional landscape, making it difficult for broader-based economic growth to take hold. The institutional design in these societies reflected the power dynamics and distributional conflicts, not purely economic efficiency. The persistence of these institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.3) observes, underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions are shaped by underlying power structures and distributional struggles.

Such institutional developments, like the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) emphasize that specific institutional changes, particularly those that enhance the security of property rights, were pivotal in fostering economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by a credible commitment to enforce property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment was critical for the development of markets and the sustained growth of the economy. The role of institutions in economic growth varies across different historical contexts.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes the critical role of technological innovations and the dissemination of knowledge in fostering economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use, illustrating that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns. This compatibility necessitates a resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs, underscoring the importance of the sovereign state in peacefully managing such transitions. In this framework, the sovereign state, underpinned by loyalty and a sense of community, plays a crucial role in resolving these conflicts and ensuring sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Kuznets 1973, p.6). This perspective suggests that economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is essential for long-term economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges the possibility of coordination failures and the need for policies to rectify them, indicating that the growth of economic reasonableness is contingent on effective institutional frameworks (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the multifaceted nature of long-term economic growth, where both technological innovations and institutional frameworks are indispensable for sustained development.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge conventional views of African economic performance by highlighting higher per capita incomes in the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This revisionist perspective underscores the availability of substantial economic data from the colonial era, enabling a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic development. By reconstructing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Broadberry and Gardner further elucidate that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, with variations in growth patterns influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This highlights the multifaceted nature of African economic development, where both internal and external factors played crucial roles. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly, suggesting that the impact of these factors was not uniform across different regions. This variability underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence and their relation to long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This culture of mistrust is a direct result of the historical trauma inflicted by the transatlantic slave trade, which created deep-seated mistrust among African communities. The mechanism through which this mistrust operates is multifaceted, involving both internal and external factors. Internally, the slave trade fostered a norm of mistrust within communities, as individuals became wary of one another due to the constant threat of capture and enslavement. Externally, the legacy of the slave trade has left a lasting impact on the rule of law and the trustworthiness of institutions, further eroding social trust. Austin demonstrates that the colonial administrations' responses to indigenous economic and political action shaped institutional prescriptions, which in turn influenced the development of trust within these communities (Austin 2008, p.15). This historical context is crucial for understanding the contemporary levels of social trust in Africa, as the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence social dynamics and economic development. The implications of this mistrust are profound, affecting various aspects of societal functioning, including economic development, international trade, and political institutions. The persistent mistrust in African societies is a significant barrier to economic growth, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and cooperation.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the creation and enforcement of property rights, which are fundamental to economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, can hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, which often prioritize short-term gains over long-term development, are a direct result of historical and political contexts. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is essential for fostering secure property rights, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England illustrates how political factors can significantly influence economic development. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, as the changes in England's political landscape led to the development of secure property rights and a robust capital market (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The legacy of these institutional changes is evident in the long-term economic performance of countries, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development.
