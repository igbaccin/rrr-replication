The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergence in economic outcomes across regions and over time. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One key position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic development. This view is supported by scholars who emphasize the impact of institutional quality on economic performance. Another position contends that the impact of institutions is often overstated and that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, are more determinant. This debate is important because it informs policy decisions and theoretical frameworks in economics. The institutions-first position often emphasizes the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes, highlighting how extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers can have lasting negative effects. The institutions-first position also underscores the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argued that the development of financial institutions and instruments was pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was a critical factor in the long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The success of these institutions was also evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights facilitated economic exchanges. The political factors underpinning economic growth were crucial, as the regime's commitment to enforcing rules was a critical component of this institutional development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) noted that the degree to which the regime was committed to these rules significantly influenced economic growth and market development. However, the institutional changes were not universally revolutionary, as evidenced by the similar economic performance of France before the French Revolution. This suggests that while institutional development was crucial, other factors also played a significant role in economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European mortality rates in these regions determined the type of institutions set up, whether extractive or growth-promoting. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, where the primary goal was to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In contrast, low mortality rates encouraged European settlement and the development of institutions that enforced the rule of law and protected property rights. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these institutions over time meant that former colonies with extractive institutions experienced lower economic growth compared to those with growth-promoting institutions. The long-term effects of these colonial institutions are evident in the current economic performance of these regions, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic development. This institutional design, rooted in colonial policies, continues to shape the economic outcomes of former colonies today.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) highlight that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust within African societies. The mistrust persists and manifests in reduced levels of trust among contemporary African populations. This mistrust is not merely a response to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. The mechanism through which the slave trade affected trust is complex, involving both external environmental factors and internal norms. Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) emphasize that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as influential as external factors in shaping current trust levels. This enduring mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a crucial factor in fostering economic growth and development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape African economic outcomes by perpetuating a culture of mistrust that hinders long-run economic growth.

The role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth is pivotal, as technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for efficient use of innovations (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both underscore that technological advancements are about the institutional frameworks that facilitate their adoption and diffusion. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in how technological progress requires concomitant institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts and integrate new innovations into the economic fabric. Kuznets highlights that modern economic growth is contingent on the resolution of growth-induced conflicts, which is facilitated by the modern national state playing a crucial role in peacefully managing such transitions (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, is instrumental in ensuring that technological progress does not lead to excessive costs or long-term political weakening. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The growth of economic reasonableness, which Mokyr describes as far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is essential for long-term economic growth. This means that while technological progress can spur economic growth, it is the institutional framework that ensures this growth is sustainable and beneficial in the long run. This interaction between technological and institutional progress is key for understanding how economic growth is maintained over extended periods.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and (Simson 2019, p.2)) challenge the prevailing narrative of African economic performance by re-evaluating the region's economic history. They argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding that challenges the notion of Africa as a region of persistent underdevelopment. This reassessment is supported by recent work in African economic history, which has demonstrated the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era and beyond. This data reveals that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. These variations underscore the importance of contextual factors in shaping economic outcomes, a point echoed by Simson. Simson (2019, p.2; 2019, p.11) argues that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly portrayed. The data reveal that while subsidies and military spending increased, this was largely a response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent, rather than a result of rent-seeking behavior. This perspective qualifies the notion that post-independence policies were uniformly detrimental to economic growth, suggesting instead that the outcomes were more nuanced and dependent on specific political and economic contexts. The interplay between local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions highlights the complex nature of economic growth in Africa, where institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics, rather than focusing on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how colonial policies and labor dynamics shaped economic outcomes in Africa. Colonial administrations, driven by fiscal security and the need to maintain stability, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, highlighting the adaptive nature of colonial policies. This suggests that the economic impact of colonial rule varied based on local conditions and the specific policies implemented by colonial administrations. Austin (2008, p.15) highlights how colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This adaptive behavior of colonial administrations illustrates the complex interplay between colonial policies, local economic conditions, and long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either growth-promoting or extractive institutions in different colonies. The presence of settlers who established institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment fostered economic development, whereas extractive states that transferred resources to the metropole hindered it. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) highlights that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are pivotal for economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth.

The adaptive behavior of colonial administrations in response to local conditions further illustrates the dynamic nature of institutional influence on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that colonial governments often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, particularly when settlers were absent. This adaptive behavior underscores the importance of local economic conditions in shaping institutional outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The political commitment to rules and their enforcement created a stable environment conducive to economic development.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between population density, prosperity, and the policies pursued by Europeans. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4), who show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The long-run effects of colonial institutions are thus shaped by a complex interplay of economic, political, and social factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underpinned by a shared finding that institutions matter (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a subject of disagreement. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may not prioritize long-run growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize the importance of credible commitment to rules and their enforcement. The historical evidence of the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9), which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, settles this disagreement. This empirical comparison shows that institutions fostering secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for long-run economic growth.
