The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules like constitutions and informal norms, shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives, resource allocation, and transaction costs. The stakes are high: a robust understanding of how institutions drive economic growth can inform policy, guide development strategies, and shed light on the historical trajectories of nations. A live disagreement exists between those who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who see them as secondary to other factors, such as geography or culture. Proponents of the institutional perspective argue that well-designed institutions can foster growth by providing secure property rights, enforcing contracts, and promoting investment. Meanwhile, those who emphasize other factors contend that institutions are shaped by underlying geographical or cultural conditions, or that they operate through other mechanisms such as technological innovation or demographic factors. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or merely a facilitator of other, more fundamental determinants. This question is particularly salient when considering the divergent economic paths of nations with similar geographic endowments but different institutional frameworks.

The institutional perspective is championed by authors like Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33), who argue that the type of institutions established in colonies by European settlers significantly impacted long-run economic outcomes. They contend that extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to the metropole, hindered economic development in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast. In contrast, settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to higher income per capita. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who posits that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were critical to economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. North's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2001, p.27) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) view that institutions matter by shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic behavior. This stream of scholarship underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth, emphasizing that the type of institutions established can have enduring effects on a nation's economic trajectory.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth. This institutional framework underpinned economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and encouraging specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework, which included the commitment to enforce property rights, was a critical factor in England's economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of their effectiveness. This underscores the importance of considering the specific institutional frameworks and their enforcement mechanisms when analyzing long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights that technological progress is a critical factor in economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on the role of technological advancement by noting that epochal innovations have been the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations require significant institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate their efficient use. Mokyr (2005, p.49) echoes this idea, suggesting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This highlights the complex interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks in driving economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further underscores this complexity by noting that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, emphasizing the importance of a strong political fabric in society.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) share the view that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, leading to better economic outcomes by the early twentieth century. The British colonial rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education by introducing new social and political institutions. This shift influenced African political leaders and families to invest in formal education, driven by cost-benefit calculations that reflected the new institutional landscape (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of these institutions created new opportunities for economic advancement, thereby fostering a more educated population. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also highlight that the growth patterns were intricately linked to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while British colonial rule facilitated educational development, the broader economic context, including trade and political institutions, also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Therefore, the impact of British colonial rule on economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa was multifaceted, involving both educational improvements and broader economic factors.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is shared by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman, who both argue that the institutional structures of pre-industrial societies were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and resource distribution among different social groups. In pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom and the prevalence of craft guilds can be understood as outcomes of distributional conflicts rather than as efficient responses to economic needs. Ogilvie notes that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by the power struggles between elites and the masses. This aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis, which shows that in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. The persistence of these institutions in Europe, despite their apparent inefficiency, can be attributed to the entrenched power of elites who benefited from maintaining the status quo. This dynamic is further illustrated by the role of merchant guilds, which, despite their restrictive practices, were able to maintain their influence through political alliances and control over key economic resources. The distributional conflicts that underpinned these institutions had lasting effects on economic development, as they created barriers to innovation and economic growth. However, while these institutions may have hindered economic efficiency in the short term, they also provided a degree of stability and predictability that was crucial for long-term economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) report, “These differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recentiy attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally (North, 1988; Engerman and Sokoloff, 1997; Coatsworth 1993, 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, 2000; Engerman, Haber and Sokoloff, 2000).”

These institutional differences, shaped by European policies, persisted and had profound effects on the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. This institutional design, driven by the interests of the colonizers, had lasting impacts on economic development. However, these institutions did not merely reflect geographic factors; instead, they were a direct result of European policies aimed at resource extraction. This institutional legacy continues to shape economic development in former colonies, underscoring the critical role of colonial institutions in long-run economic growth.

The literature on institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa focuses on specific interventions and their impacts (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. In this view, colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to indigenous economic and political actions. Austin (2008, p.2) further shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions based on local incentives, illustrating a dynamic interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions. The focus on 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment, is crucial for development. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the effectiveness of these institutions can vary significantly depending on local contexts and historical paths. This variability underscores the need for tailored institutional reforms that account for the specific historical and economic conditions of African countries. Understanding these dynamics is essential for designing effective poverty reduction strategies that align with the unique institutional landscapes of African nations.

Institutions are the foundation of economic growth. In his seminal work, North (1989, p.9) asserts that institutions are critical to economic growth. He argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is a key factor in economic growth. This decline in transaction costs reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further emphasize the role of institutions in economic growth by arguing that the differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions underscores the long-term impact of colonial policies on economic development. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) share a finding that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.9)(Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The specific type of institutions matters. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) also emphasizes the role of institutions in economic growth, but he argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.2) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) share a finding that institutions shape economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6)(Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also emphasizes the role of institutions in economic growth, but she argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She shows that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that these beliefs can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
