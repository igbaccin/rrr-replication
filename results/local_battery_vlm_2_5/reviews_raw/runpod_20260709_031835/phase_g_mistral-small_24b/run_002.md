The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate in the field of economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic progress over centuries. The live disagreement revolves around the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological innovation. One position emphasizes the critical role of institutions in providing the necessary framework for economic activities, while another position argues that institutions are merely one among many factors, and that their impact is often overstated. A third position holds that institutions are shaped by underlying social and political dynamics. The interplay between these perspectives highlights the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth and underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate in different historical and cultural contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, are the key drivers behind the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and colonial Africa. They reveal that institutions such as serfdom and guilds in Europe, and customary law in Africa, were shaped by power dynamics and conflicts over resource distribution. These institutions were deeply influenced by the struggle between different social groups to secure their interests. The codification of customary law in colonial Africa, for example, reinforced patriarchal alliances and marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This dynamic highlights how institutional design can be a tool for maintaining social hierarchies and distributing resources along lines of existing power. Furthermore, the persistence of inequality in the New World societies can be attributed to the institutions that evolved to support elite interests (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that these institutional frameworks, while advantageous to elites, also restricted competition and hindered broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The downstream implication is that distributional conflicts, by influencing institutional design, can perpetuate inequality and shape long-run economic growth trajectories.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Technological progress is a fundamental force that propels economic growth forward. This process is facilitated by institutions that reduce access costs to knowledge and foster an environment conducive to innovation (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The growth of useful knowledge is intrinsically linked to the evolution of institutions that support and disseminate this knowledge. This interplay between technological progress and institutional development is crucial for sustained economic growth, as it ensures that innovations are created and effectively integrated into the economic fabric.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share a finding that targeted institutional reforms can significantly alleviate poverty in Africa. One specific mechanism through which this occurs is the improvement of public goods provision. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This suggests that even ethnically diverse societies can develop progressive institutions capable of upholding the rule of law. The effectiveness of these reforms depends on the broader economic context. For instance, the distribution of income and the real wage levels in colonial Africa highlight the potential for future growth, provided that historical conditions do not cast a structural effect on long-term welfare development. This nuance is important because it underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across different regions of Africa. The implications of these findings are significant for policy makers seeking to reduce poverty through institutional reforms. By focusing on the provision of public goods and addressing historical and geographical conditions, targeted institutional changes can lead to substantial economic improvements.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with van Zanden, examine historical economic divergences and performances across different regions and periods (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; van Zanden 1999, p.1). They argue that economic growth and development were significantly influenced by factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and institutional change, which varied across regions. Broadberry and Gupta highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This underscores the role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed. Broadberry and Gardner show that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, with variations influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Van Zanden contends that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, challenging optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This indicates that while economic development may have occurred, it did not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all. This highlights the complexity of economic growth and the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that drive it.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in Africa's underdevelopment (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic development. Nunn identifies the slave trades as a critical factor in shaping Africa's subsequent economic trajectory, with a particular focus on the institutional and social changes they engendered. This perspective emphasizes the importance of historical context and the long-term effects of past events on economic development. Nunn's analysis suggests that the slave trades disrupted traditional economic and social institutions, leading to a legacy of underdevelopment that persists to this day. Frankema's work on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa during British colonial rule shows that the impact of colonial institutions varied significantly depending on the colonial power. This variation in institutional impact highlights the complexity of colonial legacies and their differential effects on economic development.
