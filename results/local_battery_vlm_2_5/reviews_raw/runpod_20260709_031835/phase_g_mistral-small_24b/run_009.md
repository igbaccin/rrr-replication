The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious topic among economists and economic historians. At stake is the fundamental understanding of what drives economic development over extended periods. One position emphasizes the importance of extractive institutions, which are designed to benefit a small elite at the expense of broader economic development. These institutions can persist over long periods, significantly affecting economic outcomes. Another position argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and political struggles, rather than purely economic efficiency. These conflicts can lead to the adoption of institutions that may not be optimal for economic growth but serve the interests of powerful groups. A third position focuses on the interaction between institutions and other factors, such as population density and prosperity, in determining economic outcomes. These differing views highlight the complexity of institutional dynamics and their role in economic growth. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding how institutions shape economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies that can promote sustainable development. The extent to which institutions can be reformed to foster economic growth remains a critical question. The role of institutions is particularly evident in the context of colonial legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that the creation of financial institutions and secure property rights underpins long-run economic growth. The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs was a direct result of increased security in the time dimension of property rights. This security was crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This dynamic highlights the critical role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which in turn drives economic growth. This commitment is essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which are fundamental for sustained economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be influenced by other factors, such as bureaucratic structures and political commitment.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial rule was influenced by European mortality rates, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of colonies. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern is exemplified by colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the institutional framework was designed to maximize the rents for European colonists rather than foster long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further argue that these extractive institutions persisted over time, influencing current income levels by inhibiting economic development. This persistence is not merely a byproduct of geographic factors but a direct result of the institutional legacies left by colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al., highlights the enduring impact of these institutions on economic outcomes. The interaction between colonial institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). However, the institutional framework alone does not fully account for the economic disparities. This nuanced understanding underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes in the context of colonial legacies.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that economic growth is primarily driven by the growth of useful knowledge and technological advancements. This is supported by Mokyr (2005, p.49), who emphasizes that technological progress alone is insufficient; institutional progress is also necessary to mitigate coordination failures and ensure the sustainability of economic improvements (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Mokyr 2005, p.54). For example, the development of scholarly societies and formal institutions reduced access costs to knowledge, fostering an environment conducive to technological advancements. These institutional adjustments, however, are not always straightforward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that conflicts arising from technological changes must be resolved peacefully to avoid weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that the success of technological advancements in driving economic growth is contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and manage societal changes effectively.

Distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue. These conflicts led to the emergence of institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. The institutions that emerged were not necessarily efficient, but rather were the result of power struggles and bargaining among different social groups. These distributional conflicts were driven by the interests of elites who sought to maintain their privileged positions. This dynamic is evident in the institutions that evolved in New World societies, where greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, as Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate. However, the role of institutions in perpetuating inequality is not limited to pre-industrial Europe or colonial societies. The persistence of inequality in modern societies can also be attributed to the institutions that have evolved from these historical roots. Ogilvie's call to transform institutions from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3) underscores the need for a deeper understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes. This perspective highlights the importance of considering the historical and social contexts in which institutions emerge and evolve.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) highlights the significance of historical and cultural factors in shaping contemporary economic outcomes in Africa. Austin argues that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which is relevant to understanding the impact of colonial administrations and the slave trade on current economic structures. Colonial administrations, influenced by the presence of European settlers and the need for fiscal security, often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior of colonial administrations underscores the dynamic nature of institutional evolution in response to local conditions, which can significantly influence long-term economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal norms of mistrust, developed as a result of the slave trade, are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust, cultivated through generations, hinders the development of trust, a crucial element for economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). Understanding the historical and cultural factors that shaped these institutions is essential for comprehending contemporary economic disparities and for formulating effective policies to address them.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into institutional reforms and poverty elimination among the bottom billion people by examining the historical antecedents of contemporary African economies. This education infrastructure, in turn, influenced the development of human capital, which is essential for economic growth. The new economic history of Africa thus offers insights into how colonial legacies continue to influence institutional quality and economic performance today. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies was more robust than previously thought, with levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions matter, they are not the sole determinants of economic outcomes. The nuanced understanding of Africa's economic history provided by these studies underscores the importance of considering both historical legacies and contemporary factors in formulating effective institutional reforms.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia, particularly before 1800, was driven by distinct patterns of economic development within Europe and Asia. Broadberry and Gupta show that the great divergence was already underway before 1800, (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) while van Zanden demonstrates that the focus of economic development in Europe shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This shift was marked by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity in developing parts of north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This implies that while institutional changes and economic development were occurring, they did not uniformly translate into improved living standards across Europe.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, underscoring the overlooked role of women in economic growth within institutional frameworks. The institutional changes in land access and customary law during colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women, who were relegated to less productive lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop cultivation. This gendered division of labor was codified through customary law, which was shaped by patriarchal alliances under colonial rule. These institutional changes not only limited women's economic contributions but also shaped the broader economic landscape. The codification of customary law under colonial rule privileged men in access to land for cash crops, propelling economic growth in sectors like cocoa production. However, Simson (2019, p.11) notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa also played a significant role in economic growth, with increased military spending and debt burdens reshaping expenditure priorities. This shift in expenditure reflects how post-independence policies, influenced by regional instability and domestic dissent, altered the economic trajectory. The interplay between gendered institutional changes and post-independence policies underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and enforcement of property rights and economic policies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted current income levels, as extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies persisted and hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is echoed by North, who emphasizes the critical role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, which lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, highlights how institutional changes can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Additionally, North points out that as economies grow, the development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement will lead to falling transaction costs, further stimulating economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This interplay between institutions and economic growth underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in long-run economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonial policies and their impact on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional perspective is supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, which influenced the economic trajectories of these regions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is further supported by the work of Hopkins, who emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). By examining the new economic history of Africa, Hopkins shows that institutional reforms can inform policy and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illustrated by the distributional conflicts and shared beliefs that influence institutional development. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups, rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, as highlighted by Ogilvie, can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, further underscoring the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This synthesis of evidence from different historical contexts and periods shows that institutions matter in economic growth, as they influence the development and enforcement of property rights and economic policies, which in turn shape long-run economic trajectories. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this debate is that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6).
