The central question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is a fundamental one in economic theory. It is a question that goes to the heart of understanding why some societies thrive while others struggle. The debate in economic development focuses on the role of institutions versus geographic or cultural factors in driving economic outcomes (Author, Year, p.N). One side of the debate posits that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run growth, arguing that certain institutional frameworks facilitate economic development by fostering secure property rights and encouraging investment. A competing view asserts that institutions alone do not drive growth; population density and prosperity also significantly influence it. Some scholars identify distributional conflicts as a key driver of institutional development, indicating that power struggles shape institutions more than efficiency considerations. To understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it is essential to explore how different scholars have approached this question, and how their findings complement or challenge one another. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They present the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as critical factors in this process. These developments significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also ensured the enforcement and adaptability of these rules, providing a stable environment for market development. The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, which underscores the importance of institutional changes in driving economic growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by highlighting how the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs. This, in turn, stimulates economic growth by increasing the volume of market exchanges. This nuanced view suggests that while institutions play a crucial role, other factors also contribute to economic development.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, contrasting with the view that institutions are the fundamental cause (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores that technological advancements, such as epochal innovations, are pivotal in sustaining long-term economic growth across regions and centuries. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate efficient use of innovations. This dynamic interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Mokyr further supports this by highlighting that institutional progress complements technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets adds that resolving growth-induced conflicts should not weaken society's political fabric. This implies that while technological progress is a driving force, the institutional environment must be robust enough to manage the societal changes that accompany economic growth. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is thus essential for sustained economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa had a profound and lasting negative impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This impact resulted from both the immediate disruption caused by the slave trade and the enduring cultural and institutional legacies it left behind. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists to this day and continues to hinder economic development. This mistrust is not merely a response to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained in the internal norms of individuals. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This suggests that the institutional environment, shaped by historical events like the slave trade, plays a crucial role in determining long-run economic growth. The extent to which these historical legacies can be overcome by institutional reforms is still an open question. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This indicates that the institutional environment, including factors such as government structure and market development, is critical for supporting economic growth. The impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic development underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions evolve and their role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty is not solely due to persistent structural impediments, as the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests. They jointly question the assumption that Africa's economic trajectory has been uniformly stagnant due to institutional failures. Austin identifies the 'reversal of fortune' thesis as oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The thesis assumes that institutions favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others, but Austin challenges this notion by highlighting that the institutional prescriptions were often responsive to indigenous economic and political action. For instance, colonial administrations in Africa adapted their policies in response to local incentives, such as when Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg further support this perspective by reconstructing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. They find that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This suggests that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa could help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This perspective implies that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth in Africa is more nuanced than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests, and that local economic and political actions played a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn and Wantchekon show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, prioritizing resource extraction over long-term economic development. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, stunted economic growth and left a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of these regions. Nunn and Wantchekon's work complements this view by demonstrating that the slave trade, facilitated by these extractive institutions, further impeded African economic development. The legacy of these institutions persists, as they continue to shape economic outcomes and perpetuate inequality. Acemoglu et al. further qualify this legacy by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even if they do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This insight underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts, not institutional efficiency, drive core economic institutions and the persistence of inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These scholars observe that the distribution of resources and power among different groups in society shapes the development of institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. For instance, Ogilvie notes that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were products of distributional conflicts rather than efficiency-driven processes. These institutions were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of elites, even if they were not the most efficient means of production (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This mechanism of institutional design based on distributional conflicts has a significant downstream implication: it leads to the persistence of inequality over time. Ogilvie also suggests that this perspective highlights the role of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions, challenging the idea that institutions are merely the result of efficiency-driven processes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This insight underscores the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design, and how these dynamics can lead to the persistence of inequality.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted and complex issue. There is a shared finding that institutions are pivotal in determining economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). The underlying point of agreement is that institutions shape economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources, the protection of property rights, and the enforcement of contracts. This perspective is supported by North, who argues that the evolution of institutions that specify and enforce property rights is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth, arguing that institutions that bind rulers to rules are essential for long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by Austin, who shows that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the impact of institutions on the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their role in economic growth. This is shown by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9).
