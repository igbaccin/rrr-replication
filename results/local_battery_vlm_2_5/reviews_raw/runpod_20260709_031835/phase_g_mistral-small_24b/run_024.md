The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. This question is central to debates about the drivers of economic development, with scholars disagreeing over whether institutions are the primary cause of growth or merely one factor among many. Some scholars argue that institutions are pivotal, emphasizing their role in creating stable property rights and fostering economic activity. Others contend that institutions are secondary to other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs. The debate centers around the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth and the extent to which they are more critical than other factors. This literature review addresses these debates by examining the causal role of institutions in long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in fostering economic growth is well-documented in the literature. Institutions create a framework for economic activity by defining property rights and enforcing contracts, which are essential for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective underscores the idea that institutions shape economic outcomes by providing a stable framework for economic activity.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the long-term economic growth of former colonies was significantly shaped by colonial institutions, which were themselves influenced by European policies. The divergent strategies that Europeans employed in their colonies led to the establishment of vastly different institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize short-term rents for European colonists, which did not foster sustainable development. This extractive institutional framework persisted over time, leading to significant differences in current income per capita among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) highlight that these institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, who often resisted institutional changes that could threaten their political power or fail to directly benefit them. As a result, the economic outcomes in these colonies were profoundly influenced by the institutional legacies of colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which shaped the opportunities for industrialization and long-run development. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes over the long term.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic growth through reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in this process. The Bank of England's establishment in 1694 marked a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. The political institutions that specified and enforced property rights were equally important. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, demonstrating how political factors underpin economic growth and market development (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political commitment to these rules was critical, showing that the regime's credibility in enforcing property rights was essential for economic growth. Institutions like the Bank of England and the political structures that supported them were not just economic facilitators but also political commitments that ensured long-term economic stability and growth. This perspective underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes, highlighting how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can drive long-term economic development.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis is often framed as a persistent poverty narrative, where African poverty is attributed to structural impediments that have lasted for centuries. The thesis does not account for the variability of historical economic paths. This variability was a function of the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Ogilvie, argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality tend to advantage elites and restrict competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2), which can in turn affect long-term economic growth. They propose that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in the New World societies led to the evolution of institutions that favored the elite, such as those governing property rights and contract enforcement. This perspective challenges the broader claim that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that the variation in factor endowments across New World societies led to different degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. These institutions, designed to maintain the elite's advantage, restricted competition and hindered economic growth. This institutional inequality, as Ogilvie suggests, can be understood as a result of distributional conflicts, where institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective suggests that economic development is deeply intertwined with the distributional conflicts that shape institutions.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental to long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, thereby facilitating modern economic growth. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own. The sustainability of economic improvement relies on complementary institutional progress, which helps to rectify coordination failures and mitigate the erosion of rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without such institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements could be limited or even eliminated, underscoring the importance of institutions in supporting long-term economic development.

The African slave trade has had enduring effects on economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust within Africa, offering an alternative explanation to institutional theories of long-run growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust, cultivated over centuries, has profound implications for economic development. Nunn elaborates on this by highlighting that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a more significant and lasting impact on economic development than colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the efficiency of resource allocation and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that the institutions established by European colonists varied significantly, with some colonies, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, benefiting from institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Other colonies, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These divergent institutional frameworks had enduring effects on economic development, with extractive institutions inhibiting long-run growth. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework not only facilitated market exchanges but also ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing these rules, thereby underpinning economic growth. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks that support secure property rights and efficient market exchanges in driving long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the impact of colonial rule on African societies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the absence of centralized governments and well-developed trading networks in many African societies hindered their ability to support trade with Europeans, exacerbating the economic impact of the slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutions can evolve in response to economic and political pressures, shaping long-run economic outcomes. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. She contends that these institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which often prioritized rent extraction over economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Together, these studies demonstrate that institutions are not merely efficient mechanisms for resource allocation but are deeply influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which can significantly impact long-run economic growth.
