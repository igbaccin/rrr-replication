The central question in the study of long-run economic growth is how institutions shape economic outcomes over extended periods. Institutions are not merely the formal rules and regulations of society; they also encompass the informal norms and conventions that guide behavior. This question is intellectually significant because it addresses the fundamental drivers of economic development and the persistence of economic disparities. The live disagreement centers around the role of institutions in economic growth, with some scholars arguing that institutions are the primary determinants of economic performance, while others posit that geographic factors or cultural beliefs play a more significant role. Some scholars also argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may not prioritize long-run growth. This disagreement is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development and reducing global inequality. Understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is essential for designing effective policies that can promote sustainable economic development. The disagreement highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue. The type of institutions established in colonies was heavily influenced by European settlers' mortality rates. Where mortality was high, Europeans often established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional framework was not conducive to long-run economic growth as it did not encourage investment or enforce the rule of law in the colonies. In contrast, where mortality rates were lower, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment, leading to more robust economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to these institutional differences, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This interaction highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping economic growth, as the type of institutions established during colonial rule continues to influence economic outcomes today.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in this process. These institutions facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This was a direct reflection of the increased security of the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, establishing a political foundation for economic growth. This transformation was not merely about rules governing economic exchange but also about the institutions enforcing these rules and governing their change. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political regime's commitment to these rules is a critical factor in economic development. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which indicates that economic growth was significantly influenced by institutional changes rather than by other factors such as population or savings. The role of institutions in fostering economic growth is evident in the historical performance of England's capital markets, which outperformed those of France despite similar economic conditions. This suggests that the institutional framework in England was more conducive to economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic development.

Mokyr and Kuznets argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements and the dissemination of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets elaborates that technological progress requires institutional adjustments to integrate new innovations efficiently. This means that the economic landscape must adapt to accommodate technological changes, which in turn fosters economic growth. Mokyr supports this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic development, suggesting that the growth of knowledge is a primary factor in long-term economic improvement. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancements. Without this institutional support, the benefits of technological progress could be undermined by coordination failures and rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets further illustrates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic growth is sustainable. This means that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the institutional framework must also evolve to support and sustain this growth. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors must work in tandem to achieve long-term economic development.

Africa's slave trades have been argued to have a profound adverse effect on long-run economic development, challenging explanations that attribute underdevelopment solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade not only depleted Africa's human capital but also engendered a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines social cohesion and cooperation, which are crucial for economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) highlight that the slave trade's impact on trust is multifaceted, affecting both external environments and internal norms. The internal norms of mistrust, in particular, are at least twice as impactful as external factors, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trade runs deep within African societies. This internal mistrust can hinder collective action and coordination, which are essential for sustainable economic growth. Nunn notes that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, was far longer than the period of colonial rule, indicating that the slave trades had a more enduring impact on Africa's economic trajectory. The institutional framework necessary for economic development, such as centralized governance and well-developed markets, was disrupted by the slave trades, further impeding Africa's economic progress. The historical context of the Kongo kingdom illustrates that centralized governance and well-developed markets were crucial for supporting trade and economic development. This underscores the importance of robust institutions in fostering economic growth, a theme that resonates across different periods and contexts of African economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that colonial governments in Africa influenced economic activities and rents. The colonial state in Africa was not a passive actor, but an active agent that shaped economic outcomes through its policies and institutions. Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This was driven by the colonial state's desire for fiscal security and its response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial governments would always seek to minimize production to maximize rents (Austin 2008, p.15). This illustrates that colonial governments responded to local economic and political contexts. Colonial governments in Africa were not merely extractive institutions but were also responsive to local economic and political dynamics. This responsiveness could lead to policies that encouraged economic growth. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that the long-term impact of colonial policies on African economic development is complex and multifaceted. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions is not straightforward.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, in ways that were not merely efficient but were also driven by power dynamics between social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that in the New World, institutions evolved to advantage elites and restrict competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality. The mechanisms through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions are complex. For example, the persistence of guilds in medieval Europe can be attributed to the cultural beliefs that supported them, which in turn were reinforced by the power dynamics of the time. This dynamic is also evident in the New World, where institutions evolved to reflect the interests of elites, who used their political power to shape institutions that maintained their economic dominance. The downstream implication of this power dynamic is that institutions can become entrenched and resistant to change, even when they are not efficient. They argue that the initial conditions of a society, including its factor endowments and the power dynamics at play, can shape the institutions that evolve and, in turn, the trajectory of economic development. This highlights the importance of understanding the initial conditions and power dynamics that shape institutions, as they can have long-lasting effects on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the development trajectories of nations. Institutions, which encompass the rules and norms governing economic interactions, significantly influence the efficiency and effectiveness of these interactions. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This highlights the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further underscored by the role of distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to politically powerful groups rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This indicates that the design and enforcement of institutions are often influenced by the interests of powerful groups, which can lead to institutions that are not conducive to economic growth.

Institutional reforms can also play a crucial role in addressing poverty and underdevelopment. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, showing that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the specific institutional changes that have occurred over time.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth. North shows that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the examination of the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and their impact on the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.
