The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions are the rules and norms that govern behavior, and they can significantly influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and contract enforcement are crucial for long-term growth. Another position highlights the role of distributional conflicts and shared beliefs in shaping institutions, suggesting that these factors can override efficiency considerations. A third position focuses on the historical and geographical context, proposing that the interaction between institutions and specific historical and geographical factors is what drives long-run economic growth. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. The crux of the debate lies in whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as distributional conflicts and historical context, play a more significant role. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of their former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) detail how European colonizers implemented different institutional strategies based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in colonies with lower mortality rates, they set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The extractive nature of these institutions, therefore, stunted economic development in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) corroborate this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, align with the long-run economic outcomes of these colonies. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to impact economic performance today. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), further underscores the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic development.

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional evolution was critical for economic growth, as it facilitated long-term capital markets and increased market exchanges. The development of the English financial system, including the creation of the Bank of England, was a direct response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change reflects a broader political model where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, illustrating how political institutions can shape economic outcomes by providing secure property rights. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent. For example, the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not significantly hinder France's economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which in turn facilitates technological progress. This mechanism is crucial because it enables the efficient use and diffusion of innovations, which are the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by pointing out that while technological progress is more monotonic and irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is less so. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the sustainability of economic improvement also depends on complementary institutional progress. Therefore, although technological and intellectual advancements are fundamental, they must be supported by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Nunn, argue that historical factors, particularly the slave trade and the resulting institutional frameworks, significantly influenced economic development in different regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade extracted labor and shaped institutions that perpetuated inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman show that in societies with high initial inequality, such as those in Latin America, institutions evolved to favor elites, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. This institutional bias, rooted in historical inequalities, persisted over time, hindering economic growth and development. Nunn adds that the slave trade's impact on economic development was profound, with long-lasting effects that went beyond immediate labor extraction. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are evident in the institutional frameworks that emerged, which often favored the interests of those in power rather than fostering broad-based economic growth. This pattern highlights how historical inequalities and the institutions they engendered continue to shape economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon note that the slave trade also instilled a culture of mistrust, which persists and affects economic development today. This mistrust, cultivated through the slave trade, manifests in reduced social capital and weaker institutions, further impeding economic growth. The long-term effects of the slave trade on institutions and economic development underscore the enduring impact of historical factors on contemporary economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) both examine how colonial rule shaped African education and public finances, respectively. They both show that colonial policies significantly influenced the development of formal education and budget policies in sub-Saharan Africa. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African leaders and families, as the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift in perceived benefits was pivotal in shaping educational outcomes. The British colonial rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education compared to other colonial powers. The British administration's policies, including the establishment of mission schools, played a crucial role in fostering educational development. In addition to education, colonial policies also shaped budgetary decisions post-independence. The post-independence period saw significant shifts in budget policies, with military spending and debt servicing becoming major expenditures. Kenya, for instance, saw subsidies and interest payments peak at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, while Tanzania faced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure. This shift in expenditure patterns highlights how colonial policies continued to influence economic decisions long after independence. The impact of these budgetary shifts on economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa remains a subject of ongoing research.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Hopkins, highlight the significant economic contributions of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite their marginalization (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization was reinforced through colonial policies that restricted women's access to land and codified customary laws that disadvantaged them. This pattern of land allocation and legal codification not only limited women's economic opportunities but also entrenched gender inequalities that persisted long after the colonial era. Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule often failed to recognize or support women's economic activities, further hindering their contributions to economic growth. The implications of this institutional bias are profound, as they not only affected women's economic participation but also shaped broader economic policies that continue to influence African development trajectories.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) find that the literature on African poverty and economic performance in the early twentieth century has overlooked the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left a gap in understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This divergence is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. However, the focus on export production and foreign trade highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how these institutions interacted with local economic activities. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence, offers a promising avenue for future research to address these gaps and provide a more comprehensive picture of Africa's economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by collapsing different historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). He and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) jointly highlight that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by complex interactions between political interests and economic incentives, rather than being solely driven by efficiency considerations. Austin illustrates this through the behaviour of colonial governments, which adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions operate. However, Ogilvie notes that institutions should be more than a fashionable concept and should be integrated into the long-term research agenda of economic historians (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their study must be grounded in a broader historical and contextual understanding.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic interactions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that colonial institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, significantly influenced the long-run development of former colonies. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Furthermore, North (1989, p.9) illustrates how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is not solely about efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, challenging the view that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency. This aligns with Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2008, p.15) further illustrates how colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, leading to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) highlights the complex interplay between institutions, distributional conflicts, and economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have also played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.8) suggests that the quality of institutions, shaped by historical and political contexts, significantly influences economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is best understood through a historical and contextual lens. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and that they may not always encourage economic development. This is consistent with Hopkins's (2009, p.18) argument that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. These shared findings (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18) show that the historical and political contexts in which institutions operate are crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth.
