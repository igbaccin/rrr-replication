The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations for long-run economic development. While some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, others point to the significance of cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. The question of whether institutions are the most crucial factor in economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role, remains a contentious issue. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for policymakers seeking to foster sustainable development and for scholars aiming to unravel the complex interplay between economic and political factors. North and Weingast argued that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North argues that the evolution of specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were pivotal for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). By reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, these institutional changes facilitated a more efficient capital market and fostered economic expansion. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, dramatically reduced transaction costs and reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights. This institutional development was a product of fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, where specific rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further illustrate this point by highlighting the role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment is evident in the success of capital markets, which provide a clear indication of the institutional factors at play. The evidence from capital markets indicates that the institutional changes in England were fundamental to the country's economic performance (Author, Year, p.N). North and Weingast also acknowledge that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV did not significantly hinder economic growth in France (Author, Year, p.N). This suggests that while institutional frameworks are crucial, their impact can vary across different historical and political contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The role of institutions in fostering or hindering technological progress is notable. Mokyr and Kuznets assert that the accumulation of knowledge is fundamental to economic development, with Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) noting that technological advancements require institutional adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.6) also highlights that the benefits of technological progress depend on resolving growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs. This suggests that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, its impact is mediated by institutional factors that facilitate or hinder the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. Mokyr (2005, p.54) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures in technological progress, suggesting that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancements. This implies that while technological progress is crucial for economic growth, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that reduce access costs and prevent rent-seeking behaviors. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is therefore complex, with each factor influencing the other in the long run.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers of inequality and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also suggests that the persistence of certain institutions can be attributed to the shared beliefs of the ruling class, which reinforced their power and maintained the status quo. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies discussed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), where the institutional frameworks were designed to perpetuate the inequality that favored the elite. This nuanced understanding highlights the complex interplay between distributional conflicts, institutional design, and long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies by shaping their economic trajectories through the establishment of extractive or inclusive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In areas with lower mortality, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences persist, significantly affecting current income per capita. The specific outcomes of these institutions also depend on historical and cultural contexts, which can vary significantly across different societies. This nuanced understanding highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions, institutional design, and long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) have challenged the prevailing narrative of African economic history, showing that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the importance of historical economic data, which has been relatively scarce until recently. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence has enabled a more nuanced understanding of Africa's economic performance. This data reveals that economic growth in many African countries was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further emphasize the significance of real wage levels and trends in understanding the economic development of British African colonies. The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule provides a deeper understanding of the economic conditions and the potential for future growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation highlights the complex interplay between economic growth and the institutional and geographical conditions of African economies.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who highlight the enduring impact of historical factors such as the slave trade on contemporary economic and social conditions in Africa. One specific mechanism through which historical factors influence African economic development is the legacy of colonial policies. Austin demonstrates that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This adaptability suggests that colonial policies evolved based on local conditions and incentives. For example, in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production. This adaptability in colonial policies indicates that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was more nuanced than often portrayed. Nunn and Wantchekon qualify this perspective by emphasizing the persistent effects of the slave trade on contemporary African societies. They argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to affect economic development. This mistrust, in turn, influences the effectiveness of contemporary institutions, suggesting that the legacy of historical factors extends beyond immediate institutional frameworks. The interplay between historical factors, colonial policies, and contemporary economic conditions underscores the complex nature of African economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. The extractive institutions imposed in regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, differed from the more inclusive institutions set up in settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. These inclusive institutions enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to sustained economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North emphasizes the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs, which is essential for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century exemplify how institutional changes can lower transaction costs and facilitate economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are pivotal in this process, as they create an environment conducive to long-term capital markets and economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic conditions is further underscored by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon, who highlight the enduring effects of the slave trade on African societies. The mistrust fostered by the slave trade persists today, influencing the effectiveness of contemporary institutions and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with Austin's argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between historical periods and paths. Austin contends that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences of older industrial economies must be considered in the context of their long-term development (Austin 2008, p.2). The colonial policies that encouraged indigenous commodity production and fiscal security in the absence of European settlers illustrate how institutional prescriptions can adapt in response to local economic and political actions, shaping long-term economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15).

The distributional conflicts that shape core economic institutions are another critical aspect of this debate. Ogilvie argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency-based theories. Shared beliefs and cultural factors play a significant role in the institutional structure of societies, influencing economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast similarly highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The political factors underpinning economic growth include the institutions governing public choice and the enforcement of rules, which are crucial for the development of markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France shows that institutional changes were pivotal in driving economic growth, even when initial economic performances were comparable (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with historical factors, colonial policies, and contemporary economic conditions. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Austin contends that the institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences of older industrial economies must be considered in the context of their long-term development (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France shows that institutional changes were pivotal in driving economic growth, even when initial economic performances were comparable (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This evidence settles the debate by demonstrating that the type of institutions established and their evolution over time are crucial determinants of long-run economic growth.
