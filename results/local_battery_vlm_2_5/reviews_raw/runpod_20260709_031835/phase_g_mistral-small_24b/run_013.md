The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature, as scholars debate the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, such as geography or culture, or whether they are endogenous, evolving in response to political and economic pressures. Some scholars argue that institutions are the result of deliberate political choices, while others contend that they are the product of historical accidents and path dependencies. The stakes are high, as the answer to this question has significant implications for understanding the economic trajectories of nations and for designing effective policies to promote economic development. For instance, if institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors, then policy interventions may be limited in their effectiveness. If institutions are endogenous, policy may have more scope to influence economic outcomes. The debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, as well as the importance of considering the historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve. The legacy of colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, continues to influence economic outcomes in many former colonies today. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutions established by European colonizers varied widely, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design was driven by factors such as European mortality rates and the settler mortality rate, which in turn shaped the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies. For instance, in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions that hindered economic development. In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that promoted investment and economic growth. This institutional design had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies, as extractive institutions impeded the accumulation of human and physical capital, thereby stunting economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and not to geographic factors. These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes in many former colonies today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering economic growth. The Bank of England's establishment in 1694 and the introduction of new financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This reduction in transaction costs was crucial for the development of a long-term capital market and economic growth. The political model outlined by North highlights that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, illustrating how political institutions can facilitate economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights. These factors include the regime's commitment to property rights and the enforcement of rules, which were critical for economic growth. The implication is that secure property rights, facilitated by effective institutions, are essential for economic development.

The divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This is due to the service sector's role in facilitating economic growth during the early modern period. Pamuk (2007, p.23) explains that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. These changes enabled regions like Holland to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities due to their institutional flexibility. This institutional flexibility allowed for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing under weak monopolies, which contrasts with the strengthening of existing monopolies in regions like Tuscany. This institutional flexibility in northwestern Europe facilitated economic development and urbanization, which were key indicators of productivity and economic development in the pre-modern period. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this by noting that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that the benefits of early modern development were not uniformly distributed.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are crucial for sustained economic performance (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This highlights the importance of institutions in facilitating and sustaining technological progress. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) qualifies this by noting that while institutions like guilds played a role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, they also formed local cartels that restricted entry into trades. This dual role of institutions in both facilitating and hindering economic growth underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic performance. Institutions that support the growth of useful knowledge can drive economic growth, but their effectiveness is contingent on their ability to balance the transfer of skills with market accessibility.

Similarly, Nunn (2008, p.2) identifies the slave trades as a key factor in Africa's underdevelopment, distinct from more recent events like colonialism (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon further elucidate that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust. This dual channel highlights the enduring legacy of the slave trades on African societies.
