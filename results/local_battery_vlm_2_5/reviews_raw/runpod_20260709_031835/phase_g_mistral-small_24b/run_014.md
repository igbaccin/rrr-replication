The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one among several factors. Institutions are defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, and self-imposed codes of conduct). A central debate pits those who argue that institutions directly cause economic growth against those who contend that other factors, such as technology or factor endowments, are the primary drivers. Institutions are central to economic development because they influence how resources are allocated, how incentives are structured, and how conflicts are resolved. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if they are merely one of several factors. The debate is further complicated by the fact that institutions themselves are shaped by a variety of factors, including geography, culture, and historical events. The core of the disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions can be seen as endogenous or exogenous to the economic growth process. This debate has significant implications for policy and practice, as understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform strategies for development and economic reform. Institutions are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth as they provide the framework within which economic activity occurs. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, shared finding (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional development is critical for economic growth, as it enables a long-term capital market. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that institutions formed by politically powerful groups can be extractive, maximizing rents and not promoting long-run growth. This institutional design can hinder economic development, as seen in former colonies where European colonists established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources. This pattern suggests that the type of institutions established during colonization can have lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions likely to persist and significantly affect current income per capita.

The historical evolution of institutions governing public choice, as discussed by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12), is critical for understanding how secure property rights can foster economic growth. The development of institutions that ensure property rights and enforce contracts is central to economic growth. North and Weingast illustrate this through their analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the establishment of institutions that constrained the monarchy's power and secured property rights facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework allowed for the emergence of capital markets and the development of a stable economic environment. Ogilvie supports this view by emphasizing the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, highlighting their indispensable role in economic history. One specific mechanism through which institutions foster economic growth is by providing a stable framework for economic transactions. This stability encourages investment and innovation, as individuals and businesses can operate with greater predictability and security. Ogilvie adds that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth can be influenced by underlying social and political dynamics. Consequently, the type of institutions established and their evolution over time can have profound implications for long-term economic development.

Institutional frameworks, which evolve over time, are pivotal in shaping the trajectory of long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and knowledge-driven factors are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective is grounded in the idea that technological progress and the diffusion of knowledge create opportunities for economic growth. Kuznets elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. He describes epochal innovations as major breakthroughs in human knowledge that drive sustained growth over long periods. Kuznets also highlights the importance of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, institutional adjustments are necessary to harness its full potential. Mokyr recognizes that coordination failures can restrict the sustainability of economic improvement. Mokyr points out that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements might be frittered away by rent-seeking. This implies that while technological and knowledge-driven factors are primary drivers of economic growth, institutional frameworks play a complementary role in sustaining and maximizing these gains.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories. Both authors show that the institutions that emerged in these societies were highly unequal, favoring elites and restricting economic competition. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that this inequality was rooted in the initial factor endowments of the colonies, which led to extreme disparities in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The specific historical context of colonial settlement patterns and the resulting institutional frameworks played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic development. This historical context, characterized by extreme inequality and the exploitation of labor, had profound implications for the institutional frameworks that emerged in these societies. Nunn's analysis shows that the absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets in Africa hindered trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of these historical institutions persists in contemporary economic disparities, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide insights into how historical economic performance in Africa was tied to foreign trade and export markets. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, and that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective challenges the notion of persistent poverty in Africa. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence demonstrates that African economies were more dynamic than previously thought. These economies experienced fluctuations tied to global trade patterns. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the importance of external factors in shaping African economic development during this period. The absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and its relationship to other parts of colonial Africa.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800. In their examination of early modern economic growth, they show that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector arose from merchant distribution and finance, with the service sector playing a crucial role in economic growth (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Van Zanden supports this perspective, identifying a shift in economic development towards the North Sea countries during the seventeenth century, with England emerging as a leader after 1700 (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This shift is marked by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a broader economic transformation. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the distribution of economic growth. While Broadberry and Gupta highlight the uneven distribution of economic benefits, with skilled laborers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), van Zanden's analysis suggests that economic development did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living across Europe (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This disparity underscores the complexities of economic growth and its distribution, revealing that the benefits of early modern economic development were not evenly spread.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is highlighted by the divergent paths of development in European colonies, where extractive institutions in high-mortality regions persistently affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of these institutions, which often prioritized the extraction of resources for the metropole, illustrates how initial colonization strategies can have enduring economic consequences. This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they define property rights and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, for instance, led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights, which are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(North 1989, p.9) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long term.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the evolution of public choice institutions in seventeenth-century England, which fostered secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast argue that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, showing that political factors underpinning economic growth are crucial. The degree to which a regime is committed to enforcing rules and property rights is a critical factor in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al.'s analysis complements this perspective, showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This extractive nature of institutions is evident in the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.2)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) demonstrates that the design and enforcement of institutions significantly influence economic growth trajectories.

The persistence of inequality in New World societies is another key area where institutions play a pivotal role. Sokoloff and Engerman show that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The shared finding (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4)(Ogilvie 2007, p.12) reveals that institutions shaped by distributional conflicts and elite interests can perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, encompassing the design of property rights, the enforcement of rules, and the resolution of distributional conflicts. The historical evidence of the English financial revolution and the contrasting development paths of European colonies settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in economic growth, showing that secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for long-run economic development (North 1989, p.9)(Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
