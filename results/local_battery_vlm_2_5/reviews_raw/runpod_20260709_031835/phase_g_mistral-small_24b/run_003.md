The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the centrality of institutions in driving growth and those who propose alternative or complementary factors. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that govern behavior, including laws, property rights, and social norms. The crux of the disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and theoretical frameworks aimed at fostering economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions in colonies, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors to influence economic growth. The debate is far from settled, as scholars continue to explore the nuances of institutional impact on economic growth, seeking to understand the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence and the conditions under which they are most effective.

This evolution of secure property rights in England, as argued by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29), underscores the critical role that institutional changes can play in fostering economic growth. They highlight the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments as key factors in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional framework facilitated economic growth by enabling the development of a long-term capital market. North's analysis of transaction costs further illustrates how political institutions that specify and enforce property rights contribute to a growing economy, as falling transaction costs and increased market exchanges drive economic expansion. The emergence of these institutions decreased the costs per transaction and increased the aggregate volume of exchanges, reflecting the growing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast's focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforcing rules, underscores the importance of institutional stability in fostering economic development. The implication is that secure property rights, underpinned by effective institutions, are essential for sustained economic growth, as they create an environment conducive to investment and market expansion. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, particularly the establishment of the Bank of England, played a crucial role in this process.

Extending this idea to a global scale, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They suggest that European colonists established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In regions where mortality was high, colonists set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, often at the expense of local economic development. Lower mortality rates led to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) find that these institutional differences persist and significantly affect current income per capita. This implies that the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of their former colonies, shaping the economic outcomes of these regions for centuries to come.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures such as serfdom and guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that these conflicts, not efficiency concerns, drove the development of institutions that served the interests of powerful elites. These institutions often restricted competition and maintained inequalities, thereby influencing long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that shared beliefs, not efficiency, are integral to the institutional structure of a society. This is exemplified by the persistence of institutions that maximized rents for European colonists, which Acemoglu et al. (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) show were designed to benefit elites at the expense of long-run growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate that the factor endowments and initial conditions in New World societies led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that favored elites. These institutions, by restricting competition and maintaining inequalities, contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions should be viewed as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian, emphasizing the need to incorporate them into long-term research programs. This underscores the importance of understanding the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions and their impact on economic growth.

Building on this, Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the expansion of useful knowledge and technological progress. This perspective underscores the role of technological innovation as a key factor in economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial because they facilitate the integration of new technologies into the economic fabric, thereby sustaining long-term growth. This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that can adapt to and integrate new technologies effectively. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is vital for modern economic growth. This implies that the institutional framework must adapt to technological changes and manage the social and political tensions that arise from these changes.

This perspective is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who argue that historical patterns of economic growth and income levels in Africa and Asia challenge conventional narratives about these regions' economic development. Both papers contend that the economic performance of these regions was more robust than previously thought, with significant levels of per capita income and productivity in key sectors. The mechanism through which this growth was achieved involved a complex interplay between trade, institutional changes, and technological advancements. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes, as different institutional settings could either facilitate or hinder economic growth.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The presence of poor institutions is linked to Africa's dismal economic record, and good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered. This highlights the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the role of institutions in African economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Frankema 2012, p.15) describe the role of women and education in African economic growth as significant, particularly during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women's contributions to economic growth were substantial, as they were often responsible for food farming on marginal lands. This was further reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law that privileged men in access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This pattern of women's economic contributions was not limited to agriculture; it also extended to education. Frankema's work (Frankema 2012, p.1) shows that British rule facilitated the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education was influenced by both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family calculations, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule significantly impacted the educational landscape. Women and education played crucial roles in African economic growth, but their contributions were often constrained by patriarchal alliances and customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This highlights the complex interplay between gender, education, and economic development in colonial Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) both identify historical and cultural factors as pivotal in shaping African economic development, focusing on the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrators, in the absence of European settlers, often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with colonial interests. This indicates that colonial policies were not uniformly extractive but could be responsive to local economic and political dynamics. This responsiveness suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic but varied based on local contexts, which could influence long-term economic outcomes. This mistrust, stemming from historical exploitation, can hinder the formation of institutions that support long-term economic growth. The persistence of mistrust due to historical exploitation is a barrier to the development of institutions that support long-term economic growth.

Historic institutions, shaped by early European colonization strategies, laid the groundwork for modern economic structures. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to varied institutional outcomes. In some cases, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In others, they created rule-of-law-based institutions that encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional design, driven by initial colonization strategies, persisted over centuries and significantly influenced current income per capita. North (1989, p.9) underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments in the 17th century dramatically reduced transaction costs, fostering long-term economic growth. This historical precedent shows that the institutional legacy of early colonization continues to shape economic outcomes today. Austin (2008, p.2) complements this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which argues that wealthy regions became poor and vice versa, oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors influencing economic development. The thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, making it essential to consider the specific institutional contexts of each region.

The role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions is another critical factor. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that these conflicts provide a better explanation for the persistence of institutions like serfdom, community structures, and guilds in pre-industrial Europe. The shared beliefs and values within societies, such as those about political legitimacy and individual rights, significantly influence the adoption and persistence of institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-term growth. This highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further emphasizes the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, essential for development. The new economic history of Africa, which focuses on governance and institutional reforms, shows that good institutions matter for economic growth. The historical evidence and empirical comparisons of institutional design and economic outcomes in former European colonies reveal that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape long-term economic development. This ongoing influence is evident in the persistence of extractive institutions and the challenges faced by regions with a history of exploitative colonial practices. The specific historical evidence of colonial institutions, such as those documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), and the empirical comparisons of institutional design and economic outcomes, such as those provided by Hopkins (2009, p.18), settle the disagreement by showing the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development.
