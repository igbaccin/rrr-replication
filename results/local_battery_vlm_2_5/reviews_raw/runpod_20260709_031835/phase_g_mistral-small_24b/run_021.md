The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary economic policies. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who posit alternative explanations. Some scholars emphasize that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic development. This perspective is often associated with the work of Douglass North and his emphasis on the political delivery of secure private property rights (North 1989, p.6). Other scholars suggest that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs within societies play a significant role in shaping economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This disagreement underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need to carefully examine the evidence supporting each position. The debate around the role of institutions in economic growth is multifaceted, with scholars offering various causal mechanisms to explain how institutions influence long-term economic outcomes. One stream of research highlights the importance of colonial institutions in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century are cited as examples of institutional changes that led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin challenges the notion that colonial institutions were the sole determinant of economic growth, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). These scholars contend that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule were shaped by the interests of the colonial powers and the local populations, and that these frameworks had lasting effects on economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that the extractive institutions established by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates had a profound and lasting impact on the economic development of these areas. The colonizers' strategies varied significantly based on local conditions; in regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions were tailored to maximize short-term gains for the colonizers, which did not foster local development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) provide robust evidence that this institutional framework persisted, influencing current income per capita. The persistence of these extractive institutions can be attributed to the political and economic power dynamics of the time, where local populations had limited influence on institutional design. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) underscore this dynamic by arguing that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice are instrumental in fostering economic growth, especially through the establishment of secure property rights, which are crucial for economic development. This decline was a direct result of increased security in property rights, which in turn facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutional changes, including the trade of certain rights to Parliament, were crucial in resolving fiscal crises and laying the groundwork for economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in creating a stable environment where property rights were enforced, and economic rules were committed to, thereby fostering growth. This perspective is further supported by the observation that even when economic performance between countries was similar, institutional differences played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The intricate relationship between political stability and economic growth is clear: stable institutions that enforce property rights and commit to economic rules are essential for sustained economic development. However, this relationship is not without its nuances, as the complexity of bureaucratic structures can also influence economic outcomes, suggesting that the impact of institutions on growth is multifaceted and context-dependent.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that unequal institutions in New World societies favored elites through government policies, limiting competition and perpetuating inequality. These unequal institutions, which were shaped by the initial factor endowments of the colonies, led to societies with extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. The long duration of these slave trades created a legacy of mistrust and underdevelopment, which persisted even after the end of colonial rule. This mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show, is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The internal norms of mistrust, developed through centuries of exploitation and inequality, are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law. This suggests that the legacy of institutional inequality in New World societies is deeply ingrained and continues to shape economic outcomes long after the initial factors that created it have changed. The downstream implication of this institutional inequality is that it creates a self-reinforcing cycle of underdevelopment, where mistrust and inequality perpetuate each other, making it difficult for these societies to break out of the cycle and achieve sustained economic growth.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for social mobility and economic advancement. This mechanism suggests that the introduction of formal education systems during colonial rule could have long-term effects on economic development by fostering a more skilled workforce and enhancing human capital. This variation underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional context of each African country when assessing the impact of colonial rule on economic development.

Technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, driving sustained development over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both assert that the growth of useful knowledge underpins economic development. This technological progress involves institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets highlights that technological changes require complementary institutional adaptations to resolve conflicts arising from economic growth and to ensure that societal structures can accommodate these changes without long-term weakening of the political fabric. This is echoed by Mokyr, who argues that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is crucial for sustaining economic improvement. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional frameworks play a significant role in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements. However, Kuznets qualifies this by noting that the pace and sectors affected by technological progress vary across regions and centuries, indicating that the specific historical and institutional context matters greatly in determining the impact of technological advancements on economic growth. This variation underscores the importance of considering the particular institutional and ideological adjustments that different societies must make to fully realize the benefits of technological progress.

Ogilvie and Austin argue that distributional conflicts, along with institutions, are the driving force behind the development of key economic structures in pre-industrial Europe. They propose that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, cannot be explained by efficiency alone. The historical context of distributional conflicts is crucial. The craft and merchant guilds, for example, were about power struggles between different social groups. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional context in which these distributional conflicts played out. This perspective does not entirely negate the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Austin notes that the older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, institutions still play a role in economic growth, albeit one that is shaped by these conflicts. The intricate interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional development highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of economic growth, one that acknowledges the complex and multifaceted nature of institutional change. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment that fosters investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights how initial institutional frameworks can have lasting impacts on economic development. Similarly, North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore that institutions matter in economic growth, as they provide the framework necessary for long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the work of Austin and Ogilvie. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These insights show that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures, shaping long-run economic growth. Austin also highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, further illustrating the dynamic nature of institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is crucial for understanding how institutions can facilitate or hinder economic growth over time.

Despite the agreement on the importance of institutions, there remains a debate on the specific mechanisms through which they influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective contrasts with North's view that institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights how institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on their design and enforcement.
