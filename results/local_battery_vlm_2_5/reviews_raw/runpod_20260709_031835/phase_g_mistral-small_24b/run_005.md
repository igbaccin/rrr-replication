The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. Institutions, in this context, refer to the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and political interactions. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The debate is nuanced, with some scholars arguing that institutions are the critical factor, while others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a complex puzzle, with geographic endowments and cultural factors also playing crucial roles. The stakes are high, as understanding the mechanisms behind economic growth can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering development in less prosperous regions.

The debate hinges on the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. This is where the role of colonial institutions becomes particularly salient. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. The paper shows that European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive institutions, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences, shaped by the mortality rates of European settlers, persist today and significantly affect current income per capita. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also emphasize the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth, highlighting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic development. They argue that credible commitment underpins growth, suggesting that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange and how these rules are enforced. This theme of institutional endurance and its impact on economic growth is a thread that carries into the next section.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They argue that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The impact of these institutional differences is profound, as extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies persist and significantly affect current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. These findings suggest that the initial conditions set by colonial institutions have lasting effects on economic development, shaping the trajectory of growth in former colonies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically declined the cost of transacting and reflected increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a critical dimension to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This institutional change was not merely about economic exchange rules but also about the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. The degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules is a critical political factor. The institutional changes that North and Weingast describe were not so revolutionary after all. North (1989, p.6) shows that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. This development results in falling costs per transaction, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that secure property rights, facilitated by effective enforcement institutions, are pivotal for economic growth, as they lower transaction costs and foster market exchanges. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on colonial legacies and economic policies in Africa explores how historical governance structures shaped contemporary development trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both argue that the institutional frameworks put in place during colonial rule have enduring effects on African economies. One key mechanism through which these legacies operate is the development of formal education systems. British colonial rule, in particular, fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers. This is because the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families shifted with the implementation of new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were tied to new economic opportunities and social mobility. This pattern had a profound impact on the human capital development in the region, laying the groundwork for future economic growth. However, Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this by noting that post-independence budget policies, influenced by these colonial legacies, were not uniformly clientelist or unproductive. Instead, they often involved significant expenditure shifts, such as increased military spending in response to regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that while colonial institutions set the stage for certain development trajectories, post-independence policies also played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa generated a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting social and economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust manifests in both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative behavior and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. This suggests that while the culture of mistrust is a significant barrier to economic growth, there were also periods and conditions under which colonial policies could foster economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the institutional structures in New World societies significantly influenced economic development through their impact on inequality. They posit that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power characteristic of many New World societies from their inception led to institutions that favored elites. This concentration of power and resources restricted competition and hindered economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) expand on this by noting that the timing and scale of growth in export production, which was a critical driver of economic development, varied significantly due to factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) qualify this by emphasizing that the initial factor endowments, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created high living standards on average but also set the stage for extreme inequality. This initial inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved over time, affecting the course of development. These findings suggest that while institutions play a crucial role in economic development, the specific pathways through which they influence growth can be complex and multifaceted.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores the role of technological innovation in driving economic development, suggesting that the accumulation of knowledge and its efficient application are crucial for sustained growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to accommodate technological changes, emphasizing that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This adjustment is not merely about adopting new technologies but also about creating the social and political conditions that allow for their effective use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on the importance of the modern national state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring that economic growth is contingent on the state's ability to manage these conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) complements this by noting that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains made through technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological innovation is a driving force, it must be accompanied by institutional adjustments to ensure long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and by enforcing property rights, which are crucial for sustained development (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduces transaction costs, fostering an environment conducive to investment and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is evident in the historical development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, which significantly lowered transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is a critical factor in the long-term economic development of nations (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions not only govern economic exchanges but also ensure that the rules are enforced and can be changed in a manner that supports economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illuminated by the colonial experiences of different regions. European settlers adopted varied colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions in their colonies. Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States and Australia, encouraged investment and economic development, while extractive institutions in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The long-term economic impacts of these institutional differences are evident in the current income per capita of these regions, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories over the long run (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, which is attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their ability to influence economic outcomes over extended periods (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to affect the economic performance of former colonies, demonstrating the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Institutional reforms and the development of progressive institutions are crucial for economic growth and poverty reduction (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions matter significantly for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). There is a remaining disagreement over whether institutional changes alone can explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. While Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the primary factor, Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the detailed examination of the institutional changes and their economic impacts in different colonial settings, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This evidence shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had a direct and lasting impact on the economic trajectories of these regions, providing a clear resolution to the disagreement.
