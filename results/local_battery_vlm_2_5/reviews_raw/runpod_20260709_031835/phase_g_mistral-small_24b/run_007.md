Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms by which they exert this influence are subject to intense scholarly debate. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily drive growth through their impact on property rights and market efficiency, or whether other factors, such as cultural beliefs and political dynamics, are more decisive. One position emphasizes the role of institutions in securing property rights and reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. One perspective emphasizes the role of political and distributional conflicts in molding institutional outcomes, indicating that institutions often serve to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. Another perspective posits that the reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies stems from institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This debate is crucial for understanding the fundamental drivers of economic development and the conditions under which institutions can effectively support growth. The role of institutions in economic growth is thus a complex and multifaceted issue, with various scholars offering different perspectives on how institutions shape long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in 1694 significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The institutions created to address fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War, such as the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, exemplify how political institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. The resulting decline in transaction costs facilitated economic growth by enabling more efficient market exchanges and increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further argue that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which were essential for sustained economic growth. These institutional changes have varying implications. This point stresses the significance of credible commitment in institutional design, showing that economic growth depends on both the presence of institutions and the credibility of their enforcement.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied greatly, shaped by factors such as mortality rates and the settlers' ability to adapt to local conditions. In colonies where Europeans encountered high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions aimed at quickly transferring resources to the metropole. These institutions reflected the interests of politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, who aimed to maximize their rents. This extractive approach to institutional design had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) stress that these 'equilibrium institutions' were not intended to foster economic development. The persistence of these extractive institutions over time has been a critical factor in the long-run economic stagnation of many former colonies. For instance, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, the legacy of extractive institutions continues to hinder economic growth by limiting investment and discouraging productive activities. The specific mechanism by which these institutions operate is through the creation of barriers to entry and the concentration of economic power in the hands of a few, which stifles innovation and reduces overall productivity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions varies across former colonies. They find that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic growth depends on the degree to which they are enforced and the extent to which they are adapted to local conditions. This suggests that while extractive institutions may have initially hindered economic growth, their long-run effects can be mitigated through institutional reforms that promote more inclusive and productive economic activities.

Distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) share a finding that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for these institutions. Distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping the structure of these institutions. This perspective underscores how the power dynamics within societies led to the creation of institutions that maintained the status quo and protected the interests of the elite. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate on this point by showing how institutional differences in New World societies arose from differing levels of inequality. In societies with greater inequality, institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions was the establishment of rules and norms that protected the interests of the elite. For instance, craft guilds in pre-industrial Europe often restricted entry to protect the livelihoods of their members, thereby maintaining their economic power. This process was not uniform across all societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) qualifies that the impact of these institutions varied depending on the specific context and the degree of enforcement. This variation suggests that while distributional conflicts were a driving force, the effectiveness of institutions in shaping economic growth depended on local conditions and enforcement mechanisms.

Nunn and Austin both argue that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the institutional focus of Acemoglu et al. (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn argues that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, lasting from 1400 to 1900, a period significantly longer than official colonial rule. These trades disrupted the economic and social institutions of the affected regions, making it difficult for them to support trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). Austin suggests that the impact of European colonialism on African economic development is a complex phenomenon, arguing that the historical experience of colonialism and its impact on African economic development has been shaped by a range of factors, including the timing and nature of colonial rule, the specific policies and institutions imposed by colonial governments, and the responses of African societies to these policies and institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). In fact, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This response suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development was not uniform and that the effectiveness of these institutions depended on local conditions and the degree of enforcement. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions operate, and how they shape economic growth.

British rule fostered a different institutional framework that influenced educational outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, shaping long-run economic growth by altering the perceived benefits of formal education. Frankema and Simson note that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). British rule's institutional framework altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions. This alteration in perceived benefits was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, influencing demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed the economic and political landscape, making formal education more attractive. This institutional change had a significant impact on educational outcomes, as formal education became a means to access new economic opportunities and political influence. Broadberry and Gardner further qualify this mechanism by noting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that the institutional changes brought about by British rule had tangible economic effects (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Simson points out that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, suggesting that the long-run economic growth effects of British rule's institutional framework on education did not solely depend on post-independence policies (Simson 2019, p.11). The institutional legacy of British rule in sub-Saharan Africa thus had complex and enduring effects on educational outcomes and economic growth.

Technological and intellectual progress are the primary drivers of economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role in facilitating the efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both emphasize that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. For example, the growth of useful knowledge may have been hindered by the lack of formal institutions that reduced access costs. Mokyr supports this view, noting that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. The implication is that economic growth depends on the ability of societies to adapt to technological changes. Kuznets also notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, indicating that institutional stability is necessary for sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This points to a nuanced relationship between technology and institutions, where technological progress can drive economic growth, but institutional frameworks are necessary to manage the social and political challenges that arise from such progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by defining the rules that govern economic interactions and the enforcement mechanisms that ensure these rules are followed. North argues that institutions matter because they reduce transaction costs, which are the costs associated with making exchanges and enforcing contracts (North 1989, p.6). This reduction in transaction costs facilitates market exchanges and promotes economic growth. North's argument is supported by Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment lead to higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth, as they provide the necessary incentives for investment and innovation (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further illustrate this point by highlighting the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, for example, was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This points to a nuanced relationship between technology and institutions, where technological progress can drive economic growth, but institutional frameworks are necessary to manage the social and political challenges that arise from such progress. Austin's work complements this view by emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This shared finding points to the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the framework within which economic actors operate and make decisions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its legacy. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This finding is supported by Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutions established in colonies with high mortality rates were extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This points to the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they can either promote or hinder economic development depending on their design and enforcement. The historical evidence from colonialism and its legacy shows that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment lead to higher income per capita, while extractive institutions hinder economic development. The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic factors. This is evident in the debate between Acemoglu et al.'s argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups and Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of income per capita in former European colonies, which shows that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment lead to higher income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
