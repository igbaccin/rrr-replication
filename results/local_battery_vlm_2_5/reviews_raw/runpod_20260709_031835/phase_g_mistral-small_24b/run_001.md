The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, and their quality can significantly influence economic performance. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and effective governance. Another position contends that institutions are merely one of many factors and that their impact can be overshadowed by other variables. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development and reducing global inequality. This debate is ongoing, with scholars continually refining their theories and methodologies to better understand the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The evidence from Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) suggests that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These changes were driven by the different colonization strategies Europeans employed, which resulted in the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. For instance, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic development, was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that these equilibrium institutions were not inherently designed to encourage economic development, as they were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maintain their rents and political power. This institutional framework, characterized by the absence of property rights and the rule of law, hindered long-run economic growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even after independence, has been a significant factor in the economic underdevelopment of many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This implies that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal. They show that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where the regime's commitment to rules underpinned economic growth. The political factors, including the enforcement and adaptability of rules, were critical in fostering secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implications of these developments are significant. This suggests that while institutional developments were crucial, their impact was amplified by the broader economic and political environment. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, involving not just the creation of new institutions but also the political and economic context in which they operate.

Colonial rule played a significant role in shaping institutional change in Africa, with Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) arguing that colonial policies were crucial in altering the benefits of formal education. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and social mobility. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by pointing out that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, including political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while colonial policies were influential, their impact on institutional change was not uniform and was subject to local conditions. The implications of these findings are important for understanding the long-run economic growth in Africa, as they show that colonial rule was a complex and multifaceted process that had both positive and negative effects on institutional development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) highlight how women's economic contributions and post-independence budget policies in Africa reveal specific economic factors driving growth, but they do not directly address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack detail how women were systematically marginalized from land access for cash crops during colonial rule. This marginalization was reinforced through the codification of customary law, which privileged men and limited women's economic agency. Women's economic contributions were significant in both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, for instance, was propelled by women migrating in search of farmland for cocoa. This pattern of women's economic agency highlights that, even under institutional constraints, women found ways to contribute to economic growth. Simson shows that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. This suggests that the economic policies implemented after independence, while not focusing on broad-based public goods, still had growth implications. The allocation of resources towards consumption and military spending during this period indicates that institutional frameworks did not prioritize long-term economic growth. This points to a broader implication: the institutional frameworks in post-independence Africa were often shaped by immediate political and social pressures rather than long-term economic considerations.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Austin, argue that the slave trade had profound and enduring effects on African societies, shaping cultural attitudes that persist to this day and influence economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This perspective posits that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to hinder economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalized mistrust creates a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. Austin highlights that colonial administrations, despite their varied institutional prescriptions, often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could have fostered economic growth if not for the entrenched mistrust (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is not just about the immediate disruption of economic activities but also about the long-term cultural shifts that continue to affect economic behavior and institutions. The enduring impact of this mistrust underscores the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers shaping key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is evident in the development of institutions such as serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, which were shaped by the interests of the ruling classes rather than by considerations of efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that serfdom, for example, persisted because it benefited the landowning elite, who had the power to enforce it. This perspective highlights how institutional development can be driven by the need to maintain social hierarchies and power structures. The persistence of these institutions, despite their potential inefficiencies, underscores the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate this point by examining New World societies, where extreme inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, demonstrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutional development in ways that hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that while distributional conflicts are a significant factor, they are not the only determinant of institutional development. Other factors, such as cultural beliefs and exogenous shocks, can also play a role in shaping institutions. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are a crucial driver of institutional development, they operate within a broader context of social, economic, and cultural factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical context and the specific strategies employed by colonial powers. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers implemented varied colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, significantly influenced the long-term economic trajectories of the colonies. The persistence of extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, highlights how initial colonial policies can have lasting impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by shaping property rights and transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The institutional frameworks established during colonial periods were not solely determined by economic efficiency but were also shaped by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that shared beliefs and cultural factors played a significant role in the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This aligns with Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from colonial Africa further illustrates how institutional frameworks were shaped by the interplay between European settlers, colonial governments, and indigenous populations. Austin notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutions evolve in response to various social, economic, and political factors.

These beliefs can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement.
