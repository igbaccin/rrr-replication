The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. What is at stake is the identification of the fundamental drivers of economic success and failure. This debate hinges on whether institutions, which govern the rules of the game in society, are the primary factors that determine economic outcomes over the long term. On one side, scholars argue that institutions shape economic growth through their impact on property rights, investment, and market exchanges. On the other side, there is disagreement about the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate and the extent to which they are influenced by other factors such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. The live disagreement centres around the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic growth and the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors. The question of whether institutions are the key determinant of economic growth or whether they are merely one of many factors remains unresolved. The answer to this question is critical for understanding the historical trajectories of economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not merely a theoretical question; it has practical implications for policymakers seeking to foster economic development. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. They argue that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges in turn multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges, which is a growing proportion of GNP as specialization and division of labor increase. This dynamic is evident in the historical development of former colonies, where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as in the cases of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) document that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups can be extractive, designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth. This pattern is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, a shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the critical role of political factors in economic growth, emphasizing that the regime's commitment to enforcing and maintaining rules was essential. This institutional framework allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn facilitated long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) underscores the importance of institutions in economic history, advocating for their integration into long-term research programs. She argues that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for economic historians, suggesting that a deeper understanding of institutional evolution can provide insights into economic outcomes. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.3) also warns against over-simplifying the role of institutions, noting that their impact can be complex and multifaceted. This caution is particularly relevant when considering the varied economic performance of different regions, where institutions may interact with cultural and historical factors in unpredictable ways. Economic historians must therefore approach the study of institutions with a nuanced understanding of their multifaceted roles in shaping economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutions that favored elites, a pattern that was reinforced by colonial policies and factor endowments. The New World's abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor initially led to high living standards, but extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power characterized these societies from the outset. The resulting inequality was perpetuated by institutional frameworks that restricted competition and favored the elite, preventing the emergence of a competitive market economy. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further emphasizes that the growth of modern economies requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. This implies that economic growth, driven by technological progress, is contingent on the ability of institutions to adapt and resolve conflicts arising from growth-induced changes. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49; 2005, p.54) notes that the growth of useful knowledge, which is crucial for economic growth, depends on institutional progress that complements technological progress. This suggests that without robust institutions to support and sustain technological advancements, the benefits of economic growth may be limited and unsustainable.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe during the early modern period is complex and nuanced. The relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe during the early modern period is complex and nuanced. This period saw significant shifts in economic activity and living standards, with various factors influencing the skill premium and the distribution of economic benefits. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that the skill premium, a key indicator of economic performance, is influenced by the costs of training and institutional barriers to entry into skilled trades. This implies that the economic benefits of skill development are contingent on the institutional framework that supports training and ensures fair execution of contracts. The guilds, for example, played a role in the intergenerational transfer of skills, but their monopolistic practices could also restrict entry and maintain artificially high wages. They argue that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by these services, which in turn influenced living standards by affecting the relative prices of manufactured goods and basic foodstuffs. This suggests that the economic benefits of development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies this by showing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe during this period. The urbanization ratio, for instance, does not directly correlate with improvements in living standards, indicating that economic development did not uniformly translate into better living conditions. This implies that while institutions and economic factors influenced living standards, other socioeconomic factors also played a significant role.

Historical events, such as slave trades, have significantly influenced Africa's long-term economic development and ethnic fractionation (Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Nunn argues that the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, were a pivotal factor in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. This period of exploitation and displacement had profound effects on the continent's socio-economic structures, leading to enduring underdevelopment. Peters adds to this by highlighting the processes of exclusion and class formation that emerged from these historical events, revealing how economic and social institutions were fundamentally altered. The historical impact of slave trades on Africa's economic development and ethnic fractionation highlights how specific historical events can shape long-term socio-economic outcomes. The role of institutions in economic growth is not directly addressed in this context. The absence of a centralized government and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, underscores the institutional prerequisites for sustained trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This implies that while historical events like slave trades had a detrimental impact, the institutional framework was also crucial in determining economic outcomes. Therefore, the legacy of these historical events continues to influence contemporary economic development in Africa, underscoring the complex interplay between historical events and institutional factors.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Hopkins, argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and independently affects long-run economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of poor institutions but a direct consequence of the historical trauma inflicted by the slave trade. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's impact on trust is substantial, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect on intergroup trust. This mistrust, in turn, impedes economic development by undermining social cohesion and hindering cooperation, which are crucial for sustained economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The legacy of mistrust is deeply ingrained, affecting both internal norms and external environments, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Hopkins highlights that while ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, the persistence of mistrust exacerbates this dysfunction, making it harder for African societies to develop progressive institutions that support economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema qualifies this by noting that the impact of colonial rule varied, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). This variation in colonial impact suggests that while the slave trade left a lasting imprint of mistrust, the specific institutional legacies of colonial rule also played a role in shaping economic outcomes. The complex interplay between historical events, institutional factors, and the enduring effects of mistrust underscores the multifaceted nature of Africa's economic development challenges.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) challenge conventional narratives about African economic history by highlighting the contributions of women and the effectiveness of post-independence budget policies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). Their work shows that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was reinforced through the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often relegated women to marginal lands for food farming while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The post-independence period saw significant shifts in budget policies, with increased military spending and subsidies (Simson 2019, p.11). These policies were not merely clientelist or unproductive; they were responses to regional instability and domestic dissent. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies was above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more complex picture of economic development that includes both institutional and structural factors (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis is a simplification of causation, compressing different historical periods and paths, and does not account for the nuanced economic development of Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This critique highlights the need for a more detailed examination of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. Austin argues that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which contradicts the notion of a uniform institutional prescription. Austin's analysis suggests that colonial policies were not uniformly detrimental but could be influenced by local conditions and actions, leading to varied outcomes. Their findings indicate that colonial rule did not uniformly suppress economic growth but rather had varied impacts depending on local conditions. This nuanced view of colonial policies and their varied impacts on economic development underscores the complexity of African economic history and the need for a more detailed understanding of institutional evolution.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic activity. The divergence in economic outcomes among former European colonies is largely attributable to the type of institutions established during the colonial period. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers implemented different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States and Australia, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive institutions, like those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework persists and significantly affects current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the financial revolution in England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were critical in enhancing the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects a broader trend where the emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights reduces transaction costs and fosters economic development (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and economic growth is dynamic, with institutions shaping the economic environment and economic activity reinforcing institutional structures.

The historical context of institutional development in Africa reveals a more complex picture. Austin challenges the simplistic view of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it compresses different historical periods and paths, thereby oversimplifying causation (Austin 2008, p.2). Colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that local conditions played a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view is supported by Hopkins, who emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but also by the presence of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the historical evidence of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing efficient property rights (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence of the financial revolution in England and the varied institutional outcomes in former European colonies demonstrate that institutions are the key determinant of long-run economic growth.
