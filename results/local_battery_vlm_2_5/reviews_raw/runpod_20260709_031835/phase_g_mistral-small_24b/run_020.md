The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who emphasize institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who argue that other factors, such as geographical conditions or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and development strategies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. Understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth is essential for designing effective policies that can promote long-term development. Institutions can shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints that affect investment, innovation, and productivity. The interaction between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving various factors such as property rights, legal frameworks, and governance structures. A central point of contention is whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and how they interact with other determinants of development. This review synthesizes the literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was a crucial driver of economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the emergence of new financial instruments during this period significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting the increased security of property rights. This financial revolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by enhancing the efficiency of market exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement. This institutional framework fostered a stable environment that encouraged investment and economic activity. France's economic performance was similar, despite its different institutional structure, qualifying the extent of institutional impact. North (1989, p.6) explains that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges resulted in falling transaction costs per transaction, but the aggregate volume of exchanges increased due to specialization and division of labor, leading to a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP. This dynamic underscores the importance of secure property rights in driving economic growth, as the institutional framework in England provided the necessary conditions for sustained economic development.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions, as shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's institutional structures. These institutions reflected the balance of power between different social groups, with elites often using their influence to maintain systems that favored their interests. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate this dynamic in the context of New World societies, where institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This advantage restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that economic growth is hindered by the lack of inclusive institutions that promote broad-based participation and innovation. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific outcomes are influenced by the broader historical and cultural context in which these conflicts occur.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial rule varied widely. This variation was driven by the mortality rates of European colonizers, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of the colonies. In areas with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established. These institutions were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the European metropole, with little regard for local economic development. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework facilitated long-run economic growth by promoting investment and innovation. The persistence of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that economic growth in former colonies is significantly influenced by the type of institutions established during colonial rule. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that former colonies with extractive institutions have lower current income per capita. This finding is consistent with the broader literature on institutions and economic growth, which emphasizes the importance of inclusive institutions in promoting long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the specific outcomes are influenced by the broader historical and cultural context in which these institutions occur.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. This is shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This is because "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The extent to which these institutions can drive long-run economic growth depends on broader historical and cultural contexts. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) explore the long-term picture of economic development and argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, which is a counterpoint to the recent literature emphasizing such impediments. They find that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered and reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform and depends on specific historical and geographical conditions. This raises the question of whether the specific outcomes of colonial institutions on economic growth depend on the broader historical and cultural context in which these institutions play out.

This gap in the literature is crucial, as the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both acknowledge that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) notes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.54) also recognizes the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress. Mokyr notes that the growth of economic reasonableness is less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional framework must adapt and evolve to sustain long-term economic growth.

This dynamic is evident in the legacy of the slave trade, which cultivated a pervasive culture of mistrust within Africa, persisting today and significantly impacting trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). The slave trade's legacy is particularly evident in the mistrust between ethnic groups, a dynamic that has been perpetuated through generations. This mistrust actively influences current social and economic interactions. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could have mitigated some of the mistrust. The broader institutional frameworks, including the rule of law and governance, were not sufficiently robust to counteract the deep-seated mistrust fostered by the slave trade. This enduring mistrust undermines the effectiveness of contemporary institutions, as it erodes the social capital necessary for economic development and cooperation.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and allocating resources. Acemoglu et al. show that different colonization strategies led to distinct institutional frameworks, with some colonies adopting institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North argues that the development of institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a decline in transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, by reducing transaction costs and ensuring property rights, facilitates market exchanges and encourages investment, which are essential for economic development.

Institutions impact economic growth differently across societies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth in many former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin highlights that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could have mitigated some of the institutional barriers to growth (Austin 2008, p.15). The enduring mistrust fostered by historical events, such as the slave trade, undermines the effectiveness of contemporary institutions by eroding social capital necessary for economic development and cooperation (Austin 2008, p.15). This mistrust creates a persistent barrier to trust, which is at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15) is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The new economic history of Africa, as argued by Hopkins, can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). North, however, emphasizes that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (North 1989, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which is related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
