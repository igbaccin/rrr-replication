The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustained economic development. A central disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, while others highlight the role of institutions in mediating the effects of other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors or are the result of endogenous political processes. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. assert that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional setups, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic performance.

Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The extractive institutions imposed in areas with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development. This institutional setup persisted over time, leading to lower income per capita in these regions compared to those with more inclusive institutions. Acemoglu et al. found that these institutions were not merely a response to geographic factors but were actively shaped by European policies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The variation in colonial policies, driven by factors like mortality rates and the presence of settler populations, resulted in diverse institutional frameworks that had lasting economic impacts. However, the persistence of extractive institutions was not solely due to initial colonial policies but also due to the enduring political and economic interests of the ruling elite. This dynamic is evident in the reluctance of powerful groups to initiate institutional changes that might threaten their political power or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The long-term effects of these institutions underscore the enduring influence of historical institutional choices on contemporary economic performance.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) highlight that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This financial revolution was not merely an economic development but was deeply rooted in political institutions that ensured the enforcement of property rights. North (1989, p.9) specifically notes that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, illustrating how political and economic institutions intertwined to shape economic outcomes (North 1989, p.9). This perspective challenges the neoclassical view that emphasizes population and savings, instead highlighting the role of credible commitments and secure property rights in driving economic development. This suggests that while institutional changes can be transformative, their effects are contingent on broader economic and political conditions. The interplay between political stability and economic institutions thus emerges as a critical factor in understanding long-run economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced view of institutional development.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, with technological and intellectual advancements playing a more fundamental role than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49), especially in societies that prioritize innovation. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial for sustaining economic growth. This adjustment process involves resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, which can weaken the political fabric of society if not managed properly. The sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and community feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological advancements are the primary driver of economic growth, they must be supported by appropriate institutional frameworks to ensure long-term sustainability.

The slave trades in Africa significantly impeded long-run economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2) arguing that the legacy of these trades persists through cultural and institutional channels, creating lasting barriers to economic progress. This mistrust is not merely a reaction to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained as an internal norm, making it a persistent barrier to economic development. This internal mistrust is at least twice as impactful as external factors, underscoring the profound and lasting institutional effects of the slave trades. The slave trades' legacy of mistrust and institutional weakness continues to shape Africa's economic landscape, illustrating the deep and lasting institutional effects of historical traumas on long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the narrative of persistent African poverty due to structural impediments is misleading. They challenge the conventional wisdom that African economies were stagnant and underdeveloped during the colonial period, instead suggesting that the region saw significant growth in the early twentieth century. This growth was driven by the expansion of export markets and the integration of African economies into the global trade network. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this perspective by highlighting the role of foreign trade and export markets in shaping economic growth patterns. They note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability underscores the complex interplay between economic development and institutional frameworks. The growth patterns observed in Anglophone African economies were not uniform. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing the long-term possibilities of African economic growth. This divergence suggests that while some regions experienced significant economic progress, others lagged behind, indicating that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was not uniform across the continent.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional explanations for economic development, arguing that distributional conflicts and historical paths play significant roles. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique aligns with Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Austin (2008, p.2) demonstrates that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, revealing that institutions were not static but evolved in response to local conditions. This dynamic interplay between institutions and local contexts challenges the notion of institutions as fixed determinants of economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further qualifies this by noting that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, highlighting the role of cultural factors in shaping institutions. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic development is mediated by cultural and historical contexts, which can either enhance or hinder their effectiveness.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonizers, hindered long-run economic growth, while institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment fostered development. Similarly, North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were crucial for reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. These institutions provided the necessary framework for secure property rights and effective enforcement, which are essential for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) highlights that the evolution of institutions is influenced by historical paths and distributional conflicts, which can either enhance or hinder their effectiveness. This interplay between institutions and historical contexts underscores the complex nature of economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis of capital markets provides evidence that institutional changes were pivotal in driving economic development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15; North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the role of extractive institutions in hindering economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of colonial institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This empirical comparison of colonial institutions and their economic outcomes provides a clear measure of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
