The role of quantum entanglement in shaping the measurement problem in particle physics is a central theme in contemporary physics, with scholars arguing that its properties play a critical role in understanding the nature of reality and the measurement of quantum states. (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.18) suggests that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies reflects changes in the institutions resulting from European colonialism, rather than economic development linked to geography. This argument is supported by (North 1989, p.3) which highlights the costliness of measuring the separable dimensions of human senses, arguing that such measurements are essential for defining property rights and other rules. (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.45) further supports this view by indicating that the instrumental-variables procedure will deal with endogeneity, omitted variables bias, and attenuation bias due to measurement error. However, (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.12) presents a different perspective, emphasizing that measurement error can be controlled statistically, as shown in (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.10). Despite these opposing views, (Nunn 2008, p.32) and (Nunn 2008, p.12) show that the persistence of extractive institutions and property rights explain the large differences in income per capita, rather than other factors like colonial policies. The historical evidence that settles the disagreement is found in (Feinstein 1998, p.4) and (Feinstein 1998, p.17), which provide a measurement of living standards that already used the cost-of-living index and compare it with the LW index. Therefore, the disagreement between institutional differences and economic development is resolved by examining the persistence of extractive institutions, which is already accounted for in the cost-of-living index and comparison with the LW index.
