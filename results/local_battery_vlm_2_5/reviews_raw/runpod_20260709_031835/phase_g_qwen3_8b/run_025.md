The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Scholars disagree on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic divergence or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, play a more decisive role. The literature emphasizes that institutions defined as the rules norms and organizations that govern human interaction create the conditions for investment innovation and resource allocation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies while North (1989, p.1) contends that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings. Both streams highlight the causal power of institutions but diverge on their origins and the relative weight of other variables. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are endogenous to historical processes or exogenous to economic outcomes. This tension underscores the need to distinguish between institutional frameworks that enable growth and those that perpetuate inequality. The next section explores how institutional antecedents, such as historical legacies, shape the causal mechanisms of long-run growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks for political and economic order. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, particularly extractive or inclusive structures, have persisted and continue to influence contemporary economic performance. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, are not merely historical relics but active forces that shape the trajectory of development. The colonial experience, therefore, provides a critical antecedent to the causal mechanisms of long-run growth. These findings suggest that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule are not only enduring but also structurally significant in shaping the long-term economic outcomes of former colonies. The persistence of these institutions underscores the importance of understanding their historical origins and the mechanisms through which they continue to influence economic development. This historical continuity highlights the need to examine how institutional legacies can either constrain or enable long-run growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic development. This finding aligns with the broader argument that historical legacies like colonialism and the slave trade shaped extractive institutions that hindered long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Peters 2004, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The mechanism through which these legacies operate is the erosion of social trust, which is critical for facilitating cooperation and investment. These disruptions created a legacy of mistrust that persists in contemporary economic institutions, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who link low levels of trust to diminished economic performance. The implications of this are profound the historical roots of distrust shaped the trajectory of economic development and continue to influence the effectiveness of modern institutions. This stream of literature underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on economic outcomes, setting the stage for examining how these legacies interact with contemporary policy and governance.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8) who emphasizes the role of historical institutional analysis in understanding contemporary African development. Both authors suggest that the persistence of certain economic outcomes is rooted in the ways in which power and resource distribution have shaped institutional frameworks over time. Ogilvie highlights that institutional interlinkages among markets can resist innovation unless it provides substitutes for existing structures, while Hopkins underscores that contemporary institutions are deeply embedded in historical legacies, including precolonial and colonial experiences. These insights collectively suggest that the effectiveness of institutions is not independent of the distributional dynamics that underpin them. McCloskey (2015, p.9) further reinforces this by emphasizing the centrality of ideas and ideologies in shaping economic trajectories, arguing that cultural and intellectual shifts can redefine the possibilities for growth. This stream collectively implies that the trajectory of economic development is not solely determined by formal institutions but is also influenced by the broader social and ideological contexts in which they operate. The interplay between distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs thus provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding the long-term dynamics of economic growth.

Institutions regulating training and access to professions shape long-term economic performance by influencing skill premiums and wage dynamics, as shown by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4). The interplay between institutional regulation and wage dynamics is also evident in Pamuk (2007, p.25), which shows how flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for sustained wage levels and contributed to long-term economic divergence. Temin (2002, p.6) adds that postwar institutions in Europe created a framework that encouraged productive investments and stabilized wage growth, illustrating how institutional arrangements can sustain economic performance over time. These sources collectively suggest that the capacity of institutions to regulate training and access to professions is a critical determinant of long-term economic outcomes, with implications for understanding historical divergences in growth trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19). These authors collectively emphasize that technological advancement, rather than institutions alone, drives long-term economic expansion. Mokyr highlights that while institutions are essential for sustaining technological progress, the primary engine of growth lies in the accumulation and dissemination of knowledge. This perspective is reinforced by Kuznets, who notes that modern technology requires institutional adjustments that go beyond traditional resource exploitation, underscoring the interdependence of technological and institutional evolution. Abramovitz further complicates this picture by pointing out that the catch-up process is not merely a function of technological diffusion but is also shaped by historical educational and institutional commitments. The joint argument is that economic growth is fundamentally propelled by the expansion of knowledge, which in turn necessitates supportive institutional frameworks to ensure its effective application. This interplay between knowledge and institutions suggests that the role of institutions is not to replace technological progress but to complement and sustain it. The implications of this dynamic are significant for understanding how economic growth can be both innovative and enduring.

Stern (2004, p.2) critiques the Environmental Kuznets Curve as an inadequate model for understanding development-environment relationships, arguing for more nuanced approaches. The paper highlights the econometric weaknesses of most EKC literature, noting that little attention has been paid to the statistical properties of the data or issues of model adequacy such as omitted variables bias (Stern 2004, p.2). Stern (2004, p.2) further contextualizes the EKC by tracing its origins to Kuznets' hypothesis on income inequality and its popularization through the 1992 World Bank Development Report (Stern 2004, p.1). This dual focus on methodological limitations and historical context underscores the need for alternative frameworks that better capture the complex interplay between economic development and environmental outcomes. The call for improved models aligns with broader efforts to integrate institutional and historical perspectives into environmental economics, reinforcing the idea that growth trajectories are shaped by a combination of structural and policy factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities among former colonies. This aligns with North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutions, rather than population or savings, are central to understanding historical obstacles to economic growth. Both scholars highlight the enduring impact of institutional frameworks established during colonial periods, which continue to influence economic outcomes today. The persistence of these institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show that extractive institutions set up by colonialists persisted long after independence, shaping the trajectory of development in many regions. These findings collectively suggest that institutional legacies are a critical determinant of long-term economic performance. However the sources differ in their interpretation of the origins of these institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutional development in the New World was shaped by restrictive policies that advantaged elites, perpetuating inequality. In contrast, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a better explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions. The disagreement centers on whether institutions emerged primarily from economic efficiency considerations or from broader social and political dynamics. This debate is resolved through historical analysis, as demonstrated by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who emphasizes the need to trace current institutions back to their precolonial and colonial roots to understand their long-term impact on development.
