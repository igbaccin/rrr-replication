The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with intellectual stakes in understanding how historical legacies influence contemporary prosperity. Scholars debate whether institutions emerge as deliberate choices or as unintended consequences of broader social and political forces, and whether their impact is primarily structural or contingent on other factors. The live disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which institutions shape growth, with some emphasizing their role as deliberate policy instruments and others highlighting their emergence from historical path dependencies. The debate also hinges on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or merely one of several factors, including geography, culture, or initial conditions. These tensions shape the scholarly landscape, with competing explanations for how institutions interact with other variables to produce divergent economic outcomes. The question of institutional causality remains unresolved, but the discussion reveals a shared recognition of institutions as pivotal to long-term development. Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “The present paper offers one economic historian's reﬂections on the RF thesis: its methods, historical claims and implications for theory and policy.” Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) report, “In that paper we proposed the disease environment at the time Europeans arrived as an instrument for European settlements and the subsequent institutional development of the former colonies, and used this to estimate the causal effect of institutional differences on economic performance.”

This claim is reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutional frameworks, such as those emerging from England's common law tradition, laid the groundwork for sustained economic development (North 1989, p.1). Both papers highlight how the persistence of extractive or inclusive institutions, established during colonial rule, continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The institutional legacy of colonial governance, whether through centralized or decentralized structures, has created enduring barriers or enablers for growth. van Zanden (2009, p.19) further supports this by showing how the efficiency of training institutions in medieval Europe shaped long-term economic performance, as the organization of skill transmission influenced labor markets and productivity (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These studies collectively demonstrate that institutional arrangements, rooted in historical contexts, are central to understanding the trajectory of economic development. The persistence of these institutional frameworks underscores the need to examine their origins and evolution to grasp their ongoing influence on growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial institutions and exclusion shaped long-run economic growth by creating persistent structural barriers, a claim corroborated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) who highlight how institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality through restrictive policies. The mechanisms through which these institutions entrenched exclusion are multifaceted, involving both direct legal frameworks and indirect cultural norms. Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that the slave trade disrupted local economies and fostered a culture of mistrust that persists in contemporary African societies which undermines social cohesion and economic cooperation. This pattern of exclusion is further illustrated by Peters (2004, p.19), who documents how land conflicts in Africa reveal deep-seated class formations and the erosion of customary systems, reinforcing structural inequalities. The long-term implications of these institutional legacies are significant, as they constrain access to resources, limit political participation, and perpetuate cycles of poverty. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) add nuance by showing that while African real wages were above subsistence and rose over time, these trends were shaped by colonial institutions that favored certain groups over others. Together, these studies underscore the enduring impact of exclusionary institutions on economic development, setting the stage for an analysis of how such structures interact with other factors in shaping growth trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical institutional analysis is crucial for understanding the long-term economic trajectories of African states, as institutions are not merely products of recent history but are shaped by deeper, often precolonial, processes. This claim is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which emphasizes that African economic history is not a void but a field of inquiry that can be explored through historical institutional frameworks. Together, these works highlight that long-run economic growth is shaped by institutions that emerge from historical processes, rather than being determined solely by contemporary conditions or external shocks. This congruence is further complicated by the historical legacies of colonialism, which often disrupted or reconfigured indigenous institutions in ways that continue to affect economic outcomes today. The implication is that understanding these interactions is essential for designing policies that address the root causes of underdevelopment rather than merely its symptoms. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with the historical processes that have shaped them, offering a path to more nuanced and effective development strategies.

Pamuk (2007, p.25) argues that institutional flexibility in post-Black Death Europe played a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic divergence, with the Low Countries and England demonstrating greater adaptability to shifting economic conditions. This flexibility allowed these regions to resist wage declines, thereby fostering a more dynamic labor market and setting the stage for sustained economic growth. Pamuk further notes that the Black Death contributed to a sustained decline in interest rates, which in turn supported investment and economic expansion in regions with favorable institutional frameworks (Pamuk 2007, p.21). These findings suggest that institutional resilience and adaptability were critical in determining the trajectory of economic development. The persistence of low interest rates in certain regions, linked to pre-existing institutional advantages, underscores the importance of historical institutional legacies in shaping economic outcomes. This insight aligns with broader discussions on how institutional structures influence long-term growth by enabling or constraining economic transformation.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by (Abramovitz 1986, p.19; Temin 2002, p.6; Mokyr 2005, p.54; Stern 2004, p.2). This perspective emphasizes that technological and knowledge advancement, rather than institutional structures, are the primary drivers of long-run economic expansion. The argument is grounded in the idea that while institutions may play a supportive role, they are not the central mechanism of growth. For instance, Mokyr highlights how the expansion of knowledge through education and scientific inquiry laid the foundation for industrial progress, even when formal institutions were slow to adapt. This aligns with Abramovitz’s observation that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, depending on their alignment with technological opportunities. Temin’s analysis of the Golden Age of European growth further reinforces this view by attributing rapid economic expansion to technological and productive forces rather than institutional reforms. The implication is that while institutions may shape the environment in which knowledge is applied, they do not substitute for the innovation and diffusion of new ideas. This stream thus challenges the centrality of institutions in long-run growth by foregrounding the role of technological progress as an independent and more fundamental source of economic transformation.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies drive long-run economic growth, challenging the institutionalist view that institutions are the fundamental cause. McCloskey emphasizes that cultural and intellectual shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, fostered an environment conducive to innovation and entrepreneurship, thereby shaping economic trajectories. Ogilvie complements this by highlighting how deeply ingrained beliefs and values interact with institutional structures to influence economic outcomes, suggesting that the interplay between ideology and institutions is central to understanding historical economic change. Both authors underscore that while institutions matter, they are not the primary engine of growth; instead, the diffusion of progressive ideas and the transformation of cultural norms play a more decisive role. This dynamic is evident in McCloskey’s analysis of France, where the persistence of conservative ideologies hindered economic progress until the adoption of a new rhetoric that aligned with commercial interests. Ogilvie further illustrates this by showing how institutional frameworks often reflect and reinforce prevailing cultural beliefs, making it difficult to separate the two in explaining economic development. The implication is that long-run growth hinges not only on the design of institutions but also on the ideological climate that sustains and enables them. This perspective opens the door to examining how shifts in intellectual and cultural paradigms can reshape economic possibilities over time.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that they shape development through persistent historical legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate how colonial institutions, once established, endured and structured post-colonial economic outcomes, influencing income per capita across diverse regions. This institutional persistence is further reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that the evolution of institutions in early modern Britain and Spain produced divergent economic trajectories, with institutional frameworks determining the capacity for growth. Both papers highlight the enduring impact of institutional design on economic performance, suggesting that institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of development. However the sources diverge on the relative importance of cultural beliefs in shaping these institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a more compelling explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions, emphasizing the interplay between ideological values and institutional rules. In contrast, Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the reversal thesis oversimplifies causality by compressing history, neglecting the differentiated causal relationships that underpin institutional change. The disagreement hinges on whether cultural and ideological factors are primary drivers of institutional formation or secondary to economic structures. This tension is resolved through empirical comparisons of institutional legacies in former colonies, where the persistence of extractive institutions is measured against alternative developmental paths, revealing the complex interplay between ideology, history, and economic outcomes.
