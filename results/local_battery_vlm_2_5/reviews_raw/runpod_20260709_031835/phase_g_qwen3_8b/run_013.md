The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of sustained prosperity or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, hold greater explanatory power. This disagreement hinges on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some scholars emphasizing their direct impact on economic performance and others highlighting their indirect role in shaping the conditions for growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain divergent income distributions among former European colonies, while North (1989, p.1) contends that institutions themselves are the critical determinant of economic growth, challenging neoclassical models that prioritize population and savings. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are foundational, yet they diverge in their emphasis on whether institutions are the cause or the outcome of broader historical forces. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) underscore the persistent inequality generated by restrictive institutional policies in the New World, suggesting that institutional development is deeply intertwined with the persistence of elite advantage. These perspectives collectively affirm that institutions are central to understanding long-run growth, yet they differ in their interpretation of the causal pathways through which institutions operate. The debate remains unresolved, with implications for how we conceptualize the role of institutions in both historical and contemporary economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either foster or hinder development, a claim supported by (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.30). They emphasize that the institutional legacies of colonial rule, particularly the persistence of extractive structures, have had profound and lasting effects on economic outcomes. The contrast between these institutional trajectories underscores how the nature of governance and the distribution of power within societies have been central to determining long-term economic performance. Together, these sources suggest that the institutional environment is not merely a passive backdrop to economic activity but an active force that shapes the possibilities for growth. This focus on institutional persistence and its implications for development sets the stage for examining how institutional change can be deliberately pursued to foster economic progress.

Nunn (2008, p.19) and others demonstrate that historical colonial and slave trade institutions shaped long-run economic growth by perpetuating inequality and hindering development. These institutions, rooted in the extraction of labor and resources, created enduring structural barriers that constrained economic potential. This exclusion was not merely economic but also institutional, as the slave trade fostered patterns of violence and distrust that persisted into the modern era. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) further elaborate on how these historical legacies influenced contemporary trust levels, which in turn affect economic outcomes. Their findings suggest that the internalization of mistrust, stemming from the trauma of the slave trade, has had a profound and lasting impact on social cohesion and economic performance. Together, these sources underscore how the institutional frameworks established during the colonial and slave trade periods have left a deep imprint on the trajectory of long-run economic growth. This legacy of institutional fragility continues to shape development prospects in ways that are both persistent and pervasive.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that efficient institutions in training and access to professions shaped long-term economic performance in Europe, a claim supported by Pamuk (2007, p.25) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9). These sources collectively suggest that institutional frameworks governing the transmission of skills and the regulation of labor markets were critical in determining economic trajectories. Van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of institutions regulating apprenticeships and professional access influenced the skill premium, which in turn affected long-term productivity. Pamuk further underscores that flexible institutions enabled regions like the Low Countries and England to resist wage declines after the Black Death, fostering a divergence in economic development. Broadberry and Gupta expand this by showing that institutional differences in urbanization and industrial organization contributed to the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia. The persistent link between institutional quality and economic outcomes is evident across these studies, with institutions shaping not only wage levels but also the capacity for sustained growth. This institutional influence on economic performance persists as a central theme, setting the stage for examining how historical legacies continue to shape contemporary development paths.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the narrative of African underdevelopment by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan African economies achieved per capita incomes above subsistence by the early 20th century, a finding corroborated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who argue that African real wages were not only above subsistence but also rose over time. These studies collectively suggest that historical wage and growth conditions in Africa were favorable, undermining the assumption of persistent structural impediments. The mechanisms through which this occurred include the role of precolonial commercial development and the institutional design of colonial states, which shaped labor market interventions and export dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.27) highlight how initial differences in commercial development between West and East Africa influenced the institutional frameworks of colonial governance, with East African states adopting more direct labor market controls. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further enrich this picture by emphasizing the contributions of African women to economic growth, showing that gender dynamics were integral to both production and reproduction. Together, these sources suggest that historical conditions in Africa were not inherently restrictive but rather shaped by complex interactions between institutions, markets, and human agency. This perspective opens the door to re-evaluating the long-term potential of African economies in light of their historical capacity for growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19) argue that the expansion of useful knowledge is a primary driver of long-run economic growth, rivaling institutional explanations. Abramovitz further emphasizes that the catch-up process is not merely a matter of technological adoption but is deeply influenced by the educational and institutional frameworks that shape the capacity for innovation. These sources collectively highlight the interplay between knowledge diffusion and the structural conditions that enable or constrain its application. The role of formal education, as Mokyr describes (Mokyr 2005, p.28), is particularly significant in reducing access costs and fostering a workforce capable of engaging with new technologies. Yet, the effectiveness of such institutions in promoting growth is not guaranteed, as historical evidence shows that even well-established educational systems may fail to translate knowledge into sustained economic performance. This underscores the need for complementary institutional reforms that align with the goals of knowledge-based development. The implications of these findings suggest that policies aimed at enhancing knowledge production and dissemination must be paired with efforts to reduce barriers to its practical implementation. The tension between knowledge expansion and institutional adaptation remains a central theme in understanding the drivers of long-run economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. This cluster contends that cultural and intellectual shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values or the spread of scientific rationalism, fundamentally reshape economic outcomes by altering the incentives and norms that govern human behavior. McCloskey (2015, p.9) illustrates how ideological battles over freedom, equality, and progress shaped the trajectories of nations, with the French case showing how conservative cultural values restrained economic growth for centuries. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) complements this by emphasizing the interplay between inward beliefs and institutional rules, suggesting that the persistence of certain institutions is often driven by cultural and ideological factors rather than pure efficiency. Together, these sources highlight a mechanism where broad shifts in societal values and intellectual climates create the conditions for sustained economic transformation. Stern (2004, p.2), while focused on environmental economics, reinforces this by demonstrating how the framing of development and its relationship to nature is deeply influenced by prevailing ideological paradigms. This suggests that the role of ideas is not merely peripheral but foundational to understanding long-run economic trajectories. The implications of this perspective challenge the centrality of institutional structures in growth models, redirecting attention toward the dynamic and often intangible forces of belief and ideology.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that historical institutional analysis is key to understanding African economic history, offering an alternative to the focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run growth. Their work underscores the necessity of examining how precolonial and colonial institutions shaped the trajectory of African economies, challenging the assumption that contemporary institutions alone determine economic outcomes. The implication is that African economic development cannot be understood without accounting for the long-term legacies of both indigenous and colonial institutions, which have shaped the conditions for growth and underdevelopment. This focus on historical continuity provides a framework for rethinking the role of institutions in African economic history, setting the stage for a more nuanced analysis of institutional change and its impact on long-run growth.

Temin (2002, p.6) contends that the Golden Age of European growth was rooted in pre-war disequilibrium, a claim supported by the interplay of institutional and economic forces that shaped postwar outcomes. This perspective aligns with the idea that the postwar period was not the catalyst for growth but rather a continuation of pre-existing imbalances, which were amplified by institutional arrangements designed to stabilize and enhance productivity. The institutions that emerged in the postwar era, such as those governing labor markets and international trade, were not merely reactive but built upon the structural conditions of the pre-war period. These conditions, including the uneven distribution of capital and the persistence of certain economic practices, created an environment where growth could be sustained through coordinated efforts across national borders. The mechanism through which this occurred was the reinforcement of long-term incentives for cooperation and investment, which were made possible by the institutional frameworks that emerged in the aftermath of conflict. This view is further substantiated by the observation that the rapid growth of the postwar period was not uniform across all regions, suggesting that the pre-war conditions played a critical role in determining the trajectory of economic development. The implications of this argument extend to the broader understanding of institutional change, emphasizing that the effectiveness of postwar policies was contingent upon the pre-existing economic and institutional landscape. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth is not confined to their postwar origins but is deeply embedded in the historical continuity of economic and institutional legacies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is consistently emphasized across the literature, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) highlighting their significant impact on economic performance and income differences. This aligns with North (1989, p.1), who argues that institutions are central to understanding historical obstacles to economic growth. Both studies underscore the enduring influence of institutional frameworks, suggesting that the effectiveness of economic policies is deeply rooted in the institutional legacies of a society. However, the sources diverge on the relative importance of institutional versus geographic factors. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that initial conditions, such as factor endowments, have had profound and enduring impacts on long-run paths of development, while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) emphasize the persistence of colonial-era institutions as the primary determinant of income per capita. The disagreement centers on whether historical institutional structures or geographic and environmental factors are more critical in shaping economic outcomes. This debate is resolved through empirical comparisons of colonial and postcolonial institutions, as seen in the analysis of former European colonies by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), which provides a historical framework for understanding how institutional legacies have shaped economic trajectories across different regions.
