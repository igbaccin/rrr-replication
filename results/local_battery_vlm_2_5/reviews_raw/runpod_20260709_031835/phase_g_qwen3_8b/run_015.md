The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical disparities and designing effective policy interventions. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth, or if other factors such as geography, culture, or political conflict play a more decisive role. The live disagreement centers on whether institutional structures are the root cause of economic success or merely a byproduct of other historical forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, such as extractive or inclusive political economies, significantly influenced long-term income differences, while North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that secure property rights and specific institutional arrangements are essential for fostering economic growth. These perspectives highlight the centrality of institutions but differ in their focus on the mechanisms through which they operate. Austin (2008, p.2) complicates this by suggesting that causal relationships between institutions and growth are more nuanced, involving path dependence and differentiated historical contexts. This diversity of views underscores the complexity of institutional causality and the need for further empirical and theoretical exploration.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that secure property rights and institutional arrangements are foundational to sustained economic growth, a claim corroborated by Pamuk (2007, p.25), who highlights how flexible institutional frameworks enabled northwestern Europe to adapt to post-Black Death economic shifts. These scholars collectively emphasize that institutions shape the conditions for growth and determine the pace and trajectory of economic development. The role of legal and political institutions in protecting economic rights is further underscored by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who links the efficiency of training institutions to long-term economic performance, suggesting that the organization of skill transmission influences wage dynamics and productivity. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds a technological dimension, asserting that modern economic growth necessitates both technological advancement and institutional change, as outdated structures like slavery and illiteracy hinder the adoption of new technologies. North and Weingast (1989, p.28) elaborate on how representative government and the primacy of common law courts fostered a political environment conducive to economic expansion, reinforcing the idea that institutional stability and predictability are prerequisites for investment and innovation. Together, these sources affirm that institutional arrangements are essential components of long-run economic growth and shape the conditions under which technological progress and market efficiency can flourish. This institutional foundation, as demonstrated by the stream, sets the stage for subsequent analyses of how historical and political contexts influence economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic growth by determining the trajectory of political and economic systems. These authors highlight how the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, such as extractive or inclusive structures, influenced the persistence of inequality and the capacity for sustained development. The mechanism through which colonial institutions exerted this influence lies in their role in structuring property rights, political participation, and economic opportunities, which in turn shaped the long-term potential for growth. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies, such as those in Latin America, hindered the development of inclusive political and economic systems, thereby constraining growth. Meanwhile, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade, which was often facilitated by colonial institutions, left a legacy of mistrust and social fragmentation, further undermining economic development. This stream of scholarship collectively underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, emphasizing that the historical choices made during colonial rule have had profound and lasting consequences for the trajectory of growth. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding the institutional legacies of colonialism is essential for grasping the long-run determinants of economic performance.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that institutions are central to understanding long-run economic growth, emphasizing their role in shaping economic performance, a claim shared by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), Temin (2002, p.6), and Stern (2004, p.2). These scholars collectively highlight how institutional frameworks influence both the trajectory and sustainability of economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that institutions affect the incentives for investment and innovation and shape the conditions under which economies can grow or shrink, as seen in the historical patterns of export production tied to trade and political structures. This stream collectively underscores that institutions are active forces that determine the possibilities for economic transformation and are not merely passive structures. The implications of these findings suggest that institutional analysis must account for both the historical roots and the evolving dynamics of institutional arrangements to fully grasp the mechanisms driving long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts are central to the formation of core economic institutions, which in turn shape long-run growth, challenging the notion that institutions alone are the primary drivers of economic development. Both authors emphasize that institutions are deeply embedded in social and political struggles over resource allocation and are not static entities. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further reinforces this by showing how historical institutional legacies in Africa influence contemporary development policies, underscoring the necessity of understanding institutions as products of contested social processes. The implication of these findings is that institutional change is a response to deeper distributional tensions that persist across time and is not merely a technical adjustment. McCloskey (2015, p.9) adds a complementary perspective by advocating for a 'humanomics' that prioritizes ideas and cultural values over institutional structures, arguing that these intangible factors can either enable or constrain institutional evolution. Together, these sources reveal that the role of institutions in long-run growth is mediated by the interplay of distributional conflicts, historical legacies, and cultural narratives, all of which must be considered to fully grasp the dynamics of economic transformation.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) contends that African economic history is not only recoverable but also reveals how historical processes have shaped contemporary economic conditions, challenging the notion that customary systems are inherently flexible. This perspective aligns with Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who demonstrate that African women played a central role in economic production and reproduction across pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggesting that gendered institutions and practices were foundational to economic development. Together, these sources highlight how historical narratives and customary systems are not mere relics of the past but active forces that continue to influence economic trajectories. The interplay between historical legacies and evolving economic structures underscores the need to examine how these systems have both constrained and enabled growth. Peters (2004, p.19) further complicates this picture by showing how land conflicts and class formation in Africa reveal the exclusionary nature of certain institutional arrangements, which often diverge from the idealized view of customary systems as neutral or adaptive. This stream collectively argues that the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth must be understood through the lens of historical specificity and the embeddedness of social relations. The persistence of these historical patterns suggests that addressing economic inequality requires more than institutional reform and demands a re-evaluation of the very narratives that underpin economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central, with multiple scholars emphasizing their enduring impact on development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This consensus is grounded in empirical evidence showing that institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have persisted, influencing income levels and economic performance across regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutions are shaped by historical contingencies such as population density and prosperity, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts provide a more accurate explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This disagreement reflects deeper tensions over whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by power dynamics and social values. The resolution of this debate lies in the comparative analysis of historical cases, such as the contrasting institutional developments in England and Spain, which illustrate how institutional evolution is deeply embedded in political and social contexts (North 1989, p.8). By examining these historical precedents, scholars can better understand how institutional legacies shape economic outcomes, reinforcing the need to contextualize institutional analysis within specific historical and social frameworks.
