The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy interventions. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary driver of growth, or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or historical contingency play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on whether institutional quality is an endogenous outcome of historical processes or an exogenous force that shapes economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.1) emphasizes that the reversal thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing history, neglecting the differentiated causal relationships that underpin institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies. These perspectives highlight the tension between viewing institutions as a product of historical conditions or as a causal force in their own right. The stakes lie in determining whether institutional analysis can provide a coherent framework for understanding long-term economic outcomes or whether it must be supplemented by other explanatory variables. This debate underscores the intellectual challenge of disentangling the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth. The next section examines how institutional development is shaped by historical antecedents.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks for political and economic order. Both authors highlight that the institutional legacies of colonial rule created conditions that either constrained or enabled long-term growth, depending on their design. This suggests that the durability of institutional frameworks is a critical determinant of economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding the long-term economic trajectories of African countries, as institutions are deeply rooted in historical processes. This claim is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which emphasizes that economic underdevelopment in Africa is not a natural condition but a historical outcome shaped by external forces. Together, these sources highlight that institutions are not static but evolve through historical interactions, often reinforcing or distorting economic possibilities. The congruence between contemporary and indigenous institutions, as noted by Hopkins (2009, p.8), plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of governance and the capacity to foster development. This congruence, however, is frequently undermined by colonial legacies and postcolonial policies that prioritize extractive structures over locally adapted frameworks. These findings imply that addressing long-run economic growth requires structural reforms and a deep understanding of the historical contexts that have shaped institutional frameworks. The next section explores how these historical legacies interact with technological change to influence economic development.

Colonial institutions and the slave trade shaped long-run economic growth by creating extractive systems that hindered development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic cooperation. This mistrust, rooted in historical exploitation, is linked to lower levels of trust in institutions, which in turn affects economic performance. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, a pattern that aligns with the broader claim that extractive institutions stifle growth. The persistence of these institutions, as shown by Nunn (2008, p.19), is tied to the dynamics of the slave trade, where societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans. These findings suggest that the legacy of colonialism and slavery is not merely historical but structurally embedded in contemporary economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further highlight how colonial policies were designed to extract resources, reinforcing extractive systems that continue to shape economic trajectories. The cumulative effect of these historical legacies is a long-term divergence in economic development, where societies with extractive institutions face persistent challenges in achieving inclusive growth. This analysis underscores the necessity of addressing historical institutional legacies to foster sustainable development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that technological and knowledge-driven growth is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing the role of institutions in enabling and sustaining this process (Mokyr 2005, p.54; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.25; Temin 2002, p.6; Abramovitz 1986, p.19). Institutions are not merely passive facilitators but active participants in shaping the trajectory of technological progress, as seen in the historical development of educational and training systems. For instance, the expansion of formal educational institutions in the 1740s, such as those in Gottingen, played a critical role in training future bureaucrats in scientific disciplines, thereby reducing the costs of knowledge transmission (Mokyr 2005, p.28). This institutional support for the diffusion of technical expertise was instrumental in fostering sustained innovation, which in turn contributed to long-term economic growth. The interplay between institutional frameworks and technological advancement is further illustrated by the skill premium trends in western Europe, where the efficiency of training institutions and regulatory frameworks significantly influenced labor market dynamics (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These findings underscore the idea that the institutional environment is a crucial determinant of the pace and direction of technological and knowledge-driven growth. The historical evidence suggests that the capacity of societies to harness and sustain technological progress depends on the strength and adaptability of their institutional structures, which in turn shape the long-run economic outcomes of nations.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, as evidenced by (McCloskey 2015, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). They suggest that cultural and ideological frameworks shaping societies such as the valorization of bourgeois values or the suppression of scientific inquiry directly influence economic trajectories by altering the incentives and norms that govern human behavior. This perspective challenges the institutionalist view by emphasizing that the diffusion of certain ideas, such as the Enlightenment’s emphasis on reason and individualism, can catalyze structural economic transformations. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights how institutional interlinkages often resist change unless they are accompanied by shifts in underlying beliefs, suggesting that ideological shifts can be a prerequisite for institutional innovation. McCloskey (2015, p.9) further illustrates this by showing how the cultural struggle in France, rooted in ideological conflicts over secularism and meritocracy, constrained economic growth until the adoption of a new rhetoric that aligned with bourgeois values. These sources collectively imply that the long-run economic outcomes of nations are not merely the product of institutional design but are deeply embedded in the evolving ideological landscapes that shape the conditions for innovation and productivity. This focus on ideas opens new avenues for understanding how cultural and intellectual movements can sustain economic development over time.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that African real wages and per capita incomes were above subsistence and rose over time, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who challenge the notion of persistent structural impediments to growth. These scholars demonstrate that African economies did not remain stagnant but instead experienced dynamic shifts in productivity and income levels, often influenced by external trade and market forces. The evidence suggests that the trajectory of economic development in Africa was not solely determined by institutional constraints but was also shaped by broader economic and geographic factors. For instance, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.27) highlight how colonial labor policies in East Africa were influenced by the need to address chronic labor shortages, revealing a complex interplay between institutional design and economic conditions. This perspective underscores the importance of considering both internal and external drivers in understanding long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) further emphasize that the relationship between institutions and economic performance is about incentives for investment and the mechanisms that allow for economic contraction or expansion. By highlighting these dynamics, the cluster shifts the focus from institutional determinism to a more nuanced understanding of economic development that incorporates historical and geographic contexts. This reorientation invites a reconsideration of the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the idea that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth by arguing that the reversal thesis oversimplifies historical causation. This claim is supported by Austin’s critique of the AJR formulation, which he contends compresses complex historical processes into a single narrative, thereby neglecting the differentiated causal relationships that underpin economic development. Austin’s argument aligns with Nunn’s work on Africa, where both emphasize the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal recurring patterns shaped by both similarity of situations and path dependence. By highlighting these mechanisms, the cluster underscores how economic outcomes are influenced by a combination of historical contingencies and structural conditions, rather than being solely determined by institutional frameworks. This perspective complicates the traditional view by suggesting that institutions operate within a broader context of historical and geographic factors, which shape their effectiveness and durability. The implication is that understanding economic growth requires a more nuanced approach that integrates institutional analysis with historical and comparative insights. This reorientation invites scholars to reconsider the role of institutions not as isolated determinants but as part of a complex web of interacting forces that shape long-run economic trajectories.

The literature consistently underscores that institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) who show that colonial-era institutions persist and shape current income levels (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) argues that institutional frameworks, rather than population or savings, are central to economic development (North 1989, p.1). These perspectives align in emphasizing that institutions are not merely passive structures but active determinants of growth trajectories. The sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that causal relationships between institutions and growth are more differentiated than commonly recognized, suggesting that historical and geographic contexts play a crucial role in shaping institutional effectiveness (Austin 2008, p.1). In contrast, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) asserts that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a better explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This disagreement is resolved by examining historical case studies, such as the contrasting institutional developments in England and Spain, which highlight how specific historical conditions and political dynamics influenced institutional persistence and variation (North 1989, p.8).
