The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, are more fundamental. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain income distribution reversals in former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that restrictive institutions perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites. Both streams highlight institutions as critical, yet differ on their origins and impacts. North (1989, p.1) underscores that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings, framing them as historical obstacles to growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that current institutions are shaped by precolonial and colonial histories, suggesting that understanding institutional evolution requires a deep historical lens. These perspectives collectively affirm that institutions are central to long-run growth, yet they diverge on the mechanisms through which they operate and the factors that shape their development. The next section examines how institutional antecedents, such as historical legacies and cultural norms, further shape institutional trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history are upstream determinants of long-run economic growth, as they establish enduring frameworks that influence economic outcomes across generations. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) further support this by showing that the institutional legacies of colonial rule persisted long after independence, shaping the trajectory of economic development in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These findings suggest that the historical context of institutional formation is not merely a backdrop but a critical factor in determining the long-term potential for growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) add that secure property rights and political institutions are essential for fostering economic growth, as they provide the stability needed for investment and innovation (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This aligns with the broader argument that the institutional environment, shaped by historical legacies, is a key determinant of economic performance. The persistence of colonial institutions underscores the importance of understanding historical processes in analyzing contemporary economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial legacies in Africa, particularly the historical impact of the slave trade, have left enduring marks on social trust and economic development. Their findings align with the broader claim that colonial and historical institutional legacies shape long-run economic growth by perpetuating inequality and affecting trust and development, a posture shared by (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Peters 2004, p.19; Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The slave trade, as Nunn (2008, p.19) argues, disrupted local economies and entrenched patterns of violence and exclusion that continue to hinder institutional stability. This mechanism of historical exclusion is further reinforced by Peters (2004, p.19), who highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal deep-seated class formations rooted in colonial-era power structures. The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores the need to understand historical processes as central to contemporary economic outcomes. The interplay between trust, inequality, and institutional development remains a critical axis for further inquiry.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, better explain core economic institutions and their role in long-run growth. This claim is supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9), who emphasizes the primacy of ideas over institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie highlights how institutional frameworks are often self-reinforcing and resistant to change, even when more productive alternatives exist, suggesting that the persistence of certain institutions is driven by underlying distributional tensions rather than efficiency. McCloskey expands on this by illustrating how cultural and ideological battles, such as the French resistance to Jewish inclusion in the military, shaped economic trajectories through their impact on innovation and growth. Both authors underscore the interplay between ideological values and institutional structures, showing how beliefs about fairness, hierarchy, and progress can either enable or constrain economic development. The emphasis on distributional conflicts as a catalyst for institutional formation challenges traditional narratives that prioritize institutional design as the primary driver of growth. This stream’s focus on the ideological underpinnings of institutions suggests that understanding the social and cultural contexts in which institutions emerge is as critical as analyzing their formal rules. The implications of this perspective extend to the study of historical transitions, where shifts in dominant ideologies often precede and shape institutional change.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that long-run economic growth hinges on the interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation, as these factors enable societies to overcome historical constraints and foster sustainable development. This perspective, shared by Stern (2004, p.2), underscores the necessity of institutional and technological reforms to align with the demands of modern economic systems. Kuznets highlights how the transition from traditional to industrial economies necessitates institutional changes that support large-scale production and innovation, such as the replacement of family enterprises with formalized business structures. Stern, in turn, extends this argument by emphasizing the limitations of conventional models in capturing the complex relationship between development and environmental outcomes, suggesting that institutional frameworks must evolve to accommodate new economic realities. Together, these scholars illustrate that growth is not merely a function of resource availability but of the capacity to institutionalize and sustain technological advancements. The implication of this dual focus is that policies aimed at fostering growth must address both the material and institutional conditions that underpin innovation and efficiency. This synthesis of technological and institutional dynamics provides a foundation for understanding how structural transformations shape the trajectory of economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping training and access to professions drive long-term economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) who show that political institutions influenced the scale and timing of export production. These scholars collectively demonstrate that institutional frameworks not only regulate the transmission of skills but also determine the distribution of human capital across regions. For instance, van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of apprenticeship systems and professional access shaped the skill premium in Europe, while Pamuk (2007, p.25) emphasizes how institutional flexibility allowed some regions to adapt to post-Black Death economic shifts, thereby maintaining wage levels and fostering growth. Temin (2002, p.6) further illustrates that postwar institutions in Europe created incentives for long-term investment, reinforcing the link between institutional design and sustained productivity gains. The shared insight is that institutions do not merely reflect economic conditions but actively shape them by influencing the availability and distribution of skilled labor. This mechanism is evident in Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who show that institutional differences in urbanization patterns contributed to the early modern great divergence between Europe and Asia. The implication is that institutional arrangements for training and professional access are critical in determining the trajectory of economic development.

This position is shared by (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11), who emphasize the importance of historical context in shaping contemporary institutions without explicitly connecting them to sustained economic performance. The paper suggests that institutions congruent with indigenous practices enhance governmental legitimacy, but it stops short of analyzing how this congruence translates into growth outcomes. Similarly, Hopkins (2019, p.11) underscores the historical construction of poverty in Africa, attributing underdevelopment to the legacy of European capitalism. While this perspective illuminates the role of external forces in shaping institutional trajectories, it does not provide a mechanism for how these historical processes influence long-term economic growth. The absence of a direct link between institutional structures and economic performance in these works reflects a broader tendency in the stream to prioritize historical explanation over causal analysis. This focus on historical legacies, however, opens the door to examining how institutional congruence and historical continuity might indirectly shape economic trajectories through their impact on governance and policy stability.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim reinforced by the idea that technological progress must be complemented by institutional progress to sustain long-term economic improvement. This dual emphasis on knowledge and institutions suggests that neither factor operates in isolation but rather in tandem to shape economic outcomes. The role of knowledge as a primary driver of growth is further underscored by the historical expansion of educational institutions, which, despite their limited reach, played a critical role in reducing access costs and disseminating scientific and technical expertise. These developments, however, were often constrained by the broader institutional environment, which could either facilitate or hinder the translation of knowledge into productive economic activity. The interplay between knowledge diffusion and institutional frameworks thus emerges as a central mechanism through which economic growth is sustained. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) complements this perspective by highlighting the complexity of the catch-up process, which is influenced not only by technological opportunity but also by the educational and institutional legacies of past development. These historical commitments can either enable or obstruct the trajectory of economic growth, thereby reinforcing the idea that knowledge and institutions are mutually reinforcing forces. The synthesis of these arguments suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is not only a rival cause to institutional determinism but also a mechanism through which institutional structures are continuously reshaped over time. This dynamic relationship between knowledge and institutions underscores the need for a more integrated analysis of their joint role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is consistently emphasized across the literature, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) showing that institutional changes from colonialism significantly influenced income distribution in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) argues that institutions, rather than population or savings, are central to understanding economic growth (North 1989, p.1). These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on development trajectories. However, the sources diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions operate. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, provide a better explanation for core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), whereas Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight how restrictive policies entrenched elite advantages, perpetuating inequality in the New World (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily reflect power dynamics or economic rationality. The resolution of this debate lies in the historical analysis of institutional persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show that colonial institutions endured long after independence, shaping contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).
