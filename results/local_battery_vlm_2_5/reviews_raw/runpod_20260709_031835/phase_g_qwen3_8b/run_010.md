The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more fundamental role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) propose that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists and undermines economic development. These competing explanations highlight the tension between institutional determinism and alternative historical forces. North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions matter for economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. His work underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping the conditions for sustained growth, aligning with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) in asserting that institutions significantly influence economic performance. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that historical institutional analysis is crucial for understanding contemporary African development, suggesting that institutions are shaped by long-standing historical processes. This stream reinforces the idea that institutions are not static but evolve from complex historical interactions, further complicating the causal relationship between institutions and growth. The debate over institutional causality remains unresolved, with each stream offering a distinct perspective on how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes.

Both streams converge on the idea that colonial legacies embedded extractive institutions that constrained economic potential. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) further support this by showing that institutions shaped by colonial history significantly affect income per capita, contra geographic factors, and that these institutions persisted long after colonial rule ended. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) add that secure rights and economic growth are fostered by specific institutional arrangements, such as representative government and the primacy of common law courts. These arrangements, they argue, were critical in enabling the financial revolution and long-run success in England. This raises the question of how these institutional legacies can be overcome, a challenge that remains central to the study of long-run economic development.

Institutional adjustments and historical development shape long-term economic performance by enabling sustained growth through effective governance and innovation, as demonstrated by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9). Van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of skill transmission influenced the skill premium in medieval Europe, thereby shaping long-term economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gupta, meanwhile, argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began before 1800, emphasizing the role of institutional frameworks in fostering economic development. These scholars collectively suggest that the capacity of societies to adapt to changing environments is deeply tied to the quality and flexibility of their institutional structures. The historical persistence of certain institutional arrangements, such as those regulating apprenticeship and access to professions, further underscores how governance mechanisms can either constrain or enable economic progress. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) add that institutional design in colonial contexts also played a critical role in shaping wage levels and economic integration, reinforcing the idea that historical institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic performance. The interplay between institutional resilience and adaptability thus emerges as a key mechanism through which long-term growth is sustained. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how historical institutions not only reflect but also shape the trajectory of economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that historical legacies like the slave trade shaped institutions, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. This mistrust undermines the development of inclusive institutions, which are critical for sustained economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further emphasizes that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth depends on their congruence with indigenous practices, a condition that was often compromised by colonial interventions. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, created institutional environments that hindered the formation of trust-based economic relationships, a key determinant of long-term development. Peters (2004, p.19) adds that land conflicts in Africa, which are often linked to historical patterns of exclusion and class formation, further complicate the relationship between institutions and growth by perpetuating social fragmentation. These sources collectively suggest that historical legacies such as the slave trade not only shaped the institutional frameworks of African societies but also created structural barriers to economic development that continue to influence contemporary growth trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that knowledge and technological progress, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by Abramovitz (1986, p.19) who emphasizes the complexity of the catch-up process. Both authors highlight the critical role of technological innovation in shaping economic trajectories, with Mokyr noting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Abramovitz further argues that the educational and institutional commitments from past development may stand as an obstacle to continued progress, underscoring the interplay between knowledge diffusion and structural constraints. The historical data from Maddison’s compilation, referenced by Abramovitz, reveals that productivity leadership and its shifts are central to understanding modern economic growth, reinforcing the idea that technological advancement is a key determinant. Mokyr also points out that while technological progress is more irreversible than institutional change, the sustainability of economic improvement requires complementary institutional progress to prevent the erosion of rent-seeking behaviors. This dual emphasis on technological innovation and its institutional complementarity suggests that the growth of knowledge is not only a primary cause of economic development but also a factor that interacts with institutional frameworks in shaping long-term outcomes. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation thus becomes a central theme in understanding the mechanisms of sustained economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues that ideas and ideologies are central to economic growth, as they create the cultural and institutional conditions necessary for technological adoption and innovation. This claim is supported by the observation that in France, the persistence of conservative ideologies and religious dogma hindered economic progress for centuries, even in the face of global examples of successful economic development. Similarly, in Spain, the dominance of conservative values suppressed economic growth until relatively recent times, despite the presence of capable institutions and technologies. These cases illustrate how ideological frameworks can either enable or constrain the diffusion of new economic practices. The interplay between ideas and institutions is further emphasized by the notion that economic growth is not merely a result of material conditions but is deeply embedded in the cultural and ideological environment. This perspective challenges the traditional focus on institutions alone, suggesting that the evolution of ideas is a critical driver of long-term economic change. By shaping the norms and values that govern economic behavior, ideas influence the feasibility and acceptance of new technologies and institutional arrangements. The implications of this argument are significant, as they suggest that policies aimed at fostering economic growth must also address the ideological and cultural dimensions of society. This sets the stage for examining how the diffusion of knowledge and technological progress interacts with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.6) argues that the Golden Age of European growth was shaped by pre-war disequilibrium, a claim supported by the broader analysis of how institutional frameworks interacted with economic dynamics. This perspective is further reinforced by the observation that the speed and scale of growth during this period were not merely a result of post-war policy interventions but were instead driven by the structural imbalances that persisted from before the war. The interplay between these imbalances and the emerging institutional arrangements created a unique environment in which productivity gains and wage increases were mutually reinforcing, as investors sought to capitalize on opportunities while workers benefited from higher returns. This dynamic, however, was only sustained through the development of institutions that mitigated the short-term temptations of reneging on long-term commitments. The evidence suggests that the institutional innovations of the postwar era were not the primary cause of the Golden Age but rather a response to the underlying conditions that had already been set in motion. These insights highlight the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions emerge and how they interact with broader economic forces to shape long-term growth trajectories. The role of institutions, therefore, must be seen not as an independent driver of growth but as a mechanism that channels and amplifies the effects of pre-existing structural conditions.

The consensus across the literature is that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. North (1989, p.1) similarly emphasizes that institutions shape economic outcomes by overcoming historical obstacles, reinforcing the idea that they are not merely passive features but active determinants of growth. Both streams converge on the view that institutions channel and amplify the effects of pre-existing conditions, rather than acting as independent drivers. However, the sources diverge on the origins of these institutions, with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlighting that initial conditions such as factor endowments and geography play a critical role in shaping institutional trajectories. This disagreement centers on whether institutional development is primarily a product of historical circumstances or of deliberate political choices. The resolution of this debate lies in the empirical comparison of colonial legacies and their persistence, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which demonstrates how institutions established during colonialism have long-term effects on economic performance. This historical evidence underscores the interplay between institutions and structural conditions in shaping growth.
