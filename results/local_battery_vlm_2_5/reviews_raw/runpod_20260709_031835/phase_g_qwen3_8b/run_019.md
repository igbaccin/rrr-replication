The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are causal drivers of development or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress. This disagreement hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic outcomes or exogenous, shaping the conditions under which growth can occur. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, rooted in colonial history, explain divergent income trajectories among former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize how restrictive institutions in the New World entrenched inequality by privileging elites. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that influence the trajectory of economic development. North (1989, p.1) further reinforces this causal link by highlighting how institutional evolution in early modern Britain and Spain shaped the economic outcomes of their colonial empires. These accounts collectively suggest that institutions are not only critical to growth but also deeply embedded in historical processes that determine their form and function. The intellectual stakes lie in whether understanding institutions as causal agents can provide a coherent explanation for long-run economic divergence, or whether alternative explanations rooted in geography or culture offer a more robust framework for analysis.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks that either enable or hinder development. Both authors emphasize that the durability of these institutional arrangements is not accidental but the result of historical processes that embedded certain norms and practices into the fabric of society. This institutional persistence is further reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.30) who highlight how the specific institutional arrangements in England such as the primacy of common law courts and the distribution of political power enabled the protection of economic rights and the acceleration of growth. The shared insight across these sources is that the institutional legacies of colonial rule are not merely historical relics but active determinants of economic outcomes. These frameworks, once established, shape the incentives and constraints faced by subsequent generations, influencing the trajectory of development over time. The implications of this institutional persistence suggest that understanding the historical roots of institutions is essential for explaining long-run economic divergence.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that institutional changes from colonialism shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that influence productivity and development. This claim is supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights how the efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of skill transmission affected the skill premium in Europe, thereby shaping long-term economic performance. Similarly, Pamuk (2007, p.25) shows that institutional flexibility allowed certain regions to adapt more effectively to economic shocks, such as the Black Death, which in turn influenced the trajectory of economic divergence. These sources collectively suggest that the institutional environment created during colonial and pre-colonial periods played a critical role in determining the capacity of societies to sustain growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further reinforce this by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began before 1800, indicating that institutional legacies from earlier historical periods had lasting effects on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutional frameworks, as documented across these sources, underscores their profound influence on the long-term development of economies. This institutional legacy continues to shape contemporary economic trajectories by embedding patterns of growth and stagnation that originated in historical institutional arrangements.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality, which shaped long-run economic growth patterns. Their work, alongside Nunn (2008, p.19), Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3), collectively demonstrate that historical institutional legacies, particularly those rooted in slavery and extractive systems, created enduring disparities that constrained economic development. These institutions, often designed to serve elite interests, entrenched inequality by limiting access to resources, opportunities, and political power for broader segments of society. The persistence of these structures, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3), also fostered a culture of mistrust that further hindered economic integration and cooperation. The cumulative effect of these institutional arrangements was to create a feedback loop in which inequality reinforced stagnation, while stagnation perpetuated inequality. This dynamic underscores the deep entanglement between historical institutions and long-term economic trajectories, revealing how the legacies of the past continue to shape the present. The interplay between institutional design, inequality, and growth remains a central theme in understanding the divergent paths of economic development across societies.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts are central to shaping core economic institutions, a claim supported by Peters (2004, p.19) who highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusionary patterns that underpin class formation. Both authors emphasize that institutional frameworks are not neutral but are instead shaped by underlying tensions over resource allocation and power. These tensions, in turn, influence the trajectory of economic growth by determining the types of institutions that are institutionalized and the incentives they create for investment and innovation. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further illustrate this dynamic by showing how institutional design in colonial Africa was influenced by the relative commercial development of different regions, which itself was shaped by historical distributional conflicts. McCloskey (2015, p.9) expands this perspective by arguing that ideas and cultural values, rather than just material interests, play a critical role in shaping institutions and, consequently, long-term economic outcomes. Together, these sources demonstrate that distributional conflicts are not merely a byproduct of economic institutions but are a fundamental force in their creation and evolution. This insight suggests that understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires a deeper engagement with the social and cultural forces that shape them, as these forces are integral to the institutional frameworks that sustain or hinder growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge drives long-run economic growth through institutional progress, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that technological advancement requires complementary institutional adjustments. The interplay between knowledge expansion and institutional adaptation is central to understanding sustained economic development, as illustrated by Mokyr’s observation that institutional progress is necessary to prevent the erosion of economic gains caused by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets further underscores this by highlighting how modern technologies, such as steam and electric power, necessitate institutional changes that move away from outdated systems like family enterprise and slavery. These insights collectively suggest that technological progress alone is insufficient to sustain growth without corresponding institutional evolution. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder future development, reinforcing the idea that institutions are not static but dynamic elements shaped by historical trajectories. Temin (2002, p.6) provides a historical case where postwar institutions in Europe fostered economic growth by aligning incentives and enforcing long-term contracts, demonstrating how institutional frameworks can catalyze technological and economic progress. This stream collectively establishes that the relationship between knowledge, technology, and institutions is not merely correlative but constitutive of long-run growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues that historical institutional analysis is relevant to understanding African development, but does not directly link institutions to long-run economic growth. This stance is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), which emphasizes the need to write African economic history in a way that acknowledges the complex interplay of historical forces. Both papers highlight how institutions are shaped by historical contexts, yet they avoid specifying the mechanisms through which these institutions influence economic trajectories. However Hopkins (2019, p.11) warns against overemphasizing path dependence cautioning that such an approach risks oversimplifying the diverse influences that shape outcomes. The shared focus on historical legacies underscores the importance of context in institutional analysis, yet it stops short of establishing a direct causal link between institutional structures and sustained economic growth. This focus on historical continuity raises questions about how institutional legacies can be reoriented to support modern development goals.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) affirm that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income differences among former colonies, highlighting their role in shaping economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) adds that contemporary institutions are shaped by historical legacies, including precolonial practices, which must be considered to understand their impact on development. While both authors agree that institutions are central to economic trajectories, they diverge on the relative importance of historical legacies versus geographic factors. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, suggesting that initial conditions, such as factor endowments, played a critical role. This contrasts with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.5), who emphasize that colonial institutions persistently shaped income per capita, underscoring the long-term effects of institutional legacies over geographic determinants. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional frameworks or geographic conditions are the primary drivers of long-run economic outcomes. North (1989, p.1) provides a historical comparison between England and Spain, showing how institutional evolution influenced economic consequences, offering a case where institutional change is directly linked to development paths. This empirical comparison settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutional structures, rather than geographic factors, can be the decisive force in shaping economic growth.
