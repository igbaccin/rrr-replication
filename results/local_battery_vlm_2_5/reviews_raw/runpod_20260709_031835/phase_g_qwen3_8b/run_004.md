The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary causal force behind economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more dominant role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) emphasize that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect economic outcomes today. Both streams highlight how historical institutions can have lasting impacts, yet they differ in the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate. Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the importance of historical institutional analysis in understanding contemporary African development, arguing that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of precolonial and colonial legacies. This suggests that the causal pathways of institutions are not uniform and may vary across regions and historical contexts. The debate over whether institutions are the main driver of growth or one of several factors remains unresolved, but the evidence increasingly points to their central role in shaping long-run economic trajectories.

Institutional arrangements foster long-run economic growth by shaping secure rights and reducing uncertainties, a claim supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and North (1989, p.1). North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that the evolution of institutions, such as those rooted in common law, created environments conducive to economic development by establishing predictable rules and reducing transaction costs. These institutions, as North (1989, p.1) argues, were not merely passive structures but active forces that influenced the distribution of power and the protection of property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and the rule of law are essential for economic growth, as they provide the stability needed for investment and innovation. The interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes is thus a central theme in understanding long-run development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that institutional adjustments are a key driver of long-run economic growth, as they enable and sustain technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.1); Mokyr (2005, p.54) further supports this by emphasizing the role of institutions in complementing technological advancement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Both authors highlight that without institutional progress, the benefits of technological innovation may be short-lived and vulnerable to the erosion of rent-seeking behaviors. The interplay between institutional and technological change is thus central to understanding sustained economic growth. This dynamic is also evident in Temin (2002, p.6), who shows how postwar institutions in Europe reinforced long-term contracts and productivity gains (Temin 2002, p.6), reinforcing the idea that institutional frameworks are not merely supportive but integral to the trajectory of technological progress. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional legacies on future growth trajectories (Abramovitz 1986, p.19). These sources collectively demonstrate that institutional evolution is not a passive accompaniment to technological change but a critical factor in shaping its direction and sustainability. The role of institutions in fostering a conducive environment for innovation and long-term economic development remains a central theme in the analysis of historical growth patterns.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping skill premiums and wage trends drive long-run economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), and Pamuk (2007, p.25). These scholars collectively demonstrate that the institutional regulation of skill transmission and labor markets has been a key determinant of wage differentials and economic divergence. Van Zanden highlights how the efficiency of training institutions influenced the supply of skilled labor, thereby affecting the skill premium in medieval Europe. Pamuk further shows that flexible institutions enabled northwestern Europe to adapt to post-Black Death conditions, fostering sustained economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner extend this argument to Sub-Saharan Africa, noting that institutional frameworks shaped the responsiveness of economies to market forces and trade dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize that institutional design in colonial contexts determined labor market outcomes, with East African states adopting more interventionist policies due to perceived labor shortages. These findings collectively underscore that institutional arrangements not only influence wage structures but also mediate the long-term trajectory of economic performance. The persistence of institutional legacies in shaping skill premiums and wage trends suggests that historical institutional frameworks continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that colonial history and institutional legacies are fundamental to understanding long-run economic growth, challenging the focus on institutions alone. Hopkins emphasizes that current institutions are shaped by a history that extends beyond colonial rule, incorporating precolonial and even prehistoric influences, while Nunn and Wantchekon highlight how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists in contemporary African societies. Both scholars underscore the necessity of integrating historical analysis into economic development frameworks, as institutional legacies are not merely products of modern governance but are deeply embedded in the historical processes of colonialism and precolonial societies. The persistence of these legacies, as Hopkins (2019, p.11) notes, has created conditions where underdevelopment is not an original state but a historical creation, shaped by the mechanisms of European capitalism and unequal exchange. This perspective shifts the focus from the mere existence of institutions to the historical trajectories that have shaped their forms and functions. Nunn (2008, p.19) further complements this by showing that the production of slaves through domestic warfare and raiding had direct negative impacts on the development of African societies, reinforcing the idea that historical legacies are not passive but active forces in shaping economic outcomes. These findings suggest that understanding long-run growth requires an explicit engagement with the historical roots of institutions and their interactions with broader social and economic processes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency, a stance shared by Peters (2004, p.19) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3). These scholars emphasize that class formation and struggles over resource control are central to understanding the evolution of institutions and their impact on long-run growth. Ogilvie highlights how institutional frameworks often resist change due to their self-reinforcing nature, which can perpetuate inequality and hinder innovation. Peters illustrates this through land conflicts in Africa, showing how exclusionary practices and class dynamics shape economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack further support this by demonstrating how women's roles in production and reproduction were integral to economic development, yet their contributions were often overlooked or marginalized. The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional structures suggests that economic growth is not merely a product of efficient institutions but is deeply embedded in the historical processes of class struggle and resource allocation. This perspective challenges the traditional focus on institutional design by foregrounding the social and political dynamics that underpin institutional change and its economic consequences.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies, rather than institutions, are the primary forces shaping long-run economic growth, a stance supported by Stern (2004, p.2) in its critique of the environmental Kuznets curve. Both authors challenge the conventional focus on institutional structures by emphasizing the role of cultural and intellectual shifts in driving economic transformation. McCloskey illustrates how ideological battles, such as the resistance to secularization in France, constrained economic progress by reinforcing rigid social hierarchies and suppressing innovation. Similarly, Stern highlights how the failure to account for ideological and institutional biases in economic models has led to flawed conclusions about the relationship between development and environmental outcomes. Together, these works underscore the importance of cognitive and normative frameworks in shaping the conditions for sustained economic growth. The interplay between ideological narratives and material realities suggests that economic development is not solely a function of institutional design but is deeply influenced by the prevailing ideas that legitimize or hinder certain forms of progress. This perspective invites a reevaluation of how we conceptualize the mechanisms through which societies transition from stagnation to growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) demonstrate that institutional legacies from colonial rule significantly shaped long-run income disparities among former colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Ogilvie (2007, p.27), however, challenges the primacy of institutional efficiency, arguing that distributional conflicts are a more compelling explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This divergence highlights a fundamental debate over whether institutions should be evaluated primarily through their economic efficiency or their role in mediating social and political dynamics. The disagreement is resolved through historical analysis that traces how institutional frameworks evolved in response to specific socio-political contexts, as seen in North’s (1989, p.1) examination of institutional development in England and Spain (North 1989, p.1). By comparing the institutional trajectories of these two societies, North illustrates how institutional persistence and change are deeply intertwined with historical conditions, offering a framework to assess the long-term effects of institutional design. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes the need to consider precolonial and prehistoric influences on current institutions, arguing that historical institutional analysis is crucial for understanding contemporary African development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). These sources collectively affirm that institutions are not static but are shaped by a complex interplay of historical, social, and political forces, with their long-term economic implications rooted in their historical origins and evolution.
