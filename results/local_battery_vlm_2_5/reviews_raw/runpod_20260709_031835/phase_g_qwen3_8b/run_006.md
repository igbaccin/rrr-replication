The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history, with significant implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained development. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, play a more fundamental role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geographic conditions, explain the divergence in income levels among former European colonies. This claim is reinforced by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who emphasize that restrictive institutional policies in the New World entrenched inequality by favoring elites, thereby limiting broad-based economic progress. These accounts highlight how institutional frameworks can either enable or constrain growth, depending on their design and historical context. North (1989, p.1) further supports this view by asserting that institutions, not population or savings, are the key to unlocking economic potential, as they shape the rules of the game that govern economic activity. The debate is thus not merely about the importance of institutions but about their causal mechanisms and the conditions under which they exert their influence. The next section examines how these institutional structures are shaped by historical antecedents and the broader social and political environments in which they emerge.

North (1989, p.1) complements this by tracing how institutional legacies from England and Spain, rooted in historical precedents and legal traditions, produced divergent paths of economic development in the New World. Both papers highlight that institutions do not merely reflect economic conditions but actively shape them through rules that govern property rights, political power, and resource allocation. The persistence of extractive institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further illustrates how historical choices lock economies into trajectories of growth or stagnation. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) extend this by showing that secure property rights and representative governance, embedded in institutional arrangements, are essential for sustained economic expansion. These scholars collectively demonstrate that the design of institutions determines the capacity of societies to mobilize resources and sustain development over time. The implications of this framework suggest that understanding institutional origins is critical for diagnosing and addressing long-term economic disparities.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that colonial legacies and institutional divergence shape long-run economic growth by establishing enduring institutional frameworks that influence productivity and development trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This claim is supported by evidence that colonial institutions, such as those in East Africa, were designed to address perceived labor shortages, resulting in direct state intervention in labor markets and persistent wage disparities. The institutional design of colonial states, shaped by precolonial commercial development, influenced wage levels and determined the long-term capacity of these economies to integrate into global markets. Pamuk (2007, p.25) further illustrates how institutional flexibility allowed northwestern Europe to adapt to post-Black Death economic conditions, reinforcing the role of institutions in shaping divergent developmental paths (Pamuk 2007, p.25). These examples highlight how institutional frameworks, whether colonial or preexisting, create structural conditions that either enable or constrain economic growth over time. The persistence of these frameworks suggests that understanding the historical origins of institutions is essential for explaining long-term economic outcomes. This insight underscores the need to examine how institutional legacies continue to influence contemporary economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that historical institutions shaped by the slave trade negatively impacted long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that the slave trade disrupted the development of institutions capable of facilitating trade with Europeans. These findings are further reinforced by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who emphasizes the importance of historical institutional analysis in understanding contemporary African development. The persistence of mistrust, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon, is linked to the internal mechanisms of the slave trade, which fostered social fragmentation and weakened collective action. This pattern of institutional degradation, as highlighted by Peters (2004, p.19), is evident in the enduring land conflicts that reflect exclusionary practices and class formation. The cumulative effect of these historical disruptions is a diminished capacity for institutional trust and cooperation, which continues to hinder economic growth. This legacy of mistrust and fragmented institutions underscores the long-term consequences of historical institutional legacies on economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in New World societies perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, a claim supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) who emphasizes that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, often underpin core economic institutions. These scholars highlight how institutional frameworks, shaped by historical legacies, can entrench disparities that persist over time, influencing long-run economic growth patterns. The interplay between institutional rules and cultural beliefs, as noted by Ogilvie, further complicates the analysis, suggesting that norms and values can reinforce or challenge institutional structures. McCloskey (2015, p.9) adds another dimension by advocating for a 'humanomics' approach that prioritizes ideas over institutions, arguing that cultural and ideological shifts can reshape economic trajectories. This stream collectively underscores how institutional development is not merely a technical process but is deeply embedded in social and historical contexts that shape inequality and growth. The persistence of elite advantages, as demonstrated by Sokoloff and Engerman, illustrates how institutional legacies can constrain economic potential, a theme that resonates with the broader discussion on historical institutional legacies in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a stance shared by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), Kuznets (1973, p.1), Temin (2002, p.6), and Stern (2004, p.2). This cluster of scholars collectively emphasizes that technological progress and the diffusion of knowledge are the primary engines of long-run economic development, independent of institutional frameworks. Mokyr highlights how the expansion of scientific understanding and its application in practical fields, such as chemistry and engineering, laid the groundwork for industrial transformation, even when formal institutions were slow to adapt. Abramovitz, meanwhile, underscores that the catch-up process in economic growth is not simply a matter of adopting existing technologies but involves the complex interplay of education, innovation, and institutional adaptation. Stern (2004, p.2) further complicates the narrative by challenging the environmental Kuznets curve, suggesting that growth and environmental outcomes are not neatly correlated but are shaped by broader technological and institutional dynamics. Together, these sources illustrate a consistent emphasis on the transformative power of knowledge and technology as the core drivers of economic progress, a perspective that challenges the centrality of institutions in shaping long-run growth. This focus on technological and knowledge-based growth sets the stage for examining how these forces interact with and are shaped by institutional contexts in subsequent analyses.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is consistently underscored across multiple streams of scholarship, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) emphasizing their critical role in explaining income disparities among former colonies. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) highlights how institutional frameworks in early modern Britain and Spain shaped divergent economic trajectories, reinforcing the idea that institutions are foundational to understanding growth dynamics. These perspectives converge on the view that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that channel economic potential. However, the extent to which institutions are the primary determinant of growth remains a point of contention. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency, suggesting that institutional design is deeply rooted in social and political dynamics rather than purely economic rationality. This divergence underscores a fundamental debate: whether institutions are shaped by economic forces or by broader cultural and political contestations. The resolution of this disagreement lies in the historical analysis of institutional persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which shows how colonial-era institutions have endured and shaped contemporary economic outcomes. By examining the long-term effects of institutional legacies, scholars can better distinguish between the enduring influence of institutions and the contingent factors that shape their evolution.
