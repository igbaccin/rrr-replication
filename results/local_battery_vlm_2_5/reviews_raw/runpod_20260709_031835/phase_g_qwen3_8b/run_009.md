The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary cause of economic divergence or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the root cause of growth or merely a byproduct of deeper, often unmeasured, forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional differences, shaped by colonial policies and population dynamics, are central to explaining income disparities among former European colonies. This claim is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who highlight how historical legacies, such as the slave trade, have left enduring cultural and institutional scars that continue to affect trust and economic outcomes. These perspectives emphasize that institutions are not just passive structures but active forces that shape the trajectory of economic development. The next section explores how institutions are shaped by historical and cultural antecedents, further complicating the causal relationship between institutions and growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutional arrangements and colonial history shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either enable or hinder development. These scholars highlight how the institutional legacies of colonial rule, such as extractive systems and centralized control, created enduring barriers to economic progress, while alternative institutional configurations, like those in settler colonies, fostered more inclusive growth. The persistence of these institutional structures, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores the long-term impact of colonial governance on contemporary economic outcomes, suggesting that the effects of historical institutions are not merely incidental but deeply embedded in the trajectory of development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate on how secure property rights and representative institutions are critical for sustained economic growth, emphasizing that the interplay between political and economic institutions determines the capacity for innovation and investment. This interplay is also evident in the work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who demonstrate that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, thereby constraining broader economic participation. The shared insight across these sources is that institutional frameworks, shaped by historical and colonial contexts, are central to understanding the divergent paths of economic growth. This focus on institutional persistence and its implications for growth sets the stage for examining how cultural and historical antecedents continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Historical legacies like the slave trade shaped institutions that hindered long-run economic growth in Africa. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade not only disrupted local economies but also entrenched institutions that limited trust and economic cooperation, creating persistent barriers to development. These findings are reinforced by Peters (2004, p.19), who shows how colonial land policies and historical exclusion fostered institutional arrangements that marginalized large segments of the population, further entrenching inequality. The cumulative effect of these historical processes is a legacy of institutional weakness that constrains economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.19) highlights how societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, suggesting that the slave trade disproportionately affected regions with less institutional capacity, thereby exacerbating long-term underdevelopment. This pattern is echoed in Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that precolonial commercial development influenced the institutional design of colonial states, shaping the trajectory of economic outcomes. The shared implication is that historical institutions, shaped by the slave trade and colonial rule, created structural impediments that continue to influence economic growth today. These insights underscore the necessity of understanding institutional legacies as central to explaining divergent economic trajectories in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim reinforced by Abramovitz (1986, p.19) who highlights the complexity of the catch-up process and the role of educational and institutional commitments. These scholars collectively underscore the centrality of knowledge dissemination and institutional progress in driving long-term economic development. Mokyr emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement remains constrained, as seen in the limited impact of early industrial-era educational institutions. Abramovitz further notes that past educational and institutional commitments can act as barriers to contemporary growth, suggesting that historical legacies of knowledge transmission shape current economic trajectories. Stern (2004, p.2), while focusing on environmental dynamics, aligns with this stream by advocating for models that integrate institutional and technological factors to better understand development processes. The shared posture of these sources highlights how institutional frameworks and knowledge diffusion are interdependent, with each reinforcing the other as critical drivers of sustained economic growth. This interplay underscores the necessity of examining both technological and institutional dimensions when analyzing long-run economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and ideologies drive long-run economic growth, challenging the institutionalist view. McCloskey (2015, p.9) highlights how cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, have historically enabled economic transformation by altering the rhetorical and institutional landscape. Both authors underscore the importance of understanding how ideas, such as the valorization of scientific inquiry or the rejection of rigid religious dogma, can catalyze institutional change and foster economic development. This ideological underpinning of institutional evolution suggests that the persistence of certain economic outcomes is a product of both institutional design and the prevailing cultural and intellectual climate. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further notes that the economic relevance of beliefs and values remains a critical yet underexplored dimension in institutional analysis. Together, these sources demonstrate that the ideological environment is not merely a backdrop to institutional development but an active force that shapes the trajectory of economic growth. The interplay between ideas and institutions thus offers a more comprehensive framework for understanding long-term economic change.

African economic history can be written, and it contributes to understanding economic growth and development in the region. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) together demonstrate that historical institutional analysis is essential for grasping the long-term dynamics of African economies. These sources collectively highlight how historical legacies, whether institutional or gender-based, continue to influence economic trajectories. Hopkins (2019, p.11) argues that the narrative of African economic history has been constrained by deterministic assumptions, but recent scholarship challenges this by emphasizing the agency of local actors and the contingent nature of historical development. This shift in perspective allows for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with broader socio-economic forces. By integrating historical analysis with institutional theory, these scholars provide a foundation for rethinking the causes of economic growth in Africa. The interplay between historical context and institutional design thus remains a key area for further exploration in the study of long-run economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) demonstrate that institutions have played a central role in shaping economic growth in former colonies, accounting for significant income differences through their impact on industrialization and development. This aligns with North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes that institutional frameworks, rather than mere population or savings rates, are the key to understanding historical economic outcomes. Both scholars highlight the enduring influence of institutional structures established during colonial periods, which continue to shape contemporary economic performance. However, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) challenge this by arguing that institutional differences in the New World were not solely the result of colonial policies but were also shaped by preexisting geographic and demographic conditions. Their analysis underscores the complexity of institutional development, suggesting that initial conditions such as climate, disease, and population density played a critical role in determining long-term economic trajectories. The disagreement hinges on whether institutional legacies or environmental factors are the primary drivers of economic divergence. This debate is resolved through historical institutional analysis, as Hopkins (2009, p.8) argues, which examines how institutions are embedded in precolonial and colonial histories, providing a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between institutions and economic growth.
