The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary force behind economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical contingency, play a more decisive role. The disagreement lies in the causal pathways through which institutions operate and the extent to which they can be considered the root cause of growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history significantly affect income per capita, contra geographic factors, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) emphasize that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies. These perspectives highlight the tension between institutional determinism and the influence of preexisting conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further support the idea that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies. This body of work underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic trajectories, framing them as critical determinants of long-term growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that institutional arrangements foster secure rights and long-run economic growth by shaping the rules of the game, a claim supported by North (1989, p.1) who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories emphasizing population and savings. Both scholars highlight how institutional structures, such as the primacy of common law courts and multiple veto points in representative government, create environments where economic rights are protected and political liberties are sustained. The stream collectively demonstrates that institutional arrangements are not merely passive constraints but active drivers of economic outcomes, with their design and evolution determining the trajectory of growth. The implications of these findings extend to the role of historical conditions in shaping institutional development, as seen in the case of the Low Countries and England resisting wage declines after the Black Death, which allowed for sustained economic adaptation and divergence.

Colonial institutions shaped by political forces influenced long-run economic growth through persistent institutional legacies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and Nunn (2008, p.19) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, often driven by the political interests of European powers, left enduring marks on the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or facilitative, were not merely relics of the past but active determinants of growth, shaping the conditions for investment, innovation, and human capital accumulation. Nunn (2008, p.19) further underscores that the slave trade, by promoting domestic conflict and undermining institutional development, had lasting negative effects on economic outcomes. These findings collectively suggest that the political choices made during colonial rule were critical in determining the institutional quality of postcolonial states. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) extend this argument by showing that the legacy of the slave trade also fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to impede economic cooperation and development. The cumulative evidence underscores that the institutional legacies of colonialism are deeply embedded in the structural conditions of modern economies, with profound implications for long-run growth.

Historical institutional analysis shapes the development of institutions, which are fundamental to long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that the legacy of precolonial and colonial institutions must be understood as a continuous process, not a discrete event, to fully grasp the mechanisms that underpin contemporary African development. This perspective is reinforced by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who highlights that current institutions are the product of a historical trajectory that extends beyond colonial rule, emphasizing the need to integrate long-term historical analysis into policy-making. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further develops this argument by demonstrating that African economic history is not a dead end but a critical lens through which to understand the structural conditions of modern economies. These findings suggest that institutional development is deeply rooted in historical processes, and that understanding these processes is essential for formulating effective policies aimed at long-run economic growth.

Peters (2004, p.19) demonstrates that land conflicts in Africa are not merely disputes over resources but mechanisms through which institutional exclusion is entrenched, shaping labor market dynamics that persist into modern economic structures. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) complement this by showing how colonial labor market interventions, driven by perceived shortages, created institutional frameworks that marginalized certain groups and limited wage growth. Together, these sources highlight how land-related tensions and colonial labor policies established enduring institutional patterns that constrain labor mobility and economic opportunity. The interplay between land control and labor market regulation reveals how exclusionary institutions are not static but evolve through historical processes of conflict and state intervention. This dynamic underscores the importance of historical context in understanding contemporary labor market inequalities and their broader implications for economic growth. The persistence of these institutional legacies suggests that addressing long-run economic growth requires not only policy reform but also a deeper engagement with the historical processes that shaped them.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Temin (2002, p.6) and McCloskey (2015, p.9). Ogilvie highlights how institutional frameworks are analytically essential to understanding the origins and economic impact of individual institutions, yet emphasizes that these frameworks often emerge from deeper distributional tensions. Temin, meanwhile, demonstrates how the Golden Age of European growth was driven by pre-war disequilibrium, suggesting that economic outcomes are shaped by broader systemic forces rather than static institutional structures. McCloskey extends this argument by asserting that ideas and ideologies, not interests or institutions, are the primary drivers of economic development, as seen in the historical struggle between cultural values and economic progress in France and Spain. These scholars collectively underscore that the persistence of certain economic outcomes is less about the efficiency of institutions and more about the underlying distributional conflicts that shape them. The interplay between cultural beliefs and institutional rules, as Ogilvie notes, further complicates the relationship between ideas and economic performance. This stream of scholarship suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires a focus on the ideological and distributional dynamics that underpin institutional development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that historical divergence, not institutions, is the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Their work, alongside Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), highlights how economic trajectories were shaped by external forces such as trade networks, resource availability, and the integration of regions into global markets. These scholars emphasize that the divergence between Europe and Asia, or between African economies and their colonial counterparts, was driven by the differential access to and participation in these markets, rather than by the quality of institutions alone. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner show that Sub-Saharan African economies experienced periods of growth tied to export production, which was influenced by transport costs and political institutions, yet the long-term outcomes were not primarily determined by institutional frameworks. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that women played a central role in economic production and reproduction, suggesting that gendered labor dynamics, rather than institutional structures, were key to shaping economic outcomes. This stream of scholarship underscores the importance of historical context and external economic linkages as drivers of divergence, offering a competing explanation to the institutionalist view. The implications of these findings suggest that focusing solely on institutions may obscure the broader structural forces that have historically shaped economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Abramovitz (1986, p.19). These scholars collectively emphasize that technological progress and the diffusion of knowledge are central to long-run economic expansion, offering an alternative explanation to the role of institutions in shaping growth. Mokyr highlights how the expansion of knowledge, particularly through scientific inquiry and education, laid the foundation for sustained economic development, even in the absence of formal institutional reforms. Kuznets further reinforces this by noting that modern technology requires institutional adjustments, suggesting that while institutions are important, they are secondary to the advancement of knowledge and the capacity to harness it. Abramovitz complements this by pointing out that the catch-up process is not merely a function of technological diffusion but is also influenced by the existing educational and institutional frameworks that either facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies. Together, these sources underscore the idea that knowledge growth is a self-reinforcing force, capable of driving economic development independently of institutional structures. The implications of this perspective suggest that policies aimed at fostering innovation and knowledge dissemination may be more effective in promoting long-term growth than those focused solely on institutional reform. This focus on knowledge as a primary driver of growth opens the door to examining how technological diffusion interacts with existing institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes.

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the dominance of the environmental Kuznets curve in explaining development-environment relationships, arguing that existing models fail to account for critical statistical and methodological shortcomings. This critique aligns with broader concerns that environmental factors, rather than institutional structures, may serve as a primary driver of long-run economic growth. Stern's analysis highlights the need for more rigorous empirical frameworks that consider omitted variables and model adequacy, a stance that resonates with calls for rethinking the role of institutions in shaping development trajectories. By emphasizing the limitations of conventional environmental models, Stern's work opens the door to exploring how ecological constraints interact with economic systems in ways that transcend institutional design. This perspective invites a reevaluation of the causal pathways linking environmental conditions to economic outcomes, suggesting that policy interventions targeting environmental sustainability may be as crucial as those focused on institutional reform. The implications of this shift in focus underscore the complexity of development processes, where environmental and institutional factors may operate in tandem or independently, depending on historical and contextual conditions.

The literature consistently underscores the pivotal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. This aligns with North’s assertion that institutions are central to understanding economic development, rather than factors like population or savings (North 1989, p.1). Both streams emphasize that the durability of institutional frameworks, whether extractive or inclusive, has profound implications for growth trajectories. However, the sources diverge on the origins of these institutions, with Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlighting the persistent impact of colonial-era institutional legacies, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) stresses the necessity of examining precolonial histories to fully grasp the roots of contemporary institutions. The disagreement centers on whether initial conditions, such as geography or precolonial norms, should be prioritized over institutional legacies in explaining economic outcomes. This tension is resolved through empirical comparisons that trace institutional persistence across colonial and precolonial contexts, as seen in the analysis of how extractive institutions in the New World perpetuated inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4).
