The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that sustain or hinder prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or knowledge diffusion, are more fundamental. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) emphasize that restrictive policies perpetuated by institutions entrenched elite advantages and entrenched inequality. These accounts highlight how institutions shape growth trajectories by either enabling or constraining productive activity. North (1989, p.1) further asserts that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings, framing them as the key to unlocking economic potential. Mokyr (2005, p.54) offers a complementary perspective, suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge, rather than institutions alone, is the mainspring of economic development, though he acknowledges that institutional progress is necessary to sustain technological progress. The tension between these views lies in whether institutions are the root cause of growth or merely a facilitator of broader processes. This debate remains unresolved, but the consensus is that institutions are a critical, though not exclusive, determinant of long-term economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing development paths, with historical institutional analysis providing a critical lens to understand these dynamics. Together, these works underscore the importance of historical context in shaping the institutional frameworks that either foster or hinder growth. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further develops this by emphasizing that African economic history is not a dead end but a vital source of insight into the mechanisms through which institutions have shaped development. This perspective challenges the notion that African societies lacked the capacity to generate sustained economic progress, instead highlighting the role of historical institutions in both creating and overcoming structural barriers. The shared insight across these sources is that institutions are not static but evolve through historical processes, with their design and durability determining the long-term prospects for economic growth. This historical dimension is crucial for understanding how institutions can either lock in underdevelopment or open pathways to prosperity.

Institutions foster economic growth by establishing secure rights and shaping the institutional arrangements that enable long-run development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) collectively argue that the durability of institutions, whether rooted in colonial legacies or historical legal traditions, determines the trajectory of economic performance. The persistence of these institutional patterns, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), underscores how historical decisions shape contemporary economic capabilities, often overriding geographic or resource-based explanations. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further refine this by highlighting that secure rights are not merely economic but also political, as the institutional design of representative government and legal enforcement mechanisms directly affect the ability of societies to harness economic potential. Kuznets (1973, p.1) aligns with this by noting that modern economic growth necessitates institutional adjustments alongside technological progress, reinforcing the idea that institutions are not passive but active agents in the development process. These sources collectively establish that institutional arrangements, shaped by historical legacies and political dynamics, are central to the sustained economic growth of societies.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), Pamuk (2007, p.25), and Abramovitz (1986, p.19). The expansion of knowledge, particularly through formal and informal educational institutions, has historically played a critical role in reducing the costs of innovation and enhancing the productive capacity of societies. Mokyr highlights that institutional progress is essential to sustain technological advancements, as without complementary policies, the benefits of knowledge growth may be undermined by rent-seeking behaviors. Van Zanden further emphasizes that the efficiency of training institutions and the regulation of skill transmission significantly influence the skill premium, thereby shaping long-term economic outcomes. Pamuk’s analysis of the Black Death underscores how institutional flexibility allowed certain regions to adapt more effectively to economic shocks, reinforcing the idea that knowledge diffusion is not uniform across societies. Abramovitz notes that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, suggesting that the legacy of institutional arrangements continues to shape economic trajectories. These sources collectively demonstrate that the diffusion and application of knowledge are deeply intertwined with institutional frameworks, which in turn determine the sustainability and direction of economic growth. The role of institutions in enabling knowledge-based development remains a central theme in understanding long-run economic performance.

Temin (2002, p.6) argues that institutional capture by firms and unions shaped the Golden Age of European growth through institutional dynamics, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and Stern (2004, p.2). These scholars collectively emphasize that institutional arrangements, whether domestic or international, played a crucial role in aligning economic incentives and fostering growth. Temin highlights how postwar institutions in Europe created a framework that reinforced long-term cooperation between capital and labor, countering short-term self-interest and enabling sustained productivity gains. Broadberry and Gardner further illustrate that institutional frameworks were not only pivotal for growth but also for managing the fluctuations in economic performance tied to trade and market conditions. Stern, while addressing a different topic, reinforces the idea that institutional structures can shape economic outcomes in ways that are often overlooked. The joint argument suggests that institutional dynamics were central to the unique economic performance of the Golden Age, setting a precedent for how institutions can influence growth trajectories. This insight underscores the need to examine institutional configurations as key determinants of long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional legacies in the New World perpetuated inequality by entrenching restrictive policies that advantaged elites, thereby shaping long-run economic growth through exclusionary practices. This claim is supported by Peters (2004, p.19), who highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal systemic exclusion and class formation, challenging the notion of flexible customary systems. Together, these sources underscore how institutional frameworks, once established, can lock in patterns of inequality that influence economic trajectories over time. The persistence of such structures is further illustrated by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who demonstrate that colonial institutions in Africa had lasting effects on wage levels and labor market dynamics, reinforcing the idea that historical institutional legacies continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes. These findings suggest that the interplay between institutional design and social stratification is a critical mechanism through which inequality persists and influences growth. The long-term implications of these institutional patterns are also evident in Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who show that precolonial differences in commercial development set the stage for divergent institutional trajectories between regions, further entrenching disparities in economic performance. This stream collectively reinforces the argument that institutional legacies are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape the distribution of economic opportunities and outcomes over time.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic growth, challenging the traditional focus on institutional structures. This perspective is supported by the observation that in France, the persistence of conservative ideologies, such as the emphasis on Catholicism and anti-Semitism, hindered economic development for centuries, despite the availability of modern institutions. Similarly, in Spain, the dominance of conservative rhetoric suppressed economic growth until relatively recent times, even in the face of successful examples from the Netherlands and Britain. These cases illustrate how cultural and ideological factors can shape economic outcomes independently of institutional frameworks. The interplay between rhetoric and economic performance is further highlighted by the notion that new ideas can create new circumstances, which in turn generate new ideologies, forming a dynamic feedback loop. This mechanism suggests that economic growth is not merely a function of institutional design but is deeply intertwined with the evolution of cultural and ideological narratives. The implications of this view are significant, as they shift the focus from static institutional analysis to a more fluid understanding of how ideas continuously influence and are influenced by economic development. This stream of thought thus redefines the role of institutions by positioning them as secondary to the broader cultural and ideological forces that shape long-run economic growth.

The literature consistently underscores the profound impact of institutions on long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutional frameworks are essential for understanding historical obstacles to economic growth. These perspectives align in highlighting institutions as foundational to economic trajectories. However the sources diverge on the relative importance of cultural and ideological factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) contends that beliefs and values are not merely peripheral but are integral to shaping institutional outcomes, challenging the primacy of economic efficiency in institutional analysis. This disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by economic logic or by broader cultural narratives. The resolution of this debate lies in historical institutional analysis, as Hopkins (2009, p.8) illustrates, where the persistence of precolonial and colonial institutions provides empirical grounding for understanding contemporary economic conditions. By examining the historical continuity of institutional legacies, scholars can better disentangle the interplay between institutions, culture, and economic development.
