The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and contemporary policy effectiveness. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as knowledge diffusion or cultural legacies, offer competing explanations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, particularly those shaping political and economic inequality, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) propose that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and undermines economic development. Both streams emphasize the long-term consequences of historical institutional legacies, yet they diverge in the mechanisms through which these legacies operate. Mokyr (2005, p.28) contends that the growth of useful knowledge, rather than institutions alone, is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting the role of education and technological progress in fostering innovation. This perspective complements Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), which underscores the institutional channel as a key determinant of economic performance and income differences. The tension between these views lies in whether institutions are the primary causal force or merely one of several factors influencing growth. The intellectual stakes involve the validity of institutionalist theories and their capacity to explain sustained economic development across different historical and geographical contexts.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that institutional adjustments and technological progress are indispensable for sustained economic growth, as they enable the adoption of modern technologies incompatible with traditional social structures. This claim is reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who demonstrates how the efficiency of training institutions influenced the skill premium in medieval Europe, shaping long-term economic performance through the organization of skill transmission. Pamuk (2007, p.25) further supports this by showing that regions with more flexible institutions adapted better to post-Black Death economic conditions, contributing to the rise of northwestern Europe. These sources collectively highlight that institutional frameworks and technological capabilities are interdependent forces driving productivity and economic transformation. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this by linking urban development and economic growth through the mechanisms of external economies of scale, underscoring how institutional and technological progress coalesce to foster industrialization and sustained development. The implication is that institutional reforms must align with technological advancements to unlock productivity gains and ensure long-term economic stability. This interplay between institutions and technology remains central to understanding the historical trajectories of economic growth.

Institutional structures shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) demonstrate through their analysis of extractive and inclusive institutions. Both papers highlight that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule determined the distribution of resources and influenced the capacity of societies to innovate and grow. The persistence of these institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further reinforces the long-term consequences of colonial legacies, as they continued to shape economic outcomes even after formal colonial rule ended. This institutional persistence is linked to the way colonial powers structured governance, property rights, and economic organization, which in turn affected the ability of post-colonial states to develop inclusive and dynamic economies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this argument by showing how restrictive policies, often aimed at preserving elite interests, perpetuated inequality and limited the scope for broad-based economic growth. The implication is that the institutional architecture of the colonial era created enduring structural constraints that continue to influence economic development today. These findings underscore the necessity of understanding historical institutional legacies to explain contemporary patterns of growth and inequality.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideological conditions are pivotal in shaping the adoption of new institutions, a claim that resonates with Hopkins (2019, p.11) in its emphasis on the role of ideas in African economic history. Both authors argue that the diffusion of institutional norms is not merely a function of material interests but is deeply embedded in the cultural and ideological frameworks of societies. McCloskey illustrates this through the historical trajectory of France, where conservative ideologies constrained the development of institutions conducive to economic growth, while in the Netherlands, a more bourgeois ideology fostered the emergence of institutions that supported innovation and expansion. Hopkins, in turn, highlights how African economic history has been shaped by ideological narratives that either facilitated or hindered the development of institutions capable of driving sustained growth. The interplay between ideology and institutional change, as these authors demonstrate, underscores the importance of understanding the cultural and intellectual contexts in which institutions emerge. This perspective challenges the conventional focus on institutional design alone, suggesting that the success of institutions in promoting growth depends on the broader ideological environment in which they are embedded. The implications of this argument extend to contemporary policy, where fostering the right ideological conditions may be as critical as designing effective institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and hinders long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that African real wages were above subsistence and rose over time, contradicting the view of persistent structural impediments to growth. Together, these authors highlight how historical legacies of exploitation and extraction shaped institutional distrust, which continues to affect economic outcomes. The mechanism through which this occurs is the erosion of social trust, which undermines cooperation and investment in institutions that support growth. Peters (2004, p.19) further illustrates this by showing how land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusion and class formation, reinforcing the idea that historical patterns of inequality and distrust are deeply embedded in institutional structures. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) add that the persistence of gender discrimination in economic institutions is another consequence of these historical legacies, as they were shaped by systems of exploitation that marginalized certain groups. The downstream implication of this is that addressing these legacies requires institutional reform as well as a broader rethinking of how historical injustices continue to shape contemporary economic conditions. Understanding the long-term effects of historical institutions is therefore essential for designing effective policies that promote sustainable growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others argue that useful knowledge and technological change, not institutions, are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54; Abramovitz 1986, p.19; Stern 2004, p.2; Temin 2002, p.6). This perspective emphasizes the role of innovation and the diffusion of new ideas as central to sustained economic expansion. Mokyr highlights how technological progress, particularly in the context of the Industrial Revolution, was often accompanied by the gradual institutionalization of scientific inquiry, yet the core engine of growth remained the accumulation of knowledge. The development of applied sciences and the spread of technical expertise through informal networks and educational institutions played a critical role in reducing the costs of innovation and enabling broader participation in economic activity. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further supports this view by noting that the catch-up process in productivity is not simply a matter of adopting existing technologies but involves the adaptation of institutions and education systems to support new capabilities. This suggests that while institutions may facilitate or hinder the spread of knowledge, they are not the fundamental source of growth. The implications of this are significant for policy, as they suggest that fostering innovation and access to knowledge should be central to economic development strategies. The focus on technological progress aligns with a broader shift in economic history toward understanding growth as a dynamic process driven by human ingenuity and the application of scientific knowledge.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities among formerly poor colonies. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further emphasize that secure rights and economic growth are fostered by specific institutional arrangements, particularly those that enable political freedom and economic development. These perspectives align in highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) challenges this focus by asserting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological progress and the application of scientific knowledge play a central role. This divergence reflects a broader debate over whether institutions or knowledge accumulation is the primary driver of long-term growth. The disagreement is resolved through historical evidence, such as the institutional persistence described by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which demonstrates how colonial-era institutions shaped post-independence economic outcomes. This empirical focus on institutional legacies provides a concrete basis for understanding the interplay between institutions and growth, grounding the debate in measurable historical comparisons.
