The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary force behind economic development or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical contingency, are more decisive. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) propose that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and undermines economic development. These accounts highlight the competing claims about the causal pathways through which institutions influence growth. North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions matter for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Meanwhile, Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, framing institutions as complementary rather than causal. The tension between these perspectives reveals a broader debate about whether institutions are the root cause of growth or merely a facilitator. This review synthesizes these claims to illuminate the evolving understanding of institutional influence on long-run economic trajectories.

Both authors highlight the necessity of institutional frameworks that align with technological advancements, ensuring that innovation translates into sustained productivity gains. The role of education is further underscored by Mokyr’s observation that formal institutions, such as universities, were instrumental in reducing access costs to scientific knowledge, even if their impact was limited in the early stages of industrialization. These examples illustrate how institutions not only facilitate the spread of knowledge but also shape the conditions under which it can be applied effectively. Temin (2002, p.6) extends this argument by showing how postwar institutions in Europe created incentives for long-term cooperation, reinforcing the idea that institutional design can catalyze economic growth through coordinated investment and labor productivity. The shared emphasis on institutional complementarity to technological progress suggests that the sustainability of economic growth depends on the alignment of institutional frameworks with the evolving demands of knowledge-based economies. This alignment is critical for translating innovation into broad-based prosperity.

These authors highlight how the institutional frameworks imposed during colonial rule created enduring structures that either constrained or enabled economic progress. North (1989, p.1) complements this by emphasizing that the evolution of institutions in early modern England and Spain illustrates how different institutional paths produced divergent economic outcomes. The mechanism through which colonial institutions affect growth lies in their capacity to either entrench inequality or promote broad-based participation in economic activities. This is further supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who show that restrictive policies in the New World perpetuated elite dominance and hindered widespread development. The implication of these findings is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic performance, underscoring the need to understand historical institutional legacies in analyzing long-term growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.25) demonstrates that flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for wage stability after the Black Death, which in turn contributed to long-term economic divergence. This institutional flexibility enabled these regions to adapt to new economic conditions, fostering wage growth and labor market stability that contrasted with less adaptable regions. The mechanism through which flexible institutions influenced outcomes lies in their capacity to support wage dynamics that resisted downward pressure, thereby encouraging investment in human capital and technological innovation. This pattern suggests that institutional adaptability not only stabilizes labor markets but also creates conditions conducive to sustained growth. The implication is that institutional frameworks capable of accommodating economic shocks can shape long-term trajectories of development, underscoring the importance of institutional flexibility in historical and contemporary growth processes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists and undermines long-term economic growth. This claim is supported by historical evidence showing that regions with higher exposure to the slave trade experienced greater levels of social fragmentation and lower trust in institutions, as documented by Nunn (2008, p.19) and Hopkins (2009, p.8). The mechanism through which the slave trade affected trust lies in its disruption of social cohesion and the creation of a legacy of violence and exploitation. This legacy, in turn, has made it difficult for societies to build cooperative institutions that support economic development. The implications of this argument are significant, as they suggest that historical trauma can have lasting effects on the institutional quality of a society, which in turn shapes its economic trajectory. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further emphasizes the challenges of interpreting historical processes, noting that path dependence and determinism can obscure the complex interactions between past and present. Together, these sources highlight how historical events, such as the slave trade, can create enduring cultural and institutional barriers to growth, offering an alternative explanation to the institutional determinants of economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are the primary drivers of economic growth, challenging the conventional focus on institutional structures. This argument is supported by historical examples that demonstrate how cultural and ideological shifts can catalyze economic transformation. For instance, the persistence of conservative ideologies in France and Spain hindered economic growth until relatively recent times, despite the presence of institutions that could have facilitated development. Similarly, the Netherlands' shift toward a more bourgeois and commercially oriented ideology enabled sustained economic progress, illustrating how changing ideas can reshape economic outcomes. These cases underscore the transformative power of ideological change in overcoming institutional constraints. The interplay between ideas and institutions is further highlighted by the observation that economic growth is often preceded by a shift in the dominant narrative about the role of commerce and enterprise in society. This stream of scholarship thus positions ideas as a foundational force in shaping long-run economic trajectories, offering a compelling alternative to institutional determinism. The implications of this perspective suggest that understanding the evolution of ideas is as critical as analyzing institutional frameworks in explaining economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that institutional frameworks significantly influence economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This agreement is further supported by the argument that institutions, whether shaped by colonial history or evolving through historical conditions, persist and continue to affect income levels and growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). However the precise mechanisms through which institutions operate remain a point of contention. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize the causal effect of institutional differences on economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) highlight the enduring impact of initial conditions, such as factor endowments and inequality, on institutional development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if they are shaped by broader historical and environmental factors. This divergence is resolved by examining the empirical comparisons of colonial institutions across former European colonies, where the persistence of extractive institutions is linked to long-term underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The case of English and Spanish institutional development in the New World further illustrates how different institutional trajectories led to divergent economic outcomes, reinforcing the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run growth (North 1989, p.1).
