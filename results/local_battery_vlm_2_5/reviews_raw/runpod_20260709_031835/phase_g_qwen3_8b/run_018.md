The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergence and designing effective policy. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary causal mechanism driving growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play a more fundamental role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, emphasizing how European settlers' policies shaped the institutional foundations of these economies. This claim is further supported by North (1989, p.1), who asserts that institutions matter for economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Both scholars highlight the enduring impact of institutional frameworks, suggesting that the historical evolution of these structures is critical to explaining long-term economic outcomes. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional analysis provides a robust explanatory framework or if it risks oversimplifying complex historical processes. The next section examines how institutional development is influenced by deeper antecedents, such as cultural and historical legacies.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that the transition to modern economic growth necessitates both technological progress and institutional reforms, a claim that resonates with van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights how the efficiency of training institutions and legal frameworks shapes the skill premium and long-term economic performance. Both authors emphasize that institutional adjustments are not merely supportive mechanisms but fundamental to the adoption of new technologies and the organization of labor. For instance, van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that the decline in the skill premium in western Europe was linked to the institutionalization of apprenticeship systems, which improved the transmission of skills across generations. This institutionalization, in turn, enabled the broader integration of labor markets and the sustained growth of productivity. The work of Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrates this dynamic by showing how export-oriented growth in Sub-Saharan Africa was contingent on both market access and institutional conditions that facilitated trade. These findings collectively suggest that the interplay between institutional and technological progress is a central mechanism driving long-term economic performance. This interplay also implies that the historical trajectory of economic growth is deeply embedded in the evolution of legal and training frameworks, which continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are upstream determinants of long-run economic growth. The colonial experience, as emphasized by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), created institutional paths that were difficult to alter, with the institutions of law and property rights in former colonies persisting long after independence. These enduring institutional structures, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note, are critical for fostering secure property rights and enabling economic growth, highlighting the necessity of institutional arrangements in shaping the conditions for long-term prosperity. The persistence of these colonial-era institutions thus represents a structural condition that continues to influence contemporary economic performance, reinforcing the idea that historical institutional legacies are central to understanding the long-run dynamics of economic growth.

Historical institutional analysis provides a framework to understand how institutions shape long-run economic growth by tracing their historical development and impact. Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) demonstrate that institutions are not static but deeply embedded in the historical processes that define African development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that contemporary institutions are shaped by precolonial and colonial legacies, which influence their effectiveness in promoting growth. These insights suggest that the historical context of institutional formation is critical for understanding their role in economic outcomes. Hopkins (2019, p.11) highlights the challenges of using path dependence to explain institutional continuity, noting that such an approach risks oversimplifying the complex interplay of historical forces. This stream underscores that institutional legacies are not merely passive constraints but active forces that shape economic possibilities. The persistence of institutions rooted in historical extraction and resource control continues to influence contemporary economic performance, reinforcing the need for historical analysis in policy design.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that historical institutional legacies, particularly those rooted in colonial and precolonial extraction, have shaped long-run economic growth by fostering mistrust and undermining social cohesion. This cluster of scholarship (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.25; Peters 2004, p.19; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) underscores that the persistence of these legacies is not accidental but the result of deep-seated processes that continue to influence economic outcomes. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, while those that failed to adapt were left vulnerable to exploitation. These findings suggest that the historical roots of economic disparities are not merely geographic but institutional, with colonial policies and preexisting social hierarchies leaving enduring marks on contemporary development trajectories. The persistence of these legacies implies that understanding long-run growth requires a dual focus on both the evolution of institutions and their interaction with historical contexts. This perspective sets the stage for examining how institutional legacies continue to shape economic possibilities in the modern era.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Stern (2004, p.2) argue that the growth of useful knowledge drives long-run economic growth by enabling technological progress and institutional development. Mokyr highlights how institutional progress complements technological advancement, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements, while Stern emphasizes the need to integrate knowledge-based growth into environmental models. Together, these authors underscore the centrality of knowledge in shaping both economic and ecological trajectories. The interplay between knowledge diffusion and institutional adaptation is evident in historical cases where formal education systems, such as Scottish universities and Warrington Academy, played a critical role in disseminating scientific and technical knowledge, albeit to limited audiences. This suggests that the spread of knowledge is not merely a byproduct of economic growth but a foundational driver that interacts with institutional frameworks to sustain development. Stern’s critique of the environmental Kuznets curve further illustrates how knowledge can reshape our understanding of development-environment relationships, challenging simplistic models that overlook the role of innovation and policy. The synergy between knowledge and institutions thus provides a pathway for reconciling economic growth with environmental sustainability. This dual emphasis on knowledge and institutions offers a robust framework for analyzing long-run growth in an increasingly complex world.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Temin (2002, p.6) argue that economic growth is shaped by complex, differentiated causal relationships rather than a single cause like institutions. Austin highlights how long-term historical contexts reveal recurring relationships due to similar situations and path dependence, yet stresses that these causal links are more nuanced than commonly acknowledged. Temin, meanwhile, demonstrates that the Golden Age of European growth was driven by pre-war disequilibrium, showing how institutional arrangements and economic forces interacted in ways that defied simplistic explanations. Both authors emphasize that growth is not the result of a uniform mechanism but emerges from a web of interdependent factors. This differentiation in causality challenges the notion that institutions alone can explain long-run economic trajectories. Austin’s focus on historical legacies and Temin’s analysis of institutional and economic dynamics together suggest that growth is shaped by a combination of structural conditions, historical contingencies, and evolving institutional frameworks. The implications of this perspective underscore the need to move beyond reductive narratives and embrace a more pluralistic understanding of the drivers of economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argue that ideas and distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. McCloskey emphasizes that cultural and ideological shifts, such as the rise of bourgeois values, have historically driven economic transformation by altering the rhetorical and institutional landscape. Ogilvie complements this by highlighting how distributional conflicts, rooted in social and cultural norms, shape the persistence and evolution of economic institutions. Together, these authors suggest that the interplay between ideas and power dynamics determines the trajectory of economic development. For instance, Ogilvie notes that institutional frameworks are not merely efficient mechanisms but are deeply embedded in the values and beliefs of a society, which can either foster or hinder innovation. McCloskey further illustrates this by showing how the suppression of certain ideas, such as secular education or religious tolerance, has historically constrained economic growth in countries like France and Spain. The implication is that economic outcomes are not solely the product of institutional design but are also shaped by the cultural and ideological forces that underpin those institutions. This perspective challenges the conventional focus on institutions as the primary explanatory variable, instead positioning ideas and distributional conflicts as central to understanding long-run economic change.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the consensus that they are central to explaining differences in economic outcomes across societies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This agreement is grounded in the observation that institutions, such as property rights and legal frameworks, have historically influenced the trajectory of economic development by shaping incentives and opportunities for investment and innovation. The precise mechanisms through which institutions operate remain a point of contention. While some scholars argue that institutions are primarily shaped by economic efficiency and the need to maximize productivity (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), others emphasize that distributional conflicts and ideological forces are equally, if not more, significant in determining institutional design and persistence (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This disagreement reflects a broader debate over whether institutions are best understood as technical arrangements for economic performance or as embedded in social and political struggles over power and resource allocation. The resolution of this tension lies in the historical evidence of institutional persistence, which shows that institutions often endure due to their alignment with existing power structures and cultural norms rather than their efficiency alone (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). By examining the long-term consequences of institutional legacies in former colonies and precolonial societies, scholars have demonstrated that the interplay between institutions, ideology, and historical context is essential for understanding the pathways to sustained economic growth.
