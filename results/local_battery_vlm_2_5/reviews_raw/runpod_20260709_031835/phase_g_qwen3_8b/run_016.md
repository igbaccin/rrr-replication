The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical divergences and designing effective policy interventions. Scholars debate whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological progress, play a more decisive role. The disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional structures ranging from legal frameworks to political systems can be seen as causal agents in economic transformation. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geographic conditions, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies. This claim is further reinforced by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), which highlights the critical role of institutions in accounting for current income differences through their impact on economic performance. These arguments underscore the idea that institutions are not merely passive outcomes of history but active forces shaping long-term trajectories. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional reform can be a viable pathway to sustained growth or if it is constrained by deeper, more intractable factors. This theme of institutional causality is further developed by North (1989, p.1), who emphasizes the historical obstacles to economic growth and the need to model institutional evolution as a key determinant of development outcomes. The persistence of institutional legacies, as noted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), suggests that the effects of historical institutions may be deeply embedded and resistant to change. These perspectives collectively highlight the centrality of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, setting the stage for an exploration of their antecedents and the mechanisms through which they exert their influence.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that specific historical institutions, both pre-colonial and colonial, are the key to understanding long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4). These scholars highlight how institutional frameworks shaped labor markets and economic outcomes, with colonial policies often reflecting pre-existing institutional legacies. The interplay between these institutions and economic performance is further illustrated by the role of land conflicts and class formation, as noted by Peters (2004, p.19). By emphasizing the persistence of institutional structures, the stream underscores how historical conditions laid the groundwork for divergent economic trajectories. This focus on institutional continuity also aligns with Austin’s (2008, p.2) critique of the reversal thesis, which simplifies the complex causal relationships between institutions and growth. The stream collectively demonstrates that institutional legacies, whether pre-colonial or colonial, have played a central role in shaping economic outcomes. This insight sets the stage for examining how these institutions interacted with other factors such as gender dynamics in economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that economic growth requires both technological advancement and institutional adjustments, framing institutions as a fundamental driver of long-run growth. This claim is supported by Mokyr (2005, p.54), who emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The interplay between these two forces is further illustrated by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who show that institutional frameworks in early modern Europe enabled the scaling of productive activities that were incompatible with traditional modes of production. These sources collectively underscore that technological progress alone is insufficient to sustain long-term growth; it must be embedded within supportive institutional structures. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) adds that past institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, highlighting the path-dependent nature of economic development. This suggests that the trajectory of growth is not only shaped by the availability of new technologies but also by the historical legacies of institutions that determine how effectively these technologies can be harnessed. The implication is that institutional change must be considered as an integral part of any analysis of economic growth, rather than as a secondary factor. This perspective sets the stage for examining how institutional reforms can catalyze technological adoption and sustain economic progress.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are upstream determinants of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule entrenched patterns of inequality and restricted economic opportunities, which persisted long after independence. These frameworks, often characterized by extractive institutions, were designed to serve the interests of colonial elites and limited the capacity of local populations to participate in economic development. The persistence of these institutions is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show that the legal and political structures set up by colonial powers continued to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies, often at the expense of broader societal welfare. This institutional legacy not only hindered economic growth but also reinforced social divisions, creating a barrier to inclusive development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) emphasize that secure property rights and effective governance mechanisms are essential for sustained economic growth, yet these were frequently absent or undermined in post-colonial states due to the entrenched nature of extractive institutions. The implication is that without addressing these historical institutional constraints, the potential for long-run economic growth remains limited. This insight underscores the necessity of institutional reform as a critical component of development strategies.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the legacy of the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, undermining long-run economic growth. This cultural mistrust, rooted in historical institutional degradation, is further reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, while those affected by the slave trade faced lasting developmental setbacks. The interplay between institutional erosion and cultural mistrust creates a self-reinforcing cycle that hampers economic progress. McCloskey (2015, p.9) adds that such cultural legacies are not merely economic constraints but also ideological ones, shaping the very narratives that guide economic behavior and institutional evolution. van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how the efficiency of training institutions in medieval Europe influenced the skill premium and long-term economic performance, suggesting that the institutional environment shapes not only economic outcomes but also the distribution of human capital. These findings collectively underscore the deep and enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic trajectories. The interplay between institutional and cultural legacies remains a critical factor in understanding the uneven development of economies across the globe.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that historical institutional analysis is essential for understanding African development and economic history. Their work underscores the necessity of examining institutions as enduring structures that shape economic trajectories, rather than viewing them as mere byproducts of colonial legacies. By integrating precolonial and colonial histories, these authors demonstrate how institutions have functioned as mechanisms through which power, resource distribution, and economic practices have been institutionalized. This perspective challenges the conventional focus on geography and initial conditions, instead emphasizing the role of institutional congruence in fostering legitimacy and sustainable development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that institutions congruent with indigenous systems are more likely to promote growth by enhancing governmental effectiveness and reducing the prevalence of patrimonial politics. The implications of these findings suggest that institutional legacies are not static but dynamic, capable of being reformed through deliberate policy interventions. This stream of scholarship thus reinforces the idea that institutions are central to understanding both the challenges and possibilities of African economic development.

The consensus across these studies is that institutions are foundational to long-run economic growth, shaping development trajectories through their influence on property rights, political power, and economic organization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) both emphasize that colonial institutions, often extractive in nature, entrenched inequalities that persist today, while North (1989, p.1) highlights the dynamic role of institutions in fostering or constraining economic progress. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutional frameworks are not merely passive structures but active determinants of growth outcomes. However a key disagreement lies in the relative weight of historical versus geographic factors in explaining institutional legacies Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions are the primary cause of persistent income disparities whereas Hopkins (2009, p.8) suggests that initial conditions including geography also play a significant role. This disagreement is resolved through the use of cross-country regression analysis, which allows for the measurement of institutional persistence against alternative explanations such as geographic endowments. The empirical focus on colonial-era institutional structures, as seen in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), provides a comparative basis for assessing how historical institutional choices have shaped contemporary economic performance.
