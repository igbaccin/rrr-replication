The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary driver of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. Central to this discussion is the assertion that institutions specifically the legal and political frameworks that govern economic activity create the conditions necessary for investment innovation and long-term stability. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' responses to population density and local conditions, laid the foundation for divergent economic trajectories in the former colonies. These institutions, they claim, are not merely a byproduct of geography but a critical determinant of economic performance. North (1989, p.1) reinforces this view by emphasizing that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings, as they define the rules of the game that shape economic outcomes. The intellectual stakes lie in whether we prioritize institutional design as a lever for development or accept that historical and environmental factors are the ultimate arbiters of economic success. This framing sets the stage for examining how institutions are both shaped by and shape the broader historical and cultural context of economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Temin (2002, p.6) argue that historical legacies and structural factors, as forms of institutions, are fundamental to long-run economic growth. Temin highlights how postwar institutions in Europe created incentives for long-term productivity gains, reinforcing the role of structural frameworks in sustaining growth. (Temin 2002, p.1) shows that these institutions were not only domestic but also international, promoting specialization and efficiency. Pamuk (2007, p.25) complements this by showing how flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England enabled adaptation to post-Black Death conditions, contributing to the 'Great Divergence' through institutional resilience. (Pamuk 2007, p.25) emphasizes that regions with more flexible structures adapted more readily to changing conditions, thereby shaping long-term economic outcomes. The interplay between historical legacies and institutional adaptability underscores the complex pathways through which institutions influence economic trajectories. This dynamic highlights the need to consider both the endurance and evolution of institutional structures in assessing their impact on long-run growth.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing frameworks that either promote or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that the institutional legacies of colonial rule created enduring structural barriers to growth, particularly in the New World, where extractive institutions entrenched elite power and suppressed broad-based economic participation. These frameworks, rooted in historical contingencies, constrained the ability of societies to adapt to changing economic conditions, thereby shaping long-term trajectories of development. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and institutional arrangements, such as representative government and the rule of law, are essential for fostering economic growth and political freedom. Their analysis underscores the interplay between institutional design and the capacity of societies to sustain long-run prosperity. The persistence of these institutional structures, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), demonstrates how historical legacies can outlast colonial regimes, influencing contemporary economic outcomes through their enduring impact on governance and resource allocation. This dynamic suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth is not only a function of their design but also of their resilience over time. The implications of these findings highlight the necessity of understanding institutional evolution as a critical determinant of long-run economic performance.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) demonstrates that institutions regulating the training of apprentices and access to professions significantly shaped long-run economic performance by influencing the skill premium. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) complement this by showing that institutions also affected the scale and timing of export production, linking economic growth to the interplay of trade and political structures. Together, these sources highlight how institutions not only determine the distribution of human capital but also shape the conditions under which economic activity expands. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further illustrate that institutional frameworks in colonial settings influenced labor market dynamics, with direct state intervention in East Africa reflecting a response to perceived structural constraints. This pattern suggests that institutional design can both reflect and reinforce economic outcomes, embedding historical legacies into the mechanisms of growth. The persistence of these institutional influences underscores the need to examine how regulatory frameworks for professional training and labor markets interact with broader economic forces. These findings suggest that understanding the role of institutions requires attention to both their capacity to shape human capital and their integration into the structures of economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial legacies and extractive institutions shaped long-term economic outcomes through persistent distrust and institutional degradation, a claim corroborated by Nunn (2008, p.19) who links the slave trade to the erosion of institutional capacity in African societies. The mechanisms through which these historical processes unfolded are deeply embedded in social structures, as evidenced by the persistent cultural mistrust identified by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), which they argue has roots in the historical exploitation of African societies. This pattern of institutional degradation is further reinforced by Peters (2004, p.19), who highlights how land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusionary practices that have become entrenched over time, undermining the potential for inclusive economic development. The persistence of these social and institutional patterns is not merely a byproduct of history but a structural condition that continues to shape contemporary economic trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the necessity of historical analysis to understand how current institutions are the product of a past that extends beyond colonial rule, revealing the deep entanglement of historical legacies with present-day economic outcomes. These findings suggest that the long-term impact of institutions is not only about their design but also about the social trust and political legitimacy they foster or erode. The implications of this institutional degradation for economic growth are profound, as they suggest that historical patterns of exclusion and mistrust continue to constrain the potential for development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that sustained economic growth depends on both technological advancement and institutional adaptation, a dual necessity that challenges the sufficiency of institutional focus alone. This perspective is reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.54), who highlights the interplay between technological progress and the need for complementary institutional reforms to ensure the durability of economic gains. The argument hinges on the idea that while technological innovation drives productivity, its long-term impact is contingent upon the presence of institutions capable of managing the associated risks and opportunities. For instance, the transition to modern industrial practices required not only new technologies but also institutional changes that curtailed rent-seeking behaviors and fostered economic rationality. This synthesis of technological and institutional factors underscores a broader pattern: the co-evolution of knowledge and governance structures is critical for sustained development. Kuznets (1973, p.9) further illustrates this by contrasting the institutional frameworks of different regions, showing how the adoption of modern technologies was often constrained by pre-existing social and political arrangements. These findings suggest that the role of institutions is not merely to support technological progress but to shape the conditions under which it can be effectively harnessed. The implications for long-run economic growth are significant, as they imply that neither technological advancement nor institutional reform can be pursued in isolation.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies, not institutions, are the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, a stance that resonates with Stern’s (2004, p.2) critique of the environmental Kuznets curve, which similarly challenges conventional explanations of development. Both authors emphasize the transformative power of cultural and intellectual shifts in shaping economic trajectories, suggesting that the diffusion of progressive ideas can catalyze structural change. McCloskey’s analysis of France and Spain illustrates how ideological resistance to economic modernization constrained growth for centuries, even in the face of technological and institutional possibilities. This aligns with Stern’s argument that economic outcomes are not solely determined by material conditions but are deeply influenced by the prevailing intellectual frameworks that govern policy and behavior. The interplay between ideology and economic development is further underscored by the historical cases McCloskey cites, where the triumph of bourgeois values over aristocratic traditions enabled sustained growth. These findings imply that the role of institutions is not as central as the spread of certain ideas that legitimize and institutionalize economic progress. The implications for long-run growth are profound, as they suggest that fostering an environment conducive to the proliferation of pro-growth ideologies may be as critical as reforming existing institutions. Stern (2004, p.2) reports, “The EKC is an essentially empirical phenomenon, but most of the EKC literature is econometrically weak.”

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central, with multiple scholars emphasizing their enduring impact on development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history significantly affect income per capita, challenging the primacy of geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Similarly, North (1989, p.1) contends that institutional frameworks, rather than population or savings rates, are the key determinants of economic performance, highlighting the necessity of understanding institutional evolution over time (North 1989, p.1). These perspectives underscore the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic outcomes through their influence on property rights, governance, and resource allocation. However the exact mechanisms through which institutions operate remain a point of contention. Austin (2008, p.2) suggests that the reversal thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing history, neglecting the differentiated causal relationships that underpin growth (Austin 2008, p.2). In contrast, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) emphasize that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites through restrictive policies, highlighting the role of historical legacies in shaping contemporary disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The disagreement centers on whether institutional change is primarily a result of exogenous factors or endogenous processes shaped by local conditions. The empirical comparison of colonial institutions in former European colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), provides a critical lens through which to assess these divergent explanations, revealing how institutional persistence and variation account for significant differences in long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).
