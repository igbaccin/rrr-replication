The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with profound implications for understanding how societies transition from stagnation to sustained prosperity. At stake is the identification of causal mechanisms that explain why some regions experience persistent growth while others remain impoverished, a debate that has shaped the field's theoretical and empirical foundations. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more decisive role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' responses to population density and prosperity, are central to explaining income disparities among former colonies. This claim is reinforced by their finding that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). North (1989, p.1) similarly emphasizes the causal importance of institutions, contrasting them with neoclassical models that prioritize population and savings. These scholars frame institutions as the key variable in economic development, suggesting that their structure and evolution determine the trajectory of growth. These institutional frameworks, often extractive in nature, were established by European settlers and persisted through decolonization, creating enduring barriers to growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). North (1989, p.1) similarly emphasizes that historical institutional development, rooted in the legal and political systems of colonial powers, laid the foundation for divergent economic outcomes (North 1989, p.1). The evolution of these institutions, whether through centralized or decentralized governance, has had lasting consequences for the economic trajectories of the New World (North 1989, p.8). Together, these scholars demonstrate that the institutional architecture inherited from colonial rule has been a critical determinant of economic development, with its effects extending far beyond the immediate post-colonial period. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade entrenched distrust in African societies, which continues to impede economic growth. This claim is supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who shows that the slave trade favored societies with pre-existing institutions capable of managing complex trade, thereby shaping the institutional landscape of Africa. Together, these scholars highlight how the slave trade not only disrupted economic activity but also left a legacy of institutional fragility. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further underscores that contemporary institutions in Africa are deeply rooted in historical processes, including the colonial and precolonial past, which often include the legacies of the slave trade. This historical embedding suggests that the persistence of mistrust and institutional underdevelopment is not merely a result of recent events but a continuation of long-standing structural patterns. Peters (2004, p.19) adds that land conflicts in Africa reveal the enduring impact of these historical legacies, as they reflect exclusionary practices and class formations that emerged from the same historical forces. The cumulative effect of these findings is that the slave trade and its institutional aftermath have created enduring barriers to economic development. These insights suggest that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth requires a deep engagement with the historical processes that shaped them.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the role of ideas over institutions. Both scholars argue that the persistence of certain economic practices is rooted in the struggle over power and resources and not in the efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can perpetuate existing systems, even when more productive alternatives exist, while McCloskey illustrates how cultural and ideological battles have historically shaped economic outcomes. For instance, McCloskey describes how French reactionary parties prioritized cultural and religious values over economic growth, demonstrating how ideological commitments can stifle development. Ogilvie further notes that the economic impact of institutions is often mediated by the interplay between institutional rules and inward beliefs, suggesting that the same institutional structures can yield divergent outcomes depending on the cultural context. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how distributional conflicts and ideological commitments interact to shape long-term economic trajectories. The implications of this perspective suggest that economic growth cannot be fully explained by institutional design alone, but must also account for the broader social and ideological forces that sustain or challenge these structures.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with the broader cluster's assertion that technological and knowledge-based progress, rather than institutions, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that economic growth requires advancing technology and institutional adjustments, highlighting the interplay between innovation and the social frameworks that enable its diffusion. Together, these authors underscore that the trajectory of economic development is fundamentally shaped by the accumulation and application of knowledge, which transcends the boundaries of formal institutions. The role of technological progress is further illustrated by the historical expansion of educational institutions, which, despite their limited reach, played a crucial role in disseminating scientific knowledge and fostering a culture of innovation. This suggests that the diffusion of knowledge is not merely a byproduct of institutional structures but a dynamic force that can catalyze broader economic transformation. The implications of this view are profound, as they challenge the conventional emphasis on institutional design by situating technological progress at the core of long-term growth. This shift in focus invites a reevaluation of how we understand the drivers of economic development and the mechanisms through which knowledge shapes societal and economic outcomes.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions shaping skill premium and wage dynamics drive long-run economic performance, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who show how institutional frameworks influence the trajectory of economic growth. The efficiency of training institutions, as noted by van Zanden, directly affects the supply of skilled labor, thereby modulating the skill premium and wage gaps. This dynamic is further illustrated by Pamuk (2007, p.25), who highlights how flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for sustained wage levels post-Black Death, fostering a divergence in economic outcomes across Europe. The institutional capacity to adapt to changing economic conditions, as Broadberry and Gardner emphasize, is a key determinant of long-term growth, with institutions not merely facilitating investment but also shaping the resilience of economies to shocks. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) reinforce this by showing that institutional design in colonial Africa influenced labor market dynamics, with colonial policies affecting wage levels and human capital formation. These studies collectively underscore that institutions are not static constraints but active forces that shape the distribution of skills and wages, which in turn influence the broader economic performance of societies. The persistent impact of institutional legacies on wage structures and human capital formation suggests that understanding these mechanisms is essential for grasping the long-run determinants of economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income differences among formerly poor colonies. This aligns with North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who emphasize that secure rights and specific institutional arrangements foster economic growth and political freedom. Both streams highlight the causal link between institutional design and sustained economic performance. However Ogilvie (2007, p.30) challenges the focus on economic efficiency suggesting that distributional conflicts may provide a more accurate explanation for core economic institutions. This disagreement centers on whether institutions should be assessed primarily through their economic outcomes or their role in mediating social and political tensions. The empirical comparison of precolonial and colonial institutional legacies in Hopkins (2009, p.18) offers a resolution, demonstrating that congruence between contemporary institutions and indigenous practices enhances their effectiveness in promoting growth. By integrating historical analysis with institutional persistence, these studies collectively affirm the centrality of institutions in shaping long-run economic trajectories.
