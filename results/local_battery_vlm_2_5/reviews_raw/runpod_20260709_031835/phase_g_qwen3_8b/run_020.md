The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on whether institutional frameworks are the primary determinants of development or secondary to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological progress. The live disagreement lies in whether institutions are the root cause of sustained growth or merely a reflection of deeper, often unmeasurable, historical forces. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while North (1989, p.1) emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Both streams converge on the idea that institutions are not just passive structures but active forces shaping economic trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further suggest that institutional development in the New World perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites, reinforcing the notion that institutions can entrench disparities rather than promote equity. These accounts underscore the intellectual stakes: understanding institutions as causal agents in growth requires grappling with their historical origins and the mechanisms through which they influence economic outcomes. The next section explores how institutional legacies interact with cultural and historical contexts to sustain or disrupt growth trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, shaping long-term economic performance through institutional development (Mokyr 2005, p.54; van Zanden 2009, p.19; Abramovitz 1986, p.19). The interplay between knowledge diffusion and institutional frameworks is further underscored by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), who notes that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, complicating straightforward narratives of convergence (Abramovitz 1986, p.19). These sources collectively demonstrate that institutional structures are not merely passive recipients of technological progress but active participants in shaping its trajectory and economic impact. The role of institutions in enabling or constraining knowledge dissemination is a critical mechanism through which they influence growth. This insight suggests that understanding the historical evolution of institutions is essential for grasping their contemporary role in sustaining or disrupting economic trajectories.

Institutions shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks that either enable or hinder development. These sources collectively highlight that the durability of institutional structures is a key mechanism through which colonial history influences long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of extractive institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), further illustrates how colonial governance models have shaped the trajectory of development in former colonies. This institutional persistence not only constrains economic potential but also perpetuates inequality, underscoring the need to understand historical institutional legacies in assessing contemporary growth prospects. The interplay between institutional design and historical context thus remains a critical axis for analyzing long-term economic performance.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that colonial institutions in Africa, particularly those shaped by the slave trade, have left a lasting imprint on contemporary economic conditions by fostering a culture of mistrust. This finding aligns with the broader argument that historical institutional legacies, particularly colonial institutions, shape long-run economic growth by influencing trust, governance, and development paths (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Peters 2004, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11). The persistence of these institutions, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), is not merely a byproduct of history but a structural condition that affects the capacity of societies to engage in cooperative economic activities. Trust, once eroded, becomes a barrier to investment, trade, and political stability, all of which are critical for sustained growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further emphasizes that the effectiveness of contemporary institutions depends on their congruence with indigenous systems, suggesting that the mismatch between colonial and precolonial institutions has deepened developmental challenges. This congruence, or lack thereof, shapes the legitimacy of governance structures and their ability to foster inclusive development. The implications of these findings extend beyond Africa, highlighting the need to consider historical institutional contexts in understanding economic disparities across regions. The interplay between trust, governance, and historical legacies thus remains a central theme in the analysis of long-term economic performance.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.25) highlight that institutional innovations are critical for sustained economic growth, as they shape the environment for technological advancement. Kuznets emphasizes that modern technology cannot function within outdated institutional frameworks, such as slavery or illiteracy, which must be replaced by more adaptive structures. Pamuk further illustrates this point by showing how regions with flexible institutions, like the Low Countries and England, adapted more effectively to post-Black Death economic conditions, thereby fostering long-term growth. These institutional adjustments not only enabled technological progress but also allowed societies to sustain higher productivity levels over time. The ability to reconfigure institutions in response to changing economic realities, as seen in the historical case of Europe, underscores the central role of institutional adaptability in economic development. Pamuk (2007, p.21) also notes that the Black Death accelerated institutional changes, such as the decline in interest rates, which in turn supported the conditions for sustained growth. This suggests that institutional and technological adjustments are mutually reinforcing, with each shaping the trajectory of the other. The historical evidence from Kuznets and Pamuk thus reinforces the idea that institutional frameworks are not merely passive structures but active participants in the process of economic transformation. The implications of these findings point to the necessity of institutional flexibility as a prerequisite for long-term economic progress.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the key to understanding long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the primacy of ideas over institutional structures. Both authors argue that the persistence of certain economic practices and the resistance to innovation are better explained by the underlying distributional tensions and ideological commitments rather than by the efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can create self-reinforcing systems that resist change unless they offer comprehensive substitutes for existing structures, while McCloskey illustrates how cultural and ideological battles, such as the French struggle over Catholicism and military uniformity, have historically shaped economic trajectories. These perspectives suggest that the effectiveness of institutions is often contingent on the broader ideological and distributional contexts in which they operate. The interplay between ideological commitments and institutional rules, as Ogilvie notes, is crucial for understanding how economic outcomes are shaped over time. This insight underscores the need to examine not just the formal structures of institutions but also the ideological forces that sustain or undermine them.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is firmly established across multiple streams of scholarship, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasizing that institutional legacies from colonialism significantly influence current income disparities. Similarly, North (1989, p.1) highlights how institutional frameworks, such as those emerging from common law in England, have historically determined economic trajectories. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions are not merely passive structures but active forces that shape economic outcomes through their persistence and adaptability. However, the sources diverge on the relative importance of ideological commitments versus institutional efficiency in sustaining these frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a more compelling explanation for core economic institutions than economic efficiency, suggesting that ideological forces are central to their endurance. This disagreement is further underscored by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who stress that institutional differences, such as the security of property rights, are critical to explaining variations in growth across societies. The resolution to this debate lies in the historical evidence of institutional persistence, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), which shows that colonial institutions have had lasting effects on economic performance, thereby affirming the central role of institutional legacies in long-run growth.
