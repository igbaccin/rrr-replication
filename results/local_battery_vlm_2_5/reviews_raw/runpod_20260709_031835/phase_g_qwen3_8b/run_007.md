The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central intellectual challenge, as scholars debate whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers of sustained prosperity or secondary to other forces such as geography, culture, or technological progress. At stake is the causal mechanism linking institutions to growth, with implications for policy design and historical interpretation. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the root cause of economic development or a consequence of deeper, often overlooked, factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism explain income disparities among former European colonies, emphasizing how European settlers' policies shaped the trajectory of economic institutions. This aligns with Austin (2008, p.1), who underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal differentiated causal relationships. Both streams highlight how institutions are not merely passive structures but active agents in shaping economic outcomes. The debate extends to whether institutions are the primary cause or an effect of broader historical and cultural forces. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) suggest that trust, shaped by historical experiences such as the slave trade, plays a critical role in economic development, offering an alternative explanation for the persistence of institutional legacies. This sets the stage for a deeper exploration of how institutions interact with other factors in the long-run growth process.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and undermines economic development. This claim is reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who show that historical legacies such as the slave trade shaped long-run economic outcomes through persistent institutional degradation. The mechanism through which this occurs is the erosion of trust, which affects both economic and political institutions. The persistence of mistrust, therefore, creates a barrier to the formation of credible institutions that support economic growth. This historical path dependence is further illustrated by Pamuk (2007, p.25), who shows that regions with more flexible structures and institutions adapted more readily to changing economic conditions after the Black Death. Such adaptability, rooted in historical experiences, enabled sustained growth in certain regions while others stagnated. The implication of this historical legacies is that institutional quality is not a given but a product of historical processes that continue to shape economic outcomes. These findings suggest that understanding the origins of trust and institutional degradation is essential for explaining long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) argue that institutions shaped by colonial history are fundamental to long-run economic growth, as they determine the institutional frameworks that enable or hinder development. North (1989, p.1) expands on this by emphasizing that the persistence of these institutions, even after colonial rule, was driven by the interplay between historical precedents and the evolving political economy of the post-colonial states. Both authors underscore that the institutional legacies of colonialism, whether through centralized enforcement mechanisms or fragmented legal systems, have had lasting effects on the trajectory of economic growth. This institutional persistence is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who show that the institutional structures established during the colonial era continued to shape the economic performance of former colonies long after independence. The downstream implication, as noted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), is that these institutional arrangements perpetuated inequality by advantaging elites and restricting opportunities for broader economic participation. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical processes of colonialism and the enduring influence of early institutional frameworks.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that economic growth necessitates institutional and technological progress, not merely the exploitation of resources, a stance shared by Temin (2002, p.6) who highlights the role of institutional frameworks in fostering sustained growth. Both authors underscore the incompatibility of traditional social structures with modern economic systems, illustrating how institutional adjustments are prerequisites for technological advancement. Kuznets notes that the transition to modern economic practices required replacing outdated institutions such as family enterprise and slavery with more adaptive frameworks, while Temin demonstrates how postwar European institutions facilitated productive investment and long-term economic stability. These institutional changes, as Temin argues, were critical in aligning incentives for workers and investors, thereby sustaining high growth rates during the Golden Age. The interplay between institutional reform and technological progress, as evidenced by these sources, reveals a fundamental mechanism through which economic growth is sustained over time. This dynamic suggests that the historical trajectory of growth is not merely a function of material resources but is deeply embedded in the evolution of institutional structures that support innovation and productivity. The enduring relevance of these insights underscores the necessity of institutional development as a cornerstone of long-run economic progress.

Peters (2004, p.19) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that specific institutional forms, like kinship and village institutions, shape long-run economic growth by influencing class formation and labor mobilization. These scholars highlight how localized institutions, often overlooked in mainstream growth theories, play a critical role in structuring social and economic relations. For instance, Peters demonstrates that land conflicts in Africa reveal exclusionary dynamics and class formation, challenging the notion of flexible customary systems. Austin, in turn, critiques the reversal thesis for oversimplifying historical causation, emphasizing the need to examine growth theories against long-term historical contexts. Together, these sources underscore the importance of institutional differentiation in shaping economic trajectories. The interplay between kinship-based institutions and class formation, as shown by Peters, suggests that labor mobilization is not a uniform process but is deeply embedded in the structure of local institutions. This insight aligns with Austin’s argument that causal relationships in history are more nuanced than often assumed. The implications of these findings suggest that understanding economic growth requires a more granular analysis of institutional forms and their historical evolution. This focus on localized institutions opens new avenues for examining how they interact with broader structural forces in the long run.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim supported by the expanding role of education and technological innovation in shaping long-run economic trajectories. This perspective is further reinforced by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), who emphasizes that the catch-up process is not merely a function of technological diffusion but is also influenced by the capacity of societies to absorb and adapt new knowledge. Together, these sources highlight the centrality of knowledge accumulation as a driver of economic progress, independent of institutional structures. The interplay between technological advancement and educational expansion is particularly evident in the historical spread of scientific training, as Mokyr notes the emergence of institutions that facilitated the transmission of applied knowledge during the Industrial Revolution. These developments, however, were not uniform across regions, as the effectiveness of educational systems in fostering innovation varied significantly. Abramovitz’s analysis of productivity leadership and its shifts underscores the importance of institutional and educational commitments in enabling sustained economic growth. This suggests that while knowledge is a primary driver, its impact is mediated by the capacity of societies to institutionalize and sustain it over time. The role of formal education in reducing access costs and fostering innovation thus becomes a critical mechanism through which knowledge translates into economic outcomes. These findings imply that understanding economic growth requires a dual focus on both the expansion of knowledge and the institutional frameworks that support its diffusion and application.

Stern (2004, p.2) contends that the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) provides an alternative explanation for development-environment relationships, emphasizing the need for more rigorous econometric models to capture these dynamics. This perspective challenges the institutionalist view by suggesting that environmental outcomes are not solely shaped by institutional frameworks but are also influenced by the trajectory of economic development. Stern (2004, p.1) further notes that the EKC concept, initially linked to income inequality, has been extended to environmental indicators, implying a potential inverse relationship between economic growth and pollution levels. This shift in focus highlights how environmental degradation may follow an inverted U-shaped trajectory as economies develop, offering a distinct causal pathway from the institutionalist emphasis on governance structures. The EKC model, as critiqued by Stern, underscores the importance of economic growth stages in determining environmental impact, thereby complicating the straightforward institutional explanation for long-run growth. By integrating economic development as a central variable, the EKC framework shifts the focus from institutional design to the broader processes of economic transformation. This alternative perspective raises questions about the extent to which institutions alone can account for environmental outcomes, suggesting that economic dynamics must be considered in tandem with institutional factors.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) challenge the institutionalist view by emphasizing that African economic history must be understood through the lens of colonial legacies and African agency, rather than institutions alone. This perspective highlights how precolonial structures and the subsequent imposition of colonial systems shaped long-run growth trajectories. These authors suggest that the impact of institutions on growth is mediated by historical conditions and the ways in which societies have adapted to external pressures. Hopkins (2019, p.11) reinforces this by pointing out that poverty in Africa was not an inherent condition but a historical creation, shaped by the mechanisms of European capitalism and unequal exchange. This historical framing shifts the focus from institutions to the broader processes of economic transformation, suggesting that the role of institutions must be contextualized within the long-term dynamics of colonial and postcolonial history. The implication is that understanding long-run growth requires a more integrated approach that considers both institutional structures and the historical forces that have shaped them. This historical dimension is crucial for developing policies that address the legacies of colonialism and promote sustainable development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is widely recognized as central, with multiple scholars emphasizing their enduring impact on development paths (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This consensus is grounded in empirical analyses showing that institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have persisted and continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). However, disagreement persists over the mechanisms through which institutions operate. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain income disparities among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not economic efficiency, provide a more accurate explanation for the emergence of core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This divergence highlights a fundamental tension in the literature: whether institutions are primarily shaped by material constraints or by social and political dynamics. The resolution of this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of historical cases, such as the contrasting institutional trajectories of England and Spain, which demonstrate how institutional evolution is deeply intertwined with both economic and political forces (North 1989, p.8). By examining the persistence of extractive institutions in postcolonial states, scholars can better understand how historical legacies continue to shape economic performance today.
