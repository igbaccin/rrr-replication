The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary causal force behind growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical legacies, play a more dominant role. Central to this debate is the claim that institutions particularly those that facilitate property rights enforce contracts and limit rent-seeking create the conditions necessary for economic expansion. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities, emphasizing their role in fostering industrialization among former colonies. North (1989, p.1) similarly contends that institutions matter more than neoclassical factors like population and savings, framing them as the key to overcoming historical obstacles to growth. These claims are complemented by Austin (2008, p.2), who highlights the importance of examining growth theories through long-term historical contexts, underscoring the differentiated causal relationships that institutions foster. The intellectual stakes lie in determining whether institutional change is a malleable force that can be harnessed for development or if it is shaped by deeper, more immutable factors. The next section explores how historical legacies and cultural norms may interact with institutional structures to shape long-run outcomes.

Institutions shaped by colonial history foster long-run economic growth by establishing durable frameworks that enable development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (1989, p.1) jointly argue that the institutional legacies of colonial rule created enduring structures that either facilitated or constrained economic progress, depending on their design. Colonial institutions, often extractive in nature, entrenched power imbalances that persisted long after independence, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate through the persistence of extractive institutions in former colonies. These frameworks, whether through centralized control or fragmented governance, shaped the trajectory of economic activity by influencing property rights, political participation, and resource allocation. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and inclusive political institutions are critical for fostering economic growth, a principle that aligns with the broader claim that institutional design determines developmental outcomes. The mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced growth lies in their capacity to either lock in extractive practices or enable inclusive, market-oriented reforms. For instance, the institutional frameworks established in settler colonies, such as in the United States and Australia, allowed for more open economies and greater political participation, contrasting with the more extractive systems in non-settler colonies. This distinction highlights how the nature of colonial governance, whether extractive or inclusive, became a foundational determinant of long-term economic performance. The downstream implication of these findings is that understanding the historical roots of institutional structures is essential for designing policies that address persistent developmental challenges.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) demonstrate that the historical dependence on the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists and undermines economic development. This finding aligns with the broader argument that colonial and historical legacies shape long-run economic growth by influencing institutions that affect development trajectories. Nunn (2008, p.19) further shows that societies with more developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, highlighting how institutional quality during colonial periods laid the groundwork for subsequent economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutional legacies is reinforced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who argue that colonial institutions had lasting effects on economic performance, even as they challenge the notion that African economies were inherently underdeveloped. These studies collectively suggest that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule had profound and enduring impacts on economic growth, shaping the trajectory of development in ways that continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The implications of these findings underscore the importance of historical institutional analysis in understanding the deep roots of economic performance.

Hopkins (2019, p.11) argues that historical institutions shaped economic outcomes, including poverty, by creating conditions of dependence and underdevelopment, a claim shared by Austin (2008, p.2) and other scholars in the field (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2019, p.11). These authors emphasize that the reversal thesis, which links current underdevelopment to historical processes, oversimplifies causation by compressing complex historical dynamics into deterministic narratives. Instead, they highlight the differentiated causal relationships that emerge from the interplay between precolonial, colonial, and postcolonial institutions. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows how contemporary institutions in Africa are deeply rooted in precolonial structures, with congruence between indigenous and modern institutions enhancing governance legitimacy and developmental outcomes. This insight aligns with Austin’s (2008, p.2) critique of path dependence as a catch-all explanation, which risks ignoring the intervening influences that shape historical trajectories. The implication is that understanding the historical specificity of institutional legacies is crucial for crafting effective development policies. This perspective shifts the focus from abstract institutional analysis to the material conditions shaped by historical processes, offering a more grounded understanding of long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim that resonates with van Zanden (2009, p.19) who highlights the role of institutional efficiency in shaping the skill premium and long-term economic performance. Together, these authors underscore that knowledge accumulation is not only a driver of growth but also a process that requires complementary institutional frameworks to sustain its impact. The interplay between technological progress and institutional progress is central to understanding how knowledge translates into sustained economic improvement. This suggests that the institutional environment is not merely a backdrop to technological change but an active participant in shaping its trajectory and impact. Abramovitz (1986, p.19) further complicates this picture by emphasizing that past educational and institutional commitments can either facilitate or hinder the catch-up process, reinforcing the idea that knowledge growth is deeply embedded in historical institutional legacies. The shared posture of this stream implies that economic growth is not a linear process but one shaped by the dynamic interaction between knowledge creation and institutional adaptation. This dynamic relationship offers a critical lens for analyzing the long-run determinants of economic development.

McCloskey (2015, p.9) contends that ideas and ideologies are central to economic growth, offering a compelling alternative to institutional determinism. His argument is reinforced by the historical case of France, where conservative ideologies constrained economic progress for centuries, illustrating how cultural and intellectual frameworks can shape long-run outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). Similarly, McCloskey (2015, p.9) highlights that the spread of bourgeois values in the Netherlands catalyzed economic transformation, demonstrating the power of ideological shifts to alter institutional trajectories (McCloskey 2015, p.7). These examples underscore a broader pattern: the diffusion of progressive ideas can dismantle restrictive norms and create environments conducive to innovation and growth. The implications of this are profound, as they suggest that institutional change may often be a consequence of ideological evolution rather than its cause. This perspective challenges the conventional focus on formal institutions, shifting attention to the role of cultural and intellectual currents in shaping economic development.

Temin (2002, p.6) contends that pre-war disequilibrium, rather than institutional frameworks, was the primary driver of long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Stern (2004, p.2) in its emphasis on the limitations of conventional economic models. Temin’s analysis highlights how the postwar period in Europe was marked by a unique combination of financial and political conditions that created a temporary imbalance, which in turn spurred rapid industrialization and productivity gains. This disequilibrium, he argues, acted as a catalyst for growth by altering the incentives for investment and labor, ultimately leading to a shift in the trajectory of economic development. Stern’s critique of the environmental Kuznets curve complements this by illustrating how existing models often fail to capture the complexity of growth dynamics, reinforcing the need to consider alternative explanatory factors. The shared posture of this cluster underscores a broader methodological shift toward examining the role of exogenous shocks and systemic imbalances in shaping economic outcomes. By focusing on the interplay between pre-existing conditions and institutional responses, these authors challenge the conventional emphasis on formal institutions as the primary determinants of growth. This perspective opens the door to re-evaluating the historical and structural forces that underpin economic development.

The literature consistently underscores that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) who highlight how colonial institutions persistently influence income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). North (1989, p.1) further reinforces this by arguing that institutional frameworks, rather than neoclassical factors like population and savings, are central to understanding economic development (North 1989, p.1). These authors converge on the idea that institutional structures, whether inherited from colonial regimes or evolved through historical processes, have enduring effects on economic outcomes. However the sources diverge in their emphasis on the origins of these institutions. While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) stress the role of colonial-era institutional changes in determining current income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48), Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) argue that institutional development in the New World was shaped by pre-existing conditions and factor endowments, which perpetuated inequality through restrictive policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This disagreement is resolved through the comparative analysis of historical institutional trajectories, as seen in the contrasting paths of English and Spanish colonial institutions in North America and Latin America, which North (1989, p.1) meticulously outlines (North 1989, p.8). The empirical focus on institutional persistence and its interaction with historical context provides a unified framework for understanding the role of institutions in long-run growth.
