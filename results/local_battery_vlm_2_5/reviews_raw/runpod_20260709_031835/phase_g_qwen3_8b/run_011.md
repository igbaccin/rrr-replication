The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant intellectual stakes in understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary causal force behind economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological progress, play a more dominant role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies. This claim is further supported by their findings that institutions significantly influenced economic growth and industrialization in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). North (1989, p.1) similarly emphasizes that institutions matter for economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. His work highlights how institutional frameworks shaped historical development in England and Spain, with implications for the New World. These studies collectively assert that institutions are a critical determinant of long-run economic outcomes. The next section examines how institutional development is often rooted in deeper antecedents such as cultural norms and historical legacies.

This claim is reinforced by North (1989, p.1), who highlights how institutional frameworks in England and Spain evolved from deeper historical antecedents, influencing the development of the New World. Both studies emphasize that the persistence of extractive or inclusive institutions, rooted in colonial history, determines the trajectory of economic performance. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that the institutional legacies of colonial rule in nonsettler colonies have endured, shaping the current institutional environments of countries like Australia and the United States. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, are not merely historical artifacts but active forces that continue to influence economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further elaborate that secure property rights and representative government, which emerged from specific institutional arrangements, are critical for sustained economic growth. The interplay between colonial history and institutional development underscores how past political and economic choices have created enduring structures that either hinder or enable long-term prosperity. This stream collectively demonstrates that the institutional foundations laid during colonial times are not only historical relics but foundational elements of modern economic systems, with profound implications for contemporary development trajectories.

Nunn (2008, p.19) demonstrates that the slave trade entrenched extractive institutions by fostering violence and undermining social cohesion, which in turn stifled economic development. This finding is corroborated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who highlight how colonial institutions in the New World perpetuated inequality by privileging elites through restrictive policies. Together, these studies reveal that historical legacies like the slave trade shaped extractive institutions, which hindered long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions is further illustrated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to impede economic cooperation and development in Africa. The mechanism through which the slave trade affected growth lies in its disruption of social trust and institutional stability, both of which are critical for long-term economic performance. This stream underscores how historical forces can leave enduring imprints on economic outcomes, shaping the trajectory of development for centuries. The implications of these findings are profound, as they suggest that understanding the roots of institutional development is essential for addressing contemporary economic challenges.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This claim is reinforced by McCloskey (2015, p.9), who argues that ideas and ideologies, rather than institutional structures, shape economic outcomes. Both authors emphasize that the persistence of certain institutional frameworks is not merely a product of efficiency but is deeply rooted in the distributional tensions that arise from competing interests. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can perpetuate existing systems, even when more productive alternatives exist, suggesting that the inertia of these systems is often a result of underlying distributional conflicts. McCloskey further illustrates this point by showing how cultural and ideological factors, such as the prioritization of tradition over modernization, can stifle economic growth in societies where such values are deeply entrenched. These sources collectively demonstrate that the trajectory of economic development is not solely determined by the formal rules of institutions but is significantly influenced by the distributional dynamics that shape the legitimacy and functioning of those institutions. The implications of this perspective are that policies aimed at fostering economic growth must address the broader ideological and distributional forces that underpin institutions as well as their structural features. This insight provides a critical lens through which to understand the complex interplay between ideas, institutions, and long-term economic performance.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that historical institutional analysis is the key to understanding long-run economic growth in Africa, a claim supported by Peters (2004, p.19) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4). These scholars collectively demonstrate that African economies did not suffer from persistent underdevelopment but instead experienced structural transformations shaped by institutional legacies and market dynamics. The interplay between institutional design and economic performance is further illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), who emphasize the role of women in pre-colonial and colonial economies, showing that gendered institutions shaped labor participation and productivity. This stream underscores that long-term growth in Africa is not merely a product of external forces but is deeply embedded in the historical evolution of institutions that facilitated or constrained economic activity. The persistence of these institutional patterns continues to influence contemporary development trajectories, offering a critical framework for analyzing economic performance across time and space.

Temin (2002, p.6) challenges the conventional institutionalist view of long-run growth by asserting that historical disequilibrium, rather than institutional design, was the primary engine of economic transformation. This claim is reinforced by Hopkins (2019, p.11), who similarly critiques the deterministic narratives that obscure the contingent and dynamic nature of historical development. Together, these authors argue that economic outcomes are shaped by abrupt shifts in power, resource allocation, and geopolitical conditions, rather than by the steady evolution of institutional frameworks. The mechanism through which historical disequilibrium drives growth lies in the sudden reorganization of economic incentives and the reallocation of capital and labor, as seen in the postwar European context. This perspective complicates the institutionalist focus on long-term institutional stability by highlighting the role of short-term crises and disruptions in shaping long-term trajectories. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further underscores this by emphasizing that underdevelopment in Africa was not a pre-existing condition but a product of historical processes of exploitation and dependency. The implications of this view are significant, as it shifts the analytical focus from institutional endurance to the capacity of societies to adapt to and reshape their economic environments in response to external shocks. This stream of scholarship thus redefines the role of institutions by framing them not as the sole determinants of growth, but as contingent actors within a broader historical landscape of change and contestation.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, a claim reinforced by Abramovitz (1986, p.19), who highlights the complexity of the catch-up process and the role of education and institutions in shaping productivity trajectories. Both scholars emphasize that technological progress is not self-sustaining without complementary institutional frameworks that support its diffusion and application. Mokyr’s analysis of the Industrial Revolution underscores the limited role of formal education in fostering technological adoption, noting that informal institutions and networks played a more significant part in reducing access costs and facilitating innovation. Abramovitz, meanwhile, points to the interplay between past development and current productivity, suggesting that historical institutional and educational commitments can either hinder or enable future growth. This interplay suggests that while knowledge is a primary driver of growth, its impact is mediated by the capacity of societies to institutionalize and disseminate it effectively. The historical examples provided by Mokyr reveal that technological progress often outpaces institutional adaptation, creating a tension between innovation and the structures that must support it. Abramovitz’s focus on the persistence of past capabilities further illustrates how the legacy of earlier development can shape contemporary growth paths. Together, these sources suggest that the growth of knowledge is a central force in economic development, yet its realization depends on the presence of institutional and educational systems that can translate innovation into sustained progress. This dynamic underscores the need to examine how knowledge and institutions interact to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Stern (2004, p.2) challenges the Environmental Kuznets Curve by highlighting its econometric weaknesses, arguing that the curve’s empirical appeal does not justify its explanatory power. This critique aligns with broader concerns about the limitations of single-variable models in capturing the complex interplay between economic development and environmental outcomes. Stern (2004, p.1) further contextualizes the curve’s origins, noting its roots in Kuznets’ work on income inequality and its later adaptation to environmental degradation. Together, these sources suggest that the curve’s popularity may obscure deeper structural relationships between growth, institutions, and ecological constraints. The curve’s focus on economic growth as the primary driver of environmental change risks sidelining the role of institutional frameworks in shaping sustainable development paths. By emphasizing the need for more robust models, Stern’s work underscores the importance of integrating institutional variables into environmental analyses. This shift in focus aligns with the broader argument that long-run economic growth is not merely a function of technological progress but is deeply embedded in the institutional and regulatory environments that govern resource use and environmental protection. The implications of this perspective are significant for understanding how policy design can reconcile economic development with ecological sustainability.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is consistently emphasized across multiple streams of scholarship, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) both highlighting institutions as central to explaining differences in economic performance. These authors argue that institutional frameworks, particularly those related to property rights and political structures, create the conditions necessary for sustained growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further suggest that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income disparities, reinforcing the idea that institutions are not merely byproducts of economic development but active drivers. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate how secure rights and institutional arrangements foster both economic growth and political freedom, underscoring the interplay between institutions and broader societal outcomes. However, a key disagreement persists regarding the origins of these institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutional development in the New World was shaped by restrictive policies that advantaged elites, perpetuating inequality. This contrasts with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), who emphasize that colonial institutions persisted long after independence, shaping contemporary economic outcomes. The empirical comparison of former colonies and settler colonies, as used by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), provides a critical lens to distinguish between the effects of institutional persistence and the role of geographic and historical factors in shaping long-term growth trajectories.
