The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history, with the stakes lying in understanding how political and legal frameworks can either foster or hinder development. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological progress, hold greater explanatory power. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes from colonialism, rather than geography, explain the reversal in income distribution among former European colonies, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that current institutions are shaped by a history that extends beyond colonial rule, including precolonial and even prehistoric conditions. Both scholars highlight the necessity of historical analysis to grasp the origins of institutional structures. North (1989, p.1) further reinforces the idea that institutions are critical to economic growth, contrasting them with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. These works collectively stress that institutions evolve over time influencing economic outcomes through their capacity to shape incentives and opportunities. The debate remains unresolved, but the consensus is that institutional frameworks are foundational to long-term development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) argue that the slave trade fostered a legacy of distrust in African societies, which continues to impede economic development. This claim is supported by Nunn (2008, p.19), who demonstrates that the slave trade disrupted local institutions and created conditions conducive to conflict, thereby undermining long-term growth. The shared posture of this stream is that colonial and slave trade legacies shaped extractive institutions that hindered long-run economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.19; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). These scholars collectively show that the mechanisms of institutional degradation were structural not incidental rooted in the extraction of labor and resources under systems of coercion. The persistence of these institutions, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), has had profound implications for the development of trust, which is essential for economic cooperation and investment. The cumulative effect of these historical processes is a pattern of institutional underdevelopment that continues to constrain economic prospects in many former colonies. This legacy of extractive institutions provides a critical lens through which to understand the uneven development trajectories of the Global South.

Both authors highlight how the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule whether through extractive systems or more inclusive governance have influenced the trajectory of economic performance in the post-colonial era. The mechanism through which these institutions shape growth lies in their capacity to either constrain or enable productive investment, innovation, and political stability. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) further support this by showing that the institutional legacies of colonial rule have persisted well beyond the end of formal colonialism, continuing to affect income levels and economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) add that secure property rights and the rule of law, which are often embedded in these historical institutions, are essential for fostering economic growth and political freedom. The downstream implication of these findings is that the success or failure of modern economies is deeply rooted in the institutional legacies of the past, which continue to shape the conditions for sustainable development. These insights underscore the critical role of historical institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge, a form of institutional progress, is the mainspring of long-run economic growth, a claim shared by (Abramovitz 1986, p.19; Kuznets 1973, p.1; Temin 2002, p.6). This stream emphasizes that technological advancement alone is insufficient without complementary institutional frameworks that sustain and direct its application. The institutional progress required to harness technological potential is not merely a byproduct but a necessary condition for sustained economic development. Mokyr highlights how the expansion of educational institutions, such as the universities of Glasgow and Edinburgh, played a pivotal role in disseminating scientific knowledge and reducing the costs of technological adoption. These institutions, by fostering a culture of inquiry and experimentation, laid the groundwork for the Industrial Revolution’s transformative impact. Temin (2002, p.6) illustrates how institutional arrangements in postwar Europe, including domestic and international agreements, reinforced long-term investment and productivity gains. These examples collectively underscore the interdependence of knowledge diffusion and institutional capacity. The downstream implication is that without institutional mechanisms to translate knowledge into productive outcomes, technological progress remains fragmented and unsustainable. This insight positions institutional development as a critical enabler of the knowledge-based growth model.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that historical institutional legacies shape long-run economic growth by regulating training and access to professions, a claim supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) who show how institutional frameworks in early modern Europe facilitated differential economic development. The efficiency of training institutions, as emphasized by van Zanden, directly influenced the skill premium and human capital formation, which in turn shaped long-term productivity and wage dynamics. Pamuk (2007, p.25) reinforces this by highlighting how flexible institutions in the Low Countries and England allowed for sustained wage levels post-Black Death, creating a divergence in economic trajectories compared to other European regions. These institutional arrangements not only regulated the transmission of skills but also determined the relative economic performance of different regions over time. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further illustrate how institutional design, shaped by historical legacies, influenced labor market outcomes in colonial Africa, where institutional fragmentation constrained wage growth and human capital accumulation. The shared insight across these sources is that institutional structures, whether regulating professions or shaping labor markets, are central to the long-run divergence in economic performance. This institutional conditioning of human capital and labor markets underscores the broader role of institutions in shaping the conditions for sustained economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) contends that distributional conflicts, not institutions, are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, a stance supported by McCloskey (2015, p.9) who emphasizes the primacy of ideas over institutions. Both authors argue that the persistence of certain economic practices and outcomes is better explained by the underlying distributional tensions and cultural beliefs rather than by the efficiency of institutional frameworks. Ogilvie highlights how institutional interlinkages can stifle innovation unless they provide substitutes for existing structures, while McCloskey illustrates how cultural and ideological struggles, such as the resistance to Jewish inclusion in the French military, can impede economic progress. These sources collectively suggest that the ideological and distributional dimensions of society are more critical in shaping long-term economic trajectories than the mere existence of formal institutions. The interplay between cultural values and institutional rules, as Ogilvie notes, is essential to understanding how economic outcomes are shaped, even when institutions themselves may not be efficient. This perspective challenges the conventional focus on institutional design by foregrounding the role of ideological and distributional dynamics in economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that European capitalism created underdevelopment, offering an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth compared to institutions, a claim supported by Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Hopkins (2019, p.11). These sources collectively suggest that the historical trajectory of African economies was shaped by the expansion of European capitalist systems, which imposed structural dependencies and inequalities that persisted long after colonial rule. The mechanism through which this occurred, as Hopkins (2019, p.11) notes, is the creation of underdevelopment through unequal exchange, where African economies were integrated into global markets under terms that systematically disadvantaged them. This historical process, according to Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), has obscured the potential for sustained growth by distorting the conditions of production and resource allocation. The implication of this historical causation is that contemporary economic outcomes are not merely the result of institutional arrangements but are deeply embedded in a legacy of structural transformation. This perspective challenges the conventional institutionalist focus by foregrounding the role of historical processes in shaping economic trajectories. The interplay between historical causation and institutional design thus becomes a critical axis for understanding long-run growth.

The consensus across the literature is that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, with multiple studies emphasizing their enduring impact on development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This agreement is grounded in the observation that institutional frameworks, once established, persist over time and shape the conditions for economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). However the sources diverge on the origins of these institutions with some arguing that they stem from colonial legacies and others attributing them to preexisting historical and cultural conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight how colonial institutions, particularly those reinforcing extractive practices, have had lasting negative effects on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6), while Hopkins (2009, p.8) underscores the importance of precolonial institutions in shaping contemporary African development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The disagreement centers on whether colonialism or preexisting structures are the primary determinants of institutional quality. This debate is resolved through empirical comparisons of institutional persistence across regions, such as the contrast between settler and nonsettler colonies, which reveals how colonial institutions have shaped divergent economic paths (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.5).
