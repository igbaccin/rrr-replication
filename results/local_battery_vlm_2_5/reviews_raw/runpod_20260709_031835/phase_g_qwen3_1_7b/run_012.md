What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the intersection of historical and contemporary economic analysis, where the interplay between institutional structures and developmental outcomes remains a subject of intense debate. While some argue that institutions are the primary driver of economic progress, others contend that external factors such as technology, geography, and political dynamics play a more significant role. The literature is divided into two streams: one emphasizing the structural and institutional determinants of growth, and another highlighting the dynamic and contingent nature of institutional influence. The first stream, which includes works by Austin, Ogilvie, and Acemoglu et al., posits that institutions shape economic outcomes through their ability to create or constrain the conditions for growth. The second stream, represented by Nunn and Wantchekon, and Pamuk, suggests that institutional change is often mediated by external factors, such as technological advancements or shifts in political stability. These divergent perspectives reflect a broader intellectual disagreement about the extent to which institutions can be considered as the primary force shaping long-term economic trajectories. The debate is not merely academic; it has implications for policy design, historical interpretation, and the understanding of development dynamics. Institutions, in this context, are not static entities but dynamic frameworks that evolve in response to changing conditions, making their role in economic growth both complex and context-dependent.
