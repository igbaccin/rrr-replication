What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic history and political science, and it reflects a deep intellectual concern about the mechanisms that shape societal development. While some scholars argue that institutions are the primary force behind economic progress, others contend that external factors, such as technology or geography, play a more dominant role. The debate is not merely academic, it touches on the fundamental question of how societies evolve and what conditions must be in place for sustained growth. The literature presents two competing perspectives: one that emphasizes the causal role of institutions, and another that highlights the influence of external factors. The former, which is supported by a substantial body of evidence, suggests that institutions shape economic outcomes through their influence on governance, resource allocation, and cultural norms. The latter, however, challenges this view by arguing that economic growth is often driven by technological change and global trade, rather than by institutional structures. The evidence suggests that institutions do play a significant role, but their impact is mediated by a complex interplay of historical, cultural, and external factors. Shared findings indicate that institutional quality is correlated with economic development, but the extent of this relationship varies across regions and time periods. The evidence also shows that institutions can both facilitate and hinder growth, depending on their nature and the context in which they operate. In conclusion, institutions are an important factor in long-run economic growth, but their role is not deterministic and is influenced by a range of other variables. Institutions shape economic growth by establishing structural frameworks that influence long-term development. The interdependence of institutions, as noted by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), underscores the complexity of economic growth, where the stability of legal and property rights is critical. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) corroborates this by showing that institutional quality correlates with income per capita, demonstrating that institutions are not just byproducts of economic activity but are foundational to long-term growth. The financial revolution in England, as described by North (1989, p.9), illustrates how institutional frameworks can catalyze economic development by creating secure property rights and fostering market efficiency. These findings collectively suggest that institutions are not merely reactive to economic conditions but are active in shaping the trajectory of economic growth.

This suggests that institutions act as both a catalyst and a constraint, determining the trajectory of economic development. The interplay between institutional stability and innovation is critical, as institutions that foster private property and market mechanisms can drive long-term growth, while extractive institutions may hinder it. Thus, the role of institutions is not merely descriptive but transformative, shaping the very conditions under which economic growth occurs.
