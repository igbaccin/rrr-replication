What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic history and political science, challenging whether institutional frameworks shape development trajectories or are merely byproducts of historical contingencies. While some argue that institutions are the primary driver of growth, others contend that economic outcomes are more contingent on external factors such as technological change or globalization. The debate is not about whether institutions matter, but rather how they interact with other variables to influence long-term development. This review synthesizes scholarly arguments that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic growth, particularly through their influence on resource allocation, labor markets, and the creation of durable economic structures. The evidence suggests that institutional quality and stability are key determinants of growth, as demonstrated by empirical analyses of European mortality rates, the impact of land and property rights, and the role of cultural norms in fostering economic development. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in sustaining economic progress over time, even in the face of external shocks. The interplay between institutions and other factors highlights the complexity of economic development, emphasizing that institutional evolution is both a cause and a consequence of growth.
