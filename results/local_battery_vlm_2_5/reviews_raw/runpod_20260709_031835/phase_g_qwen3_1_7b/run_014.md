What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic historiography, where the interplay between structural constraints and institutional frameworks shapes development trajectories. The literature presents a complex tapestry of competing perspectives, with some arguing that institutions are both a driver and a constraint, while others emphasize their stabilizing function. The debate is not merely academic, it reflects deeper tensions about the nature of economic progress and the conditions under which it unfolds. Scholars agree that institutions influence growth through their capacity to shape economic behavior, but they differ on the extent to which they determine it. Some contend that institutional quality is a prerequisite for growth, while others argue that historical contingencies and external forces play a more decisive role. The evidence suggests that institutions are not static but evolve in response to changing economic conditions, yet their cumulative impact remains a subject of contention. The question remains: can institutions alone account for long-term growth, or are there other factors that mediate this relationship? The evidence supports the view that institutions are a critical component in shaping economic trajectories, but the degree to which they are the primary determinant remains unresolved. The role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth is thus a contested yet central theme in contemporary economic historiography. This claim is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), which highlights how pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than economic efficiency, offering a complementary perspective on the nature of institutional influence. The shared finding that institutions affect growth is evident in the empirical analyses of European mortality rates and institutional quality, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19). The interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, as discussed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), underscores the complexity of institutional effects on growth. The evidence from these sources collectively suggests that institutions do not operate in isolation but are embedded in historical and structural contexts that shape economic outcomes. This interdependence implies that understanding institutional influence requires considering both the nature of the institutions and the broader sociohistorical environment in which they operate. The role of institutions in shaping growth is thus not merely a matter of selecting favorable institutions but also involves the dynamic interplay between institutional frameworks and historical conditions.

Broadberry and Gardner argue that institutional adaptation and technological advancement are fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, as these factors shape the economic landscape and lead to sustained growth. Their evidence spans both historical and contemporary cases, demonstrating that institutional evolution and technological innovation are not only necessary but also deeply intertwined with economic development. The paper highlights how institutional changes in factor markets, such as the shift from slavery to land use rights in Ghana, facilitated the reallocation of resources and enabled productive shifts in the economy. Similarly, the integration of new technologies, like steam and electric power, required institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure their effective utilization. These findings suggest that the interplay between institutional frameworks and technological progress is a key mechanism through which long-term economic growth is achieved. The evidence collectively underscores the importance of adaptive institutions in fostering sustained economic development, as technological advancements are both a product and a driver of institutional change.
