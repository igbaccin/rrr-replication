What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question is central to understanding how societal structures shape economic outcomes over time. The literature presents a complex landscape where institutions are both a driver and a constraint, with varying degrees of influence depending on context and historical conditions. One stream of scholarship argues that institutions are essential for fostering economic growth through the creation of stable environments that encourage investment, innovation, and institutionalized norms. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing historical periods, suggesting that institutional evolution is more nuanced and context-specific. Similarly, van Zanden (2009, p.27) notes that the skill premium in western Europe fell sharply in the late medieval period, illustrating how institutional frameworks can shape economic outcomes through the distribution of resources and the development of formalized structures. Another stream of research emphasizes the interdependence of institutions, with Ogilvie (2007, p.27) arguing that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe are better explained by distributional conflicts than by economic efficiency. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping the conditions under which economic growth is possible. The role of institutions, therefore, is not merely a static factor but one that is dynamically shaped by historical, cultural, and political contexts, influencing the trajectory of economic development over time.
