Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more benign than commonly believed, challenging the perception that colonialism was inherently detrimental. This claim is supported by Frankema (2012, p.14), which highlights how the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered, and instead reconstructs and compares real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule. The paper demonstrates that African real wages in British Africa from 1880 to 1965 were well above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, suggesting that colonialism did not necessarily hinder economic growth. This finding aligns with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17), which shows that despite periods of shrinking, economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa was above subsistence levels in many economies by the early 20th century. The shared posture that colonialism's institutional and structural changes were key drivers of long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa is thus substantiated by multiple sources, demonstrating that institutional frameworks are not merely contingent but are essential to the trajectory of economic growth.

Institutions that enable the accumulation of resources and the creation of stable economic environments are crucial for long-term development. The interplay between institutional structures and economic outcomes is thus a fundamental aspect of economic growth, with institutions acting as both a catalyst and a constraint in the development process. The sustained availability of resources and the stability of economic institutions are key conditions for long-run growth, as evidenced by the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories. Institutional development thus serves as a foundational mechanism in the determination of long-run economic growth, with its effects manifesting through the allocation of resources and the shaping of policy environments.

Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as institutional adjustments enable the effective utilization of knowledge and technology, leading to sustained economic development. This claim is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.13), who both argue that institutional frameworks shape the trajectory of economic progress by facilitating the adoption and dissemination of technological advancements. Kuznets highlights that technological innovation is not merely a product of economic conditions but requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be effectively implemented. Pamuk illustrates this dynamic through the historical case of the Black Death, demonstrating how institutional changes in the Low Countries and England enabled the integration of new technologies, thereby fostering economic growth. The shared mechanism of institutional adaptation across these sources underscores the critical role of institutions in mediating the relationship between technological progress and economic development. Institutional stability and flexibility are thus essential for the sustained application of knowledge and the long-term prosperity of economies.

The role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth is a central theme in contemporary economic thought, with scholars agreeing that institutional frameworks shape economic outcomes through their influence on resource allocation, innovation, and political stability. This consensus is grounded in the evidence presented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who demonstrate that institutional quality is a critical determinant of economic growth. The interdependence of institutions, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), suggests that the effectiveness of institutions is not isolated but is embedded within broader frameworks that influence economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) further supports this view by arguing that the reversal of fortune thesis over-simplifies causation, emphasizing that institutional evolution is nuanced and context-specific.
