What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the intersection of historical analysis and institutional theory, and it is central to understanding how economic development unfolds over time. While some scholars argue that institutions are both a cause and a consequence of growth, others emphasize their structural role in shaping economic outcomes. The debate is not merely academic, it reflects deeper tensions about the nature of economic development, the capacity of institutions to drive progress, and the limitations of theoretical models in capturing complex causal relationships. The literature is divided into two main streams: one that emphasizes the dynamic and evolving nature of institutions, and another that highlights their structural and explanatory power. The former, represented by Austin (2008, p.2) and van Zanden (2009, p.27), argues that institutions are not static but are shaped by historical, cultural, and political forces, and that their role in growth is contingent on these conditions. The latter, as seen in Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), suggests that institutions are more foundational, offering a stable framework for economic development. These two perspectives conflict over whether institutions are primarily a response to external pressures or a driver of growth. The question remains unresolved, but the evidence suggests that institutions play a complex and multifaceted role in shaping long-run economic trajectories, often interacting with other factors such as technological change, political stability, and external shocks.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that institutions significantly influence income per capita, as evidenced by the correlation between European mortality rates and institutional quality. This finding aligns with North and Weingast (1989, p.2), which highlights the role of political commitment in enforcing rules and fostering economic growth. The shared evidence from Acemoglu et al. and North and Weingast underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories. Institutions, as shown by Austin (2008, p.2), are not merely responses to external pressures but are foundational in determining long-run growth. The evidence collectively suggests that institutions act as a structural mechanism that influences economic development over time, with their effectiveness dependent on political stability and the presence of enforceable rules. This perspective is further supported by North (1989, p.9), who argues that institutional differences lead to economic consequences, challenging the neoclassical view that population and savings are the primary determinants of growth. The cumulative evidence from these sources indicates that institutions are not only a response to external factors but also a driver of long-term economic development, shaping the trajectory of economic growth through the establishment and maintenance of stable frameworks.

van Zanden's work highlights the critical role of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic growth by establishing stable environments for innovation and investment. This is corroborated by Mokyr, who emphasizes that institutional progress complements technological advancement and is essential for sustaining economic improvement. The skill premium, as a proxy for institutional quality, reflects the interplay between factor endowments and the effectiveness of institutions, demonstrating that stable frameworks are not merely responses to external pressures but active contributors to economic development. Institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as they shape the conditions under which economic development occurs. Bryant (2006, p.3) argues that institutional frameworks determine the trajectory of economic progress by constraining or enabling technological innovation and social mobility. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) highlights that institutional quality influences the adaptability of a nation, as education and governance determine the trajectory of technological opportunity. Simson (2019, p.19) illustrates that institutional policies in post-independence Africa did not differ significantly from other regions in terms of growth, yet they constrained the distribution of patronage, demonstrating how institutions can shape the conditions of economic development without necessarily prioritizing patronage. The shared finding that institutions influence economic growth through their role in shaping technological opportunity and social mobility underscores the importance of institutional development as a foundational mechanism for long-term economic progress.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that historical contributions to economic development are fundamental in shaping long-run economic growth through the establishment of institutions. Their evidence shows that pre-colonial and colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping the economic trajectories of African societies, as evidenced by the codification of customary law and the marginalization of women in land access. These institutional legacies, established during colonial rule, laid the groundwork for the economic transformations observed in the post-colonial period. The persistence of these institutional frameworks, even in the face of political instability, underscores the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes. The shared finding that institutions shape economic growth through their role in establishing the conditions for technological opportunity and social mobility further supports the claim that historical contributions are essential in shaping long-term economic progress.
