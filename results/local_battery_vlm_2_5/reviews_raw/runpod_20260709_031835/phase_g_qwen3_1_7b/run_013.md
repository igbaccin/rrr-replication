What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question addresses a fundamental tension in economic historiography: whether institutions, as the primary driver of growth, are the dominant force shaping economic trajectories, or whether other factors, such as technological change, political stability, or external shocks, play a more critical role. The literature presents a divided but evolving picture, with some scholars arguing that institutions are the key to long-term growth, while others emphasize the interplay of multiple factors. The debate is not about whether institutions are important, but rather about the extent to which they are the primary mechanism through which they shape growth. The evidence suggests that institutions influence growth through their ability to create the conditions for innovation, investment, and stability, yet the complexity of historical contexts and the variability of institutional arrangements mean that the relationship is not straightforward. The role of institutions is thus both essential and contingent, shaped by the specific historical, cultural, and political circumstances in which they operate. The debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to determine long-run economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further emphasizes that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than purely economic efficiency, illustrating how institutional frameworks are deeply embedded in the socio-political fabric of a society.
