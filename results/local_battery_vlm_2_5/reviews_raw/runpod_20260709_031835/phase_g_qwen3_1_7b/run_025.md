What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic historiography and political science, challenging whether institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories or are merely byproducts of historical contingencies. The literature presents a divided yet evolving discourse, with some scholars emphasizing the enduring impact of institutions on growth, while others argue for the dynamic and context-specific nature of institutional influence. The debate centers on whether institutions are foundational to economic development or are contingent on historical and cultural factors. (Austin 2008, p.2) and (Austin 2008, p.25) highlight the tension between institutional evolution and economic growth, suggesting that institutions are not merely passive structures but active agents in shaping economic outcomes. (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19) and (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) reinforce the empirical link between institutional quality and income per capita, providing evidence that institutions can drive long-term growth. (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) and (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) introduce the role of cultural institutions in shaping economic outcomes, arguing that non-economic factors can influence institutional frameworks and, in turn, economic performance. (Hopkins 2009, p.18) and (Hopkins 2009, p.12) underscore the complex interplay between colonialism and institutional development, showing how historical legacies can persist even after decolonization. (Pamuk 2007, p.13) and (Pamuk 2007, p.22) illustrate how institutional changes can drive productivity and wage differentials, demonstrating the material consequences of institutional evolution. The literature collectively suggests that institutions are neither wholly deterministic nor entirely contingent, but rather part of a dynamic and multifaceted process that shapes economic growth over time. The debate remains open, but the consensus is that institutions play a crucial role in determining long-run economic outcomes, though the nature and extent of this role are subject to historical and contextual variation.
