What is the role of institutions in determining long-run economic growth? This question lies at the heart of economic history and political science, and it reflects a fundamental tension between structural determinism and the agency of human action. While some scholars argue that institutions are the primary force shaping economic development, others emphasize the dynamic and contingent nature of institutional evolution. The literature is divided into two main streams: one that addresses a related but distinct question, how institutions interact with economic outcomes, and another that identifies an antecedent to the topic's causal variable, suggesting that institutional conditions may have shaped the emergence of economic growth. The first stream, which focuses on the interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, highlights the complexity of institutional determinism, while the second stream provides a contextual framework that underscores the historical and structural antecedents to growth. The former stream, represented by sources such as Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.27), argues that institutions are not static but evolve in response to changing conditions, challenging the notion of institutionalist determinism. The latter stream, as seen in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) and Frankema (2012, p.14), suggests that institutional conditions are shaped by historical and political factors, and that the development of economic growth is contingent on the interplay of these factors. These two streams converge in their recognition that institutions are not merely passive structures but active agents that shape economic trajectories over time. The question remains: how do institutions both constrain and enable economic growth, and what role does historical context play in shaping these dynamics?

Ogilvie highlights that pre-industrial European institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than economic efficiency, demonstrating how institutional dynamics are embedded in historical and cultural contexts. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the interdependence of institutional frameworks, such as the relationship between property rights and law, plays a critical role in determining economic outcomes. This interdependence suggests that institutions are not isolated entities but are part of a broader, dynamic system that influences growth. The evidence from Ogilvie and Acemoglu et al. collectively shows that institutional frameworks are not only shaped by historical conditions but also by the ways in which these frameworks interact and evolve over time. This dynamic interplay implies that institutions are not static but are continuously redefined through the processes of change and adaptation, thereby contributing to long-term economic growth. Institutions are thus not just a byproduct of economic development but are the foundational mechanism through which economic growth is realized.
