The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are viewed as foundational in shaping economic outcomes, but the mechanisms through which they operate and their relative importance compared to other factors remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, while others highlight the complex interplay between institutions, culture, and historical legacies. A key issue is whether institutions are primarily drivers of growth or if they are shaped by other forces that indirectly influence economic outcomes. This debate is particularly salient in the context of former colonies, where the legacy of colonial rule has left enduring institutional structures that continue to affect development trajectories. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for identifying pathways to sustained economic growth. The discussion of institutions in economic development is enriched by diverse perspectives, including the role of historical legacies, the interdependence of institutional frameworks, and the influence of cultural norms. These perspectives collectively illuminate the multifaceted nature of institutional impacts on long-run growth. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) report, “Patriarchal alliances and customary law: The invention of custom One key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era was through customary law.”

This perspective is further reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in seventeenth-century England for fostering commitment and economic growth. The mechanism through which institutions drive growth is evident in the way they shape the incentives for investment and the enforcement of property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on how equilibrium institutions, even when extractive, are designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than long-run growth. This insight underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contribute to this discussion by emphasizing how institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. These findings collectively suggest that the nature and evolution of institutions play a central role in determining long-term economic trajectories. This theme of institutional influence can be extended to examine how different types of institutions interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the role of institutions in guaranteeing the fair execution of economic contracts, as seen in the emergence of guilds in western Europe, which helped transfer skills across generations while also facing criticism for restricting market entry (van Zanden 2009, p.19); this institutional development is linked to long-term economic performance, as shown by the relationship between the skill premium and GDP growth in the nineteenth century (van Zanden 2009, p.25). Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on technological advancement and institutional adjustments, with institutions playing a crucial role in enabling the application of new technologies (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These insights are reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who argues that the Enlightenment's promotion of useful knowledge and rationality laid the institutional foundation for modern economic growth, particularly through the establishment of enforceable contracts and trust (Mokyr 2005, p.3). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) further support this by showing that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). These findings collectively demonstrate that institutions are not only pivotal in facilitating economic development but also in ensuring the conditions necessary for sustained growth over time.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust, which has long-term negative effects on economic development. This claim is supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that historical legacies continue to shape economic outcomes. This mechanism is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.2), who emphasizes that the adverse effects of the slave trade on long-term economic development are substantial, given the prolonged duration of the trade compared to colonial rule. Peters (2004, p.5) adds that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation, indicating that historical legacies are not only economic but also social and political, shaping the structures that influence development outcomes today. These findings collectively demonstrate that historical legacies such as the slave trade and colonialism have enduring effects on economic development in Africa, a theme that can be further explored in relation to other institutional factors.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonial power structures and distributional conflicts shape institutional formation, a perspective that challenges the assumption that economic efficiency alone drives institutional development. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) similarly contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, are central to understanding core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, emphasizing that institutional arrangements are often the result of competing interests rather than rational design. Ogilvie's analysis of interdependent institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) further illustrates how institutions are not isolated entities but part of a broader system that includes family structures, credit markets, and labor arrangements, all of which are shaped by underlying power relations. These insights collectively suggest that understanding long-run economic growth requires examining how institutional formations emerge from complex power dynamics rather than from purely economic considerations. This perspective opens the door to further exploration of how such institutional legacies interact with other factors in shaping development trajectories.

This perspective aligns with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption that economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa prior to the 1980s was solely a result of European colonial interventions, highlighting instead the potential for higher per capita incomes than previously thought. These findings suggest that long-run economic growth may have been more robust and less constrained than previously believed, as historical economic narratives have often overlooked the agency and contributions of local populations. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further support this revisionist view by arguing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This implies that the persistence of poverty in some regions may not be as deeply rooted in historical conditions as previously assumed, and that alternative factors, such as policy choices and institutional reforms, may have played a more significant role in shaping economic outcomes. These insights collectively suggest that re-evaluating historical economic narratives can provide a more nuanced understanding of the drivers of long-run economic growth, opening the door to further exploration of how policy interventions and institutional changes have shaped development trajectories.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced economic outcomes in former colonies. This is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who show that various measures of institutional quality consistently correlate with economic performance, reinforcing the idea that institutions are a key determinant of growth. These findings align with the argument made by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that institutional changes driven by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, rather than geographic factors. Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight how colonial policies, particularly those related to land access and customary law, shaped economic trajectories in Africa, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) challenges the assumption that institutional quality alone drives growth, suggesting that interdependencies among institutions and broader social and economic contexts must be considered. This disagreement is settled by the empirical evidence presented in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which provides a detailed analysis of how institutional quality relates to historical factors such as population density, offering a concrete measurement that supports the role of institutions in long-run economic growth.
