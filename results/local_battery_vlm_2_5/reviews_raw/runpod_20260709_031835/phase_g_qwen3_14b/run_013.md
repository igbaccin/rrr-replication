The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth has been a central focus of economic scholarship, with competing perspectives on whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or if other factors, such as geography, technology, or cultural norms, play a more significant role. At stake is the understanding of how institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories and whether these effects are durable or contingent on other conditions. Some scholars emphasize the direct influence of institutions, particularly those that promote the rule of law, property rights, and political accountability, arguing that these are foundational for sustained economic development. Others highlight the interplay between institutions and broader social, cultural, or technological forces, suggesting that institutional effects are mediated by these factors. This review examines key streams of scholarship that explore these dynamics, beginning with the argument that institutions are the primary causal force behind long-run growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita, emphasizing the contrasting institutional legacies of European colonization in different regions. Their analysis shows that extractive institutions, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast, led to lower levels of economic development compared to inclusive institutions in places like the United States or Australia. These findings are reinforced by subsequent work that examines the long-term effects of colonialism on institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The next section explores how other scholars have examined the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, including the ways in which they interact with cultural, social, and historical forces.

Their work highlights how colonial institutions shaped the trajectory of economic development, with extractive institutions in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast leading to lower levels of economic growth compared to inclusive institutions in the United States or Australia. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) similarly emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering economic growth, pointing to seventeenth-century England as a case where credible commitment to institutional frameworks enabled sustained economic expansion. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) further support this view by showing how divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, with the United States and Canada achieving sustained economic growth earlier than other regions. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.3) adds that political and economic institutions significantly influence economic growth by shaping transaction costs and the development of property rights, which in turn affect the efficiency of markets. These findings collectively suggest that the quality of institutions plays a central role in determining economic outcomes, with long-term implications for development trajectories. This emphasis on institutions as a key driver of growth provides a foundation for examining how they interact with other factors such as culture and history in shaping economic performance.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that long-term economic growth depends on historical trends, technological change, and institutional adjustments, a perspective supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of institutions such as guilds in shaping economic development in western Europe. These institutions, while fostering the intergenerational transfer of skills, also faced criticism for restricting market competition, illustrating the complex relationship between institutional structures and economic performance. Van Zanden (1999, p.18) further complicates this narrative by showing that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living, suggesting that institutional and technological factors alone may not fully explain long-term growth trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) add that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, pointing to earlier institutional and economic developments that shaped these differences. Mokyr (2005, p.3) underscores the Enlightenment’s contribution to modern economic growth through the promotion of useful knowledge and rationality, linking intellectual shifts to broader economic transformations. These findings collectively suggest that long-term economic performance is shaped by a combination of historical conditions, technological advancements, and institutional evolution, setting the stage for further exploration of how these factors interact with cultural and political dynamics in driving sustained growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical experiences, particularly the transatlantic slave trade, have left enduring legacies on institutional and social development in Africa, shaping contemporary levels of trust and economic performance. This finding aligns with Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the prolonged impact of the slave trades on Africa’s long-term economic development, linking them to increased ethnic fractionalization and insecurity. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further elaborate on how colonial institutions entrenched patriarchal structures, reinforcing the subordinate status of women through the codification of customary law, which in turn affected land access and economic opportunities. These historical legacies manifest in both institutional and cultural dimensions, as shown by the ongoing mistrust and fragmented social environments that impede collective action and economic cooperation. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that colonial rule did not necessarily foster the development of formal education, a critical institution for long-term growth, suggesting that the foundations for modern economic institutions were not laid during this period. These interconnected historical processes including slavery, colonial governance, and institutional codification continue to influence the trajectory of economic development in Africa, highlighting the deep and complex relationship between historical legacies and contemporary institutional outcomes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonial power structures are deeply embedded in the class-based logics that shape both the colonizer and the colonized, a perspective that challenges the notion of institutions as solely drivers of economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) similarly contends that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, are central to understanding the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutional frameworks are shaped by power dynamics and social hierarchies rather than by rational or utilitarian considerations. These insights highlight the role of institutional development as a product of distributional conflicts and colonial power structures, offering an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further emphasizes the interdependence of individual institutions within a broader framework, illustrating how labor markets, credit systems, and family structures are interconnected, which complicates the idea of institutions as independent variables influencing economic outcomes. Stoler (1989, p.4) notes that colonial studies often overlook the internal political tensions among the colonizers themselves, underscoring the need to examine the class-based interests that shape colonial authority and economic institutions. This perspective reveals that institutional development is deeply influenced by the distribution of power and the conflicts that arise from it. Understanding these dynamics is essential for grasping the historical roots of contemporary economic institutions and their impact on long-run growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how colonial powers established different institutional frameworks that significantly influenced economic outcomes in their colonies. These frameworks, whether extractive or inclusive, have lasting effects on economic development, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize the rents of European colonists rather than promote long-term growth. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional quality in determining economic trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further illustrate the complex interplay between institutions and economic development by examining how customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, which in turn affected women's roles in the economy. This dynamic reveals that institutional development is shaped by external powers as well as by internal social norms and power relations. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) emphasize the role of trust in economic development, showing that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic outcomes in Africa. This finding highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic performance. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that the interdependencies among different institutions are crucial for understanding economic growth, suggesting that isolated institutional changes are insufficient to explain economic outcomes. These diverse perspectives collectively demonstrate that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms through which they influence growth remain a subject of debate. The specific historical evidence of institutional changes in former colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), provides a critical empirical comparison that helps to clarify the impact of different institutional frameworks on economic development.
