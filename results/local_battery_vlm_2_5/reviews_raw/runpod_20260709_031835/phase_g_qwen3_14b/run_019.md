The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical patterns of prosperity and poverty. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of economic outcomes, while others emphasize the influence of geographic, cultural, or historical factors. This literature review explores the causal relationship between institutional quality and long-run economic growth, focusing on how different streams of scholarship frame the role of institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are the key determinant of income per capita, as evidenced by the divergent colonial legacies in regions such as the United States and the Congo. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support these findings by emphasizing the institutional reversal in former European colonies due to colonialism, which created equilibrium institutions that favored European colonists over long-run growth. These accounts underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, but they also raise questions about the conditions under which such institutions emerge and persist.

They highlight how colonial powers established institutions that either promoted the rule of law and investment or favored extractive states that prioritized resource extraction over long-run development. This institutional divergence shaped equilibrium institutions that influenced economic trajectories for centuries. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.29) further emphasize that constitutional rules and institutions are crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England, where institutional stability enabled sustained economic development. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.3) expands on this by showing how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) corroborate these findings by demonstrating how institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. These studies collectively establish that the quality of institutions is a fundamental driver of economic growth, with long-term implications for development outcomes. This institutional focus provides a framework for understanding how different historical and political contexts shape economic trajectories, setting the stage for further exploration of the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology, institutional change, and ideological shifts, establishing a foundational understanding of the interplay between these factors. This perspective is reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of formal institutions such as guilds in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, although these institutions may also have hindered market efficiency by restricting entry into trades. Similarly, Mokyr (2005, p.3) emphasizes the importance of the Enlightenment’s promotion of useful knowledge and rationality in driving modern economic growth, suggesting that ideological shifts can catalyze technological progress and institutional reform. These insights are further supported by van Zanden (1999, p.18), who notes the complex relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living, illustrating how institutional and ideological factors can influence long-term growth trajectories. Stern (2004, p.1) contributes to this discussion by examining the environmental Kuznets curve, although his critique of its statistical foundation underscores the need for more robust models that incorporate institutional and technological variables. Together, these studies illustrate that economic growth is not solely the product of technological advancement but also depends on the interplay of institutional frameworks and ideological contexts, shaping the conditions under which growth can be sustained and broadened.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) highlights that historical power dynamics and colonial legacies significantly shape institutional frameworks, a perspective echoed by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who emphasizes the complexity of institutional development in Africa. These studies reveal that institutions are not solely products of economic efficiency but are deeply influenced by historical contexts, such as distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe and the legacies of colonial rule. Ogilvie’s analysis of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) shows that interdependent institutional systems, such as family structures and credit markets, played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. Similarly, Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism created specific power relations that influenced both the colonizer and the colonized, shaping class-based logics that persisted beyond the colonial period. These insights suggest that understanding long-run economic growth requires examining the historical and power-based origins of institutions. Such frameworks provide a lens through which contemporary institutional challenges can be contextualized, revealing that the quality of institutions is often a reflection of past power struggles and colonial imprints. This historical perspective on institutions thus contributes to a broader understanding of how past legacies shape current economic conditions and institutional quality.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-term negative effects on economic development, a perspective supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These findings suggest that historical events such as the slave trade and colonialism have shaped contemporary economic conditions by embedding structural disadvantages that persist across generations. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further explain that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has had a lasting effect on economic development, a mechanism that is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.2), who emphasizes the adverse impact of the slave trades on long-term economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.17) adds that the ecological and political conditions during colonial rule influenced the development of formal education, which in turn affects economic outcomes. These insights suggest that historical legacies influence both trust and social cohesion and also shape institutional frameworks that affect economic performance. This understanding of historical legacies provides a foundation for examining how past events continue to influence contemporary economic and institutional dynamics.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, challenging the views of Pomeranz and Parthasarathi, and this perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who similarly revise historical economic narratives by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s. These studies highlight the importance of re-evaluating historical economic data to understand long-term economic performance more accurately. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) contribute to this revision by emphasizing the significant economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods, which challenges neglectful historical narratives that have underestimated their roles. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further support this revision by demonstrating that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings collectively indicate that historical economic narratives have often been shaped by biases and incomplete data, leading to misinterpretations of economic performance. This revised understanding of historical economic conditions provides a more nuanced foundation for examining the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth.

Institutional quality has been identified as a key determinant of long-run economic growth, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasizing that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced economic outcomes in former European colonies. This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who demonstrate that various measures of institutional quality consistently correlate with economic performance, reinforcing the idea that institutions are central to economic development. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the role of trust as a critical component of institutions, showing its direct impact on economic development and political institutions. This underscores the importance of both formal and informal institutional structures in shaping economic trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further elaborates on the interdependencies among different institutional frameworks, suggesting that economic growth is better understood through the lens of interconnected institutions rather than isolated ones. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who illustrate how institutional arrangements, such as customary law, historically affected economic opportunities for women, thereby influencing broader economic outcomes. The consensus among these studies is that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) points out that the degree to which institutional quality can be changed and its effect on growth is still debated, noting that distributional conflicts may be more significant than economic efficiency in driving institutional development. This disagreement is settled by the empirical evidence presented in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), which shows that institutional changes can be measured and have significant effects on economic performance, thus affirming the role of institutional quality in driving long-run economic growth.
