The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding the divergent paths of economic development across nations. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography or historical events, play a more decisive role. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the most critical variable in shaping long-term growth. This perspective emphasizes the importance of inclusive institutions that protect property rights, enforce the rule of law, and promote political accountability. Other scholars, however, highlight the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, such as historical legacies, cultural norms, and economic opportunities, suggesting that institutions are not the sole or even the primary driver of growth. These differing views underscore the importance of understanding how institutions interact with other variables to influence economic development. This discussion sets the stage for examining the various streams of scholarship that explore the causal relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that determine economic outcomes, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering commitment and economic growth. The mechanism through which institutions influence growth is by establishing secure property rights and enforcing the rule of law, as seen in the cases of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where institutions encouraged investment and long-term development. In extractive states such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, institutions were designed to maximize the rents of colonists and promote economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional divergence has long-term implications for economic performance, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which reflects institutional changes caused by colonialism rather than geographic determinism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). These findings highlight the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as they influence the incentives for investment, innovation, and sustained growth. The role of institutions is further reinforced by the observation that economic growth depends on advancing technology and necessary institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This underscores the interplay between institutional development and technological progress in driving long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical legacies such as the slave trade have enduring effects on economic development by shaping social norms and institutions, particularly through the persistence of mistrust. This finding is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights how the extensive duration of the African slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a more profound impact on long-term economic development than the relatively shorter period of colonial rule. These historical legacies influenced the development of institutions by fostering environments of uncertainty and insecurity, which in turn affected the ability of communities to form cooperative relationships essential for economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further illustrate how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through the codification of customary law, which marginalized women and limited their access to land and economic opportunities, thereby shaping institutional frameworks that persisted beyond the colonial era. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that the impact of colonial rule on formal education was not benign, suggesting that the institutional structures established during this period had lasting consequences on human capital development. These historical legacies thus set the stage for the role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, as they influence the incentives for investment, innovation, and sustained growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that the intellectual environment shaped by the Enlightenment, emphasizing useful knowledge and rationality, played a crucial role in fostering modern economic growth. This perspective aligns with Stoler (1989, p.6), who highlights how colonial ideologies and class-based logics influenced the structures of power and production, shaping the conditions under which economic and institutional development could occur. The interplay between cultural and ideological factors, as demonstrated by these authors, reveals how the spread of Enlightenment ideas and the legacies of colonial rule created a complex backdrop for economic transformation. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further illustrates this by showing that the quality of institutions in Africa is influenced by historical and cultural contexts as well as ethnic fragmentation. These findings suggest that cultural and ideological influences are deeply embedded in the development of institutions, shaping their trajectory in ways that are not always immediately apparent. The mechanisms through which these influences operate such as the promotion of rationality and the reinforcement of colonial hierarchies continue to affect economic and institutional development across regions and periods. This understanding of cultural and ideological legacies provides a broader context for examining the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that reconstructing historical economic data is essential to understanding long-term economic performance, as demonstrated by the analysis of skill premiums in western Europe and their relationship to subsequent GDP growth. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption of persistent underdevelopment in Sub-Saharan Africa by presenting evidence of higher per capita incomes before the 1980s. These findings highlight the importance of historical data reconstruction in identifying alternative explanations for economic divergence. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.18) shows that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, which contradicts revisionist optimism about long-term economic progress. This suggests that economic performance may be influenced by factors beyond institutional development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) further contribute to this discussion by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, challenging the views of Pomeranz and Parthasarathi. These studies collectively emphasize the need for careful data reconstruction to uncover the complex interplay of factors that shape economic development over time. This approach provides a nuanced understanding of historical economic performance that complements institutional analyses by offering alternative explanations rooted in historical data.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) challenges the assumption that economic efficiency is the primary driver of institutional development in pre-industrial Europe, emphasizing instead the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions. Ogilvie further contends that the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework is crucial for understanding economic growth, as illustrated by the European family system's relationship with labor markets and credit systems. This interdependency suggests that institutions do not operate in isolation but are part of a complex web of interactions. Austin similarly critiques the deterministic narratives of Acemoglu et al., noting that the African experience demonstrates a more nuanced relationship between institutions and economic development. These arguments collectively suggest that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by distributional conflicts and the historical context in which institutions emerge. This understanding of institutional development as a product of complex, context-specific interactions provides a more comprehensive view of long-run economic growth that accounts for the multifaceted nature of historical processes.

The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a subject of extensive scholarly debate, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) arguing that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita. Their analysis highlights how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with the former being associated with lower levels of economic development. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges this deterministic narrative by suggesting that the African experience demonstrates a more nuanced relationship between institutions and economic development. This perspective underscores the importance of historical context and the dynamic nature of institutional evolution. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further complicate the picture by emphasizing the role of gender in shaping institutional development, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, where patriarchal alliances and customary law played a significant role in the subordination of women. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) add another layer by showing how the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development. These diverse perspectives collectively suggest that the impact of institutions on economic growth is multifaceted, influenced by a range of historical, social, and economic factors. The debate over the deterministic versus nuanced views of institutional effects on growth remains unresolved, but the empirical evidence from different regions and historical periods provides a rich foundation for further exploration.
