The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions are often framed as foundational to economic performance, yet their causal mechanisms and interactions with other factors remain contested. Some scholarship emphasizes the direct influence of institutional quality on growth, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and broader social, cultural, or technological forces. This tension underscores the complexity of institutional effects and their embeddedness in historical and contextual conditions. The debate is not merely academic; it shapes how institutions are perceived as either enablers or constraints of economic development. Understanding the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth is crucial for formulating effective policies and interventions. This review explores these dynamics by examining streams of scholarship that either challenge or build upon the institutionalist perspective. One such stream argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are the primary determinants of long-term economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) emphasize how colonial institutions in different regions shaped economic trajectories, with extractive institutions leading to stagnation and inclusive ones fostering growth. Their analysis highlights the importance of institutional design in shaping economic performance, as seen in the contrast between the United States and the Congo. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support this perspective by arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes caused by colonialism, and not geographic determinism. They demonstrate that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were frequently structured to benefit European colonists, and not to encourage long-run growth. This view is further supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who discusses the role of formal institutions like guilds in shaping economic development in western Europe. These institutions, while criticized for restricting entry into trades, also facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, contributing to long-term economic performance. Other streams of scholarship question the central role of institutions by focusing on the influence of other factors, such as cultural norms and historical legacies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development. They find that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel through which the slave trade affected trust. This indicates that cultural and social factors, and not only institutional ones, might significantly influence economic outcomes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also highlights the importance of technological advancement and necessary institutional and ideological adjustments in sustaining economic growth. He argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that it demands. This perspective underscores the interdependence between institutions and other factors, such as technology, in driving long-run economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) contribute to this discussion by highlighting the role of African women in economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that the de-privileging of women in access to land in the context of cash crops was reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions alone determine economic outcomes, by emphasizing the contributions of marginalized groups and the impact of colonial policies on gender dynamics. These diverse perspectives illustrate the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional effects and their interactions with other factors.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) claim that institutions, and not geography or other factors, are the main driver of long-run economic growth; Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) back this claim by pointing to the importance of institutional development and initial factor endowments in shaping different economic development paths in the New World. This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either encouraged investment and the rule of law or, conversely, created extractive states that prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering credible commitments, which are essential for economic growth, as illustrated by the development of capital markets in seventeenth-century England. These findings collectively suggest that the type of institutions in place significantly influences the trajectory of economic growth. North (1989, p.6) underscores how the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduces transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and contributing to economic growth. These insights highlight the central role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes and their enduring impact on long-term development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth relies on technological progress, institutional reforms, and ideological shifts, a perspective corroborated by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who highlights the Enlightenment's role in fostering a culture of useful knowledge and rationality that underpins modern growth. This interplay between institutions and technological change is further illustrated by the historical development of scientific societies, as shown in Mokyr (2005, p.54), which demonstrates how the proliferation of such societies in the eighteenth century coincided with improvements in law, order, and economic efficiency. These institutional and ideological adjustments helped in the adoption of new technologies and also fostered an environment that supported sustained innovation. Stern (2004, p.1) acknowledges the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories, even as he critiques the environmental Kuznets curve for its statistical weaknesses. This underscores the broader point that while institutions may influence growth through multiple channels, their role in enabling technological and ideological progress remains central. The alignment of these insights reveals that institutions and technological change are deeply intertwined in driving long-run growth, with their combined effects being more robust than either factor in isolation. This synthesis provides a foundation for examining how specific institutional arrangements can either support or hinder the diffusion of technological advancements.

This reassessment of historical narratives is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the view of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings suggest that economic performance and development are more complex and context-dependent than previously assumed, with factors such as skill premiums and wage trends playing significant roles in shaping long-term growth trajectories. Van Zanden (1999, p.18) adds to this by showing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of historical economic performance (van Zanden 1999, p.18). Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These studies collectively indicate that historical economic data and narratives require careful reassessment to fully understand the drivers of long-run growth.

This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who demonstrate how colonial policies reinforced patriarchal structures and marginalized women, contributing to long-term economic inequalities. These findings underscore the role of historical legacies in shaping institutional frameworks that affect economic performance. Nunn (2008, p.2) further emphasizes the long-term economic consequences of the slave trade, noting its profound impact on subsequent development trajectories. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that colonial rule did not necessarily foster the growth of formal education, complicating the narrative of benign colonial impacts. Peters (2004, p.5) highlights how land competition and exclusionary practices under colonialism contributed to class formation, revealing a pattern of systemic exclusion that continues to affect economic development. These studies collectively illustrate how historical events and colonial legacies have created enduring institutional challenges that shape economic outcomes in Africa. This understanding of historical legacies provides a critical lens for examining the complex interplay between past and present in shaping long-term economic growth.

Institutional quality is a central determinant of long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who both emphasize the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, with the former fostering economic development and the latter hindering it. North and Weingast highlight how constitutional rules and institutions in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth, showing that credible commitment underpins growth. These findings align with the broader consensus that institutions are pivotal for economic development. The precise ways in which institutions affect growth continue to be debated. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how colonial policies, such as the codification of customary law, marginalized women and affected land access, which in turn influenced economic development. This contrasts with the more general argument by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who emphasize the role of property rights and the rule of law in shaping economic outcomes. The historical evidence on land access and gender roles in colonial Africa provides a specific empirical comparison that highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional impacts on growth, showing that institutions influence economic outcomes through various channels, including social structures and land distribution.
