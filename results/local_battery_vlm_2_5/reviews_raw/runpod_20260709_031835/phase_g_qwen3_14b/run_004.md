The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central issue in development economics, with competing claims about their causal influence and the mechanisms through which they operate. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors such as historical legacies, cultural norms, and economic structures. At the heart of the debate is whether institutions are the primary determinants of growth or if they interact with other variables in complex ways. This question is not merely academic; it shapes policy interventions aimed at fostering sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, are the key drivers of long-term economic success, as evidenced by the divergent outcomes in former colonies. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges this view by suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the relationship between institutions and growth, advocating for a more dynamic and endogenous understanding of historical processes. These differing perspectives set the stage for a deeper exploration of the causal mechanisms and contextual factors that influence the role of institutions in economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-term economic development, as evidenced by their role in shaping economic outcomes across different regions and historical contexts, a claim supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate how historical events such as the slave trade have influenced institutional quality and, consequently, economic growth. The mechanisms through which institutions affect development are multifaceted, with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlighting how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than to encourage long-term growth. This dynamic is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that initial factor endowments and institutional development shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. These findings underscore the importance of understanding how institutions evolve over time and how they interact with other factors such as geography and culture. Nunn (2008, p.2) adds to this discussion by showing how the long-lasting effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, have shaped the institutional and economic landscape of Africa. These insights collectively reinforce the idea that institutions are not static but are shaped by historical processes, which in turn influence economic outcomes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism shaped the institutional and policy frameworks of colonized regions, a claim supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who highlights the enduring legacies of colonial rule on contemporary development. This dynamic is particularly evident in how colonial powers structured governance and economic systems, which in turn influenced the trajectory of institutions long after independence. For instance, Stoler (1989, p.6) explains that colonial authority was not merely imposed from above but was also shaped by the internal dynamics of the colonial state, including class-based logics and racial ideologies that reinforced hierarchical relations. These structures created a framework in which postcolonial institutions often inherited the contradictions and inequalities embedded in colonial governance. This suggests that the institutional frameworks established during colonial times have had lasting implications for the ability of postcolonial states to develop effective governance and public institutions. These insights underscore the importance of understanding how colonialism shaped the institutional foundations that continue to influence economic development today.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth hinges not only on technological advancement but also on the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective echoed by North (1989, p.6), who highlights the role of political and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Both scholars underscore that growth is not merely a function of external factors but is deeply intertwined with the capacity of societies to adapt their institutional frameworks and ideological outlooks to support evolving economic demands. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate on how the degree of commitment to institutional rules, particularly in seventeenth-century England, played a pivotal role in fostering economic growth by enabling stable and predictable environments for market exchanges. This aligns with Kuznets’ assertion that institutional adjustments are crucial for translating technological progress into sustained economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.3) adds that the Enlightenment's focus on useful knowledge and rationality contributed to the conditions necessary for modern economic growth, reinforcing the notion that ideological shifts are as important as institutional ones. These insights collectively suggest that the interplay between institutional evolution and ideological change is essential for long-term economic growth. This perspective opens the door to examining how specific institutional reforms and ideological transformations can be strategically leveraged to foster economic development in contemporary contexts.

This understanding of institutional legacies is complemented by Peters (2004, p.5) argues that land competition and customary law in sub-Saharan Africa have played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, a perspective supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, marginalizing women in land access and economic participation. These dynamics illustrate how institutional frameworks, particularly those rooted in historical and social contexts, influence economic development in ways that may differ from broader narratives emphasizing formal institutions. This institutional entrenchment of gendered inequalities, as Peters (2004, p.5) suggests, reflects broader patterns of exclusion and class formation that shape economic trajectories. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) emphasizes the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, arguing that economic outcomes are better explained by the complex interactions between different institutional systems rather than by isolated factors. This perspective aligns with the findings in Africa, where the interplay of land competition, customary law, and colonial legacies has created a unique institutional landscape that continues to influence economic outcomes. These insights underscore the importance of considering local institutional and social dynamics in understanding long-term economic growth.

This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who demonstrate that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and showed an upward trend, countering the assumption of persistent poverty due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings suggest that economic growth is not solely determined by institutional frameworks but is also influenced by historical and ecological factors that shape development trajectories. Frankema (2012, p.2) further illustrates this by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, highlighting the complexity of colonial legacies and their varied effects on economic development (Frankema 2012, p.2). Similarly, van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the skill premium in western Europe fell in the late medieval period, which has long-term implications for economic performance, suggesting that institutional and economic conditions are interwoven with broader historical trends (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These insights collectively indicate that a more nuanced understanding of economic development requires revisiting historical narratives and considering the multifaceted influences that shape long-run growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, determined the extent to which property rights were protected and how political power was distributed, with lasting effects on economic development. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) highlight how the legacy of the slave trade has contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect economic performance today. This underscores the idea that institutions are not only shaped by historical events but also have enduring consequences for economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the deterministic view of institutions by emphasizing the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic development, suggesting that historical narratives should account for the agency of societies in responding to their environments. This perspective introduces a nuance to the role of institutions, implying that while they are crucial, their impact is mediated by other factors such as technological change and social responses. The debate over the primacy of institutions versus the influence of other factors is thus settled by the empirical evidence used in these studies, such as the comparison of institutional quality across regions and the measurement of trust levels in post-slave trade societies, which provide concrete insights into the mechanisms through which institutions affect growth.
