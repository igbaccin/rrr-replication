The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and social interactions, are increasingly viewed as pivotal in shaping economic outcomes. The debate centers on how institutions influence growth, with some scholars emphasizing their capacity to foster inclusive economic opportunities and others highlighting the persistence of extractive structures. This review explores the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic development, focusing on their historical evolution, their interaction with other factors such as geography and culture, and their impact on trust and governance. The discussion draws on empirical analyses of colonial legacies, institutional reforms, and the interplay between legal systems and economic behavior. These insights reveal the complex ways in which institutions can either support or hinder long-term growth, depending on their design and enforcement. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for addressing contemporary challenges in development and for designing effective institutional frameworks. This analysis begins by examining the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes through their historical roots and their influence on governance and trust. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who highlights the interdependence of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective is grounded in the observation that colonial powers established divergent institutional structures in their colonies, with some fostering inclusive economic environments while others entrenched extractive systems that hindered long-term development. These institutional legacies continue to influence economic performance, as evidenced by the enduring effects of colonial-era institutions on modern economic conditions. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize the importance of credible commitments to institutional rules in promoting economic growth, particularly in seventeenth-century England, where constitutional arrangements facilitated market development and investment. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that institutional differences, alongside initial factor endowments, shaped divergent economic trajectories in the New World, with countries that developed inclusive institutions experiencing sustained growth. These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as they provide the foundational rules that govern economic interactions and incentivize investment. The persistence of these institutional effects across time and regions highlights their enduring influence on economic development. This emphasis on institutions as the primary determinant of growth provides a foundation for understanding the mechanisms through which they shape economic outcomes in subsequent analyses.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that institutional adaptation plays a crucial role in shaping long-term economic performance through the promotion of technological change and historical trends, as evidenced by the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This perspective aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights that sustained economic growth is contingent upon advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it (Kuznets 1973, p.1). The interplay between institutions and technological progress is further illustrated by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, in Western Europe facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, although their monopolistic tendencies could hinder market efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These findings suggest that the effectiveness of institutions in fostering economic performance depends on their ability to balance the promotion of technological innovation with the prevention of market distortions. Van Zanden (1999, p.18) adds that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 is complex, with no clear-cut correlation between the two, underscoring the need for nuanced understanding of institutional impacts over time (van Zanden 1999, p.18). This complexity reflects the broader challenge of aligning institutional frameworks with the dynamic forces of technological and economic change, setting the stage for further exploration of how institutions adapt to support sustained growth.

The need for institutional and policy reforms to support economic development in Africa is underscored by Frankema (2012, p.2); (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.11), who analyze both historical and contemporary institutional contexts in the region. Simson (2019, p.11) further illustrates the importance of post-independence budget policies in East Africa, showing that while clientelist practices were present, they were not the sole determinant of economic outcomes, as evidenced by the changes in military spending and employment distribution. These findings collectively indicate that institutional reforms must be tailored to the specific historical and socio-economic contexts of African countries to be effective. The implications of these insights extend to the design of current and future policies, which must consider the complex interplay of historical legacies and contemporary challenges in shaping sustainable economic development. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for formulating effective institutional reforms that can drive long-term economic growth in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show how historical legacies, such as the transatlantic slave trade, continue to influence economic and social conditions in Africa, particularly through their impact on intergroup trust and ethnic fractionalization. Their findings align with Peters (2004, p.5), who highlights how historical land competition and colonial-era policies continue to influence social structures and exclusion in sub-Saharan Africa, reinforcing patterns of inequality and class formation. These legacies are not merely static remnants of the past but actively shape contemporary economic outcomes by influencing social norms and institutional frameworks. Nunn (2008, p.2) further emphasizes that the prolonged duration of the slave trades spanning nearly 500 years had a more profound impact on economic development than the relatively brief period of official colonial rule. This indicates that historical processes are critical in determining long-term economic trajectories. Stoler (1989, p.6) complements these insights by showing how colonialism itself created complex power dynamics that redefined both the colonizer and the colonized, embedding structural inequalities that persist today. These interconnected historical forces slavery, colonialism, and land competition continue to affect economic development by shaping social trust, ethnic divisions, and institutional legitimacy. Understanding these legacies is essential for designing policies that address the root causes of underdevelopment. These historical influences set the stage for examining how contemporary institutional reforms can either mitigate or exacerbate these entrenched challenges.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge prevailing historical narratives in Africa by highlighting the significant economic contributions of women during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective often overlooked in traditional accounts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Their work reveals how colonial legal systems reinforced patriarchal structures, relegating women to marginal lands and limiting their economic agency (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This revision of historical narratives is complemented by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced higher per capita incomes than previously assumed before the 1980s, countering the notion of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Their analysis demonstrates that economic growth stemmed from internal developments and long-term trends as well as European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.18). Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that African real wages historically exceeded subsistence levels and showed upward trends, challenging the view that structural impediments caused enduring poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings collectively suggest that economic performance in Africa has been more dynamic and resilient than conventional wisdom acknowledges (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The revision of historical economic narratives in Africa thus provides a more nuanced understanding of the continent's economic trajectory, which can inform contemporary policy discussions on institutional reforms and development strategies.

Institutional frameworks have been identified as central to long-run economic growth, with a growing body of literature emphasizing their role in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of income per capita as illustrated by the contrasting institutional legacies left by European colonization in different regions. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who highlight how equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups rather than by economic efficiency or long-term growth. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in fostering or hindering development over time. Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through customary law, which marginalized women's economic contributions and access to land, thereby affecting broader economic trajectories. This suggests that historical and social contexts that influence economic opportunities also shape institutional arrangements alongside political elites. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) further elaborates on the interdependence of institutions within broader frameworks, arguing that institutions such as family systems, credit markets, and welfare structures interact in ways that influence economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) emphasize how historical events such as the slave trade have left lasting cultural impacts on trust and cooperation which in turn affect economic development. The disagreement between these perspectives lies in whether institutional quality alone is sufficient for growth or whether historical legacies such as mistrust must also be addressed. The empirical comparison of institutional legacies across former colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a measurement type that can help settle this disagreement by examining the long-term effects of different institutional frameworks on economic outcomes.
