The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are viewed as foundational in shaping economic outcomes, yet their causal mechanisms and scope remain contested. Some scholarship emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic performance, while others highlight the dynamic and context-dependent nature of institutional effects. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical trajectories, play a more significant role. This discussion is not merely academic; it influences how institutions are understood, designed, and reformed in the present. Institutions are seen as critical in fostering environments conducive to investment, innovation, and sustained development, yet their effectiveness may depend on historical legacies, social norms, and the interplay with other variables. The literature explores these dynamics through diverse lenses, including comparative historical analysis, econometric modeling, and case studies. This review synthesizes these streams, focusing on how institutions are conceptualized as drivers of growth and the conditions under which their impact is most pronounced. The analysis begins with the argument that institutions, particularly those established during colonial periods, have long-term effects on economic development, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and supported by subsequent empirical work. This perspective is contrasted with alternative views that emphasize the complexity and contingency of institutional effects, as seen in Austin (2008, p.25) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). These debates underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the role of constitutional rules in seventeenth-century England. This perspective is grounded in the mechanism of institutional persistence, where the type of institutions established during colonial periods whether extractive or inclusive shape long-term economic outcomes by influencing property rights political power and the rule of law. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate on this mechanism by showing how initial factor endowments interacted with institutional development to create divergent paths of economic growth across the New World. This interaction is particularly evident in the way institutions, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, often prioritize short-term rents over long-term growth, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note in their analysis of equilibrium institutions in former colonies. These findings underscore the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories, suggesting that the quality of institutions is a more decisive factor than external conditions in determining long-term growth. The implications of this institutional focus extend to the design of policies aimed at fostering sustainable development, where the creation of inclusive institutions is seen as a prerequisite for sustained economic progress.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on technological progress, institutional change, and historical trends, as seen in the interplay of knowledge diffusion and the Enlightenment's influence on economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights that long-term growth is driven by the advancement of technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it. A key mechanism identified across these works is the role of knowledge diffusion in fostering innovation, which in turn shapes economic trajectories. For instance, van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that formal institutions, such as guilds, played a significant role in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, although their effectiveness was sometimes undermined by local cartels. This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact is contingent on broader historical and technological contexts. Similarly, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, underscoring the importance of long-term historical trends in shaping economic outcomes. These findings collectively highlight the complex interplay between technological progress, institutional frameworks, and historical conditions in driving long-term economic growth.

This interplay is further illustrated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the transatlantic slave trade left a lasting legacy in sub-Saharan Africa by fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to impede economic development. This historical legacy operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law, but the former has a more pronounced effect (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Similarly, Frankema (2012, p.2) highlights that British colonial rule did not significantly improve the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting that colonial institutions may not have been as benign as some scholars have argued. These findings align with Nunn (2008, p.2), who shows that the long-term economic consequences of the slave trade were more significant than the relatively brief period of colonial rule. The persistent effects of historical legacies such as the slave trade and colonialism challenge the assumption that institutions alone drive long-run economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further complicate this picture by arguing that African real wages historically rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This suggests that historical legacies may interact with other factors, such as economic trends, to shape development outcomes. These insights underscore the importance of considering multiple historical forces in understanding economic growth.

This complexity is further explored by Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism is not a unidirectional process but one that simultaneously shapes the identities and structures of both the colonizer and the colonized, a perspective that challenges simplistic narratives of economic development (Stoler 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.25). This view is further supported by Austin (2008, p.25), who critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for compressing complex historical processes into a linear narrative of causation, thereby overlooking the dynamic and endogenous responses of societies to their environments. Austin highlights how African economies, for instance, adapted to external pressures through technological innovation and institutional change, suggesting that economic development is not solely a product of external forces but also of internal agency. This perspective aligns with Stoler’s emphasis on the internal political tensions within the colonial state, which reveal that colonial authority was not monolithic but shaped by class-based logics and power dynamics. These insights imply that understanding economic growth requires a more nuanced appreciation of how historical processes are mediated by local agency and power relations. Such an approach underscores the need to move beyond deterministic models of causation and instead consider the complex interplay of historical forces, institutional change, and local responses in shaping economic outcomes.

This nuanced understanding is further advanced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge prevailing historical narratives about Africa's economic performance and institutions, emphasizing the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that reveals a more complex and nuanced picture of economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) similarly argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, revising the view of persistent underdevelopment and demonstrating that economic growth was not solely a product of European colonial interventions. These findings collectively suggest that historical narratives about Africa's economic performance and institutions require reassessment, as they highlight the diversity of historical experiences and the need to recognize local agency and economic contributions that have been historically overlooked. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, marginalizing women in economic activities and land access, yet their contributions were significant despite these constraints. Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality, suggesting that other factors may be more influential in shaping economic outcomes. These insights imply that understanding long-run economic growth in Africa necessitates a re-examination of historical records and the recognition of the multifaceted nature of institutional development. Such a reassessment can inform more accurate and inclusive models of economic development that account for the diversity of historical experiences and institutional dynamics in Africa.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that differences in institutional quality, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinants of income per capita. Their analysis highlights how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with the latter fostering economic development in places like the United States and Australia. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that the slave trade contributed to a persistent culture of mistrust in Africa, negatively affecting economic development. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further nuanced by Austin (2008, p.25), who challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by emphasizing the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutional development in Africa. Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, underscoring the importance of recognizing diverse historical experiences in understanding institutional development. These insights collectively suggest that institutions are central to long-run economic growth, but their development is influenced by a complex interplay of historical, social, and economic factors. The debate over the determinants of institutional quality remains unresolved, as evidenced by the differing perspectives of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.25). This disagreement is settled by examining the historical evidence of institutional changes in former colonies, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which provides a detailed empirical comparison of institutional development across regions.
