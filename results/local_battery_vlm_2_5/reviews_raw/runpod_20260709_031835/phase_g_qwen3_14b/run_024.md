The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy design. At stake is the extent to which institutional quality, defined as the rules and norms that shape economic and political interactions, can explain divergent paths of economic development across countries and over time. A key debate concerns whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical events, play a more dominant role. This literature review explores this question by examining streams of scholarship that emphasize the causal role of institutions, while also acknowledging competing explanations and conditions under which institutional effects may vary. The first stream, led by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), argues that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, with a focus on how colonial legacies shaped institutional quality in former European colonies. This claim is supported by evidence showing that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, with significant consequences for economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) support these findings by suggesting that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes resulting from colonialism, and not geographic determinism. This stream highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic development, particularly through their influence on property rights, the rule of law, and political power structures.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who highlight the pivotal role of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes. This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions, which in turn influenced economic development through their impact on property rights, the rule of law, and political power structures. The mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are multifaceted, as demonstrated by North (1989, p.6), who emphasizes that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic expansion. These findings are further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), who show that the institutional reversal among former colonies is closely linked to historical patterns of urbanization and population density, which shaped the nature of institutions in place today. The implications of these institutional differences are long-lasting, as evidenced by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12), who note that divergent paths of economic development in the New World can be traced back to the early establishment of inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. These patterns underscore the enduring influence of institutions on economic trajectories, setting the stage for further exploration of how institutional quality interacts with other determinants of growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the historical legacy of the slave trade has had a persistent and significant impact on economic development in Africa, shaping cultural norms that continue to affect trust and cooperation today. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights that the slave trade, which lasted for nearly 500 years, had a more profound and enduring influence on economic outcomes than the relatively short period of colonial rule. The mechanisms through which the slave trade affected development include the creation of a culture of mistrust and increased ethnic fractionalization, as individuals were forced to rely on weapons for self-defense due to the environment of uncertainty and insecurity (Nunn 2008, p.4). These conditions hindered the development of social capital and institutional trust, which are crucial for long-term economic growth. Furthermore, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) demonstrate how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures and customary laws that marginalized women, contributing to economic stagnation by limiting their access to land and resources. These findings underscore the long-term economic consequences of historical legacies, which rival the role of institutions in explaining economic growth. Such legacies continue to shape economic trajectories, influencing the development of social norms and institutions in ways that persist into the present.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that long-term economic performance is shaped by historical trends, such as the decline in the skill premium in Western Europe during the late medieval period, which had lasting implications for economic development. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it. These findings suggest that historical trends and technological change play a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories over time. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.18) highlights that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, challenging the assumption that economic growth always leads to improved living standards. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) provide additional support for this observation by stating that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already occurring before 1800, showing that long-term economic performance is shaped by complex historical processes and not only by institutional factors. These insights collectively underscore the importance of considering historical trends and technological change as significant drivers of long-term economic performance, offering a perspective that complements but does not replace the role of institutions in economic development.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism creates both the colonizer and the colonized, requiring a reexamination of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority, a perspective that aligns with Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) assertion that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, better explain core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This shared finding highlights how institutional frameworks are shaped by power dynamics and social hierarchies, particularly in contexts where colonial and post-colonial legacies intersect. Stoler’s examination of racial ideology and class-based logic within colonial authority shows how these structures are imposed on the colonized and also adopted by the colonizers, which strengthens existing power imbalances. Ogilvie’s emphasis on the interdependency of institutions within a broader framework further supports this view, illustrating that economic growth is not driven by isolated institutional features but by complex, interrelated systems. This interdependency is evident in the ways colonial institutions influenced both economic and social structures in Europe and Africa, creating legacies that continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes. These insights underscore the importance of examining institutional legacies within their historical and social contexts, as they reveal how power relations and social hierarchies are embedded in economic systems. This understanding can inform further research on how historical institutions influence long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) reports, “If we are going to pursue cultural efﬁciency models, we need much more rigorous theories-and preferably also empirical ﬁndings-about which beliefs and values are economically beneﬁcial, in what ways, and whether they always coexist in the same culture.”

Reassessing historical economic narratives can help inform contemporary policy and development, which in turn may influence long-run economic growth, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues and supported by Frankema (2012, p.2). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding historical contexts to shape effective policies that address institutional shortcomings and promote sustainable development. Frankema (2012, p.2) further supports this by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, demonstrating that colonial legacies are complex and require nuanced interpretations to inform modern educational policies. These insights suggest that historical narratives, when critically assessed, can reveal the multifaceted nature of institutional development, influencing how policies are designed and implemented. This understanding can lead to more context-sensitive approaches in policy-making, which may enhance long-term economic performance. Such approaches recognize that historical legacies interact with contemporary challenges in ways that demand tailored solutions. This nuanced understanding of historical and institutional contexts can, therefore, play a pivotal role in shaping policies that foster inclusive and sustainable economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by multiple studies emphasizing their influence over geographic or other determinants (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The nature of these institutions, particularly their inclusiveness and effectiveness, has been shown to significantly impact economic performance, with historical legacies interacting with contemporary challenges in ways that demand tailored policy solutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The role of institutions is further underscored by the evidence that colonial legacies have led to institutional changes that continue to affect economic outcomes, as seen in the case of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). Historical narratives, when critically assessed, can reveal the multifaceted nature of institutional development, influencing how policies are designed and implemented (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).
