The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding the sources of prosperity and underdevelopment across nations. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure political, economic, and social interactions, are increasingly viewed as foundational to sustained economic progress. This perspective challenges earlier geographic determinism and emphasizes the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play a more prominent role. A key contention is whether institutions shape economic performance directly or through mediating variables such as trust, property rights, or human capital. This question is particularly salient in historical and comparative studies, where the legacy of colonialism, slavery, and other transformative events is seen as shaping institutional trajectories. The interplay between institutions and these historical forces offers a rich field for inquiry, with implications for policy and development strategies. Understanding this relationship is crucial for identifying pathways to inclusive and sustainable growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as they shape the rules and incentives that drive development, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment. The role of institutions is particularly evident in the contrasting outcomes of different colonial experiences, where extractive institutions in places like the Congo led to stagnation, while inclusive institutions in the United States promoted sustained growth. This mechanism is further elaborated by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who highlight how institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. North (1989, p.6) reinforces this by explaining how political institutions that specify efficient property rights reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. These insights collectively suggest that the nature of institutions directly influences the trajectory of economic development by shaping the incentives for investment and innovation. The persistence of extractive institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), can hinder long-term growth by entrenching the power of politically influential groups at the expense of broader economic development. This underscores the need for institutional reforms that align with the interests of the broader population to ensure sustained economic progress. The implications of these findings extend to contemporary policy, where the design of institutions remains a critical factor in determining economic outcomes.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism shaped not only the colonized but also the colonizers, creating complex power dynamics that influenced the formation of institutions and economic structures in the long run (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). This perspective highlights how colonial rule was not merely an external imposition but a process that redefined both European and indigenous social and economic systems. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.2) shows that British colonial policies in sub-Saharan Africa had lasting effects on the development of formal education, which in turn influenced the trajectory of economic growth in the region (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.17). These policies were shaped by a combination of ecological, religious, and political factors that interacted in ways that were not always aligned with the interests of the local population. Similarly, Simson (2019, p.11) highlights how post-independence budgetary policies in East Africa were influenced by the legacies of colonial rule, with spending patterns reflecting both historical constraints and new political priorities (Simson 2019, p.11; Simson 2019, p.19). These findings suggest that the institutional frameworks established during the colonial period continued to shape economic outcomes well into the post-colonial era, influencing the ability of states to develop effective governance structures and economic policies. This historical legacy underscores the importance of understanding how past institutional arrangements continue to influence contemporary economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the decline in the skill premium in Western Europe during the late medieval period was linked to the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, which played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic performance. This finding is supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who highlight the importance of urbanization and localization economies in shaping economic development, suggesting that institutional arrangements influenced the spatial distribution of economic activity. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) challenge conventional narratives about African economic history by showing that real wages in pre-colonial Africa were higher than subsistence levels and showed upward trends, indicating that economic performance was more complex than previously assumed. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) similarly reassess historical economic performance in Sub-Saharan Africa, arguing that higher per capita incomes existed before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment. These studies collectively reveal that reassessing historical economic performance involves revisiting data and challenging long-held assumptions about the role of institutions and economic development. This re-evaluation of historical data underscores the need to consider alternative explanations for economic outcomes, which can inform contemporary understandings of institutional influences on growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes in Africa, shaping development trajectories through persistent cultural and social mechanisms. Their findings suggest that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust, which continues to affect economic development negatively. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years and may have significantly influenced subsequent economic development. These historical processes, as shown by Peters (2004, p.5), were not merely static traditions but were actively shaped by colonial policies that sought to exclude certain groups from land ownership and economic participation. The interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes reveals a complex web of influences that extend beyond the immediate effects of colonialism. This understanding of historical legacies provides a foundation for examining how institutions evolve and interact with these deep-rooted social and economic patterns.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective that aligns with Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) emphasis on distributional conflicts as a key factor shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Both scholars highlight the importance of broader socio-institutional frameworks in driving economic development, suggesting that technological progress alone is insufficient without the necessary institutional and ideological adaptations. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further elaborates on this by pointing to the Enlightenment’s role in fostering an environment conducive to economic growth through the promotion of useful knowledge and rationality, which in turn supported the development of more efficient legal and economic systems. These interrelated factors, technological innovation, institutional adaptation, and ideological shifts, create a complex web of influences that shape long-term economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) underscores the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework, illustrating how economic growth in pre-industrial Europe was not the result of isolated institutional changes but rather the interaction of multiple systems, including family structures, credit markets, and labor arrangements. This interconnectedness implies that understanding economic growth requires examining the broader institutional and ideological context rather than focusing on individual components in isolation. The emphasis on these interdependencies highlights the need for a more holistic approach to analyzing the drivers of economic development.

The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature, with multiple studies emphasizing the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either encouraged investment and the rule of law or created extractive states, directly influencing long-term economic development. Similarly, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) underscores the interdependency of institutions, arguing that economic growth in pre-industrial Europe was not driven by isolated changes but by the interaction of multiple systems, such as family structures, credit markets, and labor arrangements. This interconnectedness suggests that a holistic approach is necessary to understand the drivers of economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further illustrate this by showing how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures and customary law, which had lasting impacts on women's roles in economic activities. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which negatively affects economic development, highlighting the persistent effects of historical institutions. These differing perspectives are grounded in empirical evidence, such as the analysis of institutional reversals in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36) and the impact of trust on economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence of institutional changes and their effects on economic growth, as demonstrated in comparative studies of pre-industrial Europe and African colonies, provides a clear basis for understanding the complex relationship between institutions and long-run economic development.
