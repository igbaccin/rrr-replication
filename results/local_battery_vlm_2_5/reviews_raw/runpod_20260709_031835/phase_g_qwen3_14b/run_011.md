The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of economic outcomes, surpassing other factors such as geography or culture. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic performance, suggesting that the quality of institutions shapes incentives, investment, and innovation. Other scholars suggest that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex and contingent, shaped by historical, cultural, and environmental factors. This debate emphasizes the importance of understanding both the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth and the conditions that determine how these mechanisms function. The discussion is further enriched by the recognition that institutions are not static, but can evolve in response to changing economic and social contexts. These insights underscore the importance of examining the interplay between institutions and other determinants of growth, and how this interplay varies across time and place. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing effective policies that foster sustainable economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Building on Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate that constitutional rules are essential in creating the institutional foundations necessary for sustained economic development. This perspective is reinforced by the observation that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either encouraged investment or favored extractive states, as seen in the cases of the United States and the Congo. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further highlight that institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World, showing that the security of property rights and other institutional features were critical in determining long-term growth trajectories. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and contributing to economic growth. These insights collectively suggest that the quality of institutions has a profound impact on economic outcomes, as they influence the ability of societies to sustain development over time. This understanding of institutions as a key driver of growth underscores the importance of designing policies that promote inclusive and effective governance structures.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge conventional historical narratives by demonstrating that African women played a significant role in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that has been largely overlooked in mainstream economic history. This revision of historical economic narratives is supported by evidence showing that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women in land access, yet their contributions to agricultural production and economic transformation were substantial. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) similarly argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously assumed before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment. Their analysis reveals that early economic histories of Africa often attributed growth to European colonial interventions, ignoring the continent's internal economic dynamics. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) contribute to this stream by showing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, challenging the assumption that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings collectively suggest that historical economic narratives have been shaped by biases and incomplete data, leading to an underestimation of African economic performance. This revision of historical perspectives highlights the importance of re-examining economic development through more inclusive and accurate historical records, which can inform contemporary policy discussions on economic growth and development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical events such as the slave trade have had lasting effects on long-term economic and social outcomes in Africa, with the slave trade contributing to a persistent culture of mistrust that negatively affects economic development. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the extensive duration of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, and their significant impact on subsequent economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trade influences economic outcomes involves the creation of an environment of uncertainty and insecurity, which necessitated individual investment in weapons for self-defense and contributed to high levels of ethnic fractionalization. This, in turn, has implications for economic growth, as ethnic fractionalization is known to be negatively correlated with long-term economic development. Peters (2004, p.5) adds to this discussion by examining land competition in sub-Saharan Africa, revealing that exclusion and class formation, rather than adaptive customary systems, shaped economic and social structures. These findings collectively underscore the enduring legacy of historical events in shaping contemporary economic conditions, suggesting that understanding these legacies is essential for addressing current developmental challenges. This historical perspective sets the stage for examining how institutions, shaped by such legacies, influence economic growth in the present.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective that aligns with van Zanden (2009, p.19) in emphasizing the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks. This interplay is evident in the historical development of guilds in western Europe, which, while fostering the transfer of skills, also exhibited tendencies toward forming local cartels, thus complicating the relationship between institutional structures and economic outcomes. Similarly, Mokyr (2005, p.3) highlights the role of the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality in generating modern economic growth, suggesting that ideological shifts are as crucial as technological and institutional changes. This broader view is supported by van Zanden (1999, p.18), who notes that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living, underscoring the complexity of factors influencing long-term growth. Stern (2004, p.1) further contributes to this discussion by challenging the environmental Kuznets curve, suggesting that statistical models may need revision to reflect more accurately the multifaceted determinants of economic growth. These findings collectively illustrate that economic growth is shaped by a combination of technological, institutional, and ideological factors, each interacting in complex ways to influence long-term development trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa provides crucial insights for policy-making and institutional reforms, a perspective supported by the broader recognition that historical analysis can inform contemporary economic strategies (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This stream emphasizes that understanding the evolution of social institutions and their relationship with economic development is essential for designing effective policies. For instance, Austin (2008, p.25) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing historical processes, suggesting that African responses to environmental challenges were dynamic and partly endogenous (Austin 2008, p.25). This view aligns with the argument that institutional changes in Europe were influenced by the profits from Atlantic trade, which enabled the commercial bourgeoisie to demand and obtain institutional reforms that protected their property rights (Austin 2008, p.23). These findings collectively suggest that historical insights can guide policy reforms by revealing the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. This understanding can inform contemporary efforts to build resilient and adaptive institutions that support sustained economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how different colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes that significantly influenced economic development. This is further supported by the findings of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, shaped by historical events such as the slave trade, remains a critical factor in economic development. These insights underscore the importance of institutions in fostering environments conducive to sustained growth. The relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is also evident in the work of Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who emphasize the historical contributions of African women to economic development, demonstrating that inclusive institutions can emerge from diverse historical contexts. The role of institutions involves complexity, as Austin (2008, p.25) points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis might oversimplify the dynamic interaction between institutions and economic growth by condensing historical processes. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. The historical evidence on the impact of colonial institutions, as analyzed by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a measurable framework for comparing institutional legacies across regions. This empirical comparison helps clarify the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run growth, offering a concrete basis for evaluating their role in economic development.
