The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical patterns of prosperity and underdevelopment. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary causal factor in economic outcomes, or whether other variables such as geography, culture, or historical events play a more dominant role. This literature explores how different institutional frameworks influence economic trajectories, particularly in the context of former colonies, and how these frameworks interact with other factors. Some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions, while others highlight the importance of endogenous responses to historical shocks or the complex interplay between institutions and other social forces. This review synthesizes these perspectives, focusing on the ways in which institutions shape economic growth through their influence on property rights, governance, and social norms. It also considers the limitations and contingencies of institutional explanations, as well as the potential for alternative factors to mediate their effects. The discussion begins with the foundational argument that institutions are the primary determinant of long-term economic outcomes, as presented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19). Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering economic commitment and growth. This perspective is grounded in the idea that institutions shape the incentives and constraints under which individuals and groups operate, influencing the trajectory of economic development. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups who prioritize their own rents over long-term growth. This dynamic is evident in the historical experience of former European colonies where institutional designs often served the interests of colonizers and did not promote inclusive development. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) discusses how political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. These insights underscore the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further note that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were influenced by institutional development and initial factor endowments, reinforcing the central claim that institutions are pivotal in driving long-run economic growth.

This perspective is further complicated by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) who argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have shaped long-term institutional and economic outcomes in Africa, offering an alternative explanation for economic development. This perspective is supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights the prolonged impact of Africa's slave trades on subsequent economic development, noting that these trades lasted for nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of official colonial rule. This mistrust is compounded by the historical context of insecurity and uncertainty, which led individuals to require weapons for self-defense, potentially contributing to the high levels of ethnic fractionalization observed today (Nunn 2008, p.4). These findings underscore the enduring effects of historical events on contemporary economic and social structures in Africa. Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that the impact of colonial rule on the origins of formal education was not more benign, suggesting that historical legacies continue to influence institutional development. This stream of research emphasizes that historical factors such as the slave trade and colonialism have left deep imprints on the institutional and economic landscapes of Africa, shaping development trajectories in ways that extend beyond immediate economic policies.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments that it demands, highlighting the interplay between technological progress and institutional evolution as a key driver of long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who discusses the role of formal institutions, such as guilds, in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, although their potential to form local cartels may have limited their overall impact. The emphasis on technological change and skill distribution is further reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who attributes modern economic growth to the Enlightenment's focus on useful knowledge and rationality, suggesting that intellectual progress is a crucial enabler of economic transformation. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) add that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, pointing to the early emergence of economic differences that shaped subsequent growth trajectories. These findings collectively indicate that long-term economic performance is shaped by a combination of technological innovation, skill distribution, and historical divergence, with institutions playing a mediating rather than a primary role. This insight provides a foundation for understanding how different factors interact to influence economic development over time.

Austin (2008, p.25) argues that institutional explanations of economic growth are often oversimplified, emphasizing the need for more nuanced historical and institutional analyses, a perspective supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.30); (Austin 2008, p.25). This critique highlights that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is not static but is shaped by complex historical dynamics and endogenous responses to environmental and economic conditions. These insights underscore the importance of considering the interdependencies between institutions and broader historical and social contexts. Such a perspective implies that institutional analysis must be embedded within a more comprehensive framework that accounts for the multifaceted nature of economic development. This approach sets the stage for examining how different institutional frameworks interact to influence long-term economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge simplistic historical narratives of African economic development by highlighting the significant and often overlooked contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as the ways in which colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures through customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings align with broader efforts to revise earlier assumptions about African economic history, such as those by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who argue that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, contradicting the view of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This stream emphasizes the need to consider more complex and nuanced patterns of economic development, including the role of women in agricultural production and the impact of colonial legal systems on land access and economic opportunities. Such insights suggest that historical narratives of African economic development must account for both the agency of local actors and the unintended consequences of colonial policies. These contributions underscore the importance of re-evaluating long-standing assumptions about African economic history and the factors that have shaped its trajectory. This perspective sets the stage for further exploration of how institutional frameworks interact with economic development across different regions and historical contexts.

Institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the extensive body of research on this topic. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial times significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions leading to lower levels of economic development. This is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight the persistent effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, on current levels of trust and economic development in Africa. These findings underscore the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the over-simplification of causation in the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggesting that historical narratives need to account for the dynamic and partly endogenous responses of local actors. This perspective is echoed by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who emphasize the significant contributions of African women to economic growth, which have been historically overlooked. The debate over the role of institutions in economic development is thus settled by the empirical evidence presented in these studies, which show that both the type of institutions and the agency of local actors significantly influence economic outcomes.
