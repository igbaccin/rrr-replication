The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions are viewed as foundational in shaping economic outcomes, but their exact mechanisms and conditions of effectiveness remain contested. Some scholars emphasize the direct influence of institutional quality on growth, while others highlight the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, such as historical legacies, cultural norms, and external shocks. This literature review explores these perspectives by examining streams of scholarship that address the causal role of institutions in economic development. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or if they interact with other variables in ways that complicate their influence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are central to long-run economic success, as evidenced by the contrasting outcomes of former colonies. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups can be extractive, which may hinder economic development despite their formal existence. These claims underscore the importance of institutional design and its alignment with long-term growth objectives. They highlight how the type of institutions established during colonial times significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. For instance, in regions where institutions were designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, long-term economic success followed. In regions such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive institutions were established, economic development faced significant challenges. This pattern is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering commitment and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. Their analysis shows that the success of England's economic development was rooted in the institutional changes that allowed for credible commitments and effective enforcement of rules. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) note that institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World. These findings collectively underscore the critical role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as they provide the necessary framework for sustained growth and development. This understanding of institutional influence sets the stage for examining how these frameworks interact with other factors such as cultural norms and historical legacies.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that institutions that guarantee fair execution of contracts shape long-term economic performance through historical trends and institutional changes, as illustrated by the emergence of guilds in western Europe, which provided structured mechanisms for skill transmission and contract enforcement (van Zanden 2009, p.19); this perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights the interplay between technological advancement and institutional adjustments in driving sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between institutional development and technological progress is reinforced by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who underscores the role of Enlightenment-era intellectual shifts in fostering a climate conducive to economic expansion through the promotion of law, order, and enforceable contracts (Mokyr 2005, p.3). These findings collectively suggest that the evolution of institutions over time, particularly those that ensure fair and efficient contract execution, is a critical determinant of long-term economic performance. This understanding of institutional influence sets the stage for examining how these frameworks interact with other factors such as technological innovation and intellectual movements.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical events such as the slave trade have left enduring legacies that significantly shape long-term economic outcomes in Africa, particularly through the cultivation of mistrust among populations. This perspective is reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial-era legal systems codified the subordinate status of women, contributing to persistent inequalities that may hinder economic development. These findings suggest that historical legacies, especially those rooted in colonial rule and the slave trade, have created institutional frameworks that continue to influence social and economic dynamics. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.2) emphasizes the extensive duration and impact of Africa's slave trades, linking them to adverse long-term economic consequences. Similarly, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British colonial rule did not foster the development of formal education in a more benign manner, which may have limited human capital accumulation. These interconnected legacies of mistrust, gender inequality, and limited educational development demonstrate how historical events have influenced institutional trajectories that impact contemporary economic performance. Such institutional legacies, in turn, may influence the effectiveness of modern governance and economic policies, suggesting that addressing these historical imprints is crucial for fostering sustainable development.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism creates both the colonizer and the colonized, requiring a reexamination of European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority, a perspective that aligns with Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) emphasis on the interdependencies of economic institutions. Colonial and institutional legacies shape economic and social structures in Europe and beyond, as demonstrated by the ways in which colonial authority was maintained through a complex interplay of racial ideology and class-based logic, which in turn influenced the development of local institutions. For instance, Stoler highlights how racist ideology and the protection of European women were not merely justifications for colonial rule but were integral to maintaining internal hierarchies within the colonial state, reinforcing the power dynamics that shaped both the colonizers and the colonized. Similarly, Ogilvie points to the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, such as the European family system and its connections to labor markets, credit markets, and collective welfare systems, which contributed to early industrialization. These interdependent institutional frameworks suggest that the legacy of colonial rule may have embedded structures that continue to influence economic and social development in post-colonial societies. Such legacies, in turn, may affect the ability of modern institutions to function effectively, underscoring the need to understand how historical institutional configurations shape contemporary economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) reports, “If we are going to pursue cultural efﬁciency models, we need much more rigorous theories-and preferably also empirical ﬁndings-about which beliefs and values are economically beneﬁcial, in what ways, and whether they always coexist in the same culture.”

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that reassessing African economic history can provide crucial insights for policy-making and institutional reforms, a perspective supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who challenge the assumption that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced persistent underdevelopment before the 1980s. This stream highlights the importance of reinterpreting historical economic performance to inform contemporary growth strategies, noting that earlier narratives often underestimated pre-colonial and early colonial economic activity. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) challenge the view that African real wages were consistently low, presenting evidence that wages rose over time and were historically higher than subsistence levels. These findings suggest that economic policies informed by a more accurate understanding of historical trends may better address the structural challenges facing African economies today. This nuanced view of Africa’s economic history can guide the design of more effective institutional frameworks that build on historical strengths rather than assuming a uniform legacy of underdevelopment.

This is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight how historical events such as the slave trade have left lasting cultural impacts that affect trust and, consequently, economic development. These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering environments conducive to sustained economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) contribute to this discussion by showing how colonial policies, particularly the codification of customary law, reinforced patriarchal structures that marginalized women, thereby affecting economic productivity. This perspective is complemented by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who argues that economic institutions are interdependent and must be studied within broader frameworks to understand their impact on growth. While Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus on the long-term consequences of colonial institutions, Hopkins (2009, p.18) points to the complexity of institutional development in Africa, noting that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily lead to poor institutions. The disagreement between these perspectives is settled by the empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across different regions, as seen in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), which shows that various measures of institutions consistently correlate with economic performance, reinforcing the central role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth.
