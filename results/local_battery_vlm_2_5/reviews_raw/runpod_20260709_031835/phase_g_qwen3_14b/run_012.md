The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical understanding. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that structure economic and social interactions, are increasingly seen as pivotal in shaping economic trajectories. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of growth or if other factors such as geography, culture, and historical legacies play a more significant role. This literature review explores these dynamics, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are the primary determinant of income per capita, as evidenced by the contrasting outcomes in countries like the United States and the Congo. Their findings are supported by additional measures of institutions, including property rights and the efficiency of the judiciary (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These claims highlight the importance of institutional quality in fostering economic growth, a theme that resonates across multiple studies. This focus on institutions as a causal variable sets the stage for further exploration of their antecedents and interactions with other factors. Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that institutions shaped by colonial legacies and the slave trade are central to long-run economic growth, as these historical forces directly influenced the development of governance structures and social norms. Colonial powers established institutions that either promoted broad-based economic development or entrenched extractive systems, with the former seen in settler colonies like the United States and the latter in regions such as the Congo. These institutional legacies persisted over time, shaping the trajectory of economic performance in former colonies. Nunn (2008, p.2) further underscores that the transatlantic slave trade had a lasting negative impact on economic development in Africa, contributing to persistent ethnic fractionalization and mistrust, which in turn hindered the formation of effective institutions. This historical legacy of mistrust and fragmented social cohesion, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), continues to affect economic outcomes by limiting cooperation and investment. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) complement these findings by showing how initial factor endowments and institutional development in the New World led to divergent economic paths, with institutional quality serving as a key determinant of long-term growth. These insights collectively highlight the enduring role of institutions, shaped by colonial and post-colonial forces, in shaping economic development. This focus on institutional legacies sets the stage for examining how these structures interact with other factors such as geography and human capital in shaping economic outcomes.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth relies on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, highlighting the interdependence between technological progress and institutional evolution (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective aligns with van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in fostering the intergenerational transfer of skills, even though these institutions sometimes acted as local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These insights are further supported by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who argues that the Enlightenment's focus on useful knowledge and rationality laid the institutional groundwork for modern economic growth, emphasizing the importance of law, order, and enforceable contracts in facilitating efficient resource allocation (Mokyr 2005, p.3). The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is also evident in Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21), who show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, suggesting that institutional differences were a significant factor in this process (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). These findings collectively illustrate how institutional adjustments have historically influenced economic performance, setting the stage for further exploration of how these adjustments interact with other factors such as technological innovation and policy reforms in driving long-term growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutional frameworks are central to economic growth, as demonstrated by their analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the development of enforceable political institutions facilitated long-term economic expansion. Ogilvie (2007, p.30) complements this perspective by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, noting that these conflicts played a central role in determining institutional outcomes in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the value of considering institutional frameworks as interconnected systems, as demonstrated through her analysis of family structures, labor markets, and credit markets in pre-industrial England. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on how the evolution of political institutions that define property rights and enforce economic rules reduces transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth. This mechanism, however, is nuanced by Ogilvie’s (2007, p.30) critique of cultural efficiency models, which questions whether beliefs and values alone can explain economic outcomes, pointing instead to the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutional development. These findings collectively suggest that the interplay between constitutional rules, distributional conflicts, and institutional efficiency forms a critical lens for understanding economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of how these factors interact with technological and policy-driven changes.

Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa did not have a uniquely benign impact on the origins of formal education, a claim supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial policies reinforced patriarchal structures that marginalized women in economic and legal systems. These findings highlight the influence of colonial-era institutional frameworks on long-term economic development, as colonial rule affected the distribution of land and resources while also establishing gendered hierarchies that continued to shape economic opportunities. Peters (2004, p.5) demonstrates that land competition during colonial rule reflected exclusionary practices, reinforcing the view that institutional development in Africa was shaped by exclusion and class formation. Simson (2019, p.11) observes a similar pattern in post-independence budget policies in East Africa, noting that these policies were shaped by complex interactions between colonial legacies and post-independence governance. These sources collectively suggest that the interplay between colonial institutions, post-independence policies, and social structures has been a critical determinant of economic growth in Africa. This interplay sets the stage for further exploration of how these factors interact with broader economic and technological transformations.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the extensive body of research examining their influence across different regions and historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions leading to lower levels of development compared to inclusive ones (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes caused by colonialism, rather than geographic determinism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions is further explored in the context of Africa, where Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of understanding the evolution of social institutions and their relationship with economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is complemented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate that the slave trade has had a lasting impact on intergroup trust, which in turn affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) provide a nuanced view of how institutions interact with social structures, showing that customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal norms, which limited the economic opportunities available to women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Ogilvie (2007, p.30) emphasizes the interdependencies among various institutions, suggesting that economic growth is better understood through the lens of entire institutional frameworks rather than isolated institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

The evidence consistently shows that institutions are a key determinant of economic growth, with inclusive institutions promoting long-term development. There remains a debate about the extent to which historical legacies, such as the slave trade, continue to influence current economic outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade's impact on trust is a significant factor (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4), while Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus more on the type of institutions established during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This disagreement is settled by examining the empirical evidence on the relationship between institutions and economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who show that institutional quality is a strong predictor of long-term economic outcomes.
