The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central concern in development economics, with significant implications for understanding the divergent paths of economic development across countries. Institutions are viewed as critical determinants of economic outcomes, shaping the incentives and constraints that influence investment, innovation, and governance. The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain contested as different streams of scholarship emphasize distinct causal pathways and empirical contexts. Some argue that institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce the rule of law, are the primary drivers of sustained economic growth, while others highlight the importance of historical and cultural factors that interact with institutional frameworks. The debate underscores the need to disentangle the complex interplay between institutions and other variables, such as geography, culture, and technology, in shaping economic trajectories. This review explores key streams of scholarship that address these questions, focusing on how institutions influence growth through various channels and under different historical and contextual conditions. The discussion begins with the seminal work of Acemoglu et al. Building on these insights, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by the broader institutional analysis in their 2002 work (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonial strategies led to the establishment of very different institutional frameworks in various regions, with some fostering long-term development and others entrenching extractive systems. The persistence of these institutional legacies is a key mechanism through which initial conditions shape long-run economic outcomes. This institutional path dependence is further illustrated by the role of disease environments in shaping the trajectory of institutional development, as shown in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36). The broader implication of these findings is that the quality of institutions is not merely a byproduct of economic development but a foundational element that drives it. This understanding sets the stage for examining how specific institutional features, such as the rule of law and property rights, interact with other determinants of growth in different historical and geographical contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical legacies such as the slave trade have had enduring effects on economic development in Africa, with the slave trade contributing to a persistent culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2; Peters 2004, p.5). Their analysis reveals that the impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, with the internal channel being at least twice as significant as the external one (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These legacies are not merely static remnants of the past but continue to influence social and economic structures in ways that affect development trajectories. The persistence of mistrust and the exclusionary effects of historical land competition illustrate how deep-seated historical conditions can shape the long-term economic prospects of regions, offering a critical perspective on the mechanisms through which institutions and social norms evolve.

These findings suggest that the historical narratives surrounding colonial impacts are more complex than previously assumed as colonial rule affected the structures of power and also influenced economic practices and social roles. Frankema (2012, p.2) contributes to this discussion by examining the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, emphasizing that British colonial rule was not more benign in its impact than other forms of colonial governance (Frankema 2012, p.2). This adds another layer to the understanding of colonial impacts, showing that the effects of colonialism on education were influenced by a variety of factors, including malaria ecology and the extent of native African resistance. These insights collectively indicate that a more nuanced approach is required to fully understand the long-term economic and social consequences of colonial rule, as the impacts are multifaceted and often intertwined with local conditions and historical contexts.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on technological advancement and also on the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it highlighting the interplay between innovation and the frameworks that support its implementation (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective is echoed in van Zanden (2009, p.19), who notes that the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in facilitating the transfer of skills and ensuring fair labor practices, even though these institutions were sometimes criticized for limiting market competition (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Similarly, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the assumption that Sub-Saharan Africa was uniformly underdeveloped before the 1980s, suggesting that economic performance in the region was more complex and historically varied than previously believed (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These findings collectively indicate that long-term economic performance is shaped by a combination of technological progress, institutional structures, and historical contexts, with each factor influencing the others in complex ways. Mokyr (2005, p.3) further argues that the Enlightenment's promotion of useful knowledge and rationality laid the groundwork for modern economic growth, reinforcing the idea that intellectual and institutional developments are closely intertwined (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This interplay between knowledge, institutions, and economic performance underscores the need for a more integrated approach to understanding long-term economic trends.

Institutional frameworks play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how different colonial strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either promoted or hindered economic development. These findings are reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who demonstrate that trust, influenced by historical events such as the slave trade, is a significant determinant of economic outcomes. The importance of institutions is further underscored by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who argue that constitutional rules and commitments are essential for fostering economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The extent to which institutions alone drive growth remains a subject of debate as illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) who emphasize the role of gender dynamics and customary law in shaping economic opportunities in Africa. This disagreement is grounded in the differing historical contexts and institutional legacies examined in these studies. The measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic performance, as seen in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), provides a critical empirical comparison that highlights the complexity of institutional effects on growth.
