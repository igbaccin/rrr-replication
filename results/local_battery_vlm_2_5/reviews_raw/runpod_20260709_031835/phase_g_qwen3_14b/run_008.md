The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth is a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy design and historical interpretation. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are argued by some scholars to be the primary determinant of economic outcomes, while others propose alternative explanations that challenge or complicate this causal link. The debate hinges on whether institutions operate as a foundational cause of growth or whether other factors, such as geography, cultural norms, or historical legacies, play a more direct or mediating role. This review explores these competing streams of scholarship, focusing on how institutions are conceptualized and their relationship to economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are the primary drivers of income per capita, as demonstrated by the contrasting colonial strategies in regions like the United States and the Congo. This perspective is supported by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who highlight the persistent effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, on economic development through their influence on trust and institutions. These findings indicate that institutions play a significant role and are closely tied to historical processes, influencing long-term economic outcomes in intricate ways.

Their work highlights how European colonization led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, with the former, as seen in the United States, fostering economic growth through the rule of law and investment incentives, while the latter, as in the Congo, hindered development by prioritizing resource extraction over long-term institutional stability. These findings are further corroborated by Nunn (2008, p.2), who emphasizes the long-term adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, linking them to increased ethnic fractionalization and reduced economic opportunities. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that initial factor endowments and institutional development were critical in shaping divergent economic paths in the New World, with societies that developed more inclusive institutions experiencing sustained growth. These insights collectively suggest that the legacy of historical institutions continues to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of understanding their long-term implications for development.

North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutional frameworks are essential for economic development, but their role is mediated by broader ideological and technological factors. North (1989, p.6) emphasizes that the evolution of political institutions, particularly those defining property rights and enforcing rules, reduces transaction costs and facilitates economic specialization. This process, however, is not self-sustaining; Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that sustained growth depends on the interplay between technological progress and the institutional and ideological adjustments required to harness it. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.4) notes that technological innovations generate economic surplus, which in turn fuels further research and development, creating a feedback loop that drives growth. This perspective aligns with Mokyr (2005, p.3), who argues that the Enlightenment's focus on useful knowledge and rationality laid the ideological groundwork for modern economic growth. These insights collectively suggest that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader context of technological and ideological change. This interplay underscores the complexity of economic development and the need to consider multiple factors in explaining long-term growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that historical economic performance and income levels are influenced by factors such as real wages and urban development, in addition to institutions, a perspective supported by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), who emphasize the role of urbanization in shaping economic development. This stream emphasizes that the relationship between economic growth and institutional frameworks is not straightforward, as historical data show that other variables, such as wage levels and urban structures, play a significant role in determining long-term economic outcomes. For example, van Zanden (1999, p.18) finds that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800, suggesting that economic performance is influenced by a complex set of factors beyond institutional arrangements. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, indicating that structural impediments may not have been the sole cause of persistent poverty. These findings collectively suggest that while institutions are important, they are not the only determinants of long-term economic growth, and that a broader understanding of historical economic performance requires considering multiple factors, including real wages and urban development. This perspective opens the door to further exploration of how these variables interact with institutional frameworks to shape economic outcomes.

Peters (2004, p.5) and Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argue that distributional conflicts and post-colonial institutional legacies significantly shape long-run economic growth in Africa, offering a perspective that emphasizes the role of power dynamics and historical inequalities over institutional efficiency alone. This view is supported by the observation that land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation, in addition to adaptive customary systems, as seen in the tensions between colonial authorities and local populations over agricultural practices (Peters 2004, p.5). Frankema (2012, p.2) adds that the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was not necessarily benign, pointing to complex interactions between colonial policies and local conditions that shaped educational development (Frankema 2012, p.2). These findings collectively suggest that long-run economic growth in Africa is influenced by historical power imbalances and the legacies of colonial rule, which continue to affect institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. This perspective opens the door to further exploration of how these historical legacies interact with contemporary economic policies and social dynamics to shape development trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that historical narratives and colonial legacies in Africa offer critical insights for understanding institutional reforms and economic growth, challenging the dominance of institutional quality as the sole driver of long-run development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Stoler 1989, p.6). These narratives reveal how colonial policies and their legacies shaped the structure of institutions as well as the social and economic conditions that continue to exist today. For instance, the imposition of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, marginalizing women and limiting their access to land and economic opportunities, which in turn affected broader patterns of development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Similarly, colonial authority was not merely imposed from above but was also shaped by internal class dynamics and racial ideologies within the colonial state, complicating the understanding of how institutions evolved in Africa (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). These historical legacies continue to influence contemporary economic policies and institutional frameworks, suggesting that long-run growth in Africa cannot be fully explained without considering the complex interplay between historical contexts and institutional reforms. This perspective highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how historical narratives shape current economic trajectories, offering an alternative lens through which to view the role of institutions in economic development.

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic policies, particularly in regions such as Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The role of institutions is not only about their presence but also about their nature, as shown by the distinction between extractive and inclusive institutions, which have different impacts on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Historical legacies, such as those from colonial rule, have had lasting effects on the institutional frameworks of former colonies, influencing their economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.36). These legacies are not static; they evolve and interact with other factors such as cultural norms and economic interests, which can either reinforce or challenge the existing institutional structures (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical narratives that shape current economic trajectories are complex and multifaceted, involving the legacy of colonialism as well as the roles played by different social groups and their interactions with institutions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The persistence of certain cultural traits, such as mistrust stemming from the slave trade, further complicates the relationship between institutions and economic growth, showing how historical events can have long-lasting effects on social capital and trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The synthesis of these perspectives highlights the importance of understanding the historical evolution of institutions and their interactions with other social and economic factors in explaining long-run economic growth. This understanding is crucial for developing effective policies that take into account the complex historical and institutional contexts of different regions.
