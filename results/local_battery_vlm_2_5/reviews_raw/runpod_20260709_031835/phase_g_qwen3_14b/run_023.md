The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central issue in development economics, with competing perspectives on how and why institutions matter. Some scholars emphasize the enduring impact of institutional frameworks established during colonial periods, arguing that these frameworks continue to shape economic outcomes today. Others highlight the dynamic and endogenous nature of institutions, suggesting that their evolution is influenced by a range of factors, including historical events, cultural norms, and economic opportunities. This intellectual tension reflects a broader debate over whether institutions are primarily exogenous determinants of growth or if they are shaped by internal and external forces over time. The question of how institutions influence economic development is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy design and development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions affect growth can inform efforts to foster inclusive and sustainable economic transformation. This review explores these debates by examining key streams of scholarship that have contributed to our understanding of the causal relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth. These streams offer different perspectives on the role of institutions, some of which challenge or complement the core arguments made by leading scholars in the field. Each stream is analyzed to reveal the nuances of the arguments and the evidence that supports them. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering commitment and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. These institutional differences, shaped by the actions of politically powerful groups, have long-term implications for economic development, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even if they do not encourage economic development. This suggests that the design of institutions is crucial in determining the trajectory of economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) also contribute to this understanding by pointing out that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments. These findings collectively underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, a theme that continues to be explored in subsequent analyses of economic growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that institutional developments in western Europe, such as the emergence of guilds, played a significant role in shaping long-term economic performance by facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills, even though these institutions were sometimes criticized for restricting market competition. This finding aligns with Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on technological advancement and on the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that support it. The historical evolution of institutions, therefore, appears to be a crucial mechanism through which long-term economic performance is shaped. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) further contribute to this understanding by showing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, suggesting that institutional changes were an early driver of economic performance differences. These insights collectively indicate that the trajectory of economic development is closely linked to the historical evolution of institutions, which in turn influence the capacity for sustained growth. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes thus emerges as a persistent theme across different historical periods and regions, underlining their importance in driving long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have long-term economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a perspective supported by Frankema (2012, p.2). This finding aligns with Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development, with the impact being significant enough to influence current levels of GDP per capita. These insights collectively indicate that the economic consequences of historical events such as the slave trade are substantial and persistent, shaping the trajectory of economic development in ways that are as significant as institutional factors. This understanding of historical legacies thus provides an important perspective on the drivers of long-run economic growth that extends beyond the traditional focus on institutions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) challenges the assumption that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic outcomes, a perspective supported by Austin (2008, p.25) in his critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Both argue that historical narratives are more central to explaining economic institutions than previously assumed, as they reveal how economic actors' responses to their environments and power dynamics influence institutional development. For instance, Austin highlights how African societies adapted to their circumstances, demonstrating that economic growth is determined by external factors and by internal responses and strategies. Similarly, Ogilvie points to the interdependencies among institutions, suggesting that the focus on individual institutions may overlook the broader frameworks that shape economic outcomes. This perspective is further reinforced by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who emphasize the historical contributions of African women to economic development, showing that institutional frameworks are shaped by social and gendered dynamics. These insights collectively suggest that economic institutions are the product of complex interactions among various social, economic, and historical forces. This understanding of institutions as dynamic and context-dependent offers a more nuanced perspective on the drivers of long-run economic growth that can inform future research and policy.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the extensive empirical and theoretical work in the field. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the differences in economic outcomes across countries can be largely attributed to the type of institutions that were established, with extractive institutions leading to lower levels of development compared to inclusive ones. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how institutional frameworks are influenced by social and gendered dynamics, showing that institutions are shaped by historical and cultural contexts. These insights collectively suggest that the nature of institutions is a key determinant of long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the view that institutions are the sole determinant of economic development, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing history. This disagreement is settled by the empirical evidence presented in Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), which demonstrates that various measures of institutions consistently show a strong correlation with economic outcomes, reinforcing the importance of institutional quality in driving long-run growth.
