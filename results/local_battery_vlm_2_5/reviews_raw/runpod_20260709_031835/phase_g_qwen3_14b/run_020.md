The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. Institutions, defined as the rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that shape economic and social interactions, are posited by some scholars as the primary determinant of economic outcomes, while others emphasize the importance of historical contingency, distributional conflicts, and alternative causal factors. This debate hinges on whether institutions are exogenous forces that shape economic trajectories or endogenous responses to broader historical and environmental conditions. The discussion is further complicated by the varying degrees to which institutions are seen as static or dynamic, and whether their influence is universal or context-specific. This literature review explores these competing perspectives, focusing on how different streams of scholarship conceptualize the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth. The first stream argues that institutions are the primary causal variable in long-run economic development, with their type and quality directly determining growth outcomes. The second stream challenges this view by highlighting the role of historical and environmental factors, suggesting that institutions are shaped by these conditions and are not the sole drivers of growth. The third stream introduces contingency conditions, identifying when institutional effects are most pronounced and when they may be mitigated by other factors. These streams collectively underscore the complexity of the institutional-growth relationship, revealing both its centrality and its limitations in explaining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are the primary determinant of economic growth. This claim is supported by the observation that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of vastly different institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. For instance, in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were designed to promote broad-based economic participation and enforce property rights, whereas in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, institutions were extractive and aimed at transferring resources to the metropole. These differences in institutional design have had lasting effects on economic development, as evidenced by the divergent paths of growth among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups that prioritize their own interests over long-run growth. This suggests that the persistence of extractive institutions can hinder economic development even in the presence of favorable geographic conditions. The implications of these findings highlight the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic outcomes, as demonstrated by the varying degrees of success in economic growth across different regions. These insights underscore the need for policies that promote inclusive and effective institutions to drive sustained economic growth.

Institutions that guarantee fair execution of contracts are central to shaping long-term economic performance, as demonstrated by van Zanden (2009, p.19) and supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1). The role of such institutions is particularly evident in their ability to facilitate the transmission of technological progress and the accumulation of human capital, both of which are essential for sustained economic growth. These institutions, however, were not without their drawbacks, as they sometimes attempted to restrict entry into trades and maintain artificially high wages, which could hinder broader economic efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Similarly, Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends not only on technological innovation but also on the institutional and ideological adjustments that support its implementation. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in enabling the effective deployment of technological advancements and fostering an environment conducive to long-term economic performance. The implications of these findings highlight the need for institutional arrangements that ensure fair contract execution and promote the broader conditions necessary for sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have persistent economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2). Their research highlights how the slave trade contributed to a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to negatively affect economic development. This mistrust results from direct experiences with the slave trade and persists through internal norms that shape social interactions and economic behaviors. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further show that both internal and external channels explain the effects of the slave trade on trust, with the internal channel being at least twice as significant as the external one. This suggests that historical legacies influence economic outcomes through deep-seated social norms and not just through institutional frameworks. Frankema (2012, p.17) complements this by demonstrating how colonial legacies, such as the impact of British rule on formal education, also have long-term economic consequences. These findings underscore the importance of understanding how historical events shape economic development through mechanisms that extend beyond institutional arrangements. This perspective opens new avenues for examining how historical legacies interact with institutional frameworks in shaping economic growth.

This recognition of historical influence leads to Ogilvie (2007, p.30) arguing that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, better explain the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, a perspective supported by Austin (2008, p.25) who critiques the over-simplification of historical causation in economic development models. Ogilvie highlights the interdependency of institutions within broader frameworks, suggesting that the European family system was linked to labor markets, credit markets, and collective welfare systems, all of which contributed to early industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). These perspectives suggest that economic institutions are not solely determined by their efficiency or by historical legacies, but by the complex interplay of power dynamics and social structures. This view implies that understanding economic development requires examining the distributional conflicts that shape institutional evolution and not focusing exclusively on institutional quality or historical determinism. Such an approach opens new pathways for analyzing how economic institutions emerge and change in response to shifting power relations and societal needs.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that African women played a significant role in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging historical narratives that have overlooked their contributions. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), who highlight that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously assumed before the 1980s, suggesting that economic development was not solely shaped by European colonial interventions. These findings reveal that economic growth in Africa was influenced by a variety of factors, including the roles of women in agricultural production and the existence of economic activity prior to colonial rule. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further contribute by demonstrating that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and increased over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. This implies that economic institutions in Africa may have supported growth in ways that are not fully captured by traditional analyses focused on colonial legacies. Simson (2019, p.11) adds that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not uniquely clientelist, showing that economic decisions were shaped by a complex interplay of factors beyond patronage. These insights collectively suggest that understanding long-run economic growth in Africa requires a more nuanced approach that accounts for diverse historical and institutional contexts.

Institutional quality is a central determinant of long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who emphasize that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had lasting effects on economic development. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, shaped the trajectory of growth by influencing property rights, the rule of law, and the ability of individuals and firms to invest and innovate. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, a key component of institutional effectiveness, plays a significant role in economic development and is influenced by historical events such as the slave trade. The persistence of mistrust in regions affected by the slave trade highlights how institutions can shape economic outcomes over time. However, Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the simplicity of these institutional narratives, arguing that historical contexts are more dynamic and endogenous than suggested by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. He points to the African experience as evidence that institutions and economic growth are interwoven in complex ways that may not be fully captured by static models. The debate over the role of institutions in growth is thus settled by empirical comparisons of institutional frameworks across regions and time periods, as seen in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), who use measures of property rights and political constraints to show how institutional changes correlate with economic outcomes.
