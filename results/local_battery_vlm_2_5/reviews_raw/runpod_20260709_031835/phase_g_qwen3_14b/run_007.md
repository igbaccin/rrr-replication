The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. A key intellectual debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or historical events, play a more decisive role. This question is particularly salient in understanding the divergent trajectories of former colonial states, where institutional legacies are often cited as critical to contemporary economic performance. Some scholars emphasize the causal power of institutions in shaping economic development, arguing that inclusive institutions foster growth by promoting property rights, the rule of law, and effective governance. Others propose rival explanations, highlighting the influence of geographic conditions, historical events like the slave trade, or cultural factors as alternative determinants of economic outcomes. These competing perspectives underscore the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, necessitating a nuanced synthesis of evidence from multiple streams of research. This review explores these streams, beginning with the argument that institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, as demonstrated by empirical analyses of colonial legacies and their long-term effects. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

This claim is reinforced by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the importance of constitutional rules and institutional commitment in seventeenth-century England for fostering economic growth. A key mechanism through which institutions drive economic development is by shaping the distribution of political power and the incentives of ruling elites. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, as politically powerful groups often design them to maximize their own rents rather than promote long-term growth. This insight highlights a critical condition for institutional effectiveness: the need for mechanisms that align the interests of elites with broader economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further demonstrate that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional arrangements on economic outcomes. These findings collectively suggest that the quality and inclusiveness of institutions are central to understanding long-run growth, setting the stage for examining how specific institutional features, such as property rights and political accountability, contribute to sustained economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and necessary institutional and ideological adjustments, a perspective reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of formal institutions such as guilds in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills in Western Europe. These institutional arrangements, while beneficial for skill development, also faced criticism for potentially restricting market competition and maintaining artificially high wages. This dual role of institutions underscores their complex influence on economic development, as they can both enable and hinder growth depending on their design and implementation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further demonstrate that institutional and policy changes can significantly impact economic performance, as seen in Sub-Saharan Africa, where earlier assumptions of persistent underdevelopment have been challenged by evidence of higher pre-1980s per capita incomes. This suggests that the presence of supportive institutions and the ability to adapt to changing economic conditions are crucial for long-term growth. The interplay between institutional quality and economic outcomes is thus a recurring theme across these studies, illustrating how institutions shape the trajectory of economic development over time. These findings set the stage for examining how specific institutional features, such as the balance between regulation and competition, influence economic performance in different historical contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that historical legacies, such as the transatlantic slave trade, have had a lasting impact on economic development in Africa by fostering a culture of mistrust and ethnic fragmentation. This perspective is supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who highlights how colonial legacies, including the imposition of formal education systems, have shaped the trajectory of economic development in sub-Saharan Africa. These historical processes have created persistent challenges in building cohesive institutions that support long-term growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further elaborate on the mechanisms through which historical events influence contemporary economic outcomes, emphasizing the role of trust in economic development. This is reinforced by Nunn (2008, p.2), who notes that the long duration of the slave trade compared to colonial rule underscores its profound and enduring impact on African economies. The implications of these historical legacies extend beyond economic outcomes, influencing social structures and political institutions. Peters (2004, p.5) points to the ways in which land competition and exclusion during the colonial period contributed to class formation, shaping the economic landscape in ways that persist today. These findings collectively illustrate how historical legacies continue to influence the institutional and economic development of regions, setting the stage for further exploration of how these legacies interact with other factors in shaping long-term growth.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism shapes both the colonizer and the colonized, creating complex social and economic structures that persist into the post-colonial era (Stoler 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who highlight how colonial legal systems reinforced patriarchal norms and marginalized women's roles in economic production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These mechanisms illustrate how colonial institutions embedded specific power relations that continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. For example, the dual legal system established during colonial rule reinforced male dominance and organized access to land and resources in a manner that restricted women's economic autonomy. Similarly, Simson (2019, p.11) notes how post-independence budget policies in East Africa reflected the legacies of colonial fiscal practices, with high debt burdens and military spending shaping the trajectory of economic development (Simson 2019, p.11). These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on the economic and social fabric of African nations. The persistence of these legacies suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires examining the historical roots of institutional arrangements. This theme can be further explored in relation to how contemporary reforms interact with these deep-seated structures.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the enduring influence of colonial-era structures on contemporary economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of extractive institutions, established during the colonial period, continues to affect economic outcomes in former colonies, underscoring the long-term consequences of institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These findings align with evidence showing that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The relationship between historical institutional structures and current reforms continues to be a topic of debate as noted by the new economic history of Africa, which underscores the significance of examining how institutions change over time (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The role of institutions in economic development is further complicated by the presence of cultural and social factors, such as trust and ethnic fragmentation, which can influence the effectiveness of institutional reforms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The historical evidence on the impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust provides a specific empirical comparison that highlights the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical evidence, combined with the analysis of institutional changes in former colonies, offers a comprehensive understanding of how institutions influence long-run economic growth.
