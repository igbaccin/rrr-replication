The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy interventions. Institutions are often framed as foundational to economic performance, yet their causal mechanisms and contextual conditions remain contested. Some scholarship emphasizes the direct influence of institutional quality on growth, while others highlight the interplay between institutions and other factors such as culture, geography, and historical legacies. This review explores these streams of scholarship, beginning with the argument that institutions, particularly those promoting the rule of law and property rights, are the primary determinants of economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that differences in colonial institutions, such as those established in the United States compared to extractive states in Africa, are central to explaining divergent economic trajectories. Supporting this, they note that various measures of institutional quality, including property rights and judicial efficiency, consistently yield similar results in explaining income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic growth, setting the stage for further exploration of their interdependencies and historical contexts. Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Their analysis highlights how the type of institutions established during colonization, whether extractive or inclusive, directly shaped economic outcomes, with inclusive institutions fostering sustained growth and extractive ones hindering it. This mechanism is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate how historical events, such as the transatlantic slave trade, have left enduring effects on social trust and, consequently, economic development. These findings underscore how institutions are shaped by historical processes and act as conduits for transmitting long-term developmental consequences. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) expand on this by showing how initial factor endowments interacted with institutional development to produce divergent economic trajectories in the New World. Their work illustrates that institutions are not static; they evolve in response to historical conditions, yet their impact on economic growth remains profound. This interplay between institutions and historical contexts reinforces the argument that institutions are central to understanding long-run economic development. These insights lay the groundwork for examining how institutions interact with other factors, such as cultural norms, to shape economic outcomes over time.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth hinges on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective reinforced by North (1989, p.6), who highlights the role of political and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes. This interplay between institutional frameworks and technological progress is further elaborated by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who argues that distributional conflicts better explain the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe than economic efficiency. These insights underscore the importance of institutional and ideological foundations in facilitating long-term economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate how constitutional rules and institutions were crucial in fostering commitment and economic growth in seventeenth-century England, demonstrating that the effectiveness of institutions in enforcing and changing rules is a key determinant of economic success. Mokyr (2005, p.3) adds that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality played a pivotal role in the origins of modern economic growth, linking intellectual developments to institutional changes that supported economic expansion. These findings collectively suggest that institutional and ideological adjustments are integral to the process of sustained economic growth. This understanding sets the stage for examining how these adjustments interact with other factors, such as cultural norms, to influence economic outcomes over time.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that colonialism created both the colonizer and the colonized, highlighting the need to reexamine European class cultures and internal structures of colonial authority, a perspective supported by Peters (2004, p.5), who shows how land competition in sub-Saharan Africa reveals exclusion and class formation rather than adaptive customary systems. Colonial rule reshaped economic and social institutions in ways that had lasting effects on long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the codification of customary law under colonial administration, which entrenched patriarchal alliances and reinforced the subordinate status of women, as demonstrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20). These institutional changes were part of broader processes that shaped post-colonial economic trajectories, as seen in the post-independence budget policies in East Africa, where colonial legacies influenced the allocation of resources and the persistence of certain economic practices. The dual legal system introduced during colonial rule, which privileged European courts over native courts, contributed to the development of institutions that favored certain groups and hindered inclusive economic growth. This institutional legacy continued to influence economic outcomes in the post-colonial period, as seen in the patterns of expenditure and the distribution of resources in countries like Kenya and Tanzania. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, shaping the trajectory of growth in ways that continue to be felt today.

Frankema (2012, p.2) challenges the assumption that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more benign impact on the origins of formal education, highlighting the complex and often detrimental effects of colonial institutions on long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who argue that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings suggest that historical economic performance in Africa cannot be fully explained by the legacy of colonial institutions alone, but must also consider the resilience and adaptability of local economic systems. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further contributes to this reassessment by demonstrating that the skill premium in western Europe fell significantly in the late medieval period, which may have influenced long-term economic performance in ways previously underestimated. This challenges the notion that European economic development was solely driven by institutional superiority, suggesting instead that a variety of factors, including ecological and social conditions, played a significant role. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) add to this discussion by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the view of persistent underdevelopment and suggesting that economic performance in the region has been more dynamic than traditionally assumed. These studies collectively highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of historical economic performance, one that accounts for a wide range of factors beyond institutional legacies.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who emphasize that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that the institutional changes caused by colonialism explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. These studies highlight that institutions, rather than geographic or other factors, are the primary determinants of economic development. However, the role of institutions is not limited to the colonial era; Hopkins (2009, p.18) points out that the quality of institutions in Africa is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, but also by other factors such as economic interests and religious affiliations. This suggests that the development of institutions is a complex process influenced by multiple factors. The historical evidence from Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) further illustrates how institutional changes, such as the codification of customary law under colonial rule, affected economic opportunities for women. These findings collectively show that institutions are a crucial factor in economic growth, but their development is shaped by a range of historical and social contexts. The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks in different regions, as discussed by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), provides a broader understanding of how institutions interact within wider economic systems. This synthesis underscores the need to consider the multifaceted nature of institutional development in explaining economic growth.
