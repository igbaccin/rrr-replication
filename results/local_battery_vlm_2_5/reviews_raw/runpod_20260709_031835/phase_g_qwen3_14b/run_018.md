The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy design. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that shape economic and social interactions, are widely argued to influence growth by affecting incentives, property rights, and the quality of governance. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play a more fundamental role. This literature review examines the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, focusing on how different streams of scholarship frame and evidence this connection. Some studies emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions on modern economic performance, while others highlight the dynamic and context-dependent nature of institutional effects. Still others explore how institutions interact with other variables, such as trust and cultural norms, to shape growth outcomes. These perspectives converge on the idea that institutions matter, but they diverge on the mechanisms and conditions under which they operate. This review begins by examining the argument that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run economic growth, as articulated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19). Austin (2008, p.25) reports, “I share AJR's rejection of what they call the 'simple geography' thesis, of unchanging environmental obstacles to economic development.”

This claim is supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who highlight the importance of constitutional rules and institutions in fostering credible commitments that underpin economic growth in seventeenth-century England. A key mechanism through which institutions drive growth is by shaping the incentives of individuals and groups within an economy. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even if they do not encourage economic development, because they are often designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. This underscores the importance of institutional quality in determining whether economic policies lead to sustained development. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates on how the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs, contributing to economic growth. These insights collectively illustrate that the nature and quality of institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This understanding sets the stage for examining how institutions interact with other factors such as technology and cultural norms to influence growth.

This perspective, however, is expanded upon by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) challenge conventional historical narratives by highlighting the significant economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that has been historically neglected. Their analysis reveals that colonial rule entrenched patriarchal structures through customary law, marginalizing women in land access and economic participation, yet also notes their resilience and agency in driving agricultural and economic transformations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) similarly revise historical interpretations by demonstrating that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced higher per capita incomes than previously assumed before the 1980s, countering the view of persistent underdevelopment. They argue that economic growth in regions such as West Africa was influenced by a range of factors, including internal developments and innovations, in addition to European colonial interventions. These findings collectively suggest that long-run economic growth has been shaped by a complex interplay of local and external forces, often overlooked in traditional accounts. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) contribute to this revision by showing that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and increased over time, challenging the assumption that structural impediments caused prolonged poverty. This stream of scholarship thus provides a more nuanced understanding of economic history, emphasizing the importance of re-evaluating past economic conditions and the roles of various actors in shaping growth trajectories.

This perspective is reinforced by Simson (2019, p.11), who demonstrates that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not uniquely clientelist, as the allocation of resources reflected broader economic and political conditions rather than singularly patronage-driven motives. Both authors emphasize that economic development is shaped by dynamic interactions between institutions, policies, and external factors, rather than by fixed or deterministic frameworks. For instance, Austin (2008, p.25) highlights how African societies historically adapted to their environments using available technologies, challenging static views of institutional constraints. These findings suggest that economic history must be understood through a lens that accounts for the complexity and adaptability of human responses to institutional and environmental conditions. This nuanced understanding of historical processes underscores the need for further research into how institutions evolve in response to changing economic and political landscapes.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights how institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe, such as guilds, played a critical role in shaping economic development by regulating labor markets and ensuring the fair execution of contracts. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.30), who emphasizes that distributional conflicts, rather than economic efficiency, were central to the formation of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. These findings suggest that the interplay between institutional structures and broader socio-economic conditions was essential in determining long-term economic outcomes. For instance, van Zanden (1999, p.18) demonstrates that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living, challenging assumptions about the direct link between growth and well-being. This complexity is further illustrated by Mokyr (2005, p.3), who argues that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality laid the intellectual foundation for modern economic growth. These insights collectively indicate that long-term economic development is shaped by a multifaceted interplay of institutional, technological, and ideological factors, each influencing the trajectory of economic progress in distinct ways. This nuanced understanding of historical development underscores the importance of examining institutional evolution within its broader socio-economic and cultural context.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that the historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa has had a persistent negative impact on economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic progress. This legacy is reflected in the erosion of intergroup trust as well as in the broader socio-economic structures that have emerged in its wake. Their findings align with Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights that the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, significantly shaped the continent's subsequent economic trajectory. The mechanisms through which these historical legacies operate are multifaceted, as evidenced by the internal and external channels of influence identified by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), which underscore the importance of both individual norms and institutional environments. Furthermore, Frankema (2012, p.2) contributes to this understanding by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, revealing that colonial policies did not necessarily promote benign educational development. These insights collectively suggest that historical legacies, such as the slave trade and colonial rule, have left enduring imprints on the socio-economic fabric of Africa, influencing contemporary patterns of development and trust. This understanding of historical legacies provides a crucial foundation for examining the broader structural factors that shape long-term economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the analysis of historical legacies and their influence on contemporary development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight how colonial policies established either extractive or inclusive institutions, with the former, such as those in the Congo, leading to persistent underdevelopment. This underscores the idea that institutions, rather than geography, are the primary determinant of economic outcomes. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade has left a lasting impact on trust levels in Africa, which in turn affects economic development. These findings align with the broader argument that institutions influence long-term growth by shaping trust and economic interactions. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the simplicity of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by suggesting that historical narratives are more dynamic and endogenous than previously assumed. This introduces a nuanced perspective on how institutions evolve and interact with local contexts. The disagreement between Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.25) is resolved by examining the specific historical evidence of institutional changes in former colonies, as detailed in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which provides empirical measures of institutional development and its relationship with economic outcomes. This comparative analysis highlights the complexity of institutional impacts on growth and the need for context-specific understanding.
