The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical interpretation. A key debate revolves around whether institutions are the primary causal force behind economic outcomes or if other factors, such as geography, colonial legacies, or cultural norms, play a more prominent role. Some scholars emphasize the enduring influence of institutional frameworks, while others highlight the dynamic and context-specific nature of institutional effects. This literature review explores these perspectives, focusing on streams of scholarship that either challenge, complement, or refine the institutionalist narrative. One stream argues that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who show how different colonization strategies led to divergent institutional outcomes in former colonies. Another stream, however, challenges the simplicity of this view, suggesting that historical processes and local responses are more complex than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis implies (Austin 2008, p.25). These discussions underscore the contested nature of institutional causality and its implications for understanding long-term economic development.

This perspective is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who show how the slave trade contributed to persistent cultural mistrust in Africa, which in turn negatively affects economic development. These findings highlight the enduring impact of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further support this view by emphasizing how institutional development and initial factor endowments shaped divergent paths of economic development in the New World, with differences in property rights, corruption, and social capital playing a central role. Nunn (2008, p.2) provides additional evidence by examining how Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on long-term economic development, linking the legacy of these trades to high levels of ethnic fractionalization and subsequent economic stagnation. These studies collectively underscore the importance of institutions formed during historical events, such as colonialism and the slave trade, in shaping long-run economic trajectories. This focus on institutional legacies provides a foundation for understanding how historical processes continue to influence economic development today.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective supported by North (1989, p.3), who highlights the role of political and economic institutions in shaping economic growth. This interplay between institutions and technological progress is further illustrated by the complex relationships among various institutional frameworks, as Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes, emphasizing that individual institutions are interdependent within a broader system, which may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutions are crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England, where the degree of commitment to institutional rules significantly influenced economic outcomes. These insights are complemented by Mokyr (2005, p.54), who points to the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality as a key driver of modern economic growth, linking intellectual developments to economic progress. The interdependence of institutions, technology, and ideology is thus a recurring theme in understanding long-run economic growth, with each element influencing and being influenced by the others in a dynamic process. This interconnectedness underscores the need for a holistic approach to studying economic development, which considers the multifaceted nature of institutional evolution and its impact on growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) challenges the assumption that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more benign impact on the origins of formal education, arguing instead that it did not significantly differ from other colonial powers in this regard (Frankema 2012, p.2). This perspective aligns with broader efforts to reassess historical economic performance and development, as seen in van Zanden (1999, p.18), who questions the link between economic development and improvements in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.18). These studies highlight the need to reconsider how institutions and economic performance have been historically understood. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) contribute to this reassessment by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the narrative of persistent underdevelopment (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This revision of historical narratives suggests that economic performance and development may be influenced by factors other than institutions alone. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further supports this by showing that the skill premium in western Europe declined during the late medieval period, which may have contributed to long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These findings collectively suggest that a more nuanced understanding of historical economic performance is necessary, one that moves beyond the conventional focus on institutions as the primary driver of long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) argue that historical narratives and colonial legacies significantly shape long-run economic growth by influencing institutional development and social structures, as demonstrated through their analysis of the role of customary law in reinforcing patriarchal hierarchies during the colonial era (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Stoler 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Colonial rule introduced legal systems that codified the subordinate status of women, relegating them to marginal lands for food farming while reinforcing male dominance in the economic and legal spheres (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Stoler (1989, p.6) further highlights how colonialism created complex power dynamics that influenced both the colonizer and the colonized, shaping class-based logics that maintained colonial authority and structured relations of production and power (Stoler 1989, p.6; Stoler 1989, p.4). These findings suggest that colonial legacies act as active forces that shape the trajectory of economic development through their influence on institutions and social hierarchies. This perspective opens new avenues for understanding how historical agency and colonial impacts continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutions play a central role in driving long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, with significant implications for economic development. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, shaped by historical events such as the slave trade, is a crucial factor for economic development. These findings underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over time. Austin (2008, p.25) challenges the simplicity of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that historical narratives need to be more nuanced to capture the dynamic and endogenous nature of economic growth. This divergence in perspectives is further illustrated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20), who emphasize the role of gender and customary law in shaping economic structures during the colonial period. The historical evidence of how institutions evolved in response to colonial rule, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), provides a comparative framework for evaluating these different claims. By examining the long-term effects of institutional changes, such as those documented in the context of African economies, we can better understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth.
