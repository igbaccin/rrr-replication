The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for policy and historical understanding. Institutions are viewed as foundational to shaping economic outcomes, yet debates persist about their specific mechanisms and the extent to which they operate independently of other factors. A key intellectual challenge lies in disentangling the causal influence of institutions from alternative explanations such as geography, culture, or colonial legacies. This review explores how different streams of scholarship address this issue, focusing on the ways in which institutions are theorized to influence economic growth. One stream emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic development, arguing that inclusive institutions foster sustained growth by ensuring property rights and the rule of law. Another stream investigates how historical legacies, such as the slave trade or colonial rule, shape institutions and, by extension, economic outcomes. A third stream explores the interdependencies among institutions and the broader frameworks within which they function. These perspectives highlight the complexity of institutional effects and the need to consider both their direct and indirect influences on economic growth. This discussion sets the stage for a deeper exploration of these streams and their contributions to understanding the role of institutions in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) report, “Moreover, if we compare the point estimates to estimates using the same specification and sample, but not including the interethnic slave exports control (which are −0.104, −0.126 and −0.107, respectively), we find that the point estimates are barely affected by the inclusion of the control.”

This perspective is reinforced by North (1989, p.6), who emphasizes that political and economic institutions significantly influence growth by shaping transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. The development of institutions that secure property rights and reduce expropriation risks is a key mechanism through which long-run growth is achieved, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who highlight how equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents of politically powerful groups rather than promote long-term development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further note that divergent paths of economic development in the New World were shaped by institutional development and initial factor endowments, with the security of property rights and structures of the financial sector playing a central role. These findings collectively suggest that institutions are foundational to economic growth, as they influence the incentives for investment, the efficiency of markets, and the distribution of power within societies. The persistence of institutional legacies from the colonial period underscores the long-term impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes. This emphasis on institutions as a primary determinant of growth provides a framework for understanding the historical divergence in economic performance across countries.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical legacies such as the slave trade have persistent economic consequences that rival the role of institutions in explaining long-run economic growth, a claim supported by Frankema (2012, p.2) who highlights the enduring impact of colonial rule on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. These legacies manifest through mechanisms such as the erosion of trust and the entrenchment of ethnic divisions, which have long-term effects on economic performance. Similarly, Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the prolonged duration of Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a more significant impact on economic development than the shorter period of official colonial rule. This suggests that historical events can shape economic outcomes through deep and lasting social and institutional pathways. Frankema (2012, p.17) further illustrates how colonial policies influenced the structure of education systems, which in turn affected human capital formation and economic opportunities. These findings collectively indicate that historical legacies, such as the slave trade and colonialism, have enduring economic consequences that are as significant as, if not more than, the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.3) argues that the Enlightenment's emphasis on useful knowledge and rationality played a crucial role in the emergence of modern economic growth, a perspective that aligns with the broader view that historical trends and technological change shape long-term economic performance. This stream highlights how the diffusion of knowledge and the institutional support for innovation were integral to sustained growth, as seen in the development of scientific societies and the increasing emphasis on applied research during the eighteenth century (Mokyr 2005, p.3; van Zanden 2009, p.19). Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that economic growth is fundamentally tied to technological advancement and the institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, suggesting that the capacity to supply diverse goods to a population depends on these evolving dynamics (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further illustrates how institutions such as guilds influenced the intergenerational transfer of skills, although their effectiveness was sometimes limited by their tendency to restrict market entry and maintain artificially high wages. These findings collectively suggest that while institutions are important, their role is mediated by broader historical and technological trends that underpin long-term economic performance. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks thus emerges as a key mechanism in shaping economic outcomes over time.

Ogilvie (2007, p.30) argues that distributional conflicts and political structures significantly shape institutional formation, offering an alternative explanation for long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.30); this perspective is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering credible commitments that underpin economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These insights highlight how institutions are not merely the product of economic efficiency but are deeply embedded in the power dynamics and political frameworks that govern societies. For example, Ogilvie (2007, p.30) explains how interdependencies among institutions such as the European family system with labor markets and credit systems form a complex network of interactions that affect economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that the failure of institutional frameworks in France, compared to England, stemmed from unresolved contradictions that undermined long-term economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These findings suggest that the effectiveness of institutions is closely tied to their alignment with prevailing political and social structures. This alignment, or lack thereof, shapes the capacity of institutions to support sustained economic growth. Such insights open the door to examining how post-colonial institutions in Africa further reflect these dynamics, as seen in the analysis of budgetary policies and their implications for economic development (Simson 2019, p.11).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the conventional narrative that Sub-Saharan Africa experienced persistent underdevelopment by presenting evidence that per capita incomes were higher than previously assumed before the 1980s (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This revision of historical economic narratives is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who demonstrate that real wages in African colonies were historically above subsistence levels and showed upward trends over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). These findings suggest that economic performance in Africa was more favorable than previously thought, and that structural impediments may not have been as pervasive as often assumed. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.21) contribute to this stream by arguing that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, offering a broader perspective on how economic histories have been misinterpreted (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.21). Their analysis of wage trends and urban development highlights the importance of reevaluating long-standing assumptions about economic stagnation in non-European regions. This stream collectively underscores the need to reassess historical economic data and the implications of such reassessments for understanding contemporary economic conditions. These revised narratives have significant implications for how institutions are perceived as drivers of long-run growth, suggesting that historical misinterpretations may have distorted the understanding of institutional impacts. This reconsideration of economic history opens new avenues for examining the interplay between institutions and growth in contexts previously thought to be structurally disadvantaged.

Institutional frameworks play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the extensive body of research examining their influence across different historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are the primary determinants of income per capita, emphasizing the importance of institutional quality over other factors such as geography. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that constitutional rules and institutions are crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth, as demonstrated by the case of seventeenth-century England, where institutional stability supported economic development. These findings are reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who show that trust, which is influenced by historical events such as the slave trade, is a significant factor in economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) also notes that the interdependence of institutions within a broader framework makes analysis more complex, indicating that institutions must be examined within their broader context. This interdependency raises questions about the extent to which institutional changes can be attributed to specific factors, as Frankema (2012, p.17) notes that historical and ecological factors can also shape institutional development. The empirical comparison of institutional frameworks across regions, as seen in Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), provides a measurement type that can help settle these disagreements by examining the relationship between historical conditions and institutional outcomes.
