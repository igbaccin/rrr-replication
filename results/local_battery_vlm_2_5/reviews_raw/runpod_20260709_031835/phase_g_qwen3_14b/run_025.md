The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. The central issue in this debate concerns the degree to which institutional quality, defined by property rights, rule of law, and political constraints, influences economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic performance, while others emphasize the importance of historical, cultural, and geographic factors as alternative explanations. This review explores these competing perspectives by examining key contributions from the literature. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita, citing the divergent colonization strategies of Europeans as evidence of how institutional frameworks shape economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.25) questions the simplicity of this 'reversal of fortune' thesis, proposing that historical narratives are more dynamic and endogenous than suggested by Acemoglu and colleagues (Austin 2008, p.25). These competing views set the stage for a deeper exploration of how institutions interact with other forces to influence economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a claim supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who emphasize the role of constitutional rules in fostering credible commitments essential for long-run development. This perspective is reinforced by the observation that European colonization strategies led to divergent institutional frameworks, which in turn shaped economic outcomes across former colonies. In regions such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established to encourage economic growth by protecting property rights and minimizing expropriation risks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These differences in institutional design have long-term implications for economic performance, as demonstrated by the persistent disparities in income levels between countries with extractive and inclusive institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further highlight how initial factor endowments and institutional development interact to shape economic trajectories, underscoring the importance of secure property rights and effective governance structures in promoting sustained growth. These insights together indicate that institutional quality is a key factor in economic outcomes, affecting both initial conditions and the long-term development trajectories of nations.

This understanding of institutional quality naturally leads to Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) arguing that historical narratives of African economic development have overlooked the significant contributions of women, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging the conventional view of underdevelopment. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that women were actively involved in economic activities, such as farming and land cultivation, despite being marginalized by colonial legal systems (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). These findings contribute to a broader revision of African economic history by highlighting the role of gender dynamics in shaping economic outcomes. Furthermore, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the assumption that economic growth was solely a result of European colonial interventions. This revision of historical narratives underscores the complexity of African economic development and the need to reconsider long-standing assumptions about the continent's economic trajectory. Such insights enhance our understanding of the past and also contribute to current discussions on economic growth and institutional development. These contributions illustrate that revising historical narratives can provide new perspectives on the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, which can be further explored in subsequent analyses.

Such overlooked contributions prompt Stoler (1989, p.6) to highlight the complex and multifaceted legacies of colonialism, emphasizing the need for nuanced historical analysis to understand its long-term effects, a perspective supported by Austin (2008, p.25) in his critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. This body of scholarship highlights the complex interaction between colonial institutions and local responses, showing how colonial rule influenced both the colonized and the colonizers. Frankema (2012, p.2) further contributes by examining the impact of British colonial rule on the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, revealing that colonial policies did not necessarily lead to more benign educational outcomes. These analyses collectively suggest that the legacies of colonialism are deeply embedded in the institutional frameworks that continue to influence economic trajectories. Simson (2019, p.11) adds to this by exploring post-independence budget policies in East Africa, demonstrating that these policies were not uniquely clientelist but reflected broader economic and political dynamics. The interplay between colonial and post-colonial institutions thus reveals a complex web of influences that shape long-run economic growth, necessitating a deeper understanding of these historical processes.

This nuanced perspective aligns with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) arguing that historical events such as the slave trade have shaped long-term economic outcomes in Africa, with the slave trade contributing to a persistent culture of mistrust that negatively affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This finding is supported by evidence showing that the slave trade's impact on trust operates through both internal norms of mistrust and external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, with the internal channel being at least twice as significant (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Nunn 2008, p.2). Peters further elaborates on the historical legacies of land competition in sub-Saharan Africa, demonstrating how colonial-era policies and perceptions of African landholding practices fostered exclusion and class formation, which continue to influence contemporary economic structures (Peters 2004, p.5). These historical processes are linked to broader patterns of economic underdevelopment, as the slave trade's legacy of ethnic fractionalization is associated with reduced long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). Such legacies are not only cultural but also institutional, shaping the frameworks within which economic activity is organized today. The interplay between these historical forces and their economic consequences highlights the enduring influence of past events on present-day development trajectories.

These historical insights are further elaborated by Mokyr (2005, p.3) arguing that long-term economic performance is shaped by technological change, skill premiums, and ideological shifts, as demonstrated by the historical interplay between knowledge diffusion and institutional evolution (Mokyr 2005, p.3; van Zanden 2009, p.19). The role of technological innovation in driving economic growth is underscored by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who emphasizes that sustained growth depends on advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments it demands (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These findings suggest that the spread of useful knowledge, as emphasized during the Enlightenment, played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories (Mokyr 2005, p.3). The interplay between technological progress and skill distribution is also reflected in van Zanden’s (1999, p.18) analysis of wage trends, which highlights the complex relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.18). These insights collectively indicate that long-term economic performance is not solely determined by institutional factors but is also deeply influenced by technological and ideological transformations. This perspective opens the door to examining how these forces interact with institutional frameworks in shaping development outcomes.

This interplay between knowledge and institutions invites Ogilvie (2007, p.30) to challenge the traditional narrative that economic efficiency is the primary driver of institutional development, arguing instead that distributional conflicts shape the evolution of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.30). This perspective is supported by the recognition that institutions do not operate in isolation but are interdependent within broader frameworks, as seen in the European family system’s relationship with labor and credit markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Stern (2004, p.1) further expands this alternative explanation by questioning the environmental Kuznets curve’s statistical validity and proposing new models that better capture the complex relationship between economic development and environmental outcomes (Stern 2004, p.1). These models highlight the need to consider broader systemic factors that influence both institutional and environmental trajectories. Stern’s critique of the EKC underscores the importance of revisiting assumptions about how economic development interacts with environmental and social factors, offering a more nuanced approach to modeling long-term growth (Stern 2004, p.6). These insights collectively emphasize that economic institutions and growth are shaped by a range of interacting forces beyond traditional efficiency-based explanations, setting the stage for further exploration of how these forces influence development outcomes.

Institutional frameworks significantly influence long-run economic growth by shaping the conditions under which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that the type of institutions established during colonization, such as those enforcing the rule of law or extractive states, directly affects economic outcomes, as seen in the contrast between the United States and the Congo. This is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who emphasizes the interdependence of institutions within a broader framework, suggesting that analyzing single institutions in isolation may fail to capture the full impact on economic development. These insights underscore the importance of institutional quality in fostering sustainable growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize their own rents rather than promoting long-term growth. This view is echoed by Austin (2008, p.25), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing historical processes, suggesting a more dynamic understanding of how institutions evolve over time. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth thus centers on whether they are primarily shaped by external forces or internal dynamics. This disagreement is settled by the empirical evidence provided by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.36), which uses econometric analysis to show the relationship between historical population density and current institutional quality, reinforcing the argument that institutional changes are a key driver of long-term economic outcomes.
