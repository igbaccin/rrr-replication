The role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth remains a central question in development economics, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy challenges. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules, norms, and organizations that structure economic and social interactions, are increasingly viewed as pivotal in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective challenges earlier geographic or resource-based explanations, emphasizing instead the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on growth. A key debate centers on whether institutions primarily function as enablers of economic activity or as reflections of power dynamics that may hinder development. This question is particularly salient in historical contexts where colonial legacies have left lasting institutional imprints. The literature explores these dynamics through various lenses, including the comparative analysis of colonial institutions, the interplay between trust and economic development, and the role of legal systems in shaping economic opportunities. These streams of research collectively highlight the complex and multifaceted nature of institutional influence on economic growth. They underscore the need to understand institutions not in isolation but within broader historical and social contexts. This understanding is crucial for developing effective policies that aim to foster sustainable economic development. The following sections examine these streams in greater detail, beginning with the analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Hopkins (2009, p.18) reports, “Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic frag mentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided b African governments.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, not geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of economic growth, a position reinforced by their analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The paper highlights how European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks, with extractive institutions in some regions and inclusive ones in others, shaping the trajectory of economic growth. These differences in institutional quality are shown to have lasting effects on economic outcomes, as evidenced by the comparison between countries like the United States and the Congo. This insight underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics that influence institutional design. The role of institutions is further supported by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), who demonstrate how constitutional rules and institutions are crucial for fostering commitment and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. Their analysis illustrates how credible commitment underpins the development of markets and economic exchanges. These findings collectively suggest that the quality of institutions plays a central role in shaping economic growth, a theme that carries forward into subsequent analyses of institutional determinants.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that sustained economic growth depends on advancing technology and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany it, a perspective echoed by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights the role of formal institutions such as guilds in facilitating skill transmission and economic development in western Europe. These findings suggest that technological progress and institutional change are interdependent forces that shape long-run economic growth. For instance, Kuznets emphasizes that technological innovation generates positive feedback loops by increasing economic surplus, which in turn supports further research and development. Similarly, van Zanden notes that the decline in the skill premium in western Europe during the late medieval period was associated with long-term economic performance, indicating that institutional structures that promote skill diffusion and fair labor practices contribute to sustained growth. Mokyr (2005, p.3) further elaborates on the importance of intellectual and ideological shifts, particularly the Enlightenment’s emphasis on useful knowledge, as a driver of modern economic growth. He argues that the development of scientific societies and the spread of rational ideas were crucial in fostering an environment conducive to innovation and economic expansion. These insights collectively indicate that long-term economic growth is not solely the result of institutional quality but also hinges on the interplay between technological change, ideological shifts, and institutional adaptations. This theme of multifaceted drivers of growth can be further explored in relation to the role of historical and geographical factors in shaping economic trajectories.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that historical events, such as the slave trade, have had enduring effects on economic outcomes in Africa, shaping the institutional and social fabric that influences long-term development. This perspective highlights how historical legacies, particularly those involving violence and displacement, contribute to persistent mistrust and fragmented social structures, which in turn affect economic performance. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) illustrate how colonial policies, including the codification of customary law, reinforced patriarchal structures that marginalized women and restricted their access to land, thereby limiting their economic contributions. These mechanisms suggest that historical events are not merely background conditions but active forces that shape institutions and economic trajectories. Peters (2004, p.5) further elaborates on how land competition and exclusionary practices under colonial rule contributed to class formation, undermining the development of adaptive customary systems. This underscores the complexity of historical legacies, where some events may have created exclusionary institutions while others, such as the economic contributions of African women, point to alternative pathways of development. Nunn (2008, p.2) emphasizes the long-term economic consequences of the slave trade, linking it to high levels of ethnic fractionalization that continue to affect growth. These findings collectively indicate that historical events and their institutional legacies play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes in Africa, offering a perspective that complements but also challenges the centrality of institutional quality in explaining long-run growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the European family system was interdependent with labour markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, contributing to early industrialization. This interdependence of institutions is further supported by Stoler (1989, p.6), who argues that colonial authority was shaped by a complex set of relations of production and power, including racist ideology and the protection of European women, which were part of a class-based logic that maintained colonial control. These insights reveal that institutional development is influenced by both internal dynamics and external power relations and historical conflicts. This suggests that the historical legacies of power dynamics and conflicts influence the development of institutions in ways that are not always straightforward or easily measurable. The implications of these findings extend to long-run economic growth, as the effectiveness of institutions is shaped by a complex interplay of historical, social, and economic factors. Understanding these dynamics is essential for developing policies that address the root causes of institutional inefficiencies and promote sustainable economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.2) challenges the assumption that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more benign in its impact on the origins of formal education, suggesting that colonial interventions were not the sole drivers of educational development. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4), who argue that African real wages were historically higher than subsistence levels and rose over time, countering the view that structural impediments caused persistent poverty. These findings indicate that pre-colonial and colonial economic conditions were more complex than previously assumed, with factors such as malaria ecology, pre-colonial state centralization, and native resistance playing significant roles in shaping development outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further contribute to this narrative by arguing that Sub-Saharan Africa had higher per capita incomes than previously thought before the 1980s, challenging the view of persistent underdevelopment. This cluster of research highlights the importance of re-evaluating historical economic narratives to better understand the long-term impacts of institutions on economic growth. Simson (2019, p.11) adds to this discussion by showing that East African post-independence budget policies were not uniquely clientelist, indicating that economic decisions were influenced by a range of factors beyond patronage. These insights collectively suggest that the historical development of institutions in Africa is more nuanced and multifaceted than previously recognized, offering new perspectives on the role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the quality of governance, property rights, and the rule of law. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions, rather than geography or other factors, are the primary determinant of income per capita, with European colonization strategies leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions that have long-term economic consequences (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that institutional changes caused by colonialism, rather than geographic determinism, explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These insights underscore the importance of institutional quality in fostering economic development.

The role of institutions is further complicated by the interplay of social and historical factors. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily lead to poor institutions, as societies can be ethnically homogeneous yet dysfunctional or ethnically heterogeneous yet capable of upholding the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that institutions are shaped by a variety of factors beyond ethnic composition. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) further complicate this picture by showing that the slave trade has had a persistent negative effect on intergroup trust, which in turn affects economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This highlights the enduring impact of historical events on institutional quality and economic outcomes.

A key point of agreement among these studies is that institutions are central to long-run economic growth. A remaining disagreement concerns the degree to which historical events, such as the slave trade, influence institutional quality. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade's effect on trust is significant, while Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of colonial strategies in shaping institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The specific historical evidence from the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust provides a critical empirical comparison that helps settle this disagreement, showing that historical events can have a lasting influence on institutional quality and economic development.
