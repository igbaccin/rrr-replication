The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a question of significant intellectual importance, as it seeks to understand the factors that have propelled some nations towards prosperity while others have stagnated. This review delves into the scholarly discourse surrounding this question, highlighting the live disagreement that exists between various perspectives.

At the heart of this debate lies the question of how institutions, particularly those established during European colonization, have influenced current income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the strategies adopted during colonization resulted in the establishment of institutions that either fostered investment and the rule of law, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, or created extractive states that transferred resources to the metropole, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast. On the other hand, Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, and that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Another stream of scholarship focuses on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) assert that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their subordinate status in access to land was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This subordination, they argue, hindered economic growth.

The role of British rule in facilitating formal education in sub-Saharan Africa is another area of contention. Frankema (2012, p.2) posits that British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than colonial governments, were primarily responsible for the introduction of formal education before 1940. This contrasts with the traditional view that colonial governments were the primary agents of educational development.

Finally, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They contend that this mistrust, rather than the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, is a significant factor in explaining the origins of trust.

According to Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), institutional changes resulting from European colonization significantly shaped current income levels across countries. The authors posit that the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to different associated institutions, which in turn influenced economic growth and income disparities.

On the other hand, extractive states like the Congo or the Gold Coast, set up with the intention of rapidly transferring resources to the metropole, hindered economic development. The authors' findings suggest that institutional differences account for a significant fraction of current income differences.

The authors illustrate that relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor, highlighting the role of institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth.

The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) also sheds light on the mechanism behind this pattern. The authors argue that political power was crucial for elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, enabling them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages. This exploitative system likely hindered economic growth and contributed to the observed income disparities.

They find that the internal channel, where individuals develop norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external channel, where the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others affect trust. This internal mistrust may have hindered economic development and contributed to the income disparities observed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002).

In the realm of institutions and economic growth, a significant body of scholarship, anchored by North's seminal work, highlights the crucial role of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs in fostering long-term economic development. Central to this stream are the creations of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England, as North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue.

These developments, heralded as the English financial revolution, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, a critical factor in the emergence of political institutions that specified 'efficient' property rights.

Moreover, Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this stream by acknowledging the need for methodological modification to account for the complexities of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience across different historical contexts. He suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation in economic growth, requires qualification to better understand the nuances of institutional change and its impact on economic development.

In this light, the English financial revolution serves as a compelling example of how secure property rights and reduced transaction costs can underpin the development of efficient political institutions, thereby contributing to long-term economic growth. However, as Austin (2008, p.1) points out, the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others, hinting at the complexity and context-specific nature of institutional evolution and its impact on economic development.

This stream of literature, therefore, underscores the importance of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs in shaping long-run economic growth, while also emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional change and its impact across different historical contexts.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others argue that the growth of useful knowledge was a primary driver of long-run economic growth, particularly during the British Industrial Revolution. Mokyr's (2005, p.54) work highlights the proliferation of scientific societies and intellectual discourse in the British Isles, focusing on topics related to political economy, such as free trade, infrastructure, and efficient taxation. He emphasizes the Enlightenment idea that individual self-interest often contributes to societal welfare, except in cases of redistribution and rent-seeking.

Moreover, Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that the development of law and order, trust, and enforceable contracts contributed significantly to efficient resource allocation, laying the groundwork for the Industrial Revolution. This period saw a shift from the relative institutional weakness of early sixteenth-century Spain to the more robust institutional basis supporting growth in the Industrial Revolution.

Their findings suggest that the distribution of economic growth during the Industrial Revolution may have been influenced by the institutional legacies of European colonization.

Goldin's (2006) paper offers insights into the role of institutions in shaping women's involvement in employment, education, and family roles. Specifically, the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century is identified as a significant factor in this evolution (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin (2006, p. 7) supports this claim with anecdotes, such as Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's experience of being offered a secretarial position instead of a lawyer role after graduating from Stanford Law School (Goldin 2006, p.7).

This shift from one phase to another was due to various exogenous changes, as Goldin (2006, p.3) explains. Changes in household production technology and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women further altered female labor supply.

The role of institutions in shaping women's employment and education is not limited to the early 20th century. As Goldin (2006) discusses, the post-World War II era saw a significant increase in women's participation in the workforce, driven in part by the availability of birth control and changes in social attitudes towards women working outside the home. However, these changes were not uniform across regions or industries, suggesting that the distribution of economic growth during this period may have been influenced by the institutional context in which these changes occurred.

The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), advocates for continued scholarly collaboration to refine Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia. This collaborative effort aims to enhance our understanding of long-run economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize that the estimates of past economic data are subject to errors and assumptions, with the margins of error increasing as one goes back in time. However, they argue that these margins of error can be calculated and improved through continued collaboration among scholars. This perspective complements Maddison's original strategy of producing numbers, even with limited data, to stimulate further research and refinement.

By working together, scholars can build upon Maddison's pioneering work and present a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth. For instance, the Hitotsubashi team, part of the Maddison Project, has conducted a detailed study on Asian national accounts, experimenting with various margins of error (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3). This collaborative approach not only refines existing data but also encourages the development of new methodologies and perspectives.

In this context, the work of Feinstein and Thomas, who proposed a method for estimating margins of error in historical economic data, is of particular relevance (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3). Their method has been applied in several studies, demonstrating its potential to enhance the accuracy of historical economic data.

The impact of the slave trade on contemporary economic development and the industrial revolution is a subject of interest among scholars, with a shared finding by Nunn (2008, p.2) and Humphries (2013, p.16) suggesting that Africa's slave trades had a long-lasting negative impact on the development of institutions in Africa, which in turn hindered economic growth.

He points out that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government, national currency, and well-established markets and trading networks, was the first African society to engage in trade with Europeans. In contrast, other coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade, potentially impeding economic growth.

Humphries (2013, p.16), on the other hand, focuses on the British industrial revolution and argues that a more accurate understanding of this period is achieved by considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, rather than focusing on men and high wages. He shows that the large and growing numbers of women and children among pauper populations were in part a result of family breakdown and male abandonment, which could be linked to economic, social, and political conditions.

This stream of literature implies that the long-term effects of the slave trade on institutions in Africa may have indirectly impacted the availability of labor in other regions, such as Britain, potentially shaping the pace and nature of the industrial revolution. However, further research is needed to establish a direct causal link between these phenomena.

Ogilvie's work (Ogilvie 2007, p.27) challenges the efficiency-based explanations for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, arguing instead that distributional conflicts played a more significant role. This perspective, shared by Peters (2004, p.5), offers a rival explanation to the efficiency-based models that have dominated the literature.

Ogilvie contends that the European family system, labor markets, credit markets, and community-based welfare systems were interdependent and contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Peters, on the other hand, argues that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa mask processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation (Peters 2004, p.5). Both authors suggest that understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth requires a focus on the distributional conflicts and power dynamics that underlie these systems.

Moreover, Ogilvie highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, emphasizing that we cannot study one institution in isolation (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Peters supports this notion by critiquing the 'unproblematic visions' of land tenure in Africa that ignore considerable social inequality and encroachment by some groups (Peters 2004, p.9). Together, these scholars underscore the importance of examining the complex interplay of institutions and power dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth.

Furthermore, the distributional conflict perspective offers a nuanced understanding of the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie discusses the influence of cultural theories on the structure of economic institutions and economy efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Peters, in contrast, critiques the 'evolutionary school of land rights' that fails to adequately address the issue of social differentiation in relations to land (Peters 2004, p.9). This suggests that cultural beliefs and values can have complex and often contradictory effects on economic institutions, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth.

The work of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggests a significant role for inequality in shaping the institutions that underpin long-run economic growth, particularly in New World societies. They argue that greater inequality led to institutions that favored elites, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating economic disparities.

However, they question how these differences in institutions originated, proposing that greater inequality provided the foundation for institutions that advantaged elites and limited access to economic opportunities for the majority of the population (Sokoloff & Engerman, 2000, p.2).

Where there was extreme inequality, elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time, potentially reinforcing and perpetuating economic disparities (Sokoloff & Engerman, 2000, p.12).

Furthermore, North (1988) highlights the role of political institutions in shaping economic growth, arguing that the strength of these institutions can significantly impact a society's long-term economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and other scholars propose an alternative perspective on the factors driving economic growth in less developed countries. Unlike the topic, which emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, Kuznets contends that advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are necessary but not the primary drivers of growth in these countries.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology, such as steam and electric power, is incompatible with traditional institutions and social views that prevailed in earlier times, including family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery. To participate effectively in the modern economic system, a country needs rapid changes in its labor force's location and structure, as well as in the relations among occupational status groups and between labor force and total population. This necessitates a shift from rural modes of life, large and extended families, and veneration of undisturbed nature.

Furthermore, Hornbeck (2016) supports Kuznets's argument by showing that the industrial revolution in Britain was driven by a combination of technological innovation and institutional change, particularly the emergence of a capitalist market economy and the expansion of the state's role in regulating and protecting property rights. However, Hornbeck also points out that the industrial revolution was not a universal phenomenon, as many less developed countries did not experience similar growth due to institutional barriers and unfavorable geographic conditions.

In this context, the role of institutions in shaping economic growth becomes more complex. While institutions can facilitate technological innovation and economic growth, they can also act as barriers to development in less developed countries. These changes may not be compatible with the traditional institutions that prevail in many less developed countries, leading to a need for institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate economic growth.

Englebert concludes that ethnic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Englebert, 2003).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) further argues that the European presence in Africa, particularly through the Atlantic slave trade, introduced institutions that were poorly suited to long-term growth (Hopkins, 2009, p.10). In this context, the implication is that institutional reforms in Africa should focus on addressing the historical legacy of extractive institutions introduced by European powers, while also considering the role of ethnic heterogeneity in fostering the rule of law and progressive institutions. This line of argument suggests a need for a nuanced approach to institutional reforms in Africa, one that takes into account both the historical legacy and the potential benefits of ethnic diversity.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains played a significant role in shaping agricultural development, as argued by Hornbeck (2010, p.8) and supported by evidence of reduced land protection costs and enhanced property rights enforcement. Hornbeck (2010, p.8) notes that the Kansas State Board of Agriculture stopped reporting herd laws after 1884, suggesting their widespread acceptance. This acceptance is further corroborated by Hornbeck (2010, p.32), who reports that herd-law counties experienced a 20-percentage point increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 following the introduction of barbed wire.

The increased agricultural development facilitated by barbed wire had far-reaching implications. This expansion of agricultural development not only bolstered the economic growth of the American Plains but also likely contributed to the broader economic growth of the United States.

However, it is essential to consider the potential downstream implications of this agricultural development. The increased agricultural productivity may have led to changes in land use patterns, potentially affecting the environment and local communities. Further research is needed to examine these potential consequences and to understand the broader implications of the widespread adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains.

The emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, in western Europe during the late medieval period played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium, according to van Zanden (2009, p.19). This claim, supported by a number of authors who studied the functioning of these institutions in detail, posits that these institutions ensured fair execution of apprentice contracts and facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills (van Zanden, 2009, p.19).

The guilds, which had been criticized in the past for monopolizing skills and attempting to keep their relative pay above market clearing levels, may have counterbalanced their beneficial effects on skill transfer with their efforts to form local cartels (van Zanden, 2009, p.19). However, the beneficial effects of the guilds on long-term economic performance remain a subject of debate.

In a related study, van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that the skill premium measured in this way is also a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden, 2009, p.1). This finding suggests that the development of formal institutions in western Europe during the late medieval period contributed to the region's long-term economic growth by fostering a skilled workforce and promoting fair labor practices.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-term economic growth, as evidenced by a shared finding across several studies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.10; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.1; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34). However, the specific nature of this role remains a subject of debate.

(2001, 2002) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They posit that European colonies with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, experienced long-term economic growth. On the other hand, regions with extractive institutions, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, have seen limited economic development.

In contrast, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). They highlight how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and how the codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized women. This study suggests that the development of institutions in Africa, while important, must consider the role of gender in economic growth.

The disagreement between these two perspectives can be settled by examining the specific historical evidence and empirical comparisons used in both studies. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) focus on the impact of European colonization on institutions and economic growth, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the role of gender in African economic growth. By combining these perspectives and considering the impact of both European colonization and gender on institutions, a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth can be achieved.
