The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with profound implications for our understanding of global economic disparities. At the heart of this debate lies the question of how and why some former European colonies have experienced a reversal in relative income levels, while others have prospered.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism play a crucial role in this process. They contend that the colonizers adopted different strategies, with varying associated institutions. In some cases, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to economic growth. In the Congo and the Gold Coast, however, they established extractive states, quickly transferring resources to the metropole and hindering long-term development (Author, Year).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a complementary perspective, focusing on the economic contributions of African women in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Author, Year). They highlight that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced their subordinate status in access to land. This subordination, in turn, may have had implications for economic growth.

(2001, p.27) provide evidence supporting their claim that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They find that societies with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment tend to have higher income levels today.

On the other hand, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He suggests that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments, and that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) focus on the slave trade in Africa and its impact on intergroup trust, which they argue persists today. They find that both the internal environment (culture of mistrust) and the external environment (rule of law and trustworthiness of others) are important factors in shaping this trust, but that the internal factor is at least twice as large as the external one.

Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, not through direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Author, Year). This access to education, in turn, may have had implications for economic growth.

They posit that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, to those that set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast. The results of their regressions, using a variety of alternative measures of institutions, support this view.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They emphasize that political power was crucial to elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

North highlights the creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments as examples of how institutions and consequent falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself.

The implication of these findings is that the institutional changes brought about by European colonization not only influenced current income levels but also reversed relative income levels among former European colonies over time. Furthermore, the development of secure property rights, as seen in the creation of the Bank of England, played a crucial role in fostering economic growth by enabling the creation of a long-term capital market.

They find that this internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, which operates through the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust, they suggest, has far-reaching implications for economic development, trade, political institutions, and firm management practices, as supported by other studies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

He points out that these trades lasted for nearly five centuries, significantly longer than official colonial rule, and had a profound impact on the continent's economic and social institutions. Specifically, Nunn (2008, p.19) suggests that the slave trades disrupted existing trading networks and economic structures, slowing down the development of markets and market institutions that are essential for long-term economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, are a fundamental cause of the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as argued by Ogilvie and Peters. This claim is grounded in the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, as recognized by some studies of European economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). For instance, the European family system, according to Laslett, was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions; this whole interlinked institutional system, Laslett speculates, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Cross-country comparisons suggest that favorable 'contracting' institutions, those regulating interactions between private citizens, may be partially interdependent with favorable 'property rights' institutions, those constraining the behavior of political and economic elites (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). If such interdependencies are widespread, then we cannot study one institution in isolation. We have to look at entire institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Peters (2004, p.5) complements this claim by exposing the complexities of land tenure systems in sub-Saharan Africa. Reports of negotiable landholding and use in these regions mask processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation, necessitating a focus on who benefits and loses from instances of 'negotiability' (Peters 2004, p.5). Critics like Platteau have pinpointed the problems with unproblematic visions of customary land systems, which ignore considerable social inequality and evidence of encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and/or commercial benefits (Peters 2004, p.9).

In this context, the distribution of power and resources plays a crucial role in shaping the development of economic institutions. The interplay between different institutions, their interdependencies, and the distribution of power within these frameworks are essential factors in understanding the long-term economic growth in pre-industrial Europe and beyond.

They suggest that even if other forces would have eventually led England to success, they would have done so more slowly and less decisively without these institutions. Mokyr (2005, p.54), on the other hand, emphasizes the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He acknowledges that institutions like law and order, trust, and enforceable contracts played a significant role in facilitating this growth (Author, Year).

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the success of seventeenth-century England was remarkable, particularly in capital markets, and attribute this success to political factors. They focus on the political commitment to and binding by rules that govern economic exchange, enforcement, and change. Mokyr (2005, p.3), in turn, highlights that industrial growth was not sustainable without institutions that could withstand shocks, both man-made and natural. He suggests that the Dutch Republic and Venice had early institutional bases supporting this growth, while Spain and other countries had a later and weaker institutional basis.

In this context, the role of representative government institutions in fostering economic growth and market development becomes even more apparent. These institutions provide a framework for secure rights, encourage the growth of useful knowledge, and help the economy withstand shocks. This underscores the importance of understanding the development and evolution of these institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as demonstrated by Hornbeck (2010, p.8), significantly contributed to agricultural development by facilitating property rights enforcement and reducing costs of land protection. This shift, according to Hornbeck, marked a turning point in the region's agricultural landscape, as herd laws, previously employed to manage livestock, lost their relevance after 1880 and 1884, respectively, when the details of these laws ceased to be reported in Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) analysis reveals that the widespread use of barbed wire enabled farmers to demarcate and protect their land more effectively, leading to an increase in improved farmland and total improved land from 1880 to 1890. This finding is supported by evidence that nearly all herd-law counties had less woodland than non-herd law counties, indicating that barbed wire provided a more practical solution for land protection than herd laws (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

Moreover, the stream of literature on the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains further suggests that this innovation led to a more efficient use of resources. The reduction in costs of land protection allowed farmers to invest more in the improvement of their farms, contributing to the overall growth and development of the region's agricultural sector.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a significant factor in the British industrial revolution, challenging the traditional focus on men and high wages (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that the high rates of family breakdown and male abandonment in the eighteenth century contributed to the large and growing numbers of women and children among pauper populations. This finding is consistent with other historians' depictions of the period as one of marital instability (Humphries 2013, p.2).

The author points out that the high rates of family break-up and male abandonment continued throughout nearly two centuries, indicating that the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor remained a significant factor in the British economy.

Furthermore, the paper's findings are supported by other scholars who have studied the role of women in the industrial revolution. For instance, Vincent and Humphries (Humphries 2013, p.2) argue that women's work was essential to the functioning of the textile industry, particularly in spinning. They contend that women's work was often undervalued and underpaid, but it was nonetheless crucial to the industry's growth.

In addition, the use of female and child labor was not limited to the textile industry. Goldin argues that the use of women and children in the mines was a response to the high cost of male labor, as well as the shortage of male workers due to the military conscription and emigration.

The reliance on female and child labor in the British industrial revolution had far-reaching consequences. Understanding the role of female and child labor in the industrial revolution is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the period's economic and social dynamics.

The work of Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores the multidimensional nature of economic growth in less developed countries. Kuznets argues that for sustained economic growth, these nations require not only technological advancements but also institutional and ideological modifications. These adjustments are essential for effective participation in the modern economic system.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that modern technology, such as steam and electric power, is incompatible with traditional institutions and social structures that prevailed in earlier times, including family enterprises, illiteracy, and slavery. To participate in the modern economic system, a labor force must undergo rapid changes in its location, structure, and social relations.

One specific mechanism through which these changes occur is urbanization. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that modern economic growth drives a geographical expansion, leading to an increase in urban populations. This urbanization, in turn, triggers further institutional and ideological changes, such as alterations in family formation patterns and man's views on his role in society.

As these areas adopt modern technology, they undergo similar institutional and ideological transformations.

This adaptation, in turn, contributed to economic growth.
