The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of significant debate among scholars, with differing perspectives on the causal mechanisms and the historical context in which these institutions operate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a substantial impact on current income levels across countries. On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods.

The authors argue that in some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to economic growth. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states with the aim of quickly transferring resources to the metropole, which impeded long-term development.

The authors demonstrate that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming but made significant contributions to cash crops, propelling economic revolutions, as in the case of the Gold Coast, which became the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. Furthermore, the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced women's subordination in access to land.

These two streams of scholarship offer complementary accounts of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The interplay between these factors and their impact on economic growth is a complex issue that requires further investigation.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) focus on the slave trade in Africa as an antecedent to the cultural norms that emerged in former colonies. They find that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and negatively impacts intergroup trust, economic development, and political institutions. This cultural norm of mistrust, they suggest, is a downstream consequence of the slave trade, which was facilitated by the extractive institutions established during European colonization.

(2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They attribute this reversal to the political power that enabled European elites to force low wages from the native and African populations in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture. This political power, in turn, facilitated the establishment of extractive institutions, which have persisted and continue to impact current income levels.

The emergence and effectiveness of political institutions that specify 'efficient' property rights are crucial for long-run economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that these institutions played a significant role in England's financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and contributed to economic growth. North emphasizes the importance of secure rights, particularly in the time dimension of property rights, for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North & Weingast (1989) further highlight the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, focusing on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) provides additional insights into the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework. He suggests that the European family system, labor markets, credit markets, and community-based welfare systems were interconnected, potentially contributing to early industrialization and capitalism. Cross-country comparisons also suggest that favorable 'contracting' institutions may be partially interdependent with favorable 'property rights' institutions (Acemoglu and Johnson, cited in Ogilvie, 2007). This implies that studying one institution in isolation may not fully capture its impact on economic growth.

In terms of downstream implications, the effectiveness of these institutions has persisted and continues to impact current income levels, as demonstrated in the case of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al., 2002; Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011). Kuznets's (1973, p.1) seminal work emphasizes the importance of advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data for sustained economic growth, particularly in less developed countries. This perspective underscores the crucial role of institutions in fostering economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) asserts that modern economic growth, driven by technology such as steam and electric power, necessitates a transformation of societal structures, including labor force participation, urbanization, and changes in social views. He further argues that this dynamic process, initially observed in developed countries, expanded geographically to less developed areas as transportation and communication improved.

This transformation requires rapid changes in location, occupation, and societal views.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2011) provide empirical evidence supporting Kuznets's assertions, demonstrating that the quality of institutions, particularly property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability, significantly impacts economic growth in less developed countries. They argue that these institutional factors are crucial for attracting foreign direct investment and promoting economic development.

The growth of useful knowledge, as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, is a key focus in the scholarship of Mokyr (2005, p.54). Mokyr argues that intellectual factors played a crucial role in driving technological change and industrialization, shaping the course of economic development.

As evidence, he points to the proliferation of scientific societies in Europe during this period, which focused on issues of political economy, free trade, infrastructure, law and order, and efficient taxation (Mokyr, 2005, p. 54). Moreover, Mokyr suggests that the institutional basis supporting this intellectual growth provided a foundation for the Industrial Revolution, which was a key driver of long-term economic growth (Mokyr, 2005, p. 3).

The niche areas of intellectual activity protected innovators from the threat of "roving bandits" or greedy rulers, allowing for the development of new ideas and technologies (Mokyr, 2005, p. 3). This increased efficiency in resource allocation contributed to the Industrial Revolution, which was characterized by a shift from agrarian to industrial economies, a process that was crucial for long-term economic growth.

Mokyr's (2005, p.54) work complements the findings of Akyeampong and Fofack (2011), who also highlight the importance of institutions in driving economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack show that the quality of institutions, particularly property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability, significantly impact economic growth in less developed countries (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2011). While Mokyr focuses on the intellectual foundations of economic growth, Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize the role of institutions in attracting foreign direct investment and promoting economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing assumption that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s. Supporting this claim, they argue that many African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, comparable to the largest economies in Asia (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.10). This assertion contradicts early economic histories of Africa, which attributed economic growth to European colonial influences such as railways, malaria prophylaxis, and British rule (Broadberry & Gardner, 2022, p.10).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further explain that most studies of African growth use data from 1950 or later, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. New contributions to historical national accounting in other regions have offered insights into long-term patterns of growth and development (Broadberry, 2021). The field of African economic history has seen a resurgence due to newly digitized data and innovative techniques (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016).

The implication of Broadberry and Gardner's (2022) findings is that African economies had a level of development comparable to Asia's before the 1980s, suggesting that institutions in these regions may have played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as documented by Hornbeck (2010, p.8), significantly contributed to agricultural development by enhancing property rights enforcement and reducing costs of land protection. This transformation is evident in the decline of herd laws, which regulated the movement of livestock, after 1880 and 1884, respectively, as indicated by Hornbeck (2010, p.8).

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) further demonstrates that herd-law counties, which had already adopted the herd law by 1880, experienced a substantial increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire. The paper's findings suggest that barbed wire served as a catalyst for agricultural development, enabling more efficient land utilization and fostering economic growth in the American Plains.

This development, in turn, had downstream implications for the region's population growth. As agricultural productivity increased, so did the capacity to support a larger population (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This growth, in turn, further stimulated economic activity, creating a virtuous cycle of development.

The paper by Nunn (2008, p.2) posits a significant and lasting impact of Africa's slave trades on the development of institutions in Africa, which, in turn, influenced long-run economic growth. Nunn contends that Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly five centuries, had a more enduring impact on the continent than the relatively brief period of official colonial rule.

He cites Vansina (1990) to emphasize that local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate trade before the discovery of the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The slave trades, according to Nunn (2008, p.2), disrupted the development of these essential institutions by promoting the rise of predatory, decentralized political systems that discouraged trade and economic growth.

This finding complements the claim that the development of institutions plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as observed in other contexts such as the American Plains (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). However, unlike the American Plains, where the development of institutions was fostered by improved property rights enforcement and land protection, in Africa, the slave trades appear to have had a detrimental effect on institution-building.

The downstream implications of this historical disruption are far-reaching. As economic and social institutions in Africa were slow to develop, the continent struggled to participate in the global economy and, consequently, lagged behind other regions in terms of long-term economic growth.

The paper by van Zanden (2009, p.19) offers a compelling argument that the quality of institutions in western Europe during the late medieval period played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. This perspective, while not directly linked to the role of institutions in shaping economic growth in former European colonies or pre-industrial Europe, offers a rival explanation to the factors discussed in previous sections.

These institutions, according to the author, took care of issues related to the fair execution of apprentice contracts, addressing concerns about monopolization and cartel formation that had been raised in the past (van Zanden, 2009, p.19). The quality of these institutions, therefore, had a significant impact on the skill premium in western Europe, a factor that van Zanden argues is also a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden, 2009, p.1).

This finding, when considered alongside the argument that the quality of institutions influenced the skill premium, suggests that the development of effective institutional frameworks in western Europe may have provided a competitive advantage that contributed to its long-term economic growth.

The guilds offered benefits such as the intergenerational transfer of skills, but they were also criticized for monopolizing these skills and attempting to maintain their relative pay above market levels (van Zanden, 2009, p.19). This suggests that while institutions can facilitate economic growth, they must also be carefully regulated to ensure they do not stifle competition or hinder the efficient allocation of resources.

The British influence on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was primarily shaped by the activities of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments, as argued by Frankema in his seminal work (Frankema 2012, p.2). This influence extended to the educational institutions established during this period, which were often funded and managed by these external agents.

Frankema's paper presents a comprehensive analysis of the factors that influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule.

The ability of these educational institutions to promote economic growth was influenced by various factors. For instance, the development of a sizeable and geographically concentrated mining industry, such as the copper enclaves in the Katanga region, significantly increased the demand for formal education, as it created a need for skilled labor (Frankema 2012, p.17).

Moreover, the effectiveness of these educational institutions was also contingent on the level of native African resistance against colonial rule. Areas with strong resistance saw limited educational development, as the British were unable to consolidate their rule (Frankema 2012, p.17).

Post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's (2019, p.11) study reveals that, despite high debt burdens and growing military spending, the distribution of employment in these countries favored comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward.

In Kenya, subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Tanzania also experienced a high debt burden, with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief (Simson 2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly, while jobs increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions (Simson 2019, p.11). This trend suggests that, from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) collaborate with other scholars as part of the Maddison Project, which aims to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. The project's goal is to produce and update global economic data, as Maddison's strategy was to generate numbers even if a solid basis for them did not always exist, expecting that scholars might disagree and carry out new work to show that he was wrong (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

This collaborative effort builds upon Maddison's pioneering work, which induced many scholars to work on these themes and try to quantify long-term economic development (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2). The project's focus on the early modern period and Asia is particularly significant, as estimates of national accounts in the past, especially in distant periods, are subject to certain margins of error. These margins of error are often based on partial data and certain assumptions about the links between these data and the economic activities they represent. The further one goes back in time, the larger the margins of error will probably be (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3).

The Maddison Project acknowledges that these margins of error suggest a certain degree of objectivity, whereas they are actually based on rather subjective estimates of the possible margins of error of the underlying data (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3). This subjectivity is not a weakness but rather an opportunity for further research and collaboration among scholars. The project's focus on Asia is particularly important, as data for medieval England are better than, for example, nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa, or pre-Colombian Latin America (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3).

In this way, the Maddison Project aims to present a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. Peters (2004, p.5) argues that perceptions of static custom in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa can advantage certain groups by keeping officials ignorant of changing practices. This observation is shared by Platteau (1996, 2000) and Amanor (2001), who highlight that such unproblematic visions often overlook considerable social inequality within customary systems, as well as evidence of encroachment, state appropriation, and illegal sales by 'traditional' leaders.

The final decade of colonial rule, up to c.1960, saw a push for agricultural development, during which longstanding prejudice against African landholding practices, perceived as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment, intensified (Peters, 2004, p.5). This prejudice, driven by agricultural officers in charge of 'improvement' and resettlement schemes, led to a focus on 'modernized' farming and overlooked the complexities of customary land tenure systems in sub-Saharan Africa.

Peters (2004, p.5) illustrates this point through the case of the Gold Coast (modern Ghana), where colonial officials implemented a system of 'stool lands' that placed control of land in the hands of traditional leaders.

The neglect of these complexities in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa has had significant downstream implications. For instance, Ribot (2000) and Carney and Farrington (1998) have shown how national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers, further exacerbating social inequalities.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically restructured to preserve social distinctions, thereby fostering the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies. Stoler (1989, p.6) provides a compelling argument that European class cultures were deliberately altered to create and sustain the social hierarchies of colonial authority. This restructuring was not merely a justification for continued European rule and white supremacy, but also an essential component of the system that kept potentially subversive white colonials in line (Stoler, 1989, p.6).

Stoler's (1989, p.6) work reveals that the internal political tensions among European colonials were largely overlooked in colonial studies, with most research focusing on social rankings and colonial policy. Stoler emphasizes that the everyday practice of colonial domination is crucial to understanding the class-based logic behind the restructuring of European class cultures (Stoler, 1989, p.4).

One specific mechanism that emerged from this restructuring was the creation of a sense of colonial community and context, which served to reinforce colonial authority and the relations of production and power. This was achieved through the implementation of racist ideologies, fear of the Other, preoccupation with white prestige, and efforts to protect European women from sexual assault by Asian and black males (Stoler, 1989, p.6).

The expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies, as a downstream implication of this restructuring, can be seen in the proliferation of community studies focusing on the social rankings of colonial life, albeit without addressing the internal political tensions among the British themselves (Stoler, 1989, p.4). In the realm of African economic history, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) contribute significantly to the understanding of long-run economic growth by challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty. Their research, centered on pre-colonial labor market conditions, reveals that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965). Their findings challenge the notion of persistent African poverty due to structural growth impediments, as they demonstrate that African real wages did not converge until after the Second World War.

The recorded differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth call for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development. Future research in African economic history should concentrate more on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories.

The work of Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Englebert (cited in Hopkins 2009) suggests a significant role for policy shifts in shaping institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Englebert, as cited by Hopkins, concludes that ethnic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Instead, societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Hopkins further discusses the impact of European presence on Africa's economic development. He posits that larger African populations could resist European encroachment and also occupied epidemiological zones that imposed a high rate of mortality on Europeans. In such regions, the European presence was either marginal, in which case unproductive institutions remained in place, or took the form of colonial rule, which introduced extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited to long-term development. The Atlantic slave trade is a particularly telling case, as it reduced the population from a level that was previously relatively high and encouraged the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth.

This stream of literature implies that policy shifts, such as 'bringing the state back in', can have significant implications for institutional reforms and poverty reduction in Africa. However, it is crucial to note that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, and the European presence did not always lead to the development of institutions detrimental to long-term growth. Future research should focus on identifying the specific policy shifts that can lead to the development of productive institutions and the reduction of poverty in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of extensive scholarly inquiry, with several key streams of related scholarship emerging. One significant stream argues that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a lasting impact on current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) posit that European colonizers adopted different strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes. In one extreme, as in the cases of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. In colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states, transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, which impeded long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Simultaneously, another stream of scholarship focuses on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that the de-privileging of women in access to land was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This subordinate status, they suggest, had significant implications for economic growth.

While both streams agree on the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, they differ in their focus on European colonization versus the role of African women. The disagreement between these perspectives can be resolved by considering the historical evidence of how European colonizers interacted with and influenced the institutions and roles of women in African societies. For example, the new economic history of Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18) highlights that the larger populations of some African societies could resist European encroachment, leading to the preservation of unproductive institutions or the introduction of extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited to long-term development. In such regions, the impact of European colonization on institutions and economic growth may have been more complex than the simplified dichotomy of productive versus extractive colonies suggests.
