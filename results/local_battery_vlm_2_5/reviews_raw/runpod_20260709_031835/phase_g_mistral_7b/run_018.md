The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of profound intellectual debate, with scholars exploring various facets of this relationship. At the heart of this discourse lies the question of how institutional arrangements, established during historical periods, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes across nations.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They posit that the strategies adopted during colonization, ranging from settlement and rule of law to extractive states, have shaped the institutional landscape and, consequently, the economic trajectories of these colonies. The results of their research, employing a variety of institutional measures, support the premise that the quality of institutions established during colonization plays a crucial role in shaping current income levels.

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, with their subordinate status reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This subordination, in turn, impacted the economic growth and development of African societies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He emphasizes that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) delve into the impact of the slave trade in Africa on intergroup trust, positing that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their research suggests that both the internal and external environment play a role in this culture of mistrust, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel.

In the pursuit of understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, the significance of secure rights and credible commitments emerges as a crucial determinant. This insight is substantiated by the works of Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman, who both highlight the importance of these elements in pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies, respectively.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) posits that conflicts over resource distribution played a pivotal role in shaping the economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. He argues that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This holistic institutional system, according to Ogilvie, may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

Simultaneously, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This path of institutional development may, in turn, have affected growth.

These findings collectively underscore the importance of secure rights and credible commitments in shaping economic institutions, which, in turn, influence economic growth. The nature of these institutions, whether they favor the many or the few, can have lasting impacts on current income levels, as seen in the contrasting economic trajectories of pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies.

The growth of useful knowledge, as argued by Mokyr (2005, p.54), is a fundamental driver of long-run economic growth. This growth was dedicated to making access to knowledge easier and cheaper, and intellectual factors played an essential role in this process. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that societies with a higher number of scientific societies per capita experienced greater economic growth. He suggests that the Enlightenment idea that individuals working for their own good also contributed to the welfare of society was a significant factor in this process.

This shift in gender roles further supports the idea that intellectual factors played a crucial role in long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that for economic growth in less developed countries, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and an expanded supply of tested data. This claim aligns with the stream of literature focusing on factors for economic growth in less developed countries.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that traditional institutions, such as family enterprises, illiteracy, and slavery, are incompatible with modern technology. He further argues that the dynamic forces of modern economic growth necessitate rapid changes in various aspects of society, including family formation, urbanization, and labor force structure. These changes are crucial for effective participation in the modern economic system, particularly in less developed areas.

Supporting this, Akyeampong and Fofack (2011) demonstrate that institutional changes, such as the shift from family enterprises to corporate structures, played a significant role in the economic growth of several African countries. They argue that these institutional changes allowed for increased productivity and efficiency, which in turn facilitated economic growth.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research, supported by evidence from the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports, contends that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly boosted agricultural development. This development was facilitated by reduced costs of land protection and enhanced property rights enforcement.

By the late 1880s, the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports ceased to detail herd laws, suggesting that these regulations were no longer a point of interest after the widespread adoption of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The introduction of barbed wire, according to Hornbeck, led to a marked increase in the improved fraction of farmland and total improved land between 1880 and 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

In this context, the increased adoption of barbed wire can be seen as a crucial factor in the agricultural development of the American Plains, contributing to long-run economic growth. The availability of secure land boundaries enabled farmers to invest more effectively in their properties, fostering growth in agricultural productivity. This stream of literature underscores the profound impact of technological innovations on economic development, particularly in the context of the American Plains.

The influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries played a significant role in the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa during the colonial period, primarily through their efforts rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These institutions provided a foundation for the dissemination of Western knowledge and skills, which later contributed to economic growth. The impact of British rule on education can be observed in the educational attainment levels of the region. This economic growth can, in part, be attributed to the education system established by the British during the colonial period.

However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the impact of education on economic growth was not uniform across the region. Factors such as the level of investment in education, the quality of education provided, and the extent to which education was accessible to all segments of the population varied significantly between different countries and regions (Frankema 2012, p.17). The paper by Stoler (1989, p.6) and Peters (2004, p.5) argue that European class cultures were selectively restructured in colonial settings to maintain social distinctions, a process that may have contributed to the development of certain institutions but is distinct from the topic's focus on institutions as the fundamental drivers of long-term economic growth.

Stoler (1989, p.6) contends that colonial authority and relations of production and power in Africa were shaped by a critical class-based logic. This logic extended beyond racial ideology and the protection of European women to include the management of potential dissent among European underlings in the colonies. Peters (2004, p.5), meanwhile, highlights the masking of processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation in sub-Saharan Africa. He argues that reports of negotiable landholding and use in the region often overlook the benefits accrued by national and local elites at the expense of the marginalized.

In the context of these restructured class cultures, the provision of public goods and the development of progressive institutions may have been influenced. For instance, the selective restructuring of European class cultures could have affected the distribution of resources, potentially hindering the equitable provision of public goods.

However, it is crucial to note that while these restructured class cultures may have shaped institutions, they do not necessarily explain the long-term economic growth or the lack thereof in former European colonies. As the subsequent sections will discuss, other factors such as skill premium, the growth of useful knowledge, and the impact of British rule on education play significant roles in shaping economic growth. Nonetheless, the restructuring of European class cultures in colonial settings offers valuable insights into the complex interplay of cultural, social, and political factors in the development of institutions.

Simson's (2019) analysis challenges the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Unlike previous studies suggesting that these countries' budgets were characterized by high levels of subsidies and interest payments, Simson contends that the debt burden in these countries was manageable, and military spending, while growing, did not significantly distort budget priorities.

Simson's (2019, p.11) paper reveals that, contrary to expectations, the high-level allocation of wage resources in these countries constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage. This is evidenced by the growth in employment that favored comparatively skilled and female professions, suggesting that the high-level allocation of wage resources in the post-independence period did not prioritize unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment (Simson, 2019, p.19).

Moreover, Simson (2019, p.11) demonstrates that the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly, further challenging the assumption that post-independence budget policies were unproductive and focused on patronage networks. This finding is supported by North and Weingast's (1989, p.30) argument that the distribution of resources within a political system is a key determinant of its stability and economic performance (E0001).

Simson's (2019, p.11) study, however, does not address the potential impact of corruption on budget allocation in these countries. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that corruption can significantly distort budget priorities and lead to unproductive spending, which could have implications for the economic performance of Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda (E0002).

The finding that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending opens up new avenues for research. Future studies could explore the role of corruption in budget allocation in these countries and its impact on their economic performance. Furthermore, comparative analyses of budget policies in other African countries could shed light on whether Simson's findings are generalizable beyond Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda.

The literature review on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth reveals a consensus that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization significantly influenced current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). However, a point of contention arises regarding the impact of the slave trade in Africa on intergroup trust and its subsequent effect on economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the slave trade did not have a substantial impact on the rule of law and progressive institutions in Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). In contrast, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) assert that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, which has negative implications for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This disagreement can be settled by examining the specific empirical evidence presented by both sources.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) demonstrate that both internal norms of mistrust and the external environment play a role in shaping intergroup trust in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). They find that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the slave trade's impact on trust is primarily through internal norms. On the other hand, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) focus on the impact of European colonization on institutions and economic growth, and do not directly address the role of the slave trade in shaping internal norms of mistrust (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

By investigating the specific historical evidence presented by both sources, it becomes clear that the slave trade contributed to the development of internal norms of mistrust in Africa, which in turn impacted economic growth. The slave trade forced Africans to interact with Europeans under conditions of extreme violence and exploitation, which likely shaped their perceptions of outsiders and reinforced a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust may have hindered the development of productive institutions and economic growth in Africa, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27).
