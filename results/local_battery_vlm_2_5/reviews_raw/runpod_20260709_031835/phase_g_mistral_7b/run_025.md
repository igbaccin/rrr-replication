The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding economic development across nations. At the heart of this discussion lies the question of how institutions, particularly those established during colonial rule, have influenced contemporary income levels and economic growth patterns.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights set up during European colonization have a profound impact on current income levels across countries. Frankema (2012, p.2) posits that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education primarily through the impact of British traders and Protestant missionaries, as opposed to direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Not all scholars concur that colonial institutions were the sole drivers of economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that societal factors such as ethnic fragmentation and population resistance to European encroachment also played a significant role in shaping the quality of institutions in Africa. He suggests that societies could be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) present a different perspective, focusing on the contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods. This subordination, they argue, had significant implications for economic growth and development in Africa.

In a different context, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. He suggests that the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, for instance, was interdependent with labour markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This distinctive framework, he argues, may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

This claim is supported by the findings of Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) posit that the colonization strategies of Europeans led to varied institutional outcomes, with some colonies fostering rule of law and investment, while others established extractive states that hindered long-term development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) provide evidence that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that both the external environment and internal norms of mistrust are important factors, but the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. This culture of mistrust, rooted in Africa's slave trades, could have further impeded the development of productive institutions during and after European colonization.

In this context, it is important to note that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.2). The slave trades, which lasted for nearly 500 years, had a more enduring effect on Africa than the relatively brief period of official colonial rule. Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, allowing it to support trade with the Europeans. The persisting mistrust due to the slave trades could have hindered the development of such institutions in other regions, thereby further impeding economic growth.

Greater inequality in New World societies, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), led to the development of institutions that advantage elites. This phenomenon, supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), is rooted in the interdependency of various institutions within a wider framework.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further argue that this institutional framework, favoring elites, can hinder economic growth by limiting access to opportunities for a significant portion of the population. This perspective is corroborated by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who suggests that cultural beliefs and values, with their associated institutional manifestations, can influence the structure of economic institutions and efficiency of the economy. For example, 'civic' cultures, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Not all elite-advantaging institutions produce such favorable outcomes. As Ogilvie (2007, p.27) notes, guilds, which can be seen as an institution that restricts competition, may have declined due to the disappearance of certain cultural beliefs about the afterlife. This shift could have negatively impacted the economy.

The creation of political institutions that secure property rights and provide effective enforcement is a key factor in promoting long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the English financial revolution (North and Weingast (1989, p.30); (North 1989, p.9)).

This enhanced security of property rights, as North (1989, p.9) notes, was critical in both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself.

Furthermore, the success of these institutions was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). It is important to acknowledge that while these institutions were instrumental in economic growth, their emergence was not predestined.

Williamson's work, while controversial, adds credence to the idea that the English financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success and that institutions played a necessary role in making this growth possible.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as detailed by Hornbeck (2010, p.8), significantly contributed to agricultural development by enhancing property rights enforcement and reducing the costs of land protection. This innovation, initially met with resistance due to herd laws, ultimately facilitated the transformation of the region's landscape.

Prior to barbed wire's introduction, herd laws aimed to manage cattle grazing, but these laws were often ineffective and led to overgrazing. Hornbeck (2010, p.8) reveals that after 1880, Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped including details on herd law decisions, suggesting their diminishing relevance in the face of barbed wire.

By making it easier to fence off land, barbed wire enabled more efficient land management and reduced the costs associated with maintaining herds. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) provides evidence of this, showing that herd-law counties experienced a significant increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire, whereas there was no such increase before its adoption. This shift in land use patterns underscores the role of barbed wire in promoting agricultural development in the American Plains.

In addition to its impact on land use, barbed wire also played a role in reducing conflicts over land rights. This enhancement of property rights further facilitated agricultural development, as it allowed for longer-term planning and investment in land improvement.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains therefore offers insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. By facilitating property rights enforcement and reducing costs of land protection, barbed wire promoted agricultural development and contributed to the economic growth of the region. This example underscores the importance of institutions in enabling economic development, a theme that will continue to emerge in subsequent sections of this literature review.

Mokyr and others argue that the growth of useful knowledge was a primary driver of long-run economic growth, particularly in the British Isles during the Industrial Revolution. Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the expansion of intellectual societies and the development of the Enlightenment ideology fostered the growth of useful knowledge, focusing on issues such as free trade, infrastructure, and efficient taxation. Simultaneously, Mokyr suggests that law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts facilitated the efficient allocation of resources, contributing to the growth of the economy.

In addition, Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the importance of the Dutch Republic and Venice in the growth of useful knowledge prior to the Industrial Revolution. These cities had early institutions supporting this growth, such as universities and libraries, which provided a fertile ground for intellectual development. Mokyr (2005, p.54) underscores that while industrial and less revolutionary growth occurred elsewhere, it was the British Isles that played the most significant role in sparking the Industrial Revolution.

In summary, Mokyr and others propose that the growth of useful knowledge played a crucial role in the long-run economic growth, particularly in the British Isles during the Industrial Revolution. The expansion of intellectual societies, the development of the Enlightenment ideology, and the establishment of institutions supporting growth facilitated the growth of the economy. Meanwhile, Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the contributions of the Dutch Republic and Venice in the growth of useful knowledge prior to the Industrial Revolution, setting the stage for the more significant growth that occurred in the British Isles.

This discussion underscores the importance of intellectual development and the establishment of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, a theme that will continue to emerge in subsequent sections of this literature review.

The increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, as Goldin (2006, p.7) argues, was a significant driver of the 'quiet revolution' in women's roles, which involved their increased participation in employment, education, and family life. Goldin (2006, p.7) highlights the role of exogenous changes, such as the growth of educational institutions and alterations in household production technology, that influenced the supply and demand dynamics of female labor during this period.

Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's personal anecdote of her first job interview, recounted in Goldin's (2006, p.7) paper, underscores the limited opportunities available to educated women in the 1950s, despite their academic achievements.

Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also affected female labor supply, making part-time work more accessible and reducing barriers to the employment of married women.

The shifts from one phase to the next were not only influenced by these changes in supply but also by institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women (Goldin, 2006, p.3). This development, in turn, facilitated women's increased participation in the workforce, education, and family life, marking a significant turning point in gender roles in the United States.

In this context, it is worth noting that the changing roles of women in the workforce were not limited to office work. As Humphries (1981) shows, the growth of industrial employment in the late 19th and early 20th centuries also led to increased female participation in the manufacturing sector. This trend, however, was not uniform across industries and regions, and women continued to face significant barriers to upward mobility in many sectors.

This discussion underscores the complex interplay between changes in demand, supply, and institutions in shaping the participation of women in the workforce and the broader changes in gender roles in the United States. As we move forward, we will explore how these factors have continued to evolve and their impact on women's roles in the contemporary United States.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a significant role in the success of the British industrial revolution. Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that this overlooked aspect is crucial for a more accurate understanding of the revolution, as it was not merely about high wages among men.

This turbulent family structure facilitated the employment of these individuals in the industrial sector. Humphries (2013, p.16) notes that the absence of fathers, either due to death or abandonment, left a substantial number of families in a vulnerable state, making women and children more available for work (Humphries, 2013, p.16). This availability of labor contributed to the success of the British industrial revolution, as it provided a source of cheap and amenable labor that was not subject to the same wage demands as adult males.

Moreover, the presence of these workers in the industrial sector had downstream implications. This, in turn, led to a decline in the cost of textiles, making them more affordable and contributing to their widespread adoption.

The influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule significantly shaped the institutional framework. Frankema's paper (2012) argues that British rule facilitated formal education primarily through these agents, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. British traders and missionaries introduced Western educational methods and established schools, thereby expanding access to formal education.

This assertion is supported by the evidence that these actors established schools, particularly in areas with a high concentration of British traders and missionaries. For instance, in West Africa, the establishment of the Church Missionary Society (CMS) schools led to a rapid increase in literacy rates.

Simson's paper (2019) provides evidence of the downstream implications of this expanded access to formal education. In post-independence Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, increased access to education led to a shift in the composition of employment towards comparatively skilled and female professions. This trend, in turn, contributed to the growth of the economy and the development of a more productive workforce. Simson (2019, p.11) reports, “This process was particularly marked in Kenya, where subsidies and interest peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending.”

The internal political tensions among the British themselves played a significant role in shaping the social distinctions within colonial societies, contributing to the maintenance of imperial control (Stoler 1989, p.6). Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that European class cultures were selectively restructured to create and maintain these social distinctions. Racist ideology, fear of the Other, preoccupation with white prestige, and obsession with protecting European women from sexual assault by Asian and black males were not only justifications for continued European rule and white supremacy but also part of a critical class-based logic (Stoler 1989, p.6).

For instance, the British in India and Africa implemented social rankings that reinforced the power dynamics among the colonizers (Stoler 1989, p.6). These class-based distinctions were crucial in maintaining colonial authority and the relations of production and power (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Simultaneously, these internal political tensions among the British had downstream implications for the colonized.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology and institutional and ideological adjustments are necessary. Additionally, effective participation in the modern economic system necessitates rapid changes in labor force location and structure, occupational status groups, and societal views (Kuznets 1973, p.4).

This technological imperative, however, has downstream implications. This strain can lead to social unrest and instability, potentially hindering economic growth in the long run. Furthermore, the need for institutional and ideological adjustments can be challenging, particularly in less developed countries where traditional structures may be deeply entrenched.

While institutions are indeed crucial for economic development, Kuznets underscores the importance of technological advancements and their associated societal changes, which can pose significant challenges in less developed countries. This perspective underscores the complexity of fostering economic growth in these contexts and highlights the need for a holistic approach that considers both technological and institutional factors.

These changes can be disruptive and challenging, particularly in less developed countries where traditional structures may be deeply entrenched. Understanding and addressing these challenges is crucial for fostering sustainable economic growth in these contexts.

The authors argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Colonial rule's introduction of customary law served to strengthen patriarchal alliances and further marginalize African women in economic roles.

The de-privileging of women in access to land in the context of cash crops was reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, and Roberts 2006). Polly Hill (1963) has documented how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farm land for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. This illustrates the crucial role African women played in agriculture, yet their contributions were overshadowed by patriarchal institutions.

The introduction of a dual legal system of European courts based on common and civil law and native courts reinforced the marginalization of women. This finding shares a common thread with the work of other scholars who have highlighted the impact of institutions on gender roles and economic development (e.g., Stoler 1989).

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) propose a compelling rival explanation to the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The Maddison Project, which they discuss, focuses on extending and revising Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population in the world economy, particularly in the early modern period and Asia (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This collaboration among scholars aims to provide a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

Bolt and van Zanden argue that this approach has been successful in inducing many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. The project's focus on extending and revising Maddison's estimates suggests that the quality and availability of data may play a significant role in shaping our understanding of long-term economic growth, potentially rivaling the influence of institutions.

Moreover, Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) highlight that the margins of error in these estimates are based on subjective estimates of the possible margins of error of the underlying data. This implies that the accuracy and reliability of these estimates could be improved through better data collection and analysis, further underscoring the potential impact of data quality on long-run economic growth.

In this context, it is worth noting that Kuznets (1973, p.1) also emphasized the importance of data in understanding economic growth. He argued that long-term economic growth is a complex process influenced by various factors, including data availability and accuracy. While Kuznets did not focus on the Maddison Project, his work underscores the potential significance of data quality in shaping our understanding of long-run economic growth.

Thus, the Maddison Project, through its focus on improving data quality and extending and revising Maddison's estimates, offers a compelling rival explanation to the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. By improving our understanding of data quality and its impact on economic growth, this collaboration among scholars could potentially provide a more nuanced view of the factors influencing long-term economic development.

The quality of institutions, particularly as guaranteed by guilds, played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium within late medieval Europe. This claim is substantiated by van Zanden's work, which argues that these institutions emerged in this period, taking care of apprentice contracts and ensuring fair execution (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The guilds, according to van Zanden, had a beneficial effect on the intergenerational transfer of skills, despite past criticisms that they monopolized these skills and attempted to keep their relative pay above market levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

Van Zanden's work also underscores that the skill premium in western Europe decreased significantly compared to most other parts of the world economy during the late medieval period. This global pattern, he suggests, can be explained by factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (van Zanden 2009, p.1).

The role of guilds in shaping the skill premium in late medieval Europe extends to their impact on the intergenerational transfer of skills. This transfer, in turn, has implications for long-term economic performance. Further research could explore the specific mechanisms through which guilds facilitated this transfer and the extent to which these mechanisms varied across different regions within Europe.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue, with scholars agreeing on the importance of institutions in this process (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. The specific ways in which these institutions impact economic growth remain a topic of discussion.

One stream of scholarship focuses on the role of guilds in late medieval Europe in shaping the skill premium, which in turn impacts long-term economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight the role of institutions in promoting investment and the rule of law, while Frankema (2012, p.2) emphasizes the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries in facilitating formal education.

Simultaneously, another body of research explores the impact of colonial rule on economic growth, particularly in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the demographic and epidemiological characteristics of regions influenced the European presence, leading to the establishment of institutions that were either unproductive or extractive, hindering long-term development. Conversely, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the contributions of African women to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods, suggesting that the impact of colonial rule may not have been uniformly negative.

The disagreement between these two streams of scholarship lies in the assessment of the net impact of colonial rule on long-term economic growth. While Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the European presence often led to the establishment of institutions that were poorly suited for long-term development, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth despite the challenges posed by colonial rule.

A key historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the comparison of regions with different demographic and epidemiological characteristics. For instance, regions with larger populations that could resist European encroachment, such as West Africa, may have developed institutions more conducive to long-term growth, as suggested by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15). On the other hand, regions with high mortality rates among Europeans, such as the Atlantic coast of Africa, may have seen the establishment of institutions that were less conducive to long-term growth, as argued by Hopkins (2009, p.18).
