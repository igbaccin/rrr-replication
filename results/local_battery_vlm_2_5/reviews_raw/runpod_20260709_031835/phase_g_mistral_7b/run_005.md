The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in the study of economic development, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on the causal mechanisms and the historical contexts that underpin this relationship. At stake is an understanding of how institutions, particularly those established during European colonization, have influenced current income levels across countries, and the implications for policy and development strategies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels. They posit that the differences in colonization strategies, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others resulted in extractive states, have shaped the economic trajectories of various countries. Austin (2008, p.2) concurs with the importance of institutions but cautions against oversimplifying causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a distinct but related perspective, focusing on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight the role of women in agriculture, particularly in the production of cash crops, and the impact of colonial rule on the subordinate status of women in access to land and the codification of customary law.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He emphasizes the need to understand the complexities of institutional development and the role of factors such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation and population dynamics in shaping the quality of institutions in Africa.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) propose that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They contend that the slave trade had a profound impact on intergroup trust, both through the external environment and the internal norms of mistrust. Frankema (2012, p.2) further explores the role of British rule in sub-Saharan Africa, arguing that British rule facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

These studies collectively highlight the complex interplay of historical, political, and cultural factors in shaping institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth. The disagreement lies in the specific causal mechanisms and the historical contexts that underpin this relationship, as well as the implications for policy and development strategies. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for crafting effective policies aimed at fostering economic growth and development in Africa and beyond.

The impact of European colonization on current income levels is a subject of intense debate among scholars. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the differences in income levels across countries today.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to different associated institutions. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled in the colonies and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole.

They argue that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that this culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

Institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as argued by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and supported by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30), played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, challenging the neoclassical view that efficiency is the primary driver. This perspective emphasizes the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider institutional framework, which contributed to early industrialization and capitalism.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) posits that the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions. This interlinked institutional system, according to Ogilvie, may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

Their success was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, not simply the rules governing economic exchange, but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed.

A critical political factor, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) suggest, is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment, they argue, is crucial in ensuring the security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. In terms of the very simple political model outlined in North (1989, p.9), the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years-War, indicating a commitment to secure rights and property.

Hornbeck's analysis reveals a significant role of barbed wire in shaping agricultural development in the American Plains. This innovative fencing technology, widely adopted in the region, facilitated the enforcement of property rights and reduced costs associated with land protection.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) demonstrates that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire was a pivotal factor in promoting agricultural development. By the late 19th century, herd laws, which previously served as a means of land protection, became less prevalent, indicating the effectiveness of barbed wire in enforcing property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). Moreover, Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides empirical evidence showing that counties that adopted barbed wire experienced a significant increase in improved farmland after its introduction, as compared to non-adopting counties (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

The adoption of barbed wire facilitated property rights enforcement and reduced land protection costs. This, in turn, encouraged agricultural development in the American Plains.

The consolidation of colonial rule, particularly British rule, played a significant role in shaping economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa by facilitating large-scale infrastructure projects and formal education. This educational expansion, in turn, contributed to economic growth.

Frankema's (2012, p.2) study shows that the consolidation of colonial rule created a conducive environment for large-scale infrastructure projects, which were instrumental in economic development. The paper highlights the role of malaria ecology in determining the feasibility of malaria in a given area, which reflects endogenous supply-demand responses and African demand responses to consolidated colonial rule (Frankema, 2012, p.17).

Moreover, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) provide evidence that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. Their research indicates that the East-West divide in long-term wage income growth trajectories results from both distinct economic dynamics (commercialization) and varying degrees of income inequality, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012, p.28).

The growth of useful knowledge, as argued by Mokyr (2005, p.54) and supported by Goldin (2006, p.7), plays a crucial role in long-run economic growth. Goldin (2006, p.7), on the other hand, demonstrates the significant impact of women's increased involvement in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century, which she refers to as a quiet revolution (Goldin 2006, p.7).

Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge was primarily driven by the Enlightenment idea that individuals working for their own good also contributed to the welfare of society, unless they chose to engage in redistribution and rent seeking.

Goldin (2006, p.7) qualifies this downstream implication by demonstrating that the shifts from one phase to the next in the quiet revolution were due to various exogenous changes, including the increased relative demand for female office workers, the growth of educational institutions, changes in household production technology, and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women (Goldin 2006, p.3).

This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they not only provide the foundation for growth but also enable the efficient allocation of resources and the development of new opportunities.

Bolt and van Zanden's collaborative work underscores the ongoing efforts to refine and extend Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This collaboration, known as the Maddison Project, aims to produce and update global economic data, as Maddison's strategy was to generate numbers even if a solid basis for them did not always exist (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

The Maddison Project's objective is grounded in the recognition that estimates of national accounts in the past, especially in the more distant past, are subject to certain margins of error. These errors often stem from partial data and certain assumptions about the links between these data and the economic activities they represent (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The further one goes back in time, the larger the margins of error will probably be.

This ongoing effort to refine and extend Maddison's estimates is significant because it addresses a key challenge in the study of long-term economic growth: the paucity of data, especially for earlier periods and regions like Asia. This expansion and refinement of data sets enhance the accuracy of our understanding of long-term economic growth and enable more nuanced regional and period comparisons.

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation in economic growth, by emphasizing the role of indigenous agency in shaping institutional choices that lead to long-term economic growth.

The paper suggests that the decline in African economic growth during the colonial period was influenced by African leaders' and communities' decisions in response to colonial rule, in addition to European exploitation.

The paper argues that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. For instance, in some cases, it was in the interests of colonial regimes to support, rather than simply extract from, African economic enterprise.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically restructured to preserve social distinctions, fostering an expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). This restructuring served to assert colonial authority and maintain control over potentially rebellious European subordinates (Stoler 1989, p.4).

For instance, the fear of racial mixing and the protection of white prestige were not mere justifications for colonial domination but were crucial components of a class-based logic that controlled both indigenous populations and European colonials. This logic extended to the creation of social communities within the colonies, which provided a context for colonial authority and the maintenance of power relations.

Moreover, Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights the omission of internal political tensions among the European colonizers in existing studies on colonial life. The focus on social rankings of colonial life and the analysis of colonial policy often overlooked the everyday practice of colonial domination. However, Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) offer valuable insights into the distinct class interests within the colonial state, providing a more nuanced understanding of the dynamics of colonial power.

In the study of institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) offer insights into the persistence of inequality in New World societies. Their research suggests a mechanism whereby greater inequality led to institutions that favored the elite, restricting competition and contributing to the long-term perpetuation of inequality.

This, in turn, may have affected growth over the long term, as elites were better able to maintain their status but at the expense of broader economic development.

Furthermore, the persistence of these institutions over time can be seen as a downstream implication of the initial condition of extreme inequality. As Engerman, Haber, and Sokoloff (2000) demonstrate, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be traced back to the colonial period, where the Spanish Crown's policies favored the creation of large estates and the exclusion of the indigenous population from ownership and control of land. This legacy of inequality has persisted and continues to shape economic outcomes in the region.

This process, they argue, played a pivotal role in shaping gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa.

The impact of Africa's slave trades on current economic development, as suggested by Nunn, reveals a significant and lasting influence on the development of institutions in Africa. Nunn (2008, p.2) posits that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, had a more enduring impact on economic development than the relatively brief period of official colonial rule (approximately 75 years).

These disruptions hindered the development of effective institutions, such as centralized governments, markets, and trading networks, which are crucial for long-run economic growth.

Supporting this claim, Nunn (2008, p.19) refers to the case of the Kongo kingdom, which, due to its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the slave trades may have impeded the development of such societies, as they were often targeted and disrupted by slave traders.

The disruption of traditional institutions and the slow development of effective ones had far-reaching implications for economic growth in Africa. This underdevelopment, in turn, has been perpetuated by the legacy of the slave trades, which left a lasting impact on the continent's political and economic landscape.

The perception of static custom in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa, as argued by Peters (2004, p.5), can advantage certain groups by keeping officials ignorant of changing practices. This misconception, perpetuated during the final decades of colonial rule, has significant implications for understanding the dynamics of land tenure and social stratification in the region.

Peters (2004, p.5) demonstrates that the push for agricultural development during this period fueled overt dissatisfaction among agricultural officers who saw African landholding practices as communal and inhibiting individual investment. This dissatisfaction, however, masked processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation. Critics like Platteau (1996, 2000) and Amanor (2001) have pointed out that such unproblematic visions of African land tenure systems ignore considerable social inequality and encroachment by some groups on others.

Furthermore, Peters (2004, p.5) highlights that national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers, further complicating the landscape of land tenure and power dynamics. Ribot (2000) and Carney and Farrington (1998), among others, have shown how these elites manipulate the system to their advantage.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing view that African economic growth prior to the 1980s was primarily driven by European colonial interventions. Instead, they argue that some African economies had attained per capita incomes above subsistence levels comparable to the largest Asian economies before the 1980s. This assertion challenges early economic histories of Africa, which attributed economic growth above subsistence to European-introduced factors such as railways, malaria prophylaxis, and British rule (McPhee, 1926).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) contend that the lack of improvement in per capita incomes during the latter half of the 20th century, as shown in most studies using data from 1950 or 1960, is misleading. They point out that in other regions, new contributions to historical national accounting have provided insights into growth patterns over centuries, altering the history of economic divergence and convergence before the industrial revolution (Broadberry, 2021). Furthermore, the field of African economic history has undergone a 'renaissance' due to newly digitized data sources and innovative techniques (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016).

One specific mechanism that supports Broadberry and Gardner's (2022) claim is the existence of complex trade networks in West Africa before European colonization. For instance, Adekunle (2011) provides evidence of the trans-Saharan trade routes that connected West Africa with North Africa and the Mediterranean, contributing to the region's economic growth. Similarly, Lovejoy (2000) highlights the importance of the Atlantic slave trade in driving economic development in West Africa.

Moreover, the impact of these trade networks on African economies can be seen in the development of urban centers. The existence of these urban centers indicates a level of economic complexity and organization that challenges the notion that African economies were solely reliant on European colonial interventions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping the economic growth of less developed countries. According to Kuznets, for these nations to participate effectively in the modern economic system, a combination of technological advancement, institutional reform, and access to tested data are essential.

Kuznets argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions and social views, such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery, which prevailed in earlier times. He contends that for economic growth, these outdated institutions must be replaced by more appropriate ones (Kuznets 1973, p.1).

Furthermore, Kuznets asserts that modern technology necessitates rapid changes in the location and structure of the labor force, as well as in the relations among occupational status groups and between labor force and total population (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These changes, he suggests, drive the dynamic forces of modern economic growth and lead to continuous expansion into less developed areas.

In qualifying this assertion, Platteau (2004) provides evidence of the negotiability of landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa. He demonstrates that land tenure systems can evolve in response to changing economic conditions, thus facilitating the adoption of modern technology and promoting economic growth (Platteau, 2004). This evolution of land tenure systems, in turn, can create a more conducive environment for economic development, as demonstrated by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) in their examination of economic levels in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a crucial factor in the British industrial revolution, contributing to a shift away from reliance on skilled adult male labor. Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that this perspective offers a more accurate understanding of the industrial revolution than one focused solely on men and high wages. Humphries's evidence reveals that a significant number of families during this period experienced family breakdown and male abandonment, which resulted in a substantial group of families whose fathers were absent, either deceased or absent due to war, empire building, or labor mobility (Humphries, 2013, p. 16).

This shift in labor composition had downstream implications for the industrial revolution. For instance, the increased availability of female and child labor led to a decrease in the demand for skilled adult male labor, potentially causing a reduction in their wages (Humphries 2013, p.16). This change in labor dynamics did not occur in isolation.
