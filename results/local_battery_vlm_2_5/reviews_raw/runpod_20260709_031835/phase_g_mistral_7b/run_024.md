The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development studies, with significant intellectual stakes. The debate revolves around the impact of European colonization on contemporary income levels across countries, and the role of institutions in this process. While some argue that European colonization established institutions that fostered growth and investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), others contend that the new economic history of Africa reveals a different picture, where European presence led to the development of extractive institutions detrimental to long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

In regions such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, European colonizers established extractive states, rapidly transferring resources to the metropole. These findings suggest that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries.

On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods have been overlooked (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). They highlight the role of women in the production of cash crops, such as cocoa, which propelled economic revolutions in regions like the Gold Coast. Under colonial rule, the codification of customary law reinforced women's marginalization in land access (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

In the context of Africa, Hopkins (2009, p.18) asserts that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18). He argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) focus on the impact of the slave trade in Africa on intergroup trust, arguing that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). They find that both internal norms of mistrust and the external environment play a role in this phenomenon, although the internal channel is more significant.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They also highlight that political power was important to the planters in the West Indies and other elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) contends that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940 (Frankema 2012, p.2). This finding suggests that the influence of European traders and missionaries played a significant role in the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the institutional changes resulting from different European colonial strategies have a significant impact on current income levels across countries, and account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) contend that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They argue that the European colonizers adopted very different strategies, with different associated institutions. In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In the other extreme, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also demonstrate that the results are consistent across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and the efficiency of the judiciary (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). North (1989, p.9) supports this argument by asserting that political and economic institutions play a significant role in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants (North 1989, p.9).

This finding suggests that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, led to lower wages and slower economic growth.

North and Weingast's seminal work (North and Weingast 1989, p.30) argues that the establishment of representative government, with an explicit charter of rights and a legislature accountable to the electorate, provides political protection for property rights, which in turn fosters long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by Ogilvie's analysis (Ogilvie 2007, p.27), who contends that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider institutional framework is crucial for economic development. The European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets for savings, and collective insurance and pension provision were interconnected in pre-industrial Europe, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Furthermore, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that the success of seventeenth-century England was due to its political institutions that ensured secure rights, as evidenced by the thriving capital markets of the time. This political factor, the commitment to and binding by rules, is a crucial condition for economic growth and the development of markets, as Ogilvie's work (Ogilvie 2007, p.12) also suggests.

In this context, the political commitment to secure rights and the rule of law can be seen as a downstream implication of the broader institutional framework that fosters economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of examining entire institutional frameworks, rather than individual institutions in isolation, to understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the notion that African economies were stagnant prior to European colonialism by providing evidence of economic growth in some Sub-Saharan African countries before the 20th century. This suggests a more complex and dynamic history (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that many Sub-Saharan African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early 20th century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s.

The 20th century data used in most studies of African growth, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the 20th century, is a significant limitation.

This complex history of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 20th century underscores the importance of examining entire institutional frameworks, rather than individual institutions in isolation, to understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This finding is supported by the work of North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that the character of institutions is shaped by the social and political context, which can encourage or hinder economic growth (citing North & Weingast, 1989).

In this context, the political commitment to secure rights and the rule of law can be seen as a downstream implication of the broader institutional framework that fosters economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of examining entire institutional frameworks, rather than individual institutions in isolation, to understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

This complex history of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 20th century, as well as the importance of examining entire institutional frameworks, sets the stage for the examination of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in the context of pre-industrial Europe.

Humphries (2013, p.16) suggests that the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a significant factor in shaping the British industrial revolution. Humphries's study reveals that a considerable number of working-class families in the eighteenth century were affected by marital instability, male abandonment, and family breakdown, leading to a significant group of families whose fathers, while yet-living, were not present (Humphries 2013, p.16). This finding is consistent with other historians' depictions of the period as one of marital instability (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Inventors often designed inventions to reduce the need for skilled adult workers, as they were aware of the availability of cheaper labor in the form of women and children (Humphries 2013, p.16). This trend is further supported by the observation that the high wage economy was only as reliable as the men who headed the households, and these men's ability and commitment to provide support could prove frail (Humphries 2013, p.16).

The impact of this labor availability on the British industrial revolution had downstream implications. For instance, the increased use of child labor in the textile industry led to concerns about child welfare and the introduction of child labor laws (Humphries 2013, p.2). These laws, in turn, affected the structure of the labor market and the dynamics of economic growth in the industrial revolution era.

Kuznets (1973, p.1), Mokyr (2005, p.54), and van Zanden (2009, p.19) collectively argue that technological advancement, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are critical factors for economic growth. This perspective, while not denying the importance of institutions, offers a competing explanation to the claim that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions and social structures, necessitating rapid changes in economic structure, family formation, urbanization, and societal views. For instance, the advent of steam and electric power demanded large-scale plants, which were incompatible with family enterprises, illiteracy, or slavery, all of which were prevalent in earlier times.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) supports this view by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He argues that during the Industrial Revolution, intellectual societies, concerned with issues of political economy, law and order, and efficient taxes, contributed significantly to economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further elaborates on this theme by focusing on the late medieval period. He suggests that in western Europe, the skill premium decreased significantly compared to other parts of the world economy due to factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors. The guilds, which emerged to address issues related to apprenticeship and skill transfer, played a crucial role in this development.

An interesting downstream implication of this stream is the emphasis on the role of intellectual societies and the growth of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. This is particularly relevant when considering the Industrial Revolution, as these societies were instrumental in fostering the ideas and innovations that underpinned the rapid economic growth of the period.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27)(Nunn 2008, p.2) argue that the impact of the slave trade on African culture and economy may have had a lasting influence on current economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists today. This claim is grounded in the evidence that the slave trade, by its very nature, disrupted social structures and fostered a culture of mistrust among African communities.

They find that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the slave trade significantly shaped the internal norms of African societies.

He highlights that the slave trades lasted for nearly 500 years, compared to the relatively short period of colonial rule, and argues that the slave trades disrupted social structures and fostered a culture of mistrust.

The downstream implication of this culture of mistrust, as suggested by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), is that it can have a significant impact on economic development. Trust is a crucial factor in economic development, as it facilitates cooperation, encourages investment, and promotes economic growth. Therefore, the culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade may have hindered economic development in Africa.

Frankema's paper (2012) argues that this influence facilitated education primarily, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

Frankema's study reveals that the spread of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was not solely due to the direct investment of colonial governments. Instead, he contends that the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries was instrumental in this process. The missionaries, in particular, were active in establishing schools and promoting literacy among African populations (Frankema 2012, p.2).

This subordination, in turn, may have hindered the full potential of women's contributions to economic development during the colonial period.

The impact of British influence on education and the perpetuation of gender inequalities in access to resources are interconnected themes in these studies.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly increased agricultural development and contributed to long-run economic growth (Hornbeck 2010, p.8; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This innovation, which provided a cheap and efficient means of fencing, reduced costs of land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement, key factors in agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides compelling evidence for this claim. In the aftermath of the Homestead Act of 1862, the American Plains witnessed a surge in settlement and farming, leading to overgrazing and land degradation. To address this issue, herd laws were enacted in several states to regulate the movement of livestock, but their implementation was ineffective (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The introduction of barbed wire in the late 19th century, however, revolutionized land management.

By enabling farmers to protect their land from overgrazing and encroachment, barbed wire promoted sustainable agricultural practices (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This shift in agricultural practices, facilitated by barbed wire, had far-reaching implications. As agriculture became more productive, the region's economy grew, leading to increased population and urbanization. The subsequent development of infrastructure, such as railroads and markets, further boosted economic growth in the American Plains (see Bolt and van Zanden for a broader discussion of the role of infrastructure in economic growth).

In this context, policy interventions that aim to 'bring the state back in' could potentially address these historical institutional deficiencies by strengthening the capacity of African governments to provide public goods and foster economic development. The implications of these policy shifts extend beyond institutional reforms, as they have the potential to significantly impact poverty reduction in Africa. By addressing historical institutional deficiencies and promoting the development of productive institutions, policy interventions could contribute to economic growth and ultimately help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

It also highlights the need for a nuanced approach to policy design, taking into account the specific institutional deficiencies of each region and the potential impact of policy interventions on long-term economic growth and development.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, as argued by Goldin (2006, p.7). This shift, as Goldin (2006, p.7) notes, was exemplified by the experiences of Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, who, despite graduating near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class, found it difficult to secure a lawyer position and instead took a job as a legal secretary.

The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, as Goldin (2006, p.3) explains. These changes include the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century which made "nice" jobs available, and the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s which greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women.

This shift in the role of women in the United States had significant downstream implications. For instance, the growing participation of women in the labor force contributed to the rise of consumer culture in the mid-20th century, as documented by other scholars (e.g., (Goldin 2006, p.7)). This development was further facilitated by the expansion of credit and advertising industries that catered to women's purchasing power.

Bolt and van Zanden's collaboration, a part of the Maddison Project, significantly extends and revises Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This scholarly endeavor aligns with the topic's claim that institutions are fundamental for long-run economic growth. The project's approach acknowledges the inherent uncertainties in estimating national accounts, especially in historical contexts, but it strives for objectivity by using subjective estimates of the possible margins of error of the underlying data (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, E0033).

The Maddison Project's strategy, inspired by Maddison's pioneering work, aims to induce scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, E0034). This collaborative effort has led to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. The project's focus on the early modern period and Asia is particularly significant, as these regions have been historically underrepresented in economic growth studies, thereby reinforcing the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

One downstream implication of the Maddison Project's work is the potential for a more nuanced understanding of the economic development in Asia. For instance, the Hitotshibashi team, a part of the Maddison Project, has carried out detailed studies on Asian national accounts, which could offer valuable insights into the economic growth patterns in the region (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, E0033). As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, it is likely to contribute further to our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of Asia.
