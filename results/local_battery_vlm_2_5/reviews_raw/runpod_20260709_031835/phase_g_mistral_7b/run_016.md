The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for our understanding of global economic disparities. At the heart of this debate lies the question of how institutions established during European colonization have influenced current income levels across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels. They posit that in colonies where Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered. In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where Europeans established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, economic growth was stunted (Acemoglu et al., 2001, p.27).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a complementary perspective, focusing on the role of African women in economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and the de-privileging of women in access to land in the context of cash crops was reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasize that for economic growth, a country requires advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a broader supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries. Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology is incompatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, all of which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world. He contends that effective participation in the modern economic system necessitates rapid changes in labor force location and structure, as well as in the relations among occupational status groups and between labor force and total population.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) supports this notion by asserting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He highlights that during the Industrial Revolution, intellectual societies concerned with political economy, free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and efficient, less distortionary taxes proliferated. Mokyr further posits that the Dutch Republic and Venice had early institutional foundations supporting economic growth, while Spain had a late institutional basis compared to other countries that experienced the Industrial Revolution.

In terms of downstream implications, Kuznets (1973, p.1) suggests that high aggregate growth rates are associated with rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn lead to changes in other aspects of society, such as family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role and the measure of his achievement in society.

The slave trade in Africa, by fostering a culture of mistrust, may have hindered the development of institutions conducive to long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.2). This culture of mistrust, they suggest, may have impeded the development of trustworthy institutions, a crucial factor for economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) complements this argument by showing that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. This education, in turn, could have played a role in shaping trustworthy institutions, as education has been linked to the development of trust (Knack & Keefer, 1997; Fafchamps, 2006; Tabellini, 2007; Algan & Cahuc, 2010). The impact of education on trust may have been limited due to the culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade, as suggested by Nunn and Wantchekon.

The downstream implications of this mechanism are significant. Trustworthy institutions are essential for long-run economic growth, as they provide the legal and social framework necessary for markets to function effectively (North & Weingast, 1989; North, 1990). The culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade may have hindered the development of such institutions, thereby limiting economic growth in Africa.

Furthermore, the persistence of this culture of mistrust may have had long-lasting effects. This persistence of mistrust could have hindered the development of modern institutions, such as democratic governments and efficient markets, further limiting economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) provide a compelling account of the long-term impact of inequality on institutions in New World societies. They argue that greater inequality in these societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run.

The authors suggest that where there was extreme inequality, and institutions advantaged elites and limited the access of much of the population to economic opportunities, members of elites were better able to maintain their elite status over time.

A supporting source, Engerman, Haber, and Sokoloff (2000), further elaborate on this mechanism by demonstrating how the unequal distribution of land in Latin America led to the emergence of a landed elite that controlled the political and economic systems, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality.

The implications of this mechanism are far-reaching. As Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) suggest, the path of institutional development may in turn have affected growth. Where there was extreme inequality, and institutions advantaged elites, the economic opportunities for the majority of the population were limited, potentially hindering economic growth over the long run.

This stream of literature underscores the significance of inequality in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic growth. The findings suggest that in societies where inequality was extreme, the institutions that emerged favored elites, restricting competition and potentially hindering economic growth.

Simson (2019, p.11) challenges the assumption that post-independence budget policies in Africa were unproductive or patronage-driven. Simson's analysis reveals that in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, these institutional arrangements led to high debt burdens, which may have hindered long-run economic growth.

One specific mechanism that emerges from Simson's study is the shift in military spending post-independence. Under colonial rule, the metropolis subsidized much of the defense of the colonies, making defense an imperial matter rather than a responsibility of individual colonies (Simson 2019, p.11). Domestic military spending began to grow in the independence period and escalated towards the end of the 1960s and 1970s in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent.

Another downstream implication is the geographic distribution of employment. From the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage. Changes occurred not only in the composition of employment but also the private returns to public employment (Simson 2019, p.11). Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly, while jobs increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions.

These findings suggest that post-independence budget policies in Africa, while potentially leading to high debt burdens, may not have been as unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks as previously assumed. The high-level allocation of wage resources and the shift in military spending post-independence have significant implications for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) argues that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly increased agricultural development. This transformation was facilitated by reduced costs of land protection and enhanced property rights enforcement, which in turn fostered economic growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides empirical evidence to support this claim. In the early 19th century, the American Plains were primarily used for grazing by nomadic herds. The introduction of barbed wire in the mid-19th century allowed farmers to secure their land, preventing livestock from wandering onto neighboring properties. This led to a more efficient use of land, as farmers could now cultivate crops on previously inaccessible areas.

The paper demonstrates this effect through an analysis of herd laws, which regulated the movement of livestock in Kansas. Prior to 1880, many counties in Kansas had herd laws, which aimed to restrict grazing and protect farmland. These laws were largely ineffective due to the difficulty of enforcing them on vast, open plains. After the introduction of barbed wire, herd laws became more effective, as farmers could now define and protect their property boundaries.

Counties that adopted barbed wire experienced a significant increase in the improved fraction of farmland between 1880 and 1890, compared to counties that did not adopt barbed wire. This suggests that the introduction of barbed wire facilitated property rights enforcement and contributed to agricultural development in the American Plains.

In addition to facilitating agricultural development, the widespread adoption of barbed wire also had implications for the political economy of the American West. As farmers gained more control over their land, they were able to establish more stable communities, which in turn led to the growth of towns and cities. This process of settlement and urbanization had far-reaching consequences for the political and economic development of the United States.

Goldin (2006, p.7) highlights that the increased demand for female office workers was a significant driver of the changes in women's roles in the United States during the past century. This shift was primarily due to exogenous changes, such as the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s, which increased the supply of potential office workers (Goldin 2006, p.3).

The growth of educational institutions for women, in particular, played a crucial role in this transformation. This increase in educational attainment made women more qualified for office work, which in turn increased their demand in the labor market.

However, it is important to note that the increased demand for female office workers did not necessarily lead to equal opportunities in all sectors. As Rona Jaffe's best-selling novel and movie, The Best of Everything (1958), illustrates, even graduates from prestigious institutions like Radcliffe often assumed secretarial positions with the hopes of becoming editors (Goldin 2006, p.7). This highlights that while the increased demand for female office workers was a significant driver of change, other barriers, such as discrimination and lack of opportunities, continued to limit women's roles in the workplace.

The European class cultures in colonies were strategically restructured to preserve social hierarchies, thereby reinforcing imperial control. This reconfiguration, as argued by Stoler (1989, p.6) and Peters (2004, p.5), was not merely a matter of imposing European norms on the colonized, but also an intricate process of managing internal class dynamics among the colonizers themselves.

For instance, she points out that the fear of the Other, the preoccupation with white prestige, and the obsession with protecting European women from sexual assault by Asian and black males were not justifications for continued European rule and white supremacy, but were part of a critical class-based logic. These directives aimed at maintaining order among potentially subversive white colonials and were instrumental in the apparatus that kept them in line.

On the other hand, Peters (2004, p.5) argues that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa masked processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation. He highlights that agricultural officials, in their push for agricultural development during the final decade of colonial rule, expressed significant dissatisfaction with African landholding practices, which were perceived as communal and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture. This dissatisfaction led to schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement designed to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming.

Peters (2004, p.5) further explains that these schemes often led to social inequality within many customary systems, with national and local elites capturing the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers. This highlights a downstream implication of the European class cultures' restructuring: the reinforcement of power dynamics not only between the colonizers and the colonized, but also among the colonizers themselves.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a key factor in the British industrial revolution, contributing to a shift away from reliance on skilled adult male labor (Humphries 2013, p.16). The paper substantiates this claim by pointing out that a significant group of families during this period had fathers who were absent, either due to death or abandonment, leaving a large number of women and children to take up employment in the industry (Humphries 2013, p.16).

This shift had far-reaching implications. For instance, the increased availability of female and child labor enabled the expansion of industries that were traditionally male-dominated, such as textiles and mining (Ogilvie 2006). This change was facilitated by the declining cost of maintaining families, which made it economically viable for families to have more children and for women to participate in the workforce (Ogilvie 2006).

Moreover, the availability of cheap labor led to increased productivity and efficiency in the industries. For example, the use of child labor in the textile industry allowed for faster and more intricate work, leading to the production of finer and more diverse textiles (Ogilvie 2006). Similarly, the use of women in the textile industry increased the overall output, as women were often more efficient at tasks such as spinning and weaving (Ogilvie 2006).

However, this shift also had negative consequences. The exploitation of child labor, in particular, was widespread and led to numerous abuses and poor working conditions (Ogilvie 2006). Furthermore, the increased reliance on female labor in the home led to a decline in the quality of domestic life, as women were often forced to work long hours both in the home and in the factory (Ogilvie 2006).

These institutions have been criticized for monopolizing skills and attempting to maintain a higher relative pay above market-clearing levels, potentially counterbalancing their beneficial effects on skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Nonetheless, the quality of these institutions, as van Zanden argues, played a significant role in shaping the skill premium, a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.19).
