The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of considerable debate among economists, with a particular focus on the impact of European colonization. This literature review delves into the various perspectives on this topic, highlighting the key disagreements and the evidence supporting each position.

At the heart of the debate is the question of whether the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the different colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, establishing institutions that encouraged investment and the rule of law, leading to economic growth. Conversely, in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states that transferred resources rapidly to the metropole, hindering long-term development.

However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge this view by highlighting the significant contributions made by African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced their subordinate status.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) adds to this discussion by emphasizing the implications of the new economic history of Africa for policies affecting institutional reforms and poverty reduction. He suggests that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments, and that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer another perspective, suggesting that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They argue that the internal norms of mistrust, rather than the external environment, were the primary driver of this culture.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They highlight the importance of political power in enabling Europeans to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

By establishing institutions that enforced low wages and resource extraction, European powers ensured their access to resources, thereby facilitating economic growth in their home countries at the expense of the colonies.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) collectively argue that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology, institutional, and ideological adjustments are essential. These factors create the necessary conditions for modern economic participation and growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions and social structures, such as family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which prevailed in earlier times. To participate effectively in the modern economic system, a country needs rapid changes in its location and structure, in the relations among occupational status groups, and even in the relations between labor force and total population. These changes, in turn, drive societal changes in family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role in society.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) supports this notion, asserting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He highlights that intellectual societies, such as scientific societies, were more prevalent in countries that experienced economic growth during the Industrial Revolution. These societies were concerned with issues of political economy, including free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's effect on commerce, and more efficient, less distortionary taxes. Above all, they promoted the Enlightenment idea that when individuals work for their own good, they normally also contribute to the welfare of society, unless they choose to engage in redistribution and rent seeking.

In this context, it can be inferred that the advancement of technology and the development of institutions and ideologies that support modern economic participation are interconnected. This suggests that the development of institutions and ideologies that encourage the growth of useful knowledge could be a key factor in promoting economic growth in less developed countries.

The implication of this stream is that the promotion of modern economic participation through technological advancement, institutional, and ideological adjustments can drive economic growth in less developed countries. However, the specific mechanisms through which this occurs, and the challenges in implementing these changes, are topics for further exploration in the literature.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that the Maddison Project, an ongoing collaborative effort among scholars, advocates for the continuation of extending and revising Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, even when a solid basis for these numbers does not exist. This strategy, they suggest, is aimed at improving our understanding of historical economies.

These estimates are often based on incomplete data and certain assumptions about the economic activities they represent (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). However, the Project members recognize that these margins of error can be subjective and have experimented with various methods to quantify them (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

By producing estimates with acknowledged uncertainties, the Project induces scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development more accurately. This approach, as they suggest, has been highly successful, leading to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s.

This strategy, moreover, has downstream implications for our understanding of economic growth in various historical contexts. Furthermore, the Maddison Project's approach encourages scholars to challenge and refine existing estimates, which can lead to more accurate and comprehensive accounts of economic growth in the past.
