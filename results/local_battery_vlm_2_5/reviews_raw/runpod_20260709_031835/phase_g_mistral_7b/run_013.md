The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant intellectual stakes at play. At the heart of this discourse lies a persistent disagreement over the causal factors that drive economic development, particularly in the context of former European colonies. Some argue that institutions established during European colonization have a profound impact on current income levels across countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Others contend that factors such as population demographics, disease, and colonial governance structures also play crucial roles (Hopkins 2009, p.10; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

In regions where settlers established rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth flourished. In extractive colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast, Europeans established states designed for rapid resource transfer to the metropole, hindering long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their subordinate status in customary law under colonial rule further restricted their access to land and resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). These restrictions, in turn, may have hindered economic growth.

The debate between these positions is far from resolved, with each side presenting compelling arguments.

Secure political and economic institutions, particularly those of representative government, significantly shape long-run economic growth, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.30; 1989, p.2). This claim is substantiated by the historical example of seventeenth-century England, where the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The relationship between the benefits derived from rule specification and the costs of measurement is at the heart of this model, with the costs of measuring the separable dimensions of goods and services being critical (North 1989, p.3).

The implications of this stream extend beyond England's financial revolution.

The institutional changes resulting from European colonization significantly impact current income levels, particularly in former colonies where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutions and property rights established during colonialism have far-reaching effects on economic development.

Their findings suggest that these institutional differences account for a significant portion of current income differences across countries.

In a subsequent paper (2002), Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutional changes during European colonization account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. They note that political power was crucial for European elites in extracting resources, as seen in the Atlantic slave trade, plantation agriculture, and the rule of the British East India Company (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

The work of Nunn (2008, p.2) adds a qualifying detail to this stream. He focuses on Africa's slave trades, which he argues had a negative impact on current economic development in Africa. Nunn points out that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that the institutional foundations established by some African societies may have mitigated the negative impact of the slave trades.

In the realm of pre-industrial Europe, the role of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions emerges as a significant factor (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.27)). Ogilvie posits that these conflicts, rather than efficiency, played a pivotal role in establishing and maintaining institutional frameworks. The interdependency of various institutions within a wider framework is recognized in several studies of European economic institutions. For instance, the European family system, labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions were interconnected, potentially contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.27)).

Moreover, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000 (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2)) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This finding suggests that in pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts may have been more pronounced, the establishment of institutions that catered to the elite could have had similar effects, shaping the economic landscape in lasting ways.

One implication of this stream of literature is the recognition that studying one institution in isolation may not provide a complete understanding of its impact on economic growth. Instead, it is essential to examine entire institutional frameworks, as their interdependencies can significantly influence economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007 (Ogilvie 2007, p.27)). This insight underscores the need for a comprehensive approach when investigating the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The paper by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue for a revised model of economic growth that recognizes the significant role of indigenous agency in shaping institutional choices, which in turn influence long-run economic growth in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) supports this argument by showing that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments, and that larger populations in certain regions could resist European encroachment and develop progressive institutions.

Furthermore, Austin (2008, p.2) asserts that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and that the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this argument by demonstrating that the Atlantic slave trade reduced the population from a level that was previously relatively high and encouraged the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth.

Mokyr and others contend that the growth of useful knowledge was a significant factor driving long-run economic growth, particularly in the British Isles during the Industrial Revolution. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the rise in scientific societies per capita during the period from 1600 to 1800 was indicative of this trend (Mokyr 2005, p.54). These societies, he suggests, were dedicated to discussing issues related to political economy, such as free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxation. Moreover, Mokyr emphasizes the Enlightenment idea that individual self-interest often contributed to societal welfare, except in cases of redistribution and rent-seeking.

Supporting this claim, Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that the British Isles, among other regions, had an institutional basis that supported this pattern of growth during the Industrial Revolution. He suggests that the Industrial Revolution may not have been a wholly British phenomenon, but it was the taproot of subsequent economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This growth, however, was not always sustainable and was often disrupted by shocks, both man-made and natural.

In this context, it is worth noting that the growth of useful knowledge was not the only factor shaping long-run economic growth. As other streams of literature suggest, the role of secure property rights, European colonialism, distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe, and methodological considerations in the new economic history of Africa all played significant roles in shaping economic growth. However, Mokyr's work underscores the importance of the growth of useful knowledge as a key driver of economic growth, particularly during the Industrial Revolution. This insight, in turn, highlights the need to further explore the relationship between knowledge growth and long-run economic growth in other historical and geographical contexts.

The emergence and quality of institutions in Western Europe during the late medieval period, particularly the guilds, played a significant role in shaping the skill premium, which in turn contributed to long-run economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19). According to van Zanden's paper, the skill premium in western Europe decreased significantly compared to most other parts of the world economy during this period (van Zanden). This decline was attributed to factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

While these institutions have been criticized in the past for monopolizing skills and artificially maintaining relative pay above market clearing levels, their beneficial effects on skill transfer may have outweighed their cartel-forming tendencies (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

This decline implies that the development of institutions in Western Europe during the late medieval period influenced the skill premium and set the stage for sustained economic growth (van Zanden).

In a broader context, the quality of institutions, as demonstrated by the guilds in Western Europe, can be seen as a crucial factor in shaping the skill premium and, consequently, long-run economic growth. It is crucial to examine how these institutions evolved over time and interacted with other factors, such as technological advancements and trade patterns, to fully understand their impact on economic development (van Zanden). This question will be addressed in the subsequent sections.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) argue that collaboration among scholars, as exemplified by the Maddison Project, plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth by extending and refining historical estimates of GDP and population, essential for understanding economic development.

This collaboration focuses on extending and revising Maddison's estimates, particularly for the early modern period and Asia, where data is often incomplete or based on assumptions (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

One implication of this collaborative approach is that it allows for a more detailed understanding of the margins of error in historical economic data. This improved understanding of data reliability is crucial for drawing accurate conclusions about economic growth and development.

The near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.8).

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) study reveals that, prior to the introduction of barbed wire, herd laws were widely implemented in the American Plains to control the movement of livestock and prevent overgrazing. Despite their implementation, these laws were ineffective due to enforcement difficulties, as shown by the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports no longer detailing herd law decisions after 1880 (Hornbeck 2010, E0081).

With the advent of barbed wire, the cost of land protection plummeted, enabling farmers to secure their property more effectively. This, in turn, led to an increase in the improved fraction of farmland and total improved land from 1880 to 1890, as Hornbeck (2010, E0082) shows. This surge in agricultural development, made possible by the adoption of barbed wire, underscores the importance of secure property rights in shaping long-run economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue.

In this context, it is worth noting that the introduction of barbed wire also had implications for the distribution of land and resources in the American Plains. This suggests that the benefits of improved property rights enforcement were not evenly distributed, potentially leading to increased inequality in the region.

The impact of British rule on formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, as suggested by Frankema (2012, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), emerges as a significant antecedent shaping long-run economic growth. The papers argue that British traders and Protestant missionaries played a pivotal role in facilitating the development of formal education in the region before 1940, rather than direct investment by colonial governments.

He emphasizes the role of Protestant missionaries, who were particularly active in areas with favorable malaria ecology and less resistance against colonial rule. The paper also highlights the influence of pre-colonial Islamic influence, state centralization, and native African resistance on the feasibility of malaria in a given area and, consequently, the development of formal education.

They find that real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. Their research suggests that the development of formal education under British rule contributed to the rise in real wages and, by extension, economic growth.

The development of formal education under British rule, as argued by these papers, had downstream implications for the region's economic growth. By providing education to a larger population, the papers suggest that the region saw an increase in the supply of skilled labor, which is a crucial factor in long-run economic growth. Furthermore, the development of formal education might have contributed to the dissemination of knowledge and ideas that could have fostered economic development.

The paper by Simson (2019, p.11) challenges the prevailing assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were unproductive, instead demonstrating that these policies led to high levels of subsidies and interest with significant impacts on resource allocation. In Tanzania, interest payments peaked at 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief.

Furthermore, Simson (2019, p.11) shows that changes in employment composition occurred from the 1980s onward, with jobs increasingly favoring comparatively skilled and female professions. This shift suggests that from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage.

In a related vein, Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. The dissemination of knowledge and ideas, fostered by formal education, could have contributed to the development of more efficient and productive budget policies in these African countries (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

For economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are crucial requirements, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues. Kuznets contends that modern economic growth, with its reliance on steam and electric power and large-scale plants, necessitates a replacement of traditional institutions and social views that were incompatible with these advancements.

Specifically, Kuznets highlights the need for rapid changes in the labor force's location and structure, in the relations among occupational status groups, and in societal norms regarding family formation, urbanization, and the role of individuals in society. This shift, as Kuznets suggests, is a dynamic drive of modern economic growth and has far-reaching social implications.

Moreover, the sequential spread of modern economic growth, facilitated by improvements in transport and communication, means a continuous expansion to less developed areas. This expansion, Kuznets asserts, necessitates a supply of tested data to effectively participate in the modern economic system by the labor force.

Institutions that fostered economic freedom, such as secure property rights and open trade, promoted economic growth (North 1990). On the one hand, institutions that hinder economic growth, such as extractive institutions, must be replaced or reformed. The African slave trade may have had long-lasting negative effects on trust within African societies, contributing to current economic development challenges (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Their findings suggest that the internal mistrust channel is at least twice as large as the external one, implying that the slave trade's impact on trust is primarily driven by internal norms of mistrust rather than external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn & Wantchekon, 2011, p. 27).

This internal mistrust, in turn, has significant downstream implications for economic development. Trust is crucial for economic growth, as it fosters cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and encourages investment (Knack & Keefer, 1997; Fafchamps, 2006; Tabellini, 2007; Algan & Cahuc, 2010). By promoting a culture of mistrust, the African slave trade may have hindered the development of trustworthy institutions and social norms that are essential for economic growth.

Furthermore, the persistence of this mistrust over time can be seen as a self-reinforcing mechanism. As trust levels decline, cooperation becomes more difficult, leading to less investment in social capital and trust-building institutions. This, in turn, further undermines trust, creating a vicious cycle of mistrust and underdevelopment (Putnam, 2000).
