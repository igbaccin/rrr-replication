The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding global economic disparities. At the heart of this discourse lies a contentious question: what factors contribute most to the economic fortunes of societies, and how do these factors interact? This review delves into the literature, synthesizing various streams of scholarship that address this question from different perspectives.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization have a profound impact on current income levels across countries. They posit that colonizers adopted different strategies, leading to varying degrees of rule of law and investment encouragement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that these differences have persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies today.

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women played a significant role in economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their research reveals that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, a situation exacerbated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. The subordinate status of women, thus established, has had lasting effects on economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms, with potential for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) focus on the slave trade in Africa, arguing that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their research suggests that this mistrust operates both through an individual's external environment and through their internal norms of mistrust.

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

In the following sections, we delve deeper into each of these streams, examining the evidence and arguments presented by these scholars, and exploring the implications of their findings for our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions established during European colonization significantly shape current income levels and economic growth across countries, a claim substantiated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and North (1989, p.9). The scholars argue that the differences in colonization strategies led to distinct institutional arrangements, with some colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, fostering rule of law and investment, while others, like the Congo and the Gold Coast, established extractive states to transfer resources back to the metropole.

They argue that the differences in institutional arrangements among colonies can be traced back to the colonization strategies employed by European powers. In their research, they find that countries with stronger institutions, as measured by various indices, have higher per capita incomes today.

Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) show that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. They explain that political power was crucial for European elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force native or African laborers to work for low wages. The scholars argue that these extractive institutions have had lasting effects on the economic growth of these colonies.

He argues that political and economic institutions play a significant role in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants. North emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and efficient institutions for long-term economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) highlight how the codification of customary law under colonial rule, a specific aspect of institutions, significantly shaped gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa.

This subordination, in turn, had far-reaching implications for the economic contributions of African women.

This stream of literature, therefore, underscores the significance of institutions, particularly the codification of customary law under colonial rule, in shaping gender dynamics and economic outcomes in Africa. The deprivileging of African women in access to land and the reinforcement of patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule had lasting effects on the economic growth of African societies. These findings align with the broader theoretical framework provided by North (1989, p.9), who emphasizes the importance of secure property rights and efficient institutions for long-term economic growth.

Understanding the role of institutions in shaping these contributions, as well as their lasting effects on economic growth, is essential for a comprehensive understanding of economic growth in African societies.

Institutional reforms, as argued by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.11), play a significant role in poverty reduction in Africa. Specifically, Hopkins (2009, p.18) challenges the notion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has a significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Instead, he suggests that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still be dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Simson (2019, p.11) supports this claim by examining post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. Contrary to common assumptions, Simson (2019, p.11) finds that these countries' budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Instead, Simson (2019, p.11) shows that from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage.

Furthermore, Simson (2019, p.11) also reveals that changes occurred not only in the composition of employment but also in the private returns to public employment. This suggests that institutional reforms aimed at improving public sector efficiency could have a downstream implication of reducing the distribution of patronage and promoting merit-based recruitment.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa not only had immediate negative impacts but also shaped a lasting culture of mistrust that continues to hinder economic development in Africa today.

Their findings suggest that essentially all of the estimated effect of the slave trade on intergroup trust is not explained by the effect of the slave trade on the trustworthiness of others. Instead, the internal norms of mistrust within African societies were significantly shaped by the slave trade.

Because the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, it was able to support trade with the Europeans. However, the slave trade disrupted these trading networks and undermined the trust necessary for long-term economic growth.

The downstream implication of this mistrust is that it has hindered the development of institutions that promote economic growth. For example, as shown by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.11), institutional reforms aimed at improving public sector efficiency and reducing patronage can have a positive impact on economic growth. The culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade may make it challenging for these reforms to take hold and be effective.

In pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) propose an alternative to the efficiency-based theories commonly associated with North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002). These scholars argue that distributional conflicts offer a more compelling explanation for the development of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework, suggesting that the European family system, labour markets, credit markets, and community-based welfare systems contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

One specific mechanism that these sources jointly establish is the interlinking of institutions within a wider framework. Furthermore, in societies with extreme inequality, institutions advantaged elites and limited the access of much of the population to economic opportunities, thereby maintaining elite status over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).

A downstream implication of this interdependency is that the study of one institution in isolation may not fully capture the dynamics of economic growth. Instead, researchers must consider entire institutional frameworks to fully understand their impact on economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This insight underscores the importance of a holistic approach when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

As we delve into the next section, we will explore how cultural beliefs and values have shaped economic institutions and growth in pre-industrial Europe, further enriching our understanding of this complex interplay.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) demonstrate how secure rights, particularly political liberties, have been crucial for economic growth, as seen in seventeenth-century England and other economies. This work builds upon Kuznets' (Kuznets 1973, p.1) argument that for economic growth, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data. In this context, North and Weingast argue that the financial revolution in England was underpinned by the development of a system of mutual checks and balances between the monarchy, the nobility, and the merchant class. This system, known as the 'Glorious Revolution', ensured that political power was not concentrated in the hands of any one group, thereby fostering a climate of political stability and predictability that facilitated economic growth.

Kuznets, on the other hand, highlights the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments in the face of advancing technology. He contends that modern technology, such as steam and electric power, necessitated changes in economic structure, relations among occupational status groups, and even in societal views. For instance, modern technology was incompatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which were prevalent in earlier times over much of the developed world. Therefore, the transition to modern economic growth required the replacement of these outdated institutions and social views.

Furthermore, North and Weingast's (1989, p.2) work suggests that the success of England's financial revolution was remarkable, as evidenced by the thriving capital markets. This success was due to the political factors supporting economic growth and the development of markets, as well as the institutions regulating how these rules are enforced and how they may be altered. In this regard, the 'Glorious Revolution' ensured that the rules were enforced impartially and that they could be changed through established procedures, thereby fostering a climate of predictability and stability that facilitated economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that British rule in Sub-Saharan Africa played a significant role in fostering long-term economic growth. This educational development, in turn, contributed to the region's infrastructure development, a crucial factor in long-term economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this claim by demonstrating that many Sub-Saharan African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a period when these economies were on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s.

He introduces vectors that reflect endogenous supply-demand responses and African demand responses to consolidated colonial rule. These vectors include a dummy variable for the pre-colonial influence of Islam, an index number for the extent of pre-colonial state centralization, and a newly constructed index measuring the extent of native African resistance against colonial rule.

However, they argue that new contributions to historical national accounting and the application of innovative techniques have provided insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries, rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) study contributes significantly to the understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of agricultural development in the American Plains. The paper argues that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the region significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) notes that the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped including details on herd laws after 1880 and entirely stopped reporting the law status after 1884, suggesting a shift in focus towards barbed wire. This shift is further supported by the observation that nearly all herd-law counties have less woodland than non-herd law counties, indicating a shift towards more barbed wire usage (Hornbeck, 2010, p.32).

The introduction and widespread adoption of barbed wire facilitated the enforcement of property rights, a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) qualify this downstream implication by noting that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. However, they argue that new contributions to historical national accounting and the application of innovative techniques have provided insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries, rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution.

In this context, the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains can be seen as a key institutional factor that contributed to the region's agricultural development and, by extension, its long-run economic growth. The study by Hornbeck (2010, p.8) adds to the growing body of literature that underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth, providing a unique perspective on the role of barbed wire in this process.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically reorganized to uphold social hierarchies, which, in turn, fostered the growth of cultural infrastructure in colonies. This process is evident in the work of Ann Laura Stoler (1989, p.6), who demonstrates that European class structures were selectively restructured to create and maintain the social distinctions of imperial rule.

Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that the reconfiguration of European class cultures served multiple purposes, including the establishment of a sense of colonial community, the preservation of European prestige, and the protection of white women from sexual assault by indigenous men. This restructuring, according to Stoler, was not merely a justification for continued European dominance but a crucial element in the apparatus that kept potential subversive European colonials in line.

In addition to Stoler's work, Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979) provide evidence of the internal political tensions among Europeans in the colonies, further supporting the claim that class-based logic played a significant role in colonial domination. The expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies, as Stoler suggests, was a direct result of this strategic reorganization of European class cultures.

It is worth noting that the growth of cultural infrastructure in colonies did not necessarily lead to economic growth. As North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue, credible commitment underpins growth, and the restructuring of European class cultures within imperial control may not have always ensured such commitment.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) posit the growth of useful knowledge as a key driver of long-term economic growth, particularly in Western Europe during the late medieval period. In contrast, van Zanden (2009, p.19) focuses on the role of institutions, specifically the guilds, in facilitating the intergenerational transfer of skills and ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts.

The guilds, as institutions that emerged in western Europe, played a significant role in the growth of useful knowledge by addressing issues related to the skill premium, factor proportions, and institutional quality.

One downstream implication of this mechanism is the impact on economic performance.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This collaboration among scholars aligns with the topic's claim that institutions are fundamental for long-run economic growth. Maddison's strategy was to produce numbers even if a solid basis for them did not always exist, expecting that scholars might disagree and carry out new work to show that he was wrong (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

Members of the Maddison Project have also experimented with various margins of error, with the most detailed study being carried out by the Hitotshibashi team working on Asian national accounts (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.3).

The Maddison Project's work underscores the ongoing need for collaboration among scholars to refine our understanding of long-term economic growth, particularly in regions like Asia where data may be more limited. This collaboration not only helps to reduce the margins of error in our estimates but also encourages further research and debate, much like Maddison's original strategy (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014, p.2).

As these estimates of GDP and population are crucial for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, the Maddison Project's work provides a valuable foundation for future research in this area. By continuing to refine and expand these estimates, scholars can better understand the impact of institutions on economic growth across different regions and time periods.

In the realm of economic history, a shift in focus has emerged, challenging the traditional narrative that attributes the British industrial revolution primarily to men and high wages. This new perspective, anchored in the work of Humphries (2013, p.16) and Goldin (2006, p.7), argues for the significance of cheap and amenable female and child labor in driving this transformative period.

Humphries (2013, p.16) delves into the historical records of the eighteenth century, revealing that a considerable number of families in Britain were affected by marital instability, male abandonment, and family breakdown. This turbulence, in turn, led to a substantial group of families whose fathers were either absent or deceased, providing evidence of the era's economic, social, and political challenges that impacted family structures.

Simultaneously, Goldin (2006, p.7) offers an analysis of the changes in the involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States over the past century. Goldin's research demonstrates that a quiet revolution occurred, primarily driven by the generation born in the late 1940s, which saw increased opportunities for women in various aspects of life.

This shared finding (Humphries 2013; Goldin 2006) underscores the importance of considering the role of women and children in understanding the dynamics of economic growth and industrialization. The availability of amenable female and child labor, shaped by societal, economic, and political factors, has emerged as a crucial factor in the British industrial revolution, challenging the long-held emphasis on men and high wages.

As these studies reveal, the role of women in employment, education, and family structures has undergone significant changes, contributing to the economic growth and transformation in both Britain and the United States.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of extensive scholarly inquiry, with several streams of related scholarship emerging. One prominent stream focuses on the impact of European colonization and the institutions it established on current income levels across countries. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different associated institutions. In one extreme, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. In contrast, in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, hindering long-term development.

Another stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of women in economic growth and transformation. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, which propelled the economic revolution in regions such as the Gold Coast. However, the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced women's subordinate status in access to land, particularly in the context of cash crops.

The new economic history of Africa also has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and poverty elimination among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still be dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

In the case of the slave trade in Africa, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that it led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their findings suggest that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external effects of the slave trade on trust. This culture of mistrust has implications for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

The institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). However, the extent to which British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940, remains a subject of debate (Frankema 2012, p.2). Future research could compare the educational attainment and economic growth of regions under British influence with those under other colonial powers to shed light on this question.
