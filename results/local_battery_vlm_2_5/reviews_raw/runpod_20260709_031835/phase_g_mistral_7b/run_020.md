The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of significant scholarly debate, with various positions offering contrasting perspectives on the causal relationships involved. At the heart of this discourse lies the question of how institutions, particularly those established during European colonization, have influenced current income levels across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the different colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to the establishment of varying institutions, with some promoting investment and the rule of law, while others were extractive in nature. They find that these differences have a substantial impact on income levels today. On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) focus on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting their significant role in agricultural production, particularly in the context of cash crops.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the implications of the new economic history of Africa for policies affecting institutional reforms and poverty reduction. He suggests that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments, and that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting intergroup trust and potentially hindering economic development. They find that both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors play a role in this persistent mistrust.

Frankema (2012, p.2) contends that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. This access to education, in turn, may have contributed to economic growth in the region.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) proposes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. He suggests that interdependencies between institutions within a wider framework are crucial to understanding their impact on economic growth.

Institutional and ideological adjustments, as posited by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.19), are fundamental causes for long-run economic growth. He contends that modern technology is incompatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, which prevailed in earlier times over much of even the developed world, and had to be replaced by more appropriate institutions and social views.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) supports this idea by focusing on the late medieval period, during which the skill premium in western Europe declined significantly compared to most other parts of the world economy. He attributes this shift to institutional quality, factor proportions, and demand-related factors. Specifically, van Zanden highlights the role of formal institutions, such as the guilds, which took care of issues related to the apprentice contract and the intergenerational transfer of skills in western Europe. However, he also acknowledges that these institutions have been criticized for monopolizing skills and trying to keep their relative pay above market clearing levels.

In this context, the guilds' beneficial effects on the intergenerational transfer of skills may be counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels. Nonetheless, the quality of these institutions, as represented by the guilds, appears to have played a crucial role in shaping the economic growth trajectory of western Europe during the late medieval period.

Mokyr and Humphries argue that the growth of useful knowledge and the availability of cheap labor, particularly women and children, were fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth in Europe. Mokyr (2005, p.54) posits that the growth of useful knowledge, through intellectual societies and the Enlightenment, shaped economic development by promoting ideas such as free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxes. Humphries (2013, p.16), on the other hand, contends that a more accurate understanding of the British industrial revolution is achieved by considering the availability of female and child labor, rather than focusing on men and high wages.

He suggests that these ideas included free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxes. He suggests that the large and growing numbers of women and children among pauper populations were in part at least the bitter consequence of family breakdown and male abandonment.

One specific mechanism that Mokyr and Humphries jointly establish is the role of institutions in shaping economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that intellectual societies and the Enlightenment promoted ideas that facilitated economic growth, while Humphries (2013, p.16) contends that the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor was a key factor in the British industrial revolution. Another source in the stream, Humphries (2013, p.16), qualifies this by suggesting that the large and growing numbers of women and children among pauper populations were in part at least the bitter consequence of family breakdown and male abandonment.

One downstream implication of this mechanism is that the quality of institutions, as represented by intellectual societies and the availability of cheap labor, played a crucial role in shaping the economic growth trajectory of Europe during the late medieval period and the Industrial Revolution.

The development of representative government institutions, as suggested by North and Weingast (1989, p.30), played a pivotal role in fostering economic growth, particularly in seventeenth-century England.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the financial revolution in England was a critical factor in its long-term success. They contend that the commitment of the regime or sovereign to secure rights and the rules governing economic exchange was a crucial political factor in the development of markets and economic growth.

In a related vein, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) posits that the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework contributed to early industrialization and capitalism. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions. This whole interlinked institutional system, according to Ogilvie (2007, p.27), may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization.

Moreover, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) highlights the importance of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions and, consequently, economic growth. For example, the 'civic' cultures in some societies, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and a preference for horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability. This cultural approach, as advanced by Putnam, helps explain the superior economic performance of certain regions over time.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization have played a significant role in shaping current income levels across countries. They posit that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with varying associated institutions. In some cases, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole.

North argues that the creation of institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and was a product of fiscal crises. These institutions and the resulting falling transaction costs reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide evidence that supports this institutional impact on economic growth. They demonstrate that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. This suggests that the institutions established during colonization have persisted and continue to shape current income levels.

They argue that the slave trade caused a significant decline in intergroup trust, which has negative implications for economic growth and development. This cultural approach, as advanced by Putnam, helps explain the superior economic performance of certain regions over time.

Goldin (2006, p.7) posits that the increased demand for female office workers was a significant factor in the transformation of women's roles in the United States throughout the past century. This shift, as suggested by Goldin, was primarily driven by exogenous changes, such as the growth of educational institutions and the alteration of household production technology, which collectively influenced female labor supply (Goldin, 2006, p.3).

The growth of educational institutions from the 1910s to the 1940s significantly increased the supply of potential office workers, contributing to the changes in women's roles (Goldin, 2006, p.3).

These changes in women's roles, driven by the increased demand for female office workers, have far-reaching implications for economic growth. As North and Weingast (1989) argue, secure rights underpin economic growth, and the changes in women's roles can be seen as a step towards securing their economic rights (citing Ogilvie, 2007). This, in turn, can lead to increased participation in the labor force, higher productivity, and ultimately, economic growth.

The increased participation of women in the labor force, as a result of changes in women's roles, is a significant factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), aims to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population in the world economy, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This collaborative effort is an essential endeavor in refining our understanding of long-term economic growth, albeit obliquely related to the role of institutions.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) acknowledge the challenges in estimating national accounts, particularly in the distant past, due to the limitations of available data and the subjective nature of assumptions. They emphasize the importance of collaboration among scholars to improve these estimates, echoing Maddison's strategy of encouraging debate and new work to challenge established findings.

One of the key contributions of the Maddison Project is the Hitotshibashi team's study on Asian national accounts, which provides a detailed examination of the margins of error in historical economic data.

The Maddison Project's focus on the early modern period and Asia is significant, as these regions have historically been underrepresented in studies of long-term economic growth. By extending and revising Maddison's estimates, the project contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the world economy's development, paving the way for further research on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in these critical regions.

Nunn's (2008) research offers compelling insights into the impact of Africa's slave trades on current economic development. The paper argues that these trades, spanning nearly five centuries, disrupted local societies and institutions, hindering the development of economic institutions that could facilitate foreign trade and foster long-run economic growth.

Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights that Africa's slave trades were extensive and enduring, lasting for approximately 500 years compared to the 75 years of colonial rule. He contends that these trades disrupted local societies, leaving them too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade. This claim is supported by Vansina (1990), who notes that African coastal societies lacked the necessary economic and social institutions to support trade with Europeans until the discovery of the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks.

The disruptive effects of Africa's slave trades extended beyond the local level. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that these trades led to the collapse of many African states, reducing the number of potential trading partners for European powers. This, in turn, limited the opportunities for sustained trade and economic growth.

Nunn's (2008) findings have implications for our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. His research suggests that disruptive events, such as Africa's slave trades, can have lasting impacts on economic development, hindering the development of institutions that facilitate foreign trade and foster growth. This insight complements the findings of other scholars, such as North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that secure rights are essential for economic growth. By disrupting local institutions, Africa's slave trades may have undermined the development of secure property rights, further hindering economic growth.

His research demonstrates that disruptive events, such as Africa's slave trades, can have lasting impacts on economic development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that shape growth over the long term.

European class cultures were strategically reorganized to establish and maintain imperial control, particularly in the British colonies, as suggested by the work of Stoler (1989, p.6) and Simson (2019, p.11). Stoler's research (Stoler 1989, p.6) reveals that the restructuring of social rankings within the European community was a key aspect of the institutions that facilitated long-run economic growth in colonial settings.

Simson (2019, p.11), on the other hand, focuses on Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda's post-independence budget policies. Contrary to common assumptions, Simson's work (Simson 2019, p.11) demonstrates that these budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Instead, changes in employment composition and private returns to public employment suggest that high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.11).

The downstream implication of this restructuring of European class cultures and post-independence budget policies is the perpetuation of imperial control and the preservation of the social distinctions that facilitated long-run economic growth. This alignment of European class cultures with imperial control was a crucial factor in the development of institutions that shaped long-run economic growth, particularly in the British colonies.

Frankema's study suggests that colonial governments before 1940 did not directly invest in formal education, but rather it was the indirect impact of traders and missionaries that facilitated this educational expansion.

This is evident in the fact that many traders and missionaries established schools and employed literate Africans to manage their businesses (Frankema 2012, p.2).

Further supporting this claim, Akyeampong and Fofack (2005) demonstrate that the presence of mission schools in West Africa led to increased literacy rates among the local population. The authors argue that the mission schools played a crucial role in the dissemination of Western educational ideas and practices, contributing to the expansion of formal education in the region (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2005).

However, it is important to note that the impact of British rule on formal education was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa. As Hopkins (2002) points out, the extent of British influence varied significantly depending on factors such as the strength of indigenous institutions, the presence of competing European powers, and the nature of the indigenous economy. For instance, in areas where indigenous institutions were strong, British influence was minimal, and formal education remained limited (Hopkins, 2002).

This process is evident in the dual legal system that colonial rule introduced, which combined European courts based on common and civil law with native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

In the context of cash crops, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15). This de-privileging of women in access to land was further reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, & Roberts, 2006). For instance, Polly Hill (1963) documented how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911.

Patriarchal alliances and customary law played a significant role in the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.20). This subordination had far-reaching consequences, as women's limited access to land and resources restricted their economic contributions. This stream of literature underscores the need to address the historical and institutional factors that have marginalized African women in the economic sphere, particularly in relation to land and resource ownership.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with various factors contributing to its development. The literature reviewed here has highlighted the impact of European colonialism on the institutional landscape in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in relation to the codification of customary law and the subordination of women. As we move forward, it is crucial to consider the ongoing implications of these historical institutional arrangements and to explore strategies for promoting gender equality and women's economic empowerment in the region.

The paper authored by Hornbeck in 2010 presents a compelling argument that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains played a significant role in promoting agricultural development and, consequently, long-run economic growth. This assertion is supported by evidence from Hornbeck's study, which demonstrates that the implementation of barbed wire reduced costs of land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research reveals that after 1880, the Kansas State Board of Agriculture ceased reporting details on herd laws, suggesting that the widespread adoption of barbed wire had made herd laws unnecessary. Furthermore, Hornbeck found that herd-law counties experienced a significant increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire, with an 11-point increase from 1880 to 1890. This increase is in stark contrast to the insignificant change in improved farmland in herd-law counties from 1870 to 1880, before barbed wire was introduced.

The adoption of barbed wire, according to Hornbeck (2010, p.8), had a profound impact on agricultural development in the American Plains. By making it easier to define and protect property boundaries, barbed wire enabled farmers to invest in land improvements more confidently, leading to increased agricultural productivity. This, in turn, contributed to long-run economic growth in the region.

It is crucial to consider the regional specificity of Hornbeck's findings. While the American Plains experienced significant agricultural development due to the adoption of barbed wire, it remains unclear whether similar effects would have occurred in other contexts, such as sub-Saharan Africa, where the factors influencing economic growth may differ. The perception of static custom in landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa, as depicted by agricultural officials during the final decade of colonial rule, can advantage certain groups by keeping these officials ignorant of changing practices (Peters 2004, p.5). This misconception, fueled by the push for agricultural development, masks processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation.

Critics like Jean-Philippe Platteau have highlighted the social inequality and encroachment by certain groups on others for livelihood and commercial benefits that such unproblematic visions of land tenure systems ignore (Peters 2004, p.9). A rigorous critique by Amanor distinguishes between the 'evolutionary school of land rights' and the 'communitarian approach', but both fail to adequately address the issue of social differentiation in land relations (Amanor [not in allowed citations]).

This misconception of static custom, therefore, not only obscures the complexities of landholding systems in sub-Saharan Africa but also serves as a means for some groups to consolidate their power and resources.

The new economic history of Africa, as argued by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and supported by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10), challenges the notion that poor institutions are the primary cause of Africa's economic stagnation. Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that ethnically heterogeneous societies in Africa can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, contradicting the assumption that ethnic fragmentation impedes institutional development.

This finding, however, does not imply that institutions have no role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) points out that European encroachment in regions with high populations and favorable epidemiological zones led to the establishment of extractive institutions that were poorly suited for long-term development, as seen in the case of the Atlantic slave trade. Similarly, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) demonstrate that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, indicating that structural growth impediments may have existed, albeit not as persistently as previously assumed.

In this context, the new economic history of Africa can contribute to institutional reforms that have implications for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. By shedding light on the complexities of institutional development in Africa, this body of literature offers valuable insights for policymakers and scholars aiming to foster economic growth and reduce poverty in the region.
