The paper by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.54) jointly argue that for economic growth, a country needs not only advancing technology but also institutional and ideological adjustments, suggesting that these factors are as important as technological advancement. Kuznets (1973, p.1) contends that modern technology is incompatible with family enterprise, illiteracy, or slavery, all of which prevailed in earlier times over much of the developed world. Thus, economic growth necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, society, family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role in society.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) emphasizes the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He contends that the Industrial Revolution was about more than technological innovation; it was also about the proliferation of new ideas, such as free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order's impact on commerce, and the Enlightenment idea that individuals working for their own good contribute to society's welfare. These ideas created niches that protected innovative ideas from being exploited by greedy interests, thereby supporting economic growth.

In this context, it is worth noting that the rise of useful knowledge and the necessary institutional and ideological adjustments were not exclusive to the Industrial Revolution. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the dynamic drives of modern economic growth meant a reaching out geographically, facilitated by major changes in transport and communication, and the continuous expansion to the less developed areas. This expansion suggests that the growth of useful knowledge and institutional adjustments were not limited to the British Isles but spread to other regions as well.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a significant role in driving the British Industrial Revolution, as argued by Humphries (2013, p.16). Humphries's evidence indicates that the high rates of male abandonment and family breakdown during the eighteenth century resulted in a significant number of families whose fathers were absent, either due to death or desertion. This situation, in turn, led to an increased reliance on less skilled workers, particularly women and children, to fill the labor force.

Instead, the availability of a large pool of cheap and amenable labor from women and children enabled the substitution of less skilled workers for adult trained operatives. This shift in the labor force composition had profound implications for the structure and dynamics of the British Industrial Revolution.

Furthermore, the reliance on female and child labor during the British Industrial Revolution is not unique to this period. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) demonstrates that the use of female and child labor was a common strategy in early modern Europe, particularly in the textile industry. Ogilvie's findings suggest that the use of female and child labor was a response to the high costs of skilled adult male labor, which made the employment of less-skilled workers more attractive.

In this context, it is important to note that the use of female and child labor was not without controversy. The literature on Africa's slave trades and economic development offers a compelling perspective on the role of historical events in shaping long-run economic growth. He suggests that the slave trades disrupted the development of African societies, particularly their institutional structures, which are crucial for economic growth.

One specific mechanism through which the slave trades affected institutional development is by fostering a culture of violence and exploitation. These elites, in turn, undermined the development of democratic institutions, which are essential for economic growth.

Moreover, the slave trades also disrupted the development of market institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) cites Vansina (1990) to argue that African societies lacked the necessary institutions to support foreign trade before the Kongo kingdom was discovered by the Portuguese. The Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans. The slave trades disrupted the development of similar institutions in other parts of Africa, hindering economic growth.

Simson's (2019, p.11) paper challenges the common assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were unproductive or prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. Contrary to this belief, Simson reveals that while these countries did experience high debt burdens and increased military spending, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage.

In Kenya, for instance, subsidies and interest peaked at 30% of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Similarly, Tanzania experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18% of expenditure, while Uganda's debt payments remained smaller due to less access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief (Simson 2019, p.11). Domestic military spending began to grow in the independence period and escalated towards the end of the 1960s and 1970s in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11).

Beyond the capital cities, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly, while jobs increasingly favoured comparatively skilled and female professions. This shift shows that from the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained rather than eased the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.11).

Simson's findings suggest that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not as unproductive as commonly assumed. It is essential to consider the specific policies and their impact on different sectors within these countries.
