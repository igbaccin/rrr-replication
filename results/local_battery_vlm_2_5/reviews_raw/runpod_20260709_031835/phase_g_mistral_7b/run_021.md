The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with implications that extend far beyond the realm of academia. At stake is the understanding of the factors that have shaped the economic trajectories of nations, particularly those that underwent European colonization. A central point of contention revolves around the impact of institutions established during this period and their influence on current income levels.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights set up during European colonization significantly impact current income levels across countries. They contend that differences in colonization strategies led to varying institutional structures, with some, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, fostering investment and the rule of law, while others, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, established extractive states that transferred resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge this narrative by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They demonstrate how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and how the codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized them (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Secure rights and political and economic institutions play a crucial role in fostering long-run economic growth, as demonstrated by the English financial revolution and other economies (North 1989, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.27; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

By securing property rights, this financial revolution paved the way for economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) expands on this by examining the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework. He illustrates this with the European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective provision of insurance and pensions, which together contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This comprehensive approach underscores the importance of considering entire institutional frameworks rather than individual institutions in isolation.

Furthermore, the interdependency of institutions extends to contracting and property rights institutions, as suggested by Acemoglu and Johnson (not cited, but their work is mentioned in Ogilvie, 2007, p.27). This implies that favorable contracting institutions, which regulate interactions between private citizens, may be partially interdependent with favorable property rights institutions, which constrain the behavior of political and economic elites.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) propose that the unequal distribution of wealth in New World societies led to institutions that favored the elite, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality over the long run. This claim complements the broader argument that institutions shape economic growth, as advanced by North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1988), among others.

The authors argue that in societies with extreme inequality, institutions were structured to advantage elites, limiting access to economic opportunities for the majority of the population. This phenomenon, they suggest, allowed the elite to maintain their status over time, albeit at the expense of broader economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that this path of institutional development may have further impacted growth, as it could limit competition and innovation.

Moreover, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.12) posit that this institutional structure could contribute to the persistence of inequality over time. In contrast, societies with more equitable distribution of wealth may have developed institutions that fostered competition and innovation, thereby promoting economic growth. This downstream implication is supported by the findings of North and Weingast (1989, p.30), who argue that credible commitments underpinned by institutional arrangements can foster economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and others argue that the growth of useful knowledge, particularly in scientific societies, was a key driver of long-run economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the crucial role of intellectual advancements in shaping economic development. This growth was driven by the focus on issues of political economy, including free trade, improved infrastructure, law and order, and more efficient taxes (Mokyr, 2005, p.54).

Moreover, Mokyr (2005, p.3) posits that the Industrial Revolution was not a sudden event but rather a gradual process that began much earlier in societies with an institutional basis supporting growth, such as the Dutch Republic and Venice. The growth was not sustainable and was often disrupted by shocks, both man-made and natural, but the underlying conditions that supported growth persisted (Mokyr, 2005, p.3).

In this context, the growth of useful knowledge can be seen as a crucial antecedent that triggered long-run economic growth. The downstream implication of this perspective is that societies that prioritize intellectual advancements and the development of institutions supporting growth are more likely to experience sustained economic development.

This stream of literature, therefore, underscores the importance of intellectual progress and institutional development in shaping long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) posits that the quality of institutions in Western Europe during the late medieval period played a pivotal role in shaping the skill premium, a key determinant of long-run economic growth. This claim is supported by the emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, which addressed issues related to the fair execution of apprentice contracts (van Zanden, 2009, p.19).

The guilds, as van Zanden argues, had a dual impact on the skill premium. On one hand, they facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, a beneficial effect. On the other hand, they were criticized for attempting to form local cartels, potentially counterbalancing the benefits of skill transfer by artificially inflating relative pay (van Zanden, 2009, p.19).

The skill premium, as van Zanden further explains, is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden, 2009, p.1). This downstream implication is qualified by the work of other scholars, such as Acemoglu et al., who demonstrate that skill premiums have a significant impact on income levels and economic growth (Acemoglu et al., 2008).

The impact of European colonization on current income levels is a significant area of study, with scholars such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) arguing that the institutional changes resulting from different colonization strategies have a substantial influence.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) support their argument by demonstrating that the institutions and property rights established during colonization have a significant impact on economic growth and income levels.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies. For example, relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor, such as in the case of Brazil compared to the Netherlands (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). They also highlight that political power was crucial for European elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.34).

The work of Nunn (2008, p.2) qualifies the downstream implication of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) by focusing on Africa's slave trades and their impact on current economic development in Africa. Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades had a negative impact on economic development, as they disrupted local societies and economies (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.19). This finding suggests that the negative impact of European colonization on income levels in Africa may be more profound than previously thought, due to the combined effects of slave trades and extractive institutions.

They demonstrate that the internal norms of mistrust, cultivated by the slave trade, are at least twice as significant as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, in shaping intergroup trust in Africa today.

Their findings suggest that the slave trade's impact on trust is primarily internal, shaping the norms of mistrust among ethnic groups rather than altering the external environment.

This finding complements the work of Knack and Keefer (1997), Fafchamps (2006), Tabellini (2007), Algan and Cahuc (2010), Greif (1989), Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales (2007a), Putnam (2000), and Bloom, Sadun, and Van Reenen (2008), all of whom document the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

By creating an environment of distrust, the slave trade may have hindered the development of cooperative institutions that facilitate economic exchange and promote long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that for economic growth in less developed countries, advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data are crucial factors.

In the realm of agricultural development, the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains emerges as a significant factor, as argued by Hornbeck (2010, p.8). This innovation significantly reduced costs of land protection and facilitated property rights enforcement, thereby fostering agricultural growth.

Hornbeck (2010, p.8) provides compelling evidence to support this claim. The author demonstrates that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains led to a substantial increase in agricultural development. In particular, Hornbeck (2010, p.32) reveals that herd-law counties, which were the first to adopt barbed wire, experienced a 20-point increase in the improved fraction of farmland from 1880 to 1890, following the introduction of barbed wire.

The adoption of barbed wire not only increased the improved fraction of farmland but also facilitated property rights enforcement. This is evident in the decline in the number of herd law disputes reported by Hornbeck (2010, p.8). The Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped including details on herd law decisions after 1880 and entirely stopped reporting the law status after 1884, suggesting that the widespread use of barbed wire contributed to a reduction in disputes over land boundaries.

The paper by Humphries (2013, p.16) and Goldin (2006, p.7) collectively suggest a significant role of women in shaping the British industrial revolution and the quiet revolution in the United States regarding employment, education, and family roles.

The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, such as increased demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions (Goldin, 2006, p.3).

One specific mechanism that these sources jointly establish is the role of institutions in shaping the availability and utilization of female labor.

Downstream, this availability of female labor had profound implications for economic growth.

The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3)(Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2), underscores the importance of continued collaboration among scholars to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, which are essential for understanding long-run economic growth. This collaboration seeks to improve the accuracy of historical economic data, particularly in the early modern period and Asia, acknowledging the subjective nature of such estimates.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) argue that the margins of error in historical economic data can be substantial, especially as one goes back in time. They suggest that these errors are often based on partial data and certain assumptions about the links between these data and the economic activities they represent.

Simultaneously, Maddison himself was aware of the limitations of his data and encouraged scholars to work on these themes and try to quantify long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

In essence, the Maddison Project advocates for a collaborative approach to improve historical economic data, recognizing the subjective nature of such estimates and the need for ongoing refinement to enhance our understanding of long-run economic growth. This collaboration aims to provide more accurate and comprehensive data, enabling scholars to better trace the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, a key question in the ongoing discourse on economic development.

The influence of British rule in shaping formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, as argued by Frankema (2012, p.2), is a significant downstream consequence of the institutional factors that drive long-term economic growth. British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct colonial government investment before 1940, played a pivotal role in this development.

The missionaries, driven by their religious beliefs, established schools to educate the native population, thereby promoting literacy and formal education.

In a similar vein, the need for literate workers in the expanding trading networks also contributed to the growth of formal education. British traders required literate employees to manage accounts, negotiate contracts, and communicate effectively with other traders and colonial administrators. This demand for literacy created an incentive for the expansion of formal education.

However, it is important to note that the impact of British rule on formal education was not uniform across the region.

For instance, in areas with a strong pre-colonial state, the colonial administration found it easier to build upon existing educational structures, leading to a more rapid development of formal education. Similarly, the presence of peasant cultivation of major export crops and a mining industry created a demand for literate workers, thereby promoting the growth of formal education.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the prevailing view that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. Instead, they argue that many African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s.

This claim is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) assertion that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or even 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. They contend that these data fail to capture the economic growth that occurred before the colonial period.

Moreover, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) point out that the field of African economic history has undergone a 'renaissance' based on newly digitized sources of data and the application of innovative techniques. This renaissance has allowed for a reevaluation of the history of economic growth in Africa, challenging the assumption that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth.

The perception of stable customary landholding in sub-Saharan Africa, often touted as an advantageous aspect of indigenous institutions, can mask processes of exclusion and social division that are not conducive to long-run economic growth (Peters 2004, p.5). Agricultural officers in charge of schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement during the final decade of colonial rule sought to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming, but their dissatisfaction with African landholding practices was fueled by the belief that these practices were 'communal' and inhibited individual (male) investment in agriculture (Peters 2004, p.5).

Critics like Jean-Philippe Platteau and Amanor have pointed out that such unproblematic visions of customary landholding systems in Africa ignore considerable social inequality, encroachment by some groups on others, state appropriation, and illegal sales by 'traditional' leaders (Peters 2004, p.9). Researchers like Ribot, Carney, Farrington, and Woodhouse have shown how national and local elites often capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers (Peters 2004, p.9).

In essence, the mask of stable customary landholding in sub-Saharan Africa can conceal deepening social divisions, class formation, and processes of exclusion that are detrimental to long-run economic growth. This finding complicates the understanding of the role of indigenous institutions in shaping economic growth in Africa, as it suggests that the perceived stability of these institutions may hide underlying issues that impede growth.

The complex interplay between indigenous institutions, social divisions, and economic growth in Africa continues to be a subject of ongoing research and debate.

The European class cultures were restructured to maintain imperial control, leading to the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies (Stoler 1989, p.6). Ann Laura Stoler's (1989, p.6) work underscores this point, arguing that European class cultures were selectively restructured to create and sustain the social distinctions of imperial control. Stoler demonstrates that racist ideology, fear of the Other, and obsession with protecting European women from sexual assault by Asian and black males were not mere justifications for continued European rule and white supremacy, but rather part of a critical class-based logic aimed at maintaining colonial authority (Stoler 1989, p.6).

This restructuring of European class cultures was not limited to the colonies' indigenous populations but also affected European underlings. Stoler (1989, p.6) notes that numerous accounts of British and French interaction with the colonized and their philosophies of rule analyze the political priorities informing colonial policy, but rarely treat the everyday practice of colonial domination (Stoler 1989, p.4). Efforts to go beyond the official debates and examine the distinct class interests within the colonial state include studies by Cooper (1980) and by John Lonsdale and Bruce Berman (1979) (Stoler 1989, p.4).

The restructuring of European class cultures to maintain imperial control led to the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies. This expansion, in turn, had downstream implications for the colonies' economic growth. For instance, the creation of a colonial civil service and the development of colonial education systems were crucial for the effective administration and control of colonies (Stoler 1989, p.4). These systems not only served to perpetuate European rule but also shaped the economic, social, and political landscapes of the colonies for generations to come.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) challenge the prevailing assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. Their paper reconstructs and compares real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, from approximately 1880 to 1965 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This research offers a fresh perspective on the causes of African economic development, suggesting that precolonial labor market conditions play a significant role in the continent's long-run trajectories of wage income growth.

The Kenyan case presented by Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrates that the East-West gap in Africa is not solely the result of different economic dynamics (commercialization) but also the outcome of varying degrees of income inequality, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The authors' findings call for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development, suggesting that future research should focus more on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories.

In this context, it is worth considering the implications of precolonial labor market conditions for the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. By examining the historical wage levels and trends, Frankema and van Waijenburg offer insights into the structural foundations that may have influenced the development of institutions in African societies. This stream of literature highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between labor markets, economic dynamics, and institutional development in the context of African economic history.

Hopkins's work (Hopkins 2009) offers valuable insights into the potential role of institutional reforms in addressing poverty in Africa. The scholar contends that the resurgence of state intervention in development policy during the 1990s, often described as 'bringing the state back in', can contribute significantly to institutional reforms and ultimately aid in eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

This claim is supported by Hopkins's assertion that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly impact the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This finding challenges the widely held view that ethnic diversity is detrimental to institutional development in Africa. Hopkins also highlights how the larger populations in certain regions could resist European encroachment, which in turn preserved unproductive institutions or led to the introduction of extractive ones ill-suited for long-term development (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

One specific mechanism that emerges from Hopkins's work and the findings of Englebert (2003) is the persistence of poor institutions in Africa despite the continent's ethnic diversity. Englebert concludes that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while ethnically heterogeneous societies can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that the focus on ethnic diversity as the primary obstacle to institutional development in Africa may be misguided, and that other factors, such as the nature and quality of institutions, should be prioritized.

The downstream implication of this mechanism is that institutional reforms, rather than ethnic homogeneity, may be key to addressing poverty in Africa. By identifying and addressing the root causes of poor institutions, policymakers can potentially improve the quality of public goods and services, foster economic growth, and ultimately reduce poverty.

Hopkins's work, therefore, underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the relationship between institutions, poverty, and ethnicity in Africa. By acknowledging the resilience of poor institutions in the face of ethnic diversity, policymakers can focus on institutional reforms as a means to address poverty and promote economic development.
