The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in the academic discourse, with scholars debating the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence and the relative importance of various institutional factors. At the heart of this debate lies the question of how institutions, particularly those established during European colonization, have shaped contemporary income levels across countries.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels. They posit that the differences in colonization strategies, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others resulted in extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, have had lasting consequences on economic growth.

The argument of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) is not without criticism. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth may be more complex than suggested by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).

In a related but distinct stream of scholarship, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They contend that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) offer another perspective on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth by arguing that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They find that both the internal and external environment played a role in shaping intergroup trust, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This finding complements recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development, trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

(2001, 2002), Nunn (2008, p.2), and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) claim that institutional changes resulting from European colonization significantly influence current income levels and economic growth across countries. They contend that the differences in colonization strategies led to varying institutional structures, with extractive states in some colonies transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, while others, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.

He asserts that the slave trades, which lasted for nearly 500 years, affected the continent's economic institutions and development trajectory, leaving a lasting impact on its economic growth. They find that both the internal and external environment played a role in shaping intergroup trust, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) provide evidence supporting the claim by demonstrating that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They argue that political power was important to the planters in the West Indies, and to other elites in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages.

These findings suggest that the institutional changes resulting from European colonization have far-reaching consequences for current income levels and economic growth across countries. The differences in colonization strategies led to varying institutional structures, which in turn affected the economic development trajectory of these countries. The slave trades, in particular, had a negative impact on the economic institutions and development in Africa, leaving a lasting impact on its economic growth. The culture of mistrust that resulted from the slave trade persists today and continues to affect intergroup trust, political institutions, and economic development.

The development of political institutions that secure property rights and enforce them effectively plays a crucial role in promoting long-run economic growth. This claim is substantiated by North's work, who argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, thereby increasing security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast further demonstrate that secure rights are crucial for economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework is recognized by some studies of European economic institutions. For instance, Ogilvie contends that the European family system, labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions; this whole interlinked institutional system may have contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, Putnam argues that some societies are characterized by high social capital because of their 'altruistic' or 'civic' cultures, which in turn encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

These interdependent institutions contribute to economic growth and shape the structure and effectiveness of economic transactions. North and Weingast argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment, or credible commitment, underpins growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England, where the Glorious Revolution of 1688 established a constitutional system that limited the power of the monarchy and ensured the rule of law (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The growth of useful knowledge is identified as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, with intellectual factors playing a crucial role in driving technological change and industrialization (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr argues that the Enlightenment period, particularly in the British Isles, saw a surge in scientific societies, which focused on issues related to political economy, such as free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxation (Mokyr 2005, p. 54).

Moreover, Mokyr contends that these intellectual niches provided a bulwark against Mancur Olson's "roving bandits," ensuring that the gains from growth were not squandered (Mokyr 2005, p. 3). The Industrial Revolution, while predominantly British, was the culmination of this intellectual growth, with the Dutch Republic and Venice having early institutional foundations supporting this phenomenon (Mokyr 2005, p. 3).

Furthermore, Mokyr's work underscores that law and order, the establishment of trust, and enforceable contracts facilitated more efficient allocation of resources, contributing to the industrial revolution (Mokyr 2005, p. 54).

In this context, it is worth considering the role of intellectual property rights in fostering innovation and economic growth, a topic that will be explored in the subsequent section.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor played a significant role in the British industrial revolution, as inventions were often designed to reduce the need for skilled adult workers (Humphries 2013, p.16). Humphries's study reveals that a substantial proportion of boys grew up without their fathers, due to marital instability, male abandonment, and high mortality rates (Humphries 2013, p. 16). This finding resonates with other historians' depictions of the eighteenth century as a period of marital instability (Humphries 2013, p. 16).

This labor availability facilitated the industrial revolution by enabling inventors to design labor-saving machines, which required less skilled adult labor.

It's worth noting that the availability of female and child labor was one factor among several contributing to the industrial revolution, not solely responsible for it. The growth of useful knowledge, the establishment of institutions, and the availability of labor all played a role in the industrial revolution (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

As the industrial revolution progressed, the demand for skilled labor increased, and the availability of female and child labor became less critical.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) study delves into the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains, a significant downstream consequence of the long-run economic growth mechanism identified in the broader literature. The near-universal adoption of barbed wire, according to Hornbeck, significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement.

Hornbeck's evidence reveals that the herd laws, which had previously been the focus of attention, ceased to be reported after 1884, indicating a shift in focus towards barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The study further demonstrates that herd-law counties, which had adopted the herd law by 1880, experienced a marked increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire, with a 20-percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This shift towards barbed wire, Hornbeck argues, allowed for more efficient land use and facilitated property rights enforcement, contributing to long-run economic growth in the American Plains.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that for less developed countries to effectively participate in the modern economic system, they require a combination of technological advancement, institutional reform, and access to tested data.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that modern technology is incompatible with traditional institutions and social structures, such as family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery, which had prevailed in earlier times. He contends that for less developed countries to engage in the modern economic system, they must replace these outdated institutions and social views with more appropriate ones.

He suggests that the dynamic drives of modern economic growth necessitate a wider supply of tested data, particularly in these countries, to facilitate effective participation in the modern economic system.

The paper's emphasis on institutional reform is further supported by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the quality of institutions significantly influences economic growth. Specifically, they argue that extractive institutions, which exploit and restrict economic activities, hinder economic growth, while inclusive institutions, which protect property rights and encourage economic freedom, foster economic growth.

By replacing outdated institutions and social views, these countries can create an environment conducive to economic growth, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).

This process reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land, a crucial resource for economic activities.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight how the introduction of a dual legal system during colonial rule, comprising European courts and native courts, played a pivotal role in the codification of customary law. This codification, in turn, reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities.

Polly Hill (1963) provides evidence of this marginalization in the Gold Coast, where Krobo and Akuapem farmers, predominantly women, were relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The de-privileging of women in access to land in the context of cash crops was further reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Lawrance, Osborn, and Roberts 2006). This marginalization hindered the economic growth and development potential of these women, as they were excluded from the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911.

They argue that the invention of custom served as a key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women. This further limited their economic opportunities and contributed to their continued marginalization.

The British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education and economic development, which are argued to have contributed to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The introduction of formal education, in turn, expanded the availability of skilled labor, which is a crucial factor for economic development (Frankema 2012, p.17).

He argues that the larger populations of some African societies could resist European encroachment, leading to the preservation of unproductive institutions or the introduction of extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited to long-term development. In regions where European influence was substantial, colonial rule introduced institutions more conducive to economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

Frankema (2012, p.2) further explains that the impact of British rule on educational development varied across different regions.

In addition, the development of a sizeable and geographically concentrated mining industry, such as the copper enclaves in the Katanga region, also played a role in educational development (Frankema 2012, p.17). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) also found that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

These findings suggest that the British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, through the facilitation of formal education and economic development, contributed to long-run economic growth. The impact of British rule varied across different regions, requiring further research to understand the specific mechanisms and conditions that influenced the development of productive institutions under colonial rule.

Greater inequality in New World societies, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12), led to institutions that favored elites, contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom that institutions are the fundamental cause of economic growth.

They highlight the variation in conditions relevant to growth, such as the security of property rights, the prevalence of corruption, and the inclination to work hard or be entrepreneurial. By focusing on these aspects, the authors imply that institutions in societies with greater inequality are structured to advantage elites, limiting competition and perpetuating inequality.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2000) support this institutional bias towards elites. They propose that the distribution of political power shapes the institutional environment, which in turn influences economic growth. In societies with greater inequality, the political power is concentrated among the elite, leading to the creation of institutions that restrict competition and contribute to the persistence of inequality.

The downstream implication of these institutions, as suggested by North and Weingast (2000), is that credible commitment underpins growth. When institutions are designed to restrict competition and maintain the status quo, as in societies with greater inequality, they may stifle innovation and economic growth. This finding underscores the importance of institutions that promote competition and protect property rights for long-run economic growth.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century (Goldin 2006, p.3). Goldin (2006, p.7) suggests that these shifts were influenced by external forces as well as internal factors.

As domestic appliances such as refrigerators and washing machines became more common, women's time spent on household chores decreased, freeing them up to seek employment outside the home. This shift in household production technology, combined with the increased supply of educated women, further facilitated the entry of women into the workforce.

For example, changes in household production technology also led to a decline in the demand for domestic servants, many of whom were African American women (Stoler ). This decline in demand for domestic labor had a negative impact on the economic opportunities available to African American women, highlighting the complex and multifaceted nature of changes in women's roles in the United States.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) collaborate with other scholars as part of the Maddison Project, an initiative aimed at extending and revising Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia.

The Maddison Project's strategy involves the production of estimates even when a solid basis for them may not always exist, with the expectation that scholars will challenge and refine these estimates through further research (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014). This approach has proven successful in stimulating scholarly interest in long-term economic development, as demonstrated by the numerous responses to Maddison's work (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

One significant implication of the Maddison Project's work is the potential for more accurate and detailed overviews of long-term economic growth, compared to the situation when Maddison began his project in the 1960s (Bolt & van Zanden, 2014). The project's focus on the early modern period and Asia is particularly noteworthy, as these regions have historically been underrepresented in global economic data. The project's continued collaboration among scholars is expected to further refine and update this data, providing valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

European class cultures within imperial control were strategically reshaped to preserve social hierarchies, which in turn fostered the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies. Ann Laura Stoler's (1989, p.6) work provides a compelling examination of this phenomenon.

Stoler argues that European class cultures were selectively restructured to create and maintain the social distinctions of imperial control. This restructuring served multiple purposes: it provided a sense of colonial community, context, and authority, and it established relations of production and power. Moreover, it functioned as a means of controlling potentially subversive white colonials (Stoler 1989, p.6).

Stoler's work also highlights the internal political tensions among the European colonizers themselves, a topic often overlooked in colonial studies. For instance, she points out that many community studies of the British in India focus on social rankings of colonial life but neglect the internal political tensions among the British themselves (Stoler 1989, p.4).

This strategic restructuring of European class cultures had far-reaching implications. By preserving social distinctions, it allowed for the expansion of cultural infrastructure in colonies. This expansion, in turn, contributed to the dissemination of European ideas, values, and practices, which had significant long-term effects on the colonies' economic development and social structures.

It's important to note that the specific impact of this influence varied greatly depending on the particular colonial context and the specific European powers involved. Further research is needed to fully understand the complex interplay between European class cultures, imperial control, and long-term economic growth in different colonial settings.

This strategic restructuring of European class cultures within imperial control shaped the cultural infrastructure of colonies and had profound implications for the long-term economic development and social structures of these territories.

The quality of institutions, as enforced by the fair execution of apprentice contracts within guilds, is a crucial determinant of long-run economic growth, a claim substantiated by both van Zanden (2009, p.19) and other scholars. Van Zanden argues that in the late medieval period, the skill premium in western Europe fell compared to most other parts of the world economy.

Scholars like van Zanden (2009, p.19) posit that the beneficial effects of guilds on skill transfer may be counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels, restricting entry into their trades and maintaining higher-than-market-clearing levels of relative pay. The quality of these institutions, as measured by their ability to execute apprentice contracts fairly, remains a key determinant of long-run economic growth.

This claim is supported by other scholars who have studied the functioning of guilds in detail, including those who focus on factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors (van Zanden, 2009, p.1). Simson (2019, p.11) challenges the widely held assumption that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were exceptionally unproductive or prioritized patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. This claim is supported by evidence that the three countries' spending patterns were not markedly different from other developing nations during this period (Simson 2019, p.11).

One specific pattern that emerges from the evidence is the growth in military spending in response to regional instability and domestic dissent. Under colonial rule, the metropolis had subsidized much of the defense of the colonies, but domestic military spending began to grow in the independence period (Simson 2019, p.11).

Another downstream implication of the shift in spending patterns is a change in the composition of employment. From the 1980s onward, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage. Employment increasingly favored comparatively skilled and female professions, suggesting that nepotistic or unmeritocratic practices within public sector recruitment may have been limited (Simson 2019, p.11).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge the common belief that European colonial interventions were the primary drivers of economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa before the 1980s. In contrast, they argue that many African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century, comparable to the largest economies in Asia.

The authors contend that the conventional wisdom, as expressed by McPhee (1926), attributing economic growth in West Africa to factors such as railways, malaria prophylaxis, and British rule, is misguided. Instead, they suggest that the field of African economic history has experienced a renaissance due to newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016).

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that most studies of African growth use data beginning in 1950 or 1960, which show little improvement in per capita incomes during the second half of the twentieth century. They assert that in other regions, new contributions to historical national accounting have provided insights into patterns of growth and development over centuries, rewriting the history of economic divergence and convergence since before the industrial revolution (Broadberry, 2021).

Moreover, Sinclair (2022) builds on Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) work by examining the economic growth of the Kongo Kingdom in Central Africa between the 14th and 19th centuries. Sinclair (2022) demonstrates that the Kongo Kingdom had a complex and dynamic economy, with a diversified agricultural base, a thriving trade network, and a system of tribute payments that supported a substantial urban population.

In this context, the Kongo Kingdom's economic growth was not solely dependent on European colonial interventions. Instead, it was shaped by a combination of indigenous institutions, trade networks, and agricultural practices. Sinclair (2022) argues that the Kongo Kingdom's economic growth was a result of its ability to adapt to changing economic conditions, such as the rise of the Atlantic slave trade, and its capacity to maintain political stability in the face of external threats.
