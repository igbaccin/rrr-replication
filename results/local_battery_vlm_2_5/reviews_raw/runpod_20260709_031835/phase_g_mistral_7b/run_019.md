The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has long been a subject of intense scholarly debate. At stake is the understanding of how historical events and societal structures have influenced the economic trajectories of nations, particularly in the context of European colonialism and its aftermath. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. This claim sets the stage for a lively disagreement, as other scholars question the extent to which these institutions were indeed extractive, and whether they have persisted as a determinant of economic growth in the long run.

These differing strategies, they argue, have had lasting consequences for income levels in the colonies today. Hopkins (2009, p.18), on the other hand, contends that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This perspective challenges the notion that the poor institutional quality of African societies is solely linked to the presence of European colonizers.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offer a complementary account, focusing on the role of African women in economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They demonstrate that women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and that the codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized women in terms of access to land. This account emphasizes the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which institutions emerge and their impact on different segments of society.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) provide evidence that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. The internal norms of mistrust were the primary means through which the slave trade impacted intergroup trust, according to their argument. This finding underscores the complex interplay between historical events, institutions, and societal norms in shaping economic growth.

The impact of European colonization on current income levels is a significant area of study, with Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) providing compelling evidence that the institutional changes resulting from colonialism played a crucial role in shaping economic growth and industrialization. They contend that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In contrast, other colonies, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, were set up as extractive states, intended to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole.

They find that countries with better institutions, as measured by various indices, tend to have higher income levels today. This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) build on this work by examining the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for this reversal. Specifically, they highlight that political power was crucial to the planters in the West Indies and other elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, as it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages. This exploitation of labor led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which hindered economic growth and industrialization.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by examining the contributions of African women to economic growth.

Colonial rule, through the codification of customary law, played a significant role in reinforcing women's subordinate status. Polly Hill (1963) documented how Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast migrated in search of farmland for cocoa, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911.

Emmanuel Akyeampong and Hippolyte Fofack (2014) also highlight that patriarchal alliances and customary law were instrumental in the invention of custom under colonial rule, further entrenching women's subordinate status. This is supported by Frankema (2012, p.2), who argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940.

The educational opportunities provided by British rule, however, did not extend equally to women. Frankema (2012, p.2) notes that malaria ecology does not reflect the historical incidence of malaria, which is arguably endogenous to educational development. This suggests that the subordinate status of women, reinforced by colonial institutions, may have hindered their access to education, further limiting their contributions to economic growth and development.

Greater inequality in New World societies, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), led to institutions that advantage elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run. This pattern is evident in the way these societies structured their economies, with elites dominating key sectors and limiting opportunities for the broader population.

This control allowed them to maintain their power and wealth, as they could manipulate prices and production to their advantage. This, in turn, restricted competition and stifled economic growth.

Moreover, the legal system in these societies often favored the elites, further entrenching their power. This reinforced the power of the elites and made it harder for the broader population to compete economically.

The persistence of this pattern over time is also evident. As the elites maintained their power and wealth, they were able to influence the development of institutions in ways that continued to advantage them. This contributed to the persistence of inequality over the long run.

This pattern of institutions that advantage elites, restrict competition, and contribute to the persistence of inequality is not unique to the New World societies. Sub-Saharan African economies, contrary to the prevailing view, had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, as argued by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) and supported by other studies (Austin & Broadberry, 2014; Fourie, 2016). This finding challenges the conventional wisdom that African economies did not experience significant growth prior to European colonial interventions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that these economies, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s, were not solely reliant on European colonial interventions for growth. Instead, they attribute this growth to the region's agricultural productivity, trade networks, and urbanization, which predated European colonization.

Austin and Broadberry (2014) and Fourie (2016) provide evidence supporting this claim, using newly digitized sources of data and innovative techniques. They show that African economies had a rich history of economic activity before European colonization, including the development of complex trade networks and urban centers.

The specific factors driving this growth may have varied across societies. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) discuss the contributions of African women to economic growth, particularly in West Africa, through their roles in agriculture, trade, and craft production.

These findings suggest that Sub-Saharan African economies had a more robust pre-colonial economic history than previously thought. This challenges the notion that African economies were primarily dependent on European colonization for growth and highlights the need for further research into the specific mechanisms and conditions that shaped economic development in the region.

Post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.11). Simson's analysis reveals that while these countries did experience a high debt burden and growth in military spending, the high-level allocation of wage resources constrained, rather than eased, the distribution of patronage (Simson 2019, p.19).

In the early post-independence period, Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda all faced substantial debt burdens, with interest payments peaking at 30%, 18%, and a smaller percentage, respectively (Simson 2019, p.11).

Simson's study also highlights a shift in the composition of employment, with jobs increasingly favoring comparatively skilled and female professions from the 1980s onward (Simson 2019, p.19). This trend suggests that while nepotistic or unmeritocratic practices may have existed within public sector recruitment, the high-level allocation of wage resources overall limited the distribution of patronage.

Furthermore, the geographic distribution of employment did not change markedly beyond the capital cities (Simson 2019, p.19). This finding is significant because it challenges the notion that post-independence budget policies were excessively focused on patronage networks and not socially beneficial spending.

The work of Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) contributes to the understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by focusing on precolonial labor market conditions, a specific aspect of institutions. This stream of scholarship suggests that these conditions played a role in shaping economic growth by influencing real wages.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time, challenging the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments. To support this claim, they reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The Kenyan case, as presented by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2), demonstrates that the East-West gap in African economic development is not only the result of different types of economic dynamics (commercialization), but also the result of varying degrees of income inequality, especially between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. The recorded differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth call for a reinterpretation of the path-dependence nature of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

This finding is significant because it underscores the importance of understanding the complex interplay of institutions, labor market conditions, and income inequality in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. It also highlights the need for future research to concentrate more on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories, rather than focusing solely on structural growth impediments.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) posits that for economic growth, a country requires not only advancing technology but also institutional and ideological adjustments, which are essential for effective participation in the modern economic system. This view is echoed by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who asserts that technological advancements such as steam and electric power, along with the large-scale plants needed to exploit them, are incompatible with traditional institutions and social views that prevailed in earlier times, including family enterprise, illiteracy, and slavery.

These changes include shifts in family formation, urbanization, and man's views on his role and achievement in society.

This necessitates a reorganization of relations among occupational status groups and a change in the relations between labor force and total population, albeit within narrow overall limits.

In the context of African economic development, these findings are significant. This underscores the need for future research to delve deeper into the role of these adjustments in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

The Maddison Project, led by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), advocates for ongoing collaboration among scholars to refine and expand Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, particularly focusing on the early modern period and Asia. This initiative underscores the importance of accurate data in understanding long-run economic growth, a fundamental aspect of the role of institutions in shaping economic development.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) acknowledge the inherent uncertainties in historical data, yet they argue that these margins of error can be estimated, and their method has been applied in several studies. They also note that the Maddison Project's members, such as the Hitotshibashi team, have experimented with these margins of error, particularly in Asian national accounts. This emphasis on refining historical data is crucial, as it allows for a more detailed understanding of long-term economic growth, a key concern in the discussion of institutional impact on economic development.

Furthermore, the Maddison Project's strategy of producing estimates even when a solid basis for them does not always exist encourages scholars to challenge and refine these estimates, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of long-term economic development. This dynamic, fostered by the Maddison Project, aligns with Kuznets' (1973) assertion that factors contributing to economic growth in less developed countries require continuous research and refinement.

In this context, the Maddison Project's focus on the early modern period and Asia is significant, as these regions have been historically underrepresented in economic growth studies. By extending and revising Maddison's estimates, the project sheds light on economic growth patterns that may have been overlooked due to data limitations, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, it will likely contribute significantly to our understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the early modern period and Asia. This ongoing collaboration among scholars underscores the importance of accurate data and continuous research in understanding the complex interplay of institutions, labor market conditions, and income inequality, as highlighted by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) in the context of African economic development.

The growth of useful knowledge, as posited by Mokyr (2005, p.54), emerges as a crucial driving force behind long-run economic growth, particularly during the Industrial Revolution in the British Isles. Mokyr (2005, p.54) argues that intellectual advancements, such as those concerning free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxation, played a significant role in this growth.

Moreover, Mokyr (2005, p.54) contends that the Enlightenment ideology, which emphasized the interplay between individual self-interest and societal welfare, fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. This ideology, coupled with the establishment of law and order, enforceable contracts, and efficient allocation of resources, contributed to the industrial revolution's sustainability, despite occasional shocks (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

For instance, the Dutch Republic and Venice, with their early institutional foundations, experienced growth earlier than Spain, which lagged behind until the late eighteenth century (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

In this context, it is worth noting that the growth of useful knowledge, as a mechanism for economic growth, is not isolated from other factors. For example, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate how greater inequality can lead to institutions that advantage elites, potentially hindering the dissemination and application of useful knowledge. This finding underscores the complex interplay of various factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, it will be essential to consider the role of the growth of useful knowledge in shaping economic growth, particularly in the early modern period and Asia, while also accounting for the influence of institutional factors, such as those highlighted by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). This integrated approach will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate dynamics shaping long-run economic growth.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, as argued by Goldin (2006, p.7).

Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, in her 1952 graduation year, exemplifies this trend. Despite graduating near the top of her class at Stanford Law School, she was offered a position as a legal secretary, with no California law firm offering her a position as a lawyer (Goldin 2006, p.7). This anecdote underscores the limited opportunities for women in professional roles during this period.

The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes. Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women.

This increased involvement of women in employment had significant downstream implications.

The British industrial revolution, traditionally associated with the employment of men and high wages, is reconsidered in light of the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor (Humphries 2013, p.16). According to Humphries, the eighteenth century was a period of marital instability, resulting in a significant number of women and children among pauper populations due to family breakdown and male abandonment. This finding is supported by other historians who depict the eighteenth century as a time of considerable marital instability (Humphries 2013, p.16).

Moreover, the high wage economy was only as reliable as the men who headed the households, and their ability and commitment to provide support could prove frail (Humphries 2013, p.16). This turbulence in family structures had downstream implications for poverty in working-class communities (Humphries 2013, p.16).

In this context, it is worth noting that the industrial revolution led to significant changes in family structures and dynamics, contributing to the rise in female labor force participation rates, as discussed in the previous section (Goldin 2006, p.7). This shift had implications for the gender wage gap, as the increased supply of female labor led to a decrease in wages for female office workers, although the gap between male and female wages persisted (Goldin 2006, p.7). The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor during the British industrial revolution thus had far-reaching implications for labor markets and family structures, challenging traditional views of the industrial revolution's causes and consequences.

The European class cultures were strategically restructured to maintain imperial control, as argued by Stoler (1989, p.6). This restructuring involved creating and reinforcing social distinctions among the colonizers themselves, ensuring the stability and legitimacy of their rule.

Stoler (1989, p.6) highlights the importance of racist ideology, fear of the Other, and obsession with white prestige in the colonies, not just as justifications for continued European rule and white supremacy, but as part of a class-based logic that kept potentially subversive white colonials in line. The author underscores the need to examine the internal political tensions among the British themselves, often overlooked in colonial studies.

Moreover, Stoler (1989, p.6) points out that the numerous accounts of British and French interaction with the colonized and their philosophies of rule rarely treat the everyday practice of colonial domination. The author emphasizes the significance of understanding the distinct class interests within the colonial state, a point also touched upon by Cooper (1980) and Lonsdale and Berman (1979).

This restructuring of European class cultures, in turn, had implications for the dynamics of power and control within the colonies. By creating and reinforcing social distinctions among the colonizers, the European powers ensured a more stable and orderly rule, which could be more effectively maintained and extended.

However, it is crucial to note that the specific mechanisms through which this restructuring was achieved varied across different colonies and over time. Future research could delve into these specificities to better understand the nuances of European imperial control and its impact on the colonized societies.

In the realm of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and others posit that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping these institutions to favor the interests of powerful groups over society as a whole. This perspective challenges the efficiencies-driven view of institutional development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that the interdependency of various institutions within a wider framework significantly contributed to early industrialization and capitalism in Europe. For instance, the European family system was interdependent with labor markets, credit markets, and collective provision of insurance and pensions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27).

Moreover, Acemoglu and Johnson (2005) suggest that favorable 'contracting' institutions, which regulate interactions between private citizens, may be partially interdependent with favorable 'property rights' institutions, which constrain the behavior of political and economic elites. This interdependency underscores the importance of considering entire institutional frameworks rather than focusing on one institution in isolation (Acemoglu & Johnson, 2005).

Furthermore, cultural theories, such as Putnam's theory of 'social capital', also emphasize the role of cultural beliefs and values in shaping economic institutions. Putnam contends that 'civic' cultures, characterized by trust, norms of reciprocity, and horizontal social networks, encourage institutions that favor economic growth and political accountability (Putnam, 1993).

The quality of institutions, particularly guilds in western Europe, played a crucial role in shaping the skill premium during the late medieval period. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) argues that these institutions emerged to address labor market issues and ensure fair apprenticeship contracts, despite earlier criticisms of their monopolistic tendencies.

The guilds, by providing formal structures for apprenticeships and regulating the trade, ensured a more equitable distribution of skills in the labor market. This, in turn, contributed to the fall in the skill premium in western Europe compared to other parts of the world economy during this period.

Moreover, the explanation for this global pattern of skill premiums focuses on factor proportions, the quality of institutions, and demand-related factors (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The skill premium, as measured in this study, is also posited as a predictor of long-term economic performance.

Furthermore, the role of these institutions in shaping the skill premium has implications for understanding the long-run economic growth in western Europe. By ensuring fair apprenticeship contracts and addressing labor market issues, these institutions facilitated the transfer of skills and contributed to the fall in the skill premium, which, in turn, may have had positive effects on long-term economic performance.

In this context, it is worth noting that the quality of institutions, particularly guilds, was not uniform across western Europe. Differences in the quality of institutions could have led to variations in the skill premium and, consequently, in the long-term economic performance of different regions. Future research could explore these regional differences to gain a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping the skill premium and long-run economic growth in western Europe.

Institutions, as underpinnings of economic growth, are shaped by a complex interplay of historical, political, and social factors. This stream of literature, anchored by the work of Austin (2008, p.2), North (1989, p.9), and North and Weingast (1989, p.30), posits that indigenous agency plays a significant role in determining institutional choices, which in turn influence long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths, needs qualification and methodological modification to account for the varying historical contexts in which institutions that favor economic growth are adopted.

One specific mechanism that these sources jointly establish is the importance of secure property rights in promoting economic growth. North (1989, p.9) highlights the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution. This, in turn, reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the success of the English economy was due in part to the sovereign's commitment to secure property rights, a commitment that was crucial for the development of markets.

For instance, in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, and the kinds of institutions that favored economic growth in these contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose. This underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions are adopted and the role they play in shaping long-run economic growth.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains significantly boosted agricultural development, as posited by Hornbeck (2010, p.8) and supported by other scholars.

Hornbeck's (2010, p.8) research indicates that, before the advent of barbed wire, herd-law counties experienced minimal improvement in farmland between 1870 and 1880. However, following the widespread adoption of barbed wire, these counties experienced a substantial increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32).

This increase in agricultural development is further substantiated by the work of other scholars, such as Kuznets (1973, p.1), who stress the importance of land-use improvements for economic growth in less developed countries. By offering farmers a more secure means of protecting their lands, barbed wire facilitated the kind of agricultural investment and improvement that contributes to long-term economic growth (Kuznets).

Moreover, the enhanced agricultural development brought about by barbed wire had downstream implications for the broader economy. As agricultural productivity improved, farmers were able to produce more surplus, which could be used to purchase goods and services from other sectors, thereby stimulating economic growth beyond agriculture (Kuznets).

The debate between these two streams of scholarship can be reconciled by considering the specific historical evidence. In contrast, regions with low population and high mortality among Europeans, such as the British Isles, saw the development of institutions that were conducive to long-term growth.

Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and context-dependent. While Acemoglu et al.
