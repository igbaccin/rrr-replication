The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a topic of considerable scholarly debate, with significant implications for understanding global economic disparities. At stake is the question of how and why some countries have experienced sustained economic growth while others have languished in poverty. This review organizes the literature around five streams of related scholarship, each offering distinct perspectives on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.

First, Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. Their argument rests on the premise that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In the other extreme, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al., 2001, p.27).

Second, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) challenge this narrative by arguing that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and how colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land (Akyeampong & Fofack, 2014, p.15).

Third, Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.10) posits that the Atlantic slave trade reduced the population from a level that was previously relatively high and encouraged the development of institutions that were inimical to long-term growth.

Fourth, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. They contend that political power was important to the planters in the West Indies, and to other elites in the colonies specializing in plantation agriculture, because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages (Acemoglu et al., 2002, p.34).

Finally, Frankema (2012, p.2) proposes a rival cause that does not operate through the topic's variable, arguing that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa facilitated formal education primarily through the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, rather than direct investment by colonial governments before 1940. These streams of scholarship offer complementary and competing explanations for the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu and his colleagues argue that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have a significant impact on current income levels across countries, with a focus on differences in institutions originating from different types of states and state policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48)(Nunn 2008, p.2). The paper posits that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was fostered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

One specific mechanism through which these institutional changes influenced economic growth is the impact on political power dynamics. This exploitative labor system stunted economic development in these regions.

Not all colonies with extractive institutions remained economically stagnant. Nunn argues that the African continent simultaneously experienced four slave trades for nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900 (Nunn 2008, p.2). Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). Despite its extractive institutions, the Kongo kingdom was able to support trade with the Europeans, potentially leading to economic growth in the long run.

This stream of literature suggests that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism have a complex and nuanced impact on current income levels across countries. The type and extent of institutional changes, as well as the specific conditions in each colony, play a significant role in determining the long-term economic consequences of colonization.

One specific mechanism through which this shaping occurs is the subordination of women in access to land and resources, reinforced by colonial rule.

Colonial rule established a dual legal system that enshrined the subordinate status of women in customary law, reinforcing their marginalization in land access (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

This subordination of women persisted and had downstream implications for economic growth. He suggests that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still develop progressive institutions capable of upholding the rule of law, but societies that were ethnically homogeneous but dysfunctional were more likely to have poor institutions, which are linked to Africa's dismal economic record (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

This pattern of institutional subordination and its impact on economic growth is further qualified by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). They argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. Their research indicates that the slave trade's estimated impact on intergroup trust is primarily unexplained by its effect on others' trustworthiness, but rather by an internal channel of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, in turn, has implications for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Mokyr (2005, p.54) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the claim that the growth of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic growth, particularly in less developed countries.

These niches, according to Mokyr, provided protection against greedy "roving bandits" who could potentially undermine economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by noting that effective participation in the modern economic system necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, social views, and other aspects of society, such as urbanization and family formation.

In less developed countries, the growth of useful knowledge can lead to institutional changes that facilitate economic growth. For instance, the Enlightenment ideas that emphasized the benefits of free trade, improved infrastructure, and efficient taxes were instrumental in driving economic growth in Europe (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

The growth of useful knowledge can also lead to the establishment of trust, which is crucial for economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that the Industrial Revolution was underpinned by a growing trust in law and order, which facilitated the efficient allocation of resources and the development of enforceable contracts. This aligns with the findings of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and has implications for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices.

The widespread adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as Hornbeck (2010, p.8) argues, not only enabled farmers to protect their land more effectively but also played a crucial role in enforcing property rights, thereby fostering agricultural development. This development, in turn, reduced the costs of land protection and facilitated the growth of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Furthermore, Hornbeck demonstrates that counties that adopted barbed wire experienced a significant increase in the improved fraction of farmland from 1880 to 1890. This increase in improved farmland, in turn, facilitated the growth of useful knowledge by providing a more secure environment for the accumulation and dissemination of agricultural knowledge.

The importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth is further underscored by the findings of Mokyr (2005, p.54). Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge can lead to the establishment of trust, which is crucial for economic growth. The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains can be seen as an example of this mechanism, as it provided farmers with a means to secure their property rights, thereby fostering the growth of useful knowledge and economic growth.

The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), seeks to extend and refine Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia. This endeavor is instrumental in understanding long-run economic growth.

The project's methodology involves estimating the margins of error in historical data, which can be substantial, especially for data from distant pasts (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). These estimates are, however, subjective and based on assumptions about the reliability of the underlying data.

One significant implication of the Maddison Project's work is the potential to revise and improve historical GDP estimates, particularly for Asia. For instance, the Hitotsubashi team, contributing to the project, has conducted detailed studies on Asian national accounts, providing a more nuanced understanding of economic growth in the region (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This revised data could offer valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, a topic of significant interest in the broader literature (Acemoglu et al., 2001; Nunn, 2008).

Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) collectively argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, were the primary driver of the development of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They also acknowledge that political factors underlying economic growth and market development are equally significant (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

In this context, the interdependency of individual institutions within a wider framework becomes apparent. For instance, the European family system, according to Laslett, was interdependent with labor markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions; this whole interlinked institutional system, Laslett speculates, contributed to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Similarly, the community-based welfare system in pre-industrial England, according to Solar, was interdependent with labor markets, land markets, credit markets, and the nuclear family system; this distinctive framework, he argues, may have furthered economic growth in the centuries before industrialization (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

These findings suggest that the development of economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe was a complex process driven by both efficiency and distributional considerations. The interdependency of institutions within a wider framework was crucial, as it facilitated coordinated actions and contributed to economic growth. This understanding offers valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, a topic of significant interest in the broader literature (Acemoglu et al., 2001; Nunn, 2008).

This downstream implication is further qualified by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) and (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12)). This finding underscores the need for institutions that promote equality and protect property rights to foster economic growth in the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further nuanced when considering the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, a factor often overlooked in traditional narratives of the British industrial revolution. This perspective, advanced by Humphries (2013, p.16) and Goldin (2006, p.7), challenges the conventional focus on men and high wages.

Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that the eighteenth century was a period of considerable marital instability, associated with economic, social, and political conditions. This instability led to a significant group of families whose fathers, while yet-living, were not present, alongside the perhaps 18% whose fathers had died. Goldin (2006, p.7), on the other hand, demonstrates that the increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century underwent a quiet revolution, primarily driven by the generation born in the late 1940s.

The availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor, as proposed by these scholars, had downstream implications on the nature and pace of industrialization. For instance, the increased involvement of women in employment could have led to a more flexible and adaptable workforce, capable of responding swiftly to changing economic conditions. This adaptability, in turn, could have contributed to the resilience and sustainability of economic growth.

The role of women and children in employment was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including cultural norms, legal restrictions, and economic pressures. For instance, Goldin (2006, p.7) highlights the role of educational institutions in increasing the supply of potential office workers, while Humphries (2013, p.16) points to the impact of war, empire building, and labor mobility on family structures and male employment patterns.

The papers by Stoler (1989, p.6) and North (1989, p.9) offer complementary insights into the shaping of long-run economic growth through institutions, albeit from different perspectives. Stoler (1989, p.6) argues that European class cultures were intentionally restructured to create and maintain the social distinctions of imperial control, serving as a critical apparatus to keep potentially subversive white colonials in line (Stoler 1989, p.6). This social engineering could have contributed to the resilience and sustainability of the colonial system.

In parallel, North (1989, p.9) emphasizes the significance of political and economic institutions in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants (North 1989, p.9).

This adaptability of European institutions under imperial control could have been a double-edged sword, fostering economic growth while also reinforcing the colonial power structure.

The perception of static customary landholding in sub-Saharan Africa, as suggested by Peters (2004, p.5), masks processes of exclusion and class formation (Peters 2004, p.5). This observation challenges the common assumption that African landholding practices are communal and inhibit individual investment in agriculture. Peters argues that, during the final decade of colonial rule, agricultural officers in charge of schemes of agricultural 'improvement' and resettlement sought to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming, expressing overt dissatisfaction with African landholding practices. The author further highlights the work of Platteau (1996, 2000) and Amanor (2001), who critique unproblematic visions of land tenure in Africa that overlook considerable social inequality in many customary systems and the encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and commercial benefits.

Simson (2019, p.11) offers evidence that Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions. The paper demonstrates that the high debt burden and military spending in these countries were largely the result of regional instability and growing domestic dissent, rather than a deliberate strategy to maintain power and control through resource allocation.

Peters's (2004) research suggests that the persistence of a perception of static customary landholding can provide advantages for certain groups seeking to maintain power and control by keeping officials ignorant of their activities. Simson's (2019) findings, on the other hand, indicate that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not primarily driven by a desire to maintain power through resource allocation. Both streams of literature underscore the importance of understanding the complex dynamics of resource allocation and the potential for certain groups to benefit from maintaining certain perceptions.

The interplay between perceptions of customary landholding and resource allocation in sub-Saharan Africa offers a compelling avenue for further exploration, particularly in relation to the long-run economic growth in these regions. Understanding how these dynamics have evolved over time, and the impact they have had on social and economic development, could provide valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping economic growth in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, is a complex and multifaceted issue that has garnered significant attention from scholars. A shared finding among many (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) is the critical role of European colonization in establishing institutions that have had lasting impacts on current income levels and economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the varying colonization strategies adopted by Europeans led to the establishment of institutions that either encouraged investment and the rule of law, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, or maintained extractive states, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the significant contributions made by African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods, particularly in the context of cash crops and the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

However, a point of disagreement arises regarding the impact of ethno-linguistic fragmentation on the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). While Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect, Alesina, Devleeschauwer, Easterly, Kurlat, and Wacziarg (2003) suggest that such fragmentation could be detrimental to the development of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.10).

The historical evidence that settles this disagreement can be found in the study by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This culture of mistrust, in turn, could have significant implications for the quality of public goods provided by African governments, as it may discourage cooperation and trust among different ethnic groups.
