The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in contemporary economic scholarship, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions are responsible for the economic trajectories of nations. This review focuses on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, highlighting key findings and disagreements within the literature.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that institutions established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that colonization strategies varied, with some leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others resulted in extractive states that transferred resources quickly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further assert that these institutional differences account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor.

On the other hand, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. These antecedents, flowing into the topic's causal variable, complement rather than compete with the claim that institutions shape long-run economic growth.

This institutional diversity, they contend, significantly impacts current income levels across countries.

He posits that African societies, particularly those with centralized governments, national currencies, and well-developed trading networks, were more capable of supporting trade with Europeans, thus experiencing less negative effects from the slave trades.

They suggest that these institutional changes account for a significant fraction of current income differences. The emergence of formal institutions, such as guilds, in western Europe during the late medieval period played a crucial role in shaping factor proportions, institutional quality, and demand-related factors, which in turn contributed to the region's long-run economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3).

These institutions have been criticized for monopolizing skills and potentially maintaining relative pay above market equilibrium levels (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

They emphasize the subjective nature of estimates of economic data in the past, particularly as one goes back in time, but also acknowledge the progress made in this field due to the pioneering work of scholars like Maddison (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

In qualifying the role of guilds, van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that while they may have monopolized skills, their beneficial effects on the intergenerational transfer of skills may be counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The establishment of fundamental institutions of representative government, including an explicit constitution, a parliament, and an independent judiciary, played a crucial role in providing political protection for economic rights, thereby fostering long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They contend that the financial revolution in England, which included the creation of joint-stock companies and the National Debt, was made possible by the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which established a constitutional monarchy and an independent judiciary. These institutions provided the political stability necessary for the development of financial markets and economic growth.

They argue that in societies with extreme inequality, elites were able to maintain their status by controlling the institutions that governed economic exchange, thereby limiting the economic opportunities for the majority of the population. This, in turn, hindered economic growth.

It is crucial to acknowledge that the establishment of representative institutions offers political protection for economic rights, but it does not guarantee economic growth. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that while extreme inequality can lead to institutions that disadvantage the majority, this does not mean that all societies with extreme inequality will have such institutions.

The widespread adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as Hornbeck (2010, p.8) demonstrates, significantly boosted agricultural development by enhancing land protection and enforcing property rights. Hornbeck's study reveals that the near-universal implementation of barbed wire led to a marked increase in the improved fraction of farmland and total improved land in herd-law counties.

This shift in land-use patterns, in turn, fostered agricultural development in the American Plains.

In addition to its role in securing property rights, barbed wire also played a crucial role in enabling farmers to protect their land more effectively. The increased protection offered by barbed wire likely contributed to the long-term success of agricultural development in the region (Hornbeck, 2010, p.32).

This strengthening of land protection and property rights enforcement, consequently, fostered agricultural development and set the stage for further economic growth in the American Plains. The implications of this mechanism extend beyond the immediate agricultural sector, as secure property rights are often considered a foundational element of long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

In the context of the British industrial revolution, the availability of cheap and amenable female and child labor emerged as a significant factor, shaping the development of inventions that aimed to reduce the need for skilled adult workers. Humphries (2013, p.16) argues that this perspective offers a more accurate understanding of the revolution, moving away from a focus on men and high wages. The author supports this claim by highlighting the prevalence of family breakdown and male abandonment during the eighteenth century, which resulted in a significant number of women and children among pauper populations (Humphries, 2013, p.16).

The presence of a considerable group of families whose fathers were either absent or deceased, testifies to the turbulence of the times, with war, empire building, and labor mobility straining men's links to wives and children (Humphries, 2013, p.16). This situation, in turn, created an environment where inventions were designed to minimize the reliance on skilled adult workers. For instance, the development of spinning jennies and power looms, key innovations during the industrial revolution, were primarily aimed at reducing the need for skilled adult weavers and spinners (Humphries, 2013, p.16).

Furthermore, Humphries (2013, p.16) emphasizes that the availability of cheap labor was not limited to women and children but also extended to immigrants. The author notes that the industrial revolution was characterized by a significant influx of immigrants, many of whom were willing to work for lower wages, contributing to the overall availability of cheap labor (Humphries, 2013, p.2). This influx of labor, combined with the prevalence of family breakdown and male abandonment, created a unique labor market dynamic that was instrumental in shaping the British industrial revolution.

The impact of the slave trade on African culture and economic development is a subject of significant scholarly interest, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) offering compelling insights.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) propose that the slave trade led to a culture of mistrust that persists today.

They note that the de-privileging of women in access to land under colonial rule reinforced the central role of men in the codification of customary law, which further solidified the subordinate status of women (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This subordination could potentially contribute to a culture of mistrust, as trust and cooperation are often more prevalent in societies where gender roles are more equitable.

The implications of this culture of mistrust are far-reaching. Trust is a crucial factor in fostering economic growth, as it encourages cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and promotes investment (Knack & Keefer, 1997; Fafchamps, 2006; Tabellini, 2007; Algan & Cahuc, 2010). Therefore, the culture of mistrust fostered by the slave trade could potentially hinder economic growth in Africa.

The impact of the slave trade on African culture and economic development is intricate and multifaceted. While the slave trade may have fostered a culture of mistrust, it also led to the development of new economic institutions and the integration of African economies into the global economy (Frankema, 2012; Goldin, 2014). Understanding the net impact of the slave trade on African development requires a nuanced analysis of these various factors.

The perception of static customary landholding in sub-Saharan Africa, as Peters (2004, p.5) and Simson (2019, p.11) argue, masks processes of exclusion and class formation. Peters (2004, p.5) reveals that the final decade of colonial rule, driven by the push for agricultural development, intensified prejudice against African landholding practices, perceived as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment. Simson (2019, p.11), on the other hand, demonstrates that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending, contrary to common assumptions.

Peters (2004, p.5) exposes how these unproblematic visions of land tenure in Africa overlook considerable social inequality in many customary systems, as well as evidence of encroachment by some groups on others for livelihood and commercial benefits, of state appropriation, and illegal sales by 'traditional' leaders. Simson (2019, p.11) further illustrates this dynamic by showing how, too often, national and local elites capture the benefits of decentralization to 'local' and 'traditional' managers.

These findings suggest that the benefits of decentralization and agricultural development in sub-Saharan Africa have not always been equitably distributed, with certain groups maintaining power and control through the perpetuation of the perception of static customary landholding. This realization calls for a more nuanced understanding of land tenure systems in the region, one that acknowledges and addresses the underlying social divisions and processes of exclusion.

The increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles in the United States during the past century was primarily driven by exogenous changes such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century. This shift, as Goldin (2006, p.7) argues, was not a gradual evolution but a quiet revolution, a transformation that significantly altered the social and economic landscape of the country.

Goldin (2006, p.7) presents a compelling case for this revolution, highlighting the increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century as a key driver. Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, in her recollections, underscores this point, noting that her first job interview after graduating near the top of her 1952 Stanford Law School class led her to a position as a legal secretary, with no law firm offering her a position as a lawyer (Goldin, 2006, p. 7).

This shift was not solely a result of increased demand, but was also facilitated by changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s, which altered female labor supply, and institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women (Goldin, 2006, p. 3). The growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s also greatly increased the supply of potential office workers, contributing to this transformation.

This increased involvement of women in employment, education, and family roles had significant downstream implications. For instance, it led to a narrowing of the gender wage gap, as women began to compete more directly with men in the labor market (Goldin 2006, p.7).
