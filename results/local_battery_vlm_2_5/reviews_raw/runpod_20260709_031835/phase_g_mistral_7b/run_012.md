The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a subject of intense debate among scholars. At stake is an understanding of the factors that have driven the divergent economic trajectories of nations, with some thriving while others have stagnated. The disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography, culture, or demography, in shaping economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that the differences in the colonization strategies of Europeans led to the establishment of varying institutions, with some promoting investment and the rule of law, while others were extractive in nature, transferring resources rapidly to the metropole. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), on the other hand, focus on the contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They highlight how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming and how the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced their subordinate status.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) suggests that the new economic history of Africa has implications for policies affecting institutional reforms and can contribute to eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He argues that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths. He suggests a need for qualification and methodological modification to account for this complexity.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today. They contend that this mistrust, rooted in the slave trade, has negative consequences for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The paper by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argues that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in former European colonies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) identify two extreme cases of European colonization strategies: those that focused on establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand; and those that set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast. The authors argue that the former colonies with institutions promoting investment and the rule of law have experienced greater economic growth compared to the latter.

One specific mechanism through which these institutional changes have influenced economic growth is by shaping the property rights regime. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They suggest that in colonies where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, property rights were more secure, leading to increased investment and economic growth. In colonies where Europeans established extractive states, property rights were insecure, impeding investment and economic growth.

Another downstream implication of these institutional changes is the impact on political institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.34) explain that political power was important to the planters in the West Indies and other elites in colonies specializing in plantation agriculture because it enabled them to force large masses of natives or African slaves to work for low wages. This exploitation of labor led to the development of extractive political institutions, which have persisted and continue to hinder economic growth in these regions.

In Africa, the slave trades had a negative impact on current economic development, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.2). Sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks.

Conflicts over resource distribution played a crucial role in shaping the economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the efficiency-based explanations (Ogilvie 2007, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that the European family system, labour markets for youths and women, credit markets to amass savings, and collective rather than kin-based provision of insurance and pensions were interdependent, contributing to early industrialization and capitalism (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that greater inequality in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality over the long run (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2).

One specific mechanism that these sources jointly establish is the interdependency of institutions within a wider framework.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) provides evidence of the impact of indigenous agency on institutional choice, stating that larger populations could resist European encroachment and occupied epidemiological zones that imposed a high rate of mortality on Europeans. In such regions, the European presence was either marginal, in which case unproductive institutions remained in place, or took the form of colonial rule, which introduced extractive types of economic control that were poorly suited to long-term development.

Austin (2008, p.2) further argues that economic rent and economic growth have often been joint products, including in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. The kinds of institutions that favoured economic growth in certain historical contexts were not necessarily optimal for that purpose in others. Englebert, as cited by Hopkins (2009, p.18), concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still be dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still be capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions.

The emergence of political institutions that specify 'efficient' property rights and provide effective enforcement is a key factor in long-run economic growth, as demonstrated in seventeenth-century England (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.30).

The financial revolution played a critical role in England's long-run success, and its implications extend beyond the English context. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) suggest that even if other forces would ultimately have led England to success under the Stuarts, they would have done so more slowly and probably less decisively. This implies that the emergence of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can significantly accelerate economic growth, a finding that has implications for policies aimed at promoting economic development in various contexts.

Moreover, the relationship between the benefits derived from rule specification and the costs of measurement is critical in this study (North 1989, p.3). North (1989, p.9) argues that since we receive utility from the various attributes of goods and services rather than from the entities themselves, it is the costliness of measuring the separable dimensions that is critical. This relationship has been critical in the history of property rights (common property vs. private property) and is at the heart of many of the issues related to the structure and effectiveness of enforcement.

The growth of useful knowledge is posited as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, as intellectual factors played a crucial role in driving technological change and industrialization (Mokyr 2005, p.54).

Mokyr (2005, p.54) also contends that intellectual progress was a prerequisite and key factor in the Industrial Revolution's geographical concentration. The Dutch Republic and Venice had early sixteenth-century beginnings, while Spain had a late eighteenth-century start, both in terms of institutional basis supporting growth compared to other economies. It may be that industrial and even less revolutionary changes were wholly British, but it was the taproot of growth that made them possible (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

This emphasis on intellectual factors as the mainspring of economic growth is supported by Mokyr's (2005, p.54) examination of scientific societies per capita between 1600 and 1800. As shown in Figure 7, scientific societies proliferated in the British Isles, Small Countries, France, and Italy during this period, indicating a focus on the growth of useful knowledge in these regions (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This trend is not limited to Europe, as similar patterns can be observed in other historical economies where intellectual progress was prioritized (North 1989, p.9). The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, serves as a critical link between institutions and long-run economic growth.

In the examination of the British industrial revolution, Humphries (2013, p.16) posits a shift in focus, arguing for the consideration of cheap and amenable female and child labor as a key factor. This claim complements the traditional emphasis on men and high wages. Humphries's research underscores the significant presence of families where fathers, although still alive, were absent due to various factors such as war, empire-building, and labor mobility (Humphries 2013, p.16).

This trend of family instability is corroborated by historians, as depicted by Sharpe, Snell, Kent, and Bailey (Humphries 2013, p.16). For instance, Sharpe concluded that 10% of marriages in Colyton between 1725 and 1756 ended in separation (Humphries 2013, p.16). These findings suggest that the male-breadwinner households' reliability in the high-wage economy was contingent on the men who headed them, and their ability and commitment to provide support could be fragile.

This turbulence, in turn, contributed to the growth and development of the British industrial revolution by providing a ready pool of cheap and amenable labor.

The political stability offered by colonial rule facilitated large-scale infrastructure projects that indirectly contributed to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, thereby influencing long-run economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This claim is substantiated by two mechanisms: the influence of British traders and Protestant missionaries, and the positive impact of infrastructure on education.

The missionaries were instrumental in establishing schools, while the traders, by creating a demand for literate workers, provided a practical incentive for education.

These infrastructural developments provided easier access to educational institutions and facilitated the dissemination of knowledge, thereby enhancing the quality and reach of education.

The positive impact of infrastructure on education is further qualified by the observation that the extent of infrastructure development varied across different colonies, leading to differences in the level and pace of educational development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). For instance, colonies with a more extensive infrastructure network, such as Kenya, experienced faster educational development compared to less developed colonies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue, along with Kuznets (1973, p.1), that for less developed countries to achieve long-run economic growth, they need both technological advancements and institutional and ideological adjustments. The evidence presented by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) suggests that many Sub-Saharan African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, on par with the largest economies in Asia until the 1980s. This finding challenges the view expressed in early economic histories of Africa, which assumed that any economic growth above subsistence resulted from European colonial interventions.

In the realm of economic growth, the quality of institutions, particularly guilds in late medieval western Europe, emerges as a critical factor in shaping the skill premium, ensuring fair apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

However, the guilds' role has been a subject of debate. Critics contend that these institutions monopolized skills and attempted to maintain higher relative pay levels, potentially counterbalancing their beneficial effects on skill transmission. Yet, the guilds' role in economic development has been reassessed by various authors who have studied their functioning in detail (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

This finding aligns with other research that emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, suggesting that the quality of institutions can have profound implications for long-run economic performance (North 1989, North & Weingast 1989).

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as demonstrated by Hornbeck (2010, p.8), serves as a compelling illustration of the role property rights institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth. Hornbeck's study reveals that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement.

A key finding in Hornbeck's work is the decline in the improved fraction of farmland in herd-law counties before the introduction of barbed wire, followed by a substantial increase after its adoption (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This shift suggests that the enforcement of property rights through herd laws was ineffective prior to the introduction of barbed wire, but became more efficient once barbed wire was widely adopted.

Hornbeck's research aligns with other studies that emphasize the importance of property rights institutions in economic development. For instance, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) argue that the Magna Carta, by establishing clear property rights and limiting the discretionary power of the monarch, laid the foundation for the rule of law and the subsequent economic growth in England. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) demonstrate that the quality of institutions, particularly property rights, significantly influenced the economic growth of former European colonies.

In the context of the American Plains, the adoption of barbed wire can be seen as a manifestation of the evolution and improvement of property rights institutions. This evolution, in turn, facilitated agricultural development and contributed to long-run economic growth in the region.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as a result, not only transformed the landscape but also served as a testament to the profound impact of property rights institutions on long-run economic growth. The Maddison Project, spearheaded by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), collaborates among scholars to extend and revise Angus Maddison's estimates of GDP and population, with a particular focus on the early modern period and Asia. This initiative aims to produce and update global economic data, as recognized by Bolt and van Zanden themselves (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

One specific mechanism that the Maddison Project and its collaborators have employed is the recognition and quantification of margins of error in historical economic data. These margins of error are based on subjective estimates of the underlying data's potential inaccuracies, which are larger the further one goes back in time (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This acknowledgement of error is crucial for understanding the limitations and potential biases in historical economic data, particularly in regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and pre-Colombian Latin America, where data may be more scarce and less reliable.

This improved understanding of long-term economic development is essential for addressing the complexities and nuances of economic growth in former European colonies, as explored in the previous sections. By extending and revising Maddison's estimates, the Maddison Project contributes to a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Goldin's (2006) study reveals that the increased involvement of women in these spheres was not merely a response to changing societal norms but a result of economic factors, as evidenced by the emergence of jobs previously unavailable to women.

The generation born in the late 1940s and early 1950s, often referred to as the baby boomers, played a crucial role in this transformation. Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, a member of this generation, recounted her experience of graduating from Stanford Law School near the top of her class in 1952 but being offered a secretarial position instead of a lawyer position due to the limited opportunities available for women at the time (Goldin 2006, p.7).

The shifts from one phase to the next were due to various exogenous changes, including the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, which made "nice" jobs available (Goldin 2006, p.3).

As educational institutions at the secondary level expanded from the 1910s to the 1940s, the supply of potential office workers also increased, further facilitating the employment of women in these roles (Goldin 2006, p.3).

This transformation had profound implications for women's roles in the United States.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of extensive scholarly debate, with several streams of related scholarship offering insights into this complex relationship. One stream, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), posits that the institutions and property rights established during European colonization have a significant impact on current income levels across countries. They contend that the differences in colonization strategies led to varying institutional structures, with some, like the United States, promoting investment and the rule of law, while others, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, established extractive states that hindered long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19)).

Simultaneously, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that the role of institutions in economic growth should not be limited to the post-colonial period. They highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development in pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their research demonstrates that women's roles in agriculture, particularly in the production of cash crops, played a crucial part in economic revolutions, such as the one that made the Gold Coast the world's leading cocoa producer in 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) and (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20)).

However, while both streams acknowledge the importance of institutions in economic growth, they differ in their focus on historical periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) primarily focus on the post-colonial era, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) extend their analysis to pre-colonial and colonial periods. This discrepancy underscores the need for a comprehensive historical examination of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, considering both pre- and post-colonial periods.

A potential avenue for such a comprehensive examination could be found in the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), who investigate the impact of the slave trade on intergroup trust in Africa. Their research provides empirical evidence of the long-lasting effects of historical events on contemporary institutions and economic outcomes, offering a valuable comparison point for the analysis of the role of institutions in economic growth across different historical periods (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) and (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4)).
