In the realm of economic history, a lively debate persists concerning the impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development. At stake is the understanding of how institutional changes, technological advancements, and societal dynamics interplay in shaping the economic trajectories of nations. This discourse is marked by two contrasting positions: one that posits the detrimental effects of Stalinist industrialization, and another that argues for its transformative potential.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Williamson (1996, p.24) contribute to the first position, asserting that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, and that globalization and convergence in the 19th century led to predictable economic impacts, including political alliances and policies aimed at protecting losers. Bryant (2006, p.17) also supports this view, suggesting that the revisionist position, which posits all Eurasian societies were progressing along a shared modernizing course, is empirically suspect and analytically incoherent.

On the other hand, Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) and Kuznets (1973, p.10) argue that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, and that evidence from long-term national experiences suggests that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time. Komlos (1998, p.16) adds to this stream by asserting that during the early stages of modern economic growth, given the state of technology, populations experienced a downward pressure on their nutritional status, but industrialization and its concomitant, the increased division of labor, unleashed other processes that magnified their impact on nutritional status.

The debate between these two positions is significant, as it sheds light on the complex interplay of institutional changes, technological advancements, and societal dynamics in shaping economic development. Future research should aim to reconcile these contrasting views and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development.

The results of their study provide support for the view that institutions played an important role in the process of economic growth and in the surge of industrialization among the formerly poor colonies.

This decline, they argue, was not a result of colonial exploitation but rather stemmed from differences in economic structures and institutional arrangements.

Under the lens of Lindert and Williamson's (1985) analysis, industrialization, initially, may engender increased inequality, yet this disequilibrium could be a transitory phase leading to the coexistence of economic growth and increased income equality over time. This assertion is substantiated by the authors' examination of historical national experiences, which suggests that growth and income equality can coexist in the long term.

As capital accumulation accelerates, real wages among the unskilled remain stagnant until the surplus labor pool is depleted. This turning point can be delayed with appropriate demographic factors, such as Malthusian population pressures or immigration, replenishing the initial pool (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14).

This disequilibrium, although documented in many Third World economies, was previously unclear in the context of 19th-century capitalist development.

Temin's argument that economic growth in Western Europe after World War II was driven by a developmental deficit and disequilibrium is substantiated by the interaction between two key factors: the delayed industrialization of Germany compared to Britain and the protection of German farmers against low-priced American grain. This developmental deficit, according to Temin (2002, p.8), created a unique opportunity for rapid growth in the post-war period.

The delayed industrialization of Germany, as noted by Temin (2002, p.8), meant that the country had to play catch-up in terms of industrial output and productivity. This competitive pressure drove German industries to innovate and improve efficiency, contributing to the rapid economic growth witnessed in the post-war period.

In addition, the protection of German farmers against low-priced American grain served to insulate the agricultural sector from external competition, allowing it to maintain higher prices and profits. This protection, in turn, facilitated the growth of the industrial sector by providing a stable and profitable market for agricultural products.

The implications of this developmental deficit and disequilibrium were far-reaching. As Temin (2002, p.16) notes, the agricultural labor share in Europe, which had been steadily declining since the mid-nineteenth century, experienced a sudden reversal in the post-war period. This reversal, driven by the protective measures taken to support German agriculture, had significant economic and social consequences, including the preservation of rural employment and the maintenance of agricultural incomes.

However, this period of rapid growth came to an end in the 1970s when the developmental deficit was eliminated. The removal of protectionist measures and the increased integration of European economies into the global market led to a decline in agricultural prices and profits, which in turn had a ripple effect on the rest of the economy.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) challenge the assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments, arguing instead that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time. This claim, supported by their empirical analysis, challenges the notion that colonial policies were the primary cause of African poverty.

The paper demonstrates this through a comparison of growth rates in Accra and London between 1900 and 1960. Despite starting from lower levels, the average annual growth rates in Accra were comparable to those in London during the nineteenth century (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.18). Similarly, the authors show that East African colonial governments intervened more directly in the labor market due to chronic labor shortages, unlike their counterparts in West Africa where workers were attracted by wage opportunities exceeding their subsistence farming costs (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27).

This finding complicates the understanding of African economic development during the colonial era. While it does not negate the existence of structural growth impediments, it does suggest that African real wages were not persistently low, contrary to the common assumption. This implies that the causes of African poverty may be more complex than previously thought, involving a combination of factors beyond colonial policies.
