The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development remains a subject of ongoing scholarly debate. At stake is understanding the extent to which authoritarian industrialization policies can drive growth and development, and the long-term consequences of such policies. This review examines multiple streams of related scholarship, each offering insights into different aspects of the topic.

One stream of scholarship, represented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14), argues that the institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for the reversal in relative income levels among former European colonies, where relatively rich societies in 1500 are now relatively poor. This stream, however, refines the scope, magnitude, or mechanism of the impact of forced industrialization under Stalin. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) suggest that the relationship between economic growth and equality is complex, and that evidence from long-term national experiences suggests that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time.

Another stream of scholarship, represented by Komlos (1998, p.16) and Kuznets (1973, p.10), addresses the impact of forced industrialization on the well-being of populations. Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that the absolute income gain during the Industrial Revolution was too low to offset declines in health among common people, leading to a decrease in their biological well-being compared to prior generations. Kuznets (1973, p.10) emphasizes that for economic growth, a country needs advancing technology, institutional and ideological adjustments, and a wider supply of tested data, particularly for less developed countries.

In the Portuguese case, it was the state; in the Belgian case, it was King Leopold; and in the British case, it was often private enterprises who obtained concessions or monopoly trading rights in Africa. These differences in the distribution of economic benefits could have long-lasting effects on the economic development of the colonized territories.

The role of institutions in determining economic growth is crucial in understanding the success or failure of industrialization efforts, including those under Stalin, as suggested by North and Austin (North 1989, p.10; Austin 2008, p.23). He highlights the intricate regulation of the economy under Soviet central planning, with guilds imposing price ceilings and state-owned trading companies monopolizing external trade (North 1989, p.10).

Austin (2008, p.23) supports this notion by demonstrating how profits from Atlantic trade enabled the commercial bourgeoisie of western Europe to demand and obtain institutional changes to protect their property rights, which were associated with the profits from colonialism and slavery (Austin 2008, p.23). He further explains that colonial rulers, more concerned with perpetuating their rule than with extracting revenue, had no interest in sacrificing current consumption for a gamble on longer-term industrialisation in the colonies (Austin 2008, p.20).

This implies that the success of industrialization under Stalin, like that of the colonies, was heavily influenced by the institutional framework in place. The Soviet regime prioritized maintaining control and revenue extraction over long-term industrial development, which may have impeded the growth of a self-sustaining industrial sector.

Allen et al. (2011, p.12) and Peters (2004, p.30) confirm that real wages in China were significantly lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe. Allen et al. (2011, p.12) argue that the industrial revolution raised British wages above Dutch levels, and this development intensified after 1870, as shown in their figures (Allen et al. 2011, p.12; Allen et al. 2011, p.1). On the other hand, Peters (2004, p.30) illustrates that reports of negotiable landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa mask processes of exclusion, deepening social divisions, and class formation. This suggests that the institutional framework in place in China, characterized by stagnant real wages, may have hindered the development of a self-sustaining industrial sector, similar to the colonies where colonial rulers were more concerned with maintaining their rule than with fostering industrial growth (Austin 2008, p.23).

Furthermore, the institutional framework in China, as described by Allen et al. (2011, p.12), appears to have been less conducive to the emergence of a strong, self-regulating market economy. The authors show that in the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, real wages in China stagnated and were significantly lower than those in leading economies in northwestern Europe, indicating a lack of market-driven wage growth. This contrasts with the situation in Europe, where industrialization led to increasing wages and economic development.

This suggests that the lack of a strong, self-regulating market economy in China may have been due, in part, to the exclusionary and divisive nature of its institutional framework.

The papers by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) and Temin (2002, p.8) collectively challenge the common assumption that economic growth and increased income equality are incompatible. They suggest that these two aspects can coexist over time, offering significant implications for development policies aiming to promote both economic growth and social equity.

They posit that early industrialization fails to generate rising real wages among the unskilled until the surplus labor pool is exhausted, a turning point that can be delayed with the right demographic forces (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.14). Similarly, they contend that the increase in inequality that typically accompanies early industrialization can be viewed as a manifestation of disequilibrium that all capitalist countries must pass through as they emerge from a backward agrarian past (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.27).

Temin (2002, p.8) supports this finding by arguing that the rapid economic growth in Western Europe during the Golden Age after World War II was due to a developmental deficit and disequilibrium, which ended in the 1970s when this deficit was eliminated. He notes that Germany's late industrialization and protective policies for farmers against low-priced American grain contributed to this developmental deficit (Temin 2002, p.8). Furthermore, Temin (2002, p.8) presents empirical evidence showing that the agricultural labor share, a key indicator of income equality, increased during this period, suggesting the coexistence of growth and increased income equality (Temin 2002, p.16).

These findings have implications for understanding the economic development under Stalin's forced industrialization in the Soviet Union. If the coexistence of economic growth and increased income equality can be observed in Western Europe and other contexts, it is plausible that similar dynamics could have occurred in the Soviet Union. Further investigation is required to explore the particular mechanisms and conditions under which coexistence occurred in the Soviet context.

The papers in this cluster provide evidence that the Industrial Revolution led to declines in health among common people due to a downward pressure on their nutritional status. Similarly, Feinstein (1998, p.2) contends that pessimistic views on real wages and standard of living during and after the Industrial Revolution in Britain are misguided, suggesting that the working class experienced a decline in health due to factors other than income.

Komlos (1998, p.16) asserts that the integration of previously isolated regions into a larger world market and transportation improvements exacerbated the impact of industrialization on nutritional status, as subsistence farmers, lacking full knowledge of the technology of health production, were unable to sell their products easily. Feinstein (1998, p.2) qualifies this by pointing out that the years from 1770 to 1830 witnessed a doubling of the population of England and Wales, harvest failures caused by abnormal weather conditions, and the massive impact of wars with France, making it difficult to distinguish between the effects of these powerful forces and the net outcome resulting from their interaction.

In summary, the papers in this cluster suggest that the Industrial Revolution led to declines in health among common people due to a downward pressure on their nutritional status, which was further exacerbated by the integration of previously isolated regions into a larger world market and transportation improvements. Additionally, factors such as population growth, harvest failures, and wars with France should be taken into account when examining the overall impact of early industrialization and urbanization on the standard of living of the British working class.

This finding highlights the need for further research on the specific mechanisms and conditions under which economic growth and declines in health coexisted during the Industrial Revolution. Understanding these mechanisms will provide valuable insights into the economic development under Stalin's forced industrialization in the Soviet Union, a topic that remains under-researched and in need of further investigation.

The papers by Broadberry and Gupta, and others in their line of inquiry, provide compelling evidence that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, a finding that challenges some world historians who suggest it began later. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11)) focus on the comparison between unskilled workers in India and Britain, arguing that contrary to some historians, the great divergence was already in progress before 1800. They contend that the Indian economy experienced deindustrialization due to colonialism, keeping India open to cheap British imports while blocking Indian cotton textiles from the protected British market).

Moreover, Broadberry et al. (2015, (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3)) demonstrate that India's per capita GDP declined steadily from 1600 to 1871, falling behind Britain's growth during the same period. They suggest that the origins of the Great Divergence can be traced back to the early modern period).

Allen (2001, (Allen 2001, p.9)) supports this stream's argument by showing that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained (not increased) in economic leaders. This pattern resulted in a large gap in real wages between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent at the beginning of the industrial revolution).

Tracing a downstream implication, it is worth noting that the early decline in real incomes in most European cities could have set the stage for the subsequent industrial revolution, as the low wages and high savings rates created a favorable environment for investment in new technologies (Abramovitz 1986, p.12). This finding underscores the importance of understanding the mechanisms and conditions under which economic growth and declines in health coexisted during the Industrial Revolution, as it provides valuable insights into the economic development under Stalin's forced industrialization in the Soviet Union.

The assertion that post-independence budget policies in Africa were primarily unproductive and prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending is challenged by Simson's (2019) research on Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. Simson argues that these countries' budget policies were not exceptionally unproductive or patronage-driven, contrary to common assumptions (Simson 2019, p.26).

Simson demonstrates that the extensive criticism of African nationalization policies, mismanaged industrial policy, and excessive borrowing used to finance them may not fully apply to these three countries (Simson 2019, p.26).

In addition, Simson's (2019, p.26) study reveals that public sector employment as a percentage of the labor force in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda was not significantly higher than in other countries during the 1940s to 2010 period (Simson 2019, p.13). This finding challenges the notion that African post-independence budget policies were excessively focused on patronage networks.

The implication of Simson's findings is that the perception of African post-independence budget policies as primarily unproductive and patronage-driven may be overstated. This misconception could potentially hinder the development of effective policies to address economic challenges in these countries.

The understanding of these policies' true nature could contribute to more informed decision-making and policy development in Africa.

The adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains, as Hornbeck (2010, p.10) posits, significantly impacted agricultural development by increasing productivity and security, much like the implementation of central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin. This parallel is underscored by the findings of Hornbeck (2010, p.10) and Ashton (1997).

This assumption appears plausible for two main reasons, as Hornbeck (2010, p.10) notes: first, private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution by increasing both agricultural output and labor supplied to other sectors (Ashton 1997). Second, the near-universal adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains allowed for the efficient protection of farmland and crops from damage by others' cattle, thereby enhancing productivity and security.

In a related vein, the increased agricultural output stemming from property rights enforcement and land protection can be seen as a catalyst for labor movement towards more productive sectors, as suggested by Ashton (1997). This downstream implication underscores the potential for barbed wire to have played a role in the broader economic development of the American Plains, paralleling the impact of industrialization under Stalin.

With this in mind, the increased productivity and security brought about by the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains serves as a compelling example of how property rights enforcement and land protection can drive agricultural development and, by extension, economic growth.

The paper by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) challenges the widespread belief that historical African poverty was a result of persistent structural growth impediments. Contrary to this assumption, they argue that real wages in Africa were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time. This finding, supported by evidence from Accra and Nairobi, suggests that African economies were more resilient than previously thought (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.18), (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27)).

The authors demonstrate that the average annual growth rates in Accra between 1900 and 1960 were comparable to those in nineteenth-century London, despite starting from lower bases. Moreover, they find that subsistence ratios in Nairobi were comparable to long-term levels in Asia (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.18)).

The paper also underscores that the East African labor market was more interventionist due to persistent labor shortages. Unlike in West Africa, where workers were attracted to wage labor due to its potential to exceed subsistence farming income, East African governments resorted to forced labor and direct intervention in the labor market (Frankema & van Waijenburg, 2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27)).

This stream of literature offers a nuanced perspective on the factors shaping African economic development, suggesting that historical African poverty may not have been as pervasive as previously believed.

This productivity advance, driven by globalization and industrialization, raised the demands for labor and capital, potentially influencing economy-wide real wage and labor productivity convergence.

Broadberry's work (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11) supports this notion, demonstrating that industrialization, as the "handmaiden" of globalization, played a crucial role in the economic growth of various nations. The convergence of real wages and labor productivity in industrializing economies during the 19th century can, therefore, be attributed to the combined forces of globalization and industrialization.

It is crucial to contemplate the potential repercussions of such convergence. This question underscores the need for further research to fully understand the extent and implications of these economic forces.
