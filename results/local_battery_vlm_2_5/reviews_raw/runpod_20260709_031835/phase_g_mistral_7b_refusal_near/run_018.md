North (1989, p.10) posits that political and economic institutions significantly influence economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories emphasizing population and savings as primary determinants. This claim, consistent with the topic's description, is supported by the case of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin.

Under Stalin's regime, the state imposed price ceilings on grain and controlled external trade through monopolistic grants (North 1989, p.10). This minute regulation of the economy, as North suggests, was a key institutional mechanism designed to foster economic development.

The effectiveness of these institutions was undermined by the financial strain resulting from military expenses and decreasing revenues. The Crown resorted to raising internal taxes and repeatedly going bankrupt, leading to the seizure of properties and financial assets (North 1989, p.10). This example underscores the importance of institutional stability in ensuring sustained economic growth.

In the context of Stalin's Soviet Union, the absolute bargaining power of the state allowed for the implementation of central planning and forced industrialization, despite the potential economic inefficiencies and social hardships associated with these policies (North 1989, p.7).

The implications of North's (1989) argument can be seen in the long-term economic consequences of Stalin's industrialization drive. Despite achieving rapid industrial growth, the Soviet Union faced significant economic challenges, including inefficiencies, shortages, and stagnation (North 1989, p.7). These challenges underscore the importance of institutions that foster economic efficiency and sustainability, as well as the potential risks of relying on absolute bargaining power and centralized planning.

This comparative analysis underscores the economic decline of India relative to Britain during the early modern period, a pattern that is consistent with the Great Divergence thesis.

They cite data from northern India, primarily daily rates for unskilled laborers, which they find to be broadly comparable to the British data. This comparison suggests that the wage growth experienced in Britain during the Industrial Revolution was not matched in India, thereby supporting the claim of the Great Divergence.

The economic growth in Western Europe after World War II can be attributed to a developmental deficit and disequilibrium, as argued by Temin (2002, p.8). This growth was a result of Germany starting its industrialization after Britain and the protection of German farmers against low-priced American grain in the late nineteenth century (Temin 2002, p.8).

Temin's argument (2002, p.8) suggests that the post-World War II economic boom in Western Europe was not a natural progression but rather a correction of historical imbalances. The rapid growth ended in the 1970s when this developmental deficit was eliminated (Temin 2002, p.16). This perspective aligns with the claim of the Great Divergence, as it suggests that the economic disparity between Europe and Asia was not only a product of the early modern period but also persisted into the modern era, albeit in different forms.

The developmental deficit and disequilibrium in Western Europe can be seen as a consequence of historical institutions and property rights established during European colonization, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48). These institutions and property rights facilitated economic growth in Europe but created a barrier for other regions, such as Asia, to catch up (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Therefore, the economic growth in Western Europe after World War II can be viewed as a correction of this historical imbalance, further supporting Temin's argument (2002, p.8).

The post-World War II growth in Europe can be seen as a continuation of the long-term trend of rising real wages in Britain, which began during the Industrial Revolution (Feinstein 1998, p.2). This trend, in turn, was a result of the increasing productivity of labor and capital in the manufacturing sector (Feinstein 1998, p.2). Thus, the economic growth in Western Europe after World War II can be understood as an extension of the Industrial Revolution's impact on Britain's economy.

The literature on historical perspectives of economic growth, as advanced by Austin (2008, p.23), suggests a nuanced view of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Hornbeck (2010, p.10) argues that the near-universal adoption of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement. This assumption appears plausible, as private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution by increasing both agricultural output and labor supplied to other sectors (Ashton 1997).

Returning to Hornbeck's (2010) setting, the introduction of barbed wire fencing protected farmers' land and crops from damage by others' cattle, thereby increasing the security of property rights. This, in turn, encouraged investment in land improvement and expansion, as farmers no longer had to worry about their lands being overgrazed or destroyed.

Moreover, the widespread adoption of barbed wire facilitated the enclosure of open ranges, transforming them into private farms. This transition from communal to private property allowed for more efficient use of resources, as farmers could now specialize in the production of specific crops or livestock, thereby increasing productivity and output.

Hornbeck (2010, p.10) also highlights that the introduction of barbed wire had downstream implications, as it enabled the settlement and farming of previously inaccessible low-woodland areas. This assumption appears plausible, as the development of these areas could have further contributed to agricultural growth and expansion.

The assumption of persistent historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments is challenged by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18), who argue that African real wages were above subsistence level and rose significantly over time.

Furthermore, the paper reveals that East African colonial governments intervened more directly in the labor market due to chronic labor shortages, and African workers were attracted by the prospect of wages exceeding their opportunity costs in subsistence farming activities (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27). This suggests that labor market dynamics in Africa were not inherently impeded by structural issues, but rather shaped by the policies and labor market conditions established by colonial governments.

This underscores the importance of understanding the role of colonial policies in shaping labor market dynamics and real wages in Africa during this period.

Kuznets and Abramovitz, two prominent scholars in the field of economic growth, provide compelling insights into the crucial elements shaping economic growth, particularly in less developed countries. Both scholars argue that technological advancement, institutional changes, and the exploitation of redundant workers are pivotal components in the process of economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.10) emphasizes the role of innovational requirements, particularly in the social and political structures, in driving economic growth. He points to the forced industrialization under Communist auspices as conspicuous illustrations of social invention and innovation that may be involved in economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) supports this view, suggesting that catch-up in growth among nations is influenced by both exogenous factors and changes within the convergence process itself, as well as social and political concomitants of wealth.

One specific mechanism that these scholars jointly establish is the importance of technological advancement and institutional changes in driving economic growth, particularly in less developed countries.

This finding underscores the importance of addressing labor market inefficiencies in less developed countries to drive economic growth.

The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development remains a subject of debate among scholars. On one hand, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that institutional changes resulting from European colonialism account for a significant fraction of current income differences, suggesting that the Soviet Union's authoritarian regime may have played a role in its economic development. On the other hand, Komlos (1998, p.12) posits that during the early stages of modern economic growth, given the state of technology, populations experienced a downward pressure on their nutritional status. This downward pressure was unleashed by the increased division of labor, integration of hitherto isolated regions into a larger world market, and transportation improvements, all of which were facilitated by forced industrialization under Stalin.

The disagreement between these scholars revolves around the impact of Soviet central planning on labor allocation and nutritional status. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) imply that institutional changes, such as those resulting from forced industrialization, can drive economic growth. On the contrary, Komlos (1998, p.12) proposes that these changes may have had detrimental effects on the nutritional status of populations. To settle this disagreement, a thorough examination of the Soviet Union's labor market inefficiencies and their impact on nutritional status is necessary.

Such an examination could draw on data from Komlos' study (Komlos 1998, p.12), which extends back into the seventeenth century and provides insights into the relationship between demographic and biological variables in less developed countries. Additionally, Acemoglu et al.'s work (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48) on the role of institutions in economic growth could inform this examination, as it highlights the importance of institutional changes in driving economic development. By comparing the Soviet Union's labor market and nutritional status to other less developed countries, a more nuanced understanding of the impact of forced industrialization on economic development can be achieved.
