The impact and mechanism of Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin on economic development remains a contentious issue in the scholarly literature. The debate revolves around the effectiveness of this approach in driving growth and the resulting impact on the population's well-being. This review will explore four streams of related scholarship that address this question directly or indirectly, revealing a complex picture of the Soviet industrialization experience.

The first stream, represented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), argues that institutional changes play a crucial role in economic growth and industrialization. They propose that the surge of industrialization among formerly poor colonies, including the Soviet Union, can be attributed to institutional changes, suggesting a significant impact of Stalin's forced industrialization on economic development. Similarly, Williamson (1996, p.24) highlights the role of productivity advance in driving economic convergence during globalization, a period that encompassed the Soviet industrialization drive.

Komlos (1998, p.16) presents another stream that questions the idea of unrelenting economic growth during forced industrialization. Komlos argues that the absolute income gain during the Industrial Revolution was too low to offset declines in health among common people, leading to a decrease in their biological well-being compared to prior generations. This stream suggests that the human cost of Stalin's industrialization may have outweighed the economic benefits.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) contribute to a third stream by acknowledging the complexity of the relationship between economic growth and equality. They suggest that growth and increased income equality can coexist over time, a finding that complicates our understanding of the Soviet industrialization experience, where economic growth was achieved at the expense of income equality.

Lastly, Simson (2019, p.26) sheds light on the post-independence budget policies of Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, challenging the common assumption that these policies were exceptionally unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending. While this stream does not directly address the Soviet context, it offers valuable insights into the broader debate on state-led development and the role of industrialization in shaping economic growth.

Lindert and Williamson's (1985, p.14) work, along with Abramovitz (1986, p.12) and Kuznets (1973, p.10), collectively argue that the relationship between economic growth and income equality during rapid industrialization is more complex than initially assumed. These scholars suggest that modern economic growth can occur alongside increased income inequality, challenging the notion that growth and equality are inherently opposed.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.14) argue that the surplus labor pool in early industrializing economies can postpone the rise in real wages among the unskilled for some time, as long as demographic forces replenish the initial pool. This phenomenon, they suggest, was evident in Japan since the 1880s and in America before World War I. Abramovitz (1986, p.12) supports this notion by stating that backwardness carries an opportunity for modernization in disembodied technology, allowing countries at relatively low levels of industrialization to improve productivity through labor allocation.

He emphasizes that the innovational requirements are likely to be particularly great in social and political structures, as illustrated by the violent changes in these structures in Communist countries.

These scholars collectively suggest that the relationship between economic growth and income equality during rapid industrialization is complex and influenced by both internal factors, such as labor allocation and technological modernization, and external factors, such as demographic forces and institutional adjustments. This complex interplay between growth and inequality underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the economic development process during periods of rapid industrialization.

Broadberry and Gupta's research (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.11; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) substantiates the claim that the Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, as they demonstrate India's economic decline relative to Britain during the early modern period. This decline is particularly evident in the comparative labor market data between unskilled workers in India and Britain, which suggests that the British economy was more dynamic and productive during this time.

This wage gap indicates a lower productivity level in India, suggesting that the British economy was more advanced during the early modern period.

They argue that the nationalist literature's claim of Indian deindustrialization due to colonialism is misguided, as India was kept open to cheap imports from Britain while its cotton textile exports were blocked from the protected British market (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.3). This finding suggests that the Indian economy was not stagnant but rather integrated into the global economy, albeit in an unfavorable manner.

Broadberry et al.'s (2015) research further supports the claim that India's economic decline relative to Britain began in the early modern period. They argue that India's per capita GDP declined steadily from 1600 to 1871, falling behind Britain's growth during the same period (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). This decline is attributed to the service sector, which grew more slowly in India than in Europe, particularly in urban areas. The underdevelopment of the service sector in India, combined with the wage gap between unskilled workers in India and Britain, further underscores the economic decline of India relative to Britain during the early modern period.

Contrary to the assumption that Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin would foster economic development, particularly in terms of health and income, the evidence suggests a more complex and contradictory picture. This stream of literature, anchored by the work of Komlos (1998, p.16) and Allen et al. (2011, p.12), challenges the notion of a straightforward positive impact of industrialization on health and income.

Komlos (1998, p.16) argues that during the Industrial Revolution, the absolute income gain was too minimal to offset declines in health among common people, resulting in a decrease in their biological well-being compared to previous generations.

This income disparity between Asia and Europe persisted, with little cumulative change for 200 years, due to long-run stagnation in China combined with industrialization in Japan and Europe (Allen et al. 2011, p.12).

One implication of this stream's findings is that the assumed benefits of industrialization, such as increased income and improved health, may not always materialize as expected. Instead, the process of industrialization might involve trade-offs and complex dynamics that could lead to declines in health and stagnating wages, particularly in contexts of forced industrialization and central planning, as in the case of the Soviet Union under Stalin.

This stream's findings offer a nuanced perspective on the impact of industrialization on health and income, suggesting that the relationship between the two may not be straightforwardly positive, as is often assumed. This complexity underscores the need for further research on the specific mechanisms and conditions under which industrialization can lead to positive or negative outcomes for health and income.

Under the purview of institutional development and its impact on economic growth, the work of economist Douglas North offers compelling insights into the case of Soviet industrialization. North (1989, p.10) posits that political and economic institutions play a significant role in determining economic growth, contrasting neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary determinants.

North's (1989, p.10) argument is substantiated by the intricate regulatory mechanisms established under Soviet central planning. For instance, the state imposed price ceilings on grain and controlled external trade through monopolistic grants, while internal taxes increased and the crown frequently went bankrupt, leading to the seizure of properties and financial assets (North, 1989, p.10). This minute regulation of the economy, North argues, was a vehicle for internal economic control, a mechanism that, under the wrong conditions, could lead to declines in efficiency and economic stagnation.

Furthermore, North (1989, p.10) highlights the importance of agenda power, free-rider problems, and norms of behavior in shaping economic outcomes. These elements add complexity to the economic growth narrative, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is contingent on the specific conditions and mechanisms at play, not straightforwardly positive or negative.

An intriguing implication of North's (1989, p.10) work is that the rigidity and overregulation of the Soviet economy may have inadvertently hindered its growth potential. This finding aligns with the broader literature suggesting that forced industrialization and central planning can lead to declines in health and stagnating wages, particularly in contexts like the Soviet Union under Stalin (Lindert & Williamson, 1985; Kuznets, 1973; Abramovitz, 1986).

Temin's (2002, p.8) work, echoed by other scholars, suggests a compelling account of the post-World War II economic growth in Western Europe. This growth, they argue, was driven by a developmental deficit and disequilibrium that persisted until the 1970s, when this deficit was finally eliminated.

Temin (2002, p.8) posits three reasons for this developmental deficit. First, he notes that Germany began its industrialization after Britain, which placed it at a disadvantage in terms of technological innovation and industrial organization. Second, Germany chose to protect its farmers against low-priced American grain in the late nineteenth century, which hindered the growth of its agricultural sector. Lastly, he argues that the post-World War II economic boom was fueled by a surge in demand for European goods due to the reconstruction needs of war-torn Europe and Japan.

The elimination of this developmental deficit in the 1970s, as Temin (2002, p.8) suggests, was marked by a shift in the agricultural labor share, as shown in Table 6 of his paper. The trend in the agricultural labor share, which had been steadily declining since the mid-19th century, began to level off in the 1970s, indicating a reduction in the agricultural sector's relative importance in the economy.

This shift towards a service-oriented economy, however, was not without challenges. As Allen et al. (2011, p.12) argue, the deindustrialization of Europe led to a decline in employment opportunities and stagnating wages, particularly in the manufacturing sector. This finding underscores the complex nature of economic growth and development, suggesting that while the elimination of a developmental deficit can lead to growth, it may also result in social and economic disparities.

Goldin's (2006) paper offers insightful perspectives on the transformative changes in women's roles in employment, education, and family life in the United States over the past century, primarily driven by the generation born in the late 1940s who perceived their lifetime labor force involvement as long and continuous rather than intermittent and brief (Goldin 2006, p.1). This quiet revolution, as Goldin terms it, led to significant advances in the field of modern labor economics, as economists began to study labor supply and the decisions made by families and households (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift in focus mirrors the reality of women's changing roles, underscoring the interconnectedness of societal changes and economic analysis.

Goldin's (2006) work highlights the importance of understanding the motivations and decision-making processes that drive changes in women's roles. This finding resonates with the broader literature that emphasizes the role of demographic shifts in shaping labor markets and economic development (Goldin 2006, p.1).

The increased participation of women in the labor market during this period likely contributed to the growth of the service sector, as women were more likely to be employed in service-oriented jobs (Goldin 2006, p.3). This finding aligns with the literature that indicates a shift towards a service-oriented economy in the latter half of the 20th century (Goldin 2006, p.3).

Hornbeck's (2010) study on the impact of barbed wire on agricultural development in the American Plains corroborates the substantial influence of boundary-setting measures on economic development, as observed under Stalin's central planning and forced industrialization. Like the implementation of central planning, the near-universal adoption of barbed wire significantly increased agricultural development by reducing costs of land protection and facilitating property rights enforcement (Hornbeck 2010, p.10).

As Hornbeck (2010, p.2) notes, the private enclosure of common lands in England may have contributed to the onset of the Industrial Revolution by increasing both agricultural output and labor supplied to other sectors. This finding aligns with the broader literature that emphasizes the role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.10).

Furthermore, the adoption of barbed wire in the American Plains also had downstream implications for the development of infrastructure. This finding resonates with the literature that highlights the role of transportation networks in shaping economic development (Feinstein 1965).

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) challenge the prevailing assumption that structural impediments caused persistent poverty in Africa by suggesting that real wages in Africa were above subsistence level and increased significantly over time. Their findings question the assumption of historical African poverty due to structural growth impediments.

They further contend that labor markets in East Africa integrated under British rule, driven by the demand for skilled labor and local resistance to wage labor (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.27).

The papers by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.18) also highlight the role of colonial governments in the labor market.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the persistence of inequality over the long run in New World societies was influenced by the types of economic institutions that evolved and their effects on access to resources. The evolution of these institutions, they suggest, was shaped by the initial endowments of each society, which in turn affected the types of economic activities that dominated.

The initial endowments of the New World societies varied significantly. For instance, the Caribbean islands had rich soil and relatively fewer indigenous inhabitants, which made them attractive for agricultural activities. On the other hand, the Americas, particularly South America, had vast territories with sparse populations and harsh climates, making them more suitable for extractive industries like mining and ranching.

In the Caribbean, where agriculture was the dominant activity, large plantations emerged, owned by a small elite class. These plantations were characterized by a system of forced labor, which further entrenched inequality. In contrast, in the Americas, where extractive industries were prevalent, the lack of labor led to the development of institutions that incentivized the use of indigenous labor and the importation of African slaves.

For instance, the plantation system in the Caribbean created a class of wealthy landowners who controlled the means of production and reaped the benefits of the labor of enslaved workers. Similarly, in the Americas, the extractive industries were controlled by a small group of individuals who had access to the necessary capital and technology.

The elite classes that emerged in these societies were able to maintain their power and wealth through their control of the economic institutions. This, in turn, limited competition and restricted opportunities for social mobility, further entrenching inequality.

In support of this argument, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) cite the example of Haiti, which was the richest society in the world on a per capita basis in 1790, on the eve of its Revolution. The major divergence between the United States and Canada and the rest of the hemisphere opened up during the nineteenth century as industrialization began in North America. The magnitude of the gap has been essentially constant in proportional terms since 1900.

The literature suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the Industrial Revolution in Europe led to a significant divergence in economic growth between Europe and the rest of the world, may require qualification and methodological modification to account for diverse historical paths (Austin 2008, p.23).

Furthermore, Austin (2008, p.23) contends that colonial rulers, who were often more concerned with maintaining their rule than with extracting revenue, had no interest in sacrificing current consumption for a gamble on longer-term industrialization in the colonies. This failure of post-colonial pursuits of industrialization can be qualified, as there was a significant growth of manufacturing between 1950 and Structural Adjustment (Sender and Smith, 1986).

In support of this argument, Sender and Smith (1986) demonstrate that even when colonial rulers recognized the potential economic benefits of cooperating with the colonized to capitalize on and enhance the colonial economy's existing area of comparative advantage, they still had no incentive to invest in long-term industrialization in the colonies, which might threaten metropolitan jobs.

Thus, the literature suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation in economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths, and requires qualification and methodological modification to account for diverse historical paths. Peters (2004, p.30) argues that the Soviet Union's enforced industrialization through central planning and coercion, although markedly distinct, shares a commonality with sub-Saharan Africa's land appropriation and social divisions. In Mozambique, for instance, reports by various organizations and NGOs reveal instances of land grabbing, disputes, and high-ranking government officials taking land from smallholder farmers (Peters 2004, p.30). This covert redistribution of land, Peters suggests, masks processes of exclusion and class formation, serving to bolster a new internal configuration of power in place of formal state bureaucracies (Peters 2004, p.19).

In this context, the Soviet Union's central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin can be seen as a top-down, state-led process that aimed to rapidly modernize the economy. In contrast, the land appropriation in sub-Saharan Africa, as evidenced in Mozambique, occurs more subtly, often masked by reports of negotiable landholding and use. Both cases lead to similar social outcomes, with power being consolidated in the hands of a few at the expense of the many.

Peters's (2004) findings underscore the importance of understanding who benefits and loses from such instances of 'negotiable' landholding and use in sub-Saharan Africa. This focus on the distribution of power and resources is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the impact and mechanisms of forced industrialization, be it under Stalin's Soviet Union or in the context of sub-Saharan Africa.

The Soviet central planning and forced industrialization under Stalin, while leading to a specific pattern of industrialization, was not the only instance of economic transformation with predictable impacts.

Williamson's (1996, p.24) study, focusing on industrialization and globalization, suggests that these forces led to political alliances and policies aimed at protecting losers. In the context of the Soviet Union, the regime protected losers through forced collectivization and central planning, whereas in the 19th century, protectionist policies were enacted to shield industries and workers from competition.

One specific mechanism that both instances share is the switch to deglobalization and divergence. In the Soviet Union, this shift occurred after the initial phase of forced industrialization, leading to a period of isolationism under Stalin.

Another downstream implication that can be drawn from Williamson's (1996, p.24) findings is the question of whether convergence is a good thing. He raises the issue of who gains and who loses from such processes, a question that is also relevant to the Soviet context, where the benefits of industrialization were concentrated among the elite, while the majority of the population suffered.

This claim is substantiated by the findings of Simson (2019, p.26) and other sources.

The author challenges the global enthusiasm for state-led development and the prerogatives to criticize these policies, citing Githinji's work on ten millionaires in Africa (Githinji, Ten millionaires) and other sources (Simson 2019, p.26).

Figure 3 from Simson's paper (2019, p.13) demonstrates that public sector employment as a percentage of the labor force in the 1940s to 2010 was comparable to other developing countries.

This finding is shared by other sources, such as Goldsmith (2008), who argues that the size of the state in Africa has been overstated, and Rodrik (2000), who suggests that the relationship between state size and economic growth is complex and not always negative. These shared findings (Simson 2019, p.26) support the idea that post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda were not excessively unproductive or prioritizing patronage networks over socially beneficial spending.

Although these budget policies may not have been excessively unproductive, they were not without issues. For instance, the policies may have been inefficient in certain sectors, or there may have been corruption and mismanagement in the implementation of these policies. Future research could focus on identifying these areas of inefficiency and mismanagement to better understand the post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda and their impact on economic development.

This perspective is shared by other scholars, who acknowledge the complexities of the period, including the doubling of England and Wales' population, successive harvest failures, and the impact of wars with France (Feinstein 1998, p.2).

Feinstein's (1998) work provides estimates on both inclusive and exclusive bases to directly ascertain the effects of omitting or including Ireland. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of the impact of early industrialization on the working class in Britain.
