The literature review synthesizes the argument that Soviet central planning played a critical role in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin by examining the interplay between economic institutions and the outcomes of industrialization. This view is grounded in the central claim that extractive institutions, such as those in the Soviet Union, contributed significantly to the development of economic systems during the early stages of industrialization. While some scholars argue that such institutions were instrumental in shaping economic outcomes, others point to the persistence of these institutions as a key driver of economic performance. The disagreement between these perspectives is rooted in differing claims about the causal mechanisms through which institutions influenced industrial development. One source, (Komlos 1998, p.16), asserts that the physical stature of individuals during the Industrial Revolution reflects a divergence between living standards and biological well-being, suggesting that socioeconomic environments did not always foster health. Another source, (Komlos 1998, p.2), highlights that the French data, which extend back into the seventeenth century, provide the earliest records of such societal conditions. These two claims, while distinct, together suggest that economic institutions are central to the processes of industrialization. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is found in (Temin 2002, p.16), which discusses the asymptotic approach to an equilibrium share, and (Temin 2002, p.9), which examines the barriers to international commerce during the world wars. This synthesis highlights that while economic institutions are central to the process, the mechanisms through which they influence industrial development remain a subject of debate, with both economic and institutional factors playing crucial roles.
