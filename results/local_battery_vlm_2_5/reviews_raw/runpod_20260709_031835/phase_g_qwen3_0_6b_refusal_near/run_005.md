The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is a central question in the literature. This stream of related scholarship explores the intellectual debate surrounding the question, focusing on the conflicting perspectives of different scholars. One primary disagreement is whether the Soviet system fostered economic growth through centralized planning or if it led to stagnation and reduced living standards. Two scholars argue that Soviet central planning was instrumental in driving industrialization, as it facilitated the mobilization of labor and the accumulation of capital, while another perspective suggests that the system did not inherently foster economic growth, as the perceived improvements in living standards were offset by declining physical health. This stream synthesizes these perspectives, arguing that central planning did not inherently lead to economic stagnation but rather created conditions that allowed for the transformation of labor into capital. This argument is supported by evidence from two sources, showing that Soviet central planning did not inherently reduce living standards but rather enabled the transformation of labor into capital, thereby shaping the outcomes of forced industrialization under Stalin. The literature review synthesizes several streams of scholarship to argue that Soviet central planning played a significant role in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin. One stream of scholarship, supported by (Komlos 1998, p.16), argues that the reversal in relative income reflects changes in institutions rather than economic development, as seen in the Soviet Union’s transition to a centrally planned economy. Another stream, supported by (Komlos 1998, p.2), posits that physical stature during this period reflects a divergence between living standards and biological well-being, suggesting that socioeconomic environments did not always foster health. The disagreement between these two streams lies in their interpretations of the causal mechanisms behind the outcomes: while (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.45) suggests that the reversal is due to institutional factors, (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35) argues that economic development linked to geography was the cause. This disagreement is resolved by examining the empirical evidence in (Allen 2001, p.29) and (Allen 2001, p.9), which show that the divergence in real incomes was due to wage rates and price changes, not economic growth itself. The synthesis of these perspectives highlights how central planning influenced both economic and biological outcomes, emphasizing the complexity of institutional factors in shaping industrialization under authoritarian regimes.
