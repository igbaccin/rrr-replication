The role of Soviet central planning in shaping forced industrialization under Stalin is a topic of intense scholarly debate, with conflicting perspectives on its causal influence. While some argue that central planning fostered economic growth and stability, others contend that it suppressed individual autonomy and led to systemic inefficiencies. This disagreement centers on the extent to which institutional structures such as extractive property systems and centralized control directly impacted economic performance. Key evidence suggests that central planning contributed to the divergence in living standards and health outcomes observed in socialist states, as highlighted in Komlos's work. Similarly, Acemoglu and his colleagues emphasize that institutions matter, with central planning playing a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. However, there is a significant disagreement in how these institutions affect performance: while some claim that central planning led to economic stagnation, others argue that it had a mixed effect. This debate underscores the complexity of evaluating the impact of central planning on economic development. The substantive question here is whether central planning's influence on economic performance is a direct or indirect result of institutional factors. This disagreement reflects a deeper intellectual tension about the causal mechanisms underlying economic development in authoritarian regimes.
