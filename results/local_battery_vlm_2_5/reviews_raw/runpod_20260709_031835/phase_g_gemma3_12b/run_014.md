The enduring puzzle of long-run economic growth centers on the role of institutions, a question with profound implications for understanding historical trajectories and designing effective development strategies. A central debate revolves around the relative importance of formal rules, informal norms, and the interplay between them in fostering sustained prosperity, with some scholars emphasizing the role of deliberate design while others highlight the significance of unintended consequences and path dependency. Early perspectives often attributed economic success to the establishment of secure property rights and limited government, but subsequent research has complicated this narrative by revealing the ways in which institutions can be shaped by powerful interests and can even perpetuate inequality (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This review examines scholarship that sheds light on the complex causal relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, organizing arguments around distinct streams of analysis that illuminate different facets of this crucial dynamic. The persistence of extractive institutions, even when demonstrably detrimental to long-term growth, underscores the challenges of institutional reform and the importance of understanding the political forces that shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43).

The "reversal of fortune" thesis, as articulated by Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, proposes a causal link between colonial institutions and subsequent economic development, but this perspective has been challenged for oversimplifying historical processes (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) contends that this framework overlooks the complexities of colonial history, noting that colonial regimes sometimes supported existing indigenous property rights systems rather than abolishing them, demonstrating that institutional legacies are not always straightforward (Austin 2008, p.15). Therefore, differences in economic trajectories result from a more intricate web of historical contingencies, not solely from the imposition of particular institutional types (Austin 2008, p.2).

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) initially emphasized the importance of constitutional design in establishing secure property rights and fostering economic growth, and their work has been re-evaluated in light of subsequent scholarship (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) acknowledges the importance of rules and enforcement mechanisms, but suggests that beliefs and values often play a more fundamental role in shaping institutions than formal rules alone; institutions frequently result from distributional conflicts, not purely efficiency-seeking behavior (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further illuminates this point by demonstrating that western Europe’s comparatively low skill premium, linked to efficient labor markets and robust human capital formation, contributed to its long-run economic divergence (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that contemporary development policies often overlook the historical legacies of colonial rule and the agency of local populations, contributing to simplistic prescriptions that fail to account for the complex interplay of institutions and power (Hopkins 2009, p.17).

The role of institutions is further complicated by the recognition that they can be self-reinforcing and resistant to change, even when they are demonstrably inefficient (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that institutional frameworks can become analytically essential to understanding the origins and impact of individual institutions, creating a system where innovations are rejected unless they can substitute for existing markets (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective resonates with Acemoglu et al.’s (2002, p.43) observation that elites may block investments in new technologies if they fear political turbulence or the loss of their power, even if those technologies could ultimately benefit society as a whole (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Secure property rights, constrained by constitutional limits on government power, represent a foundational element for economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further contextualize this by showing how divergent historical institutions in the New World, stemming from varying patterns of elite control, created vastly different trajectories of economic development. They argue that the persistence of inequality, exacerbated by insecure property rights, can actively impede long-run growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Hornbeck (2010, p.2) offers a historical example of this dynamic, demonstrating how the introduction of barbed wire facilitated agricultural development by clarifying property boundaries and reducing conflict over land use. The ability to secure land claims, even through seemingly simple technological innovations, can have profound implications for investment and productivity (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). Ultimately, the creation and maintenance of institutions that safeguard property rights and limit arbitrary government action are crucial for fostering sustained economic advancement.

Guilds, while sometimes criticized for restricting entry and artificially inflating wages, also provided crucial institutions that guaranteed the fair execution of apprentice contracts and facilitated intergenerational skill transfer (van Zanden 2009, p.19), a benefit seemingly counterbalanced by their tendency to form local cartels. This emphasis on institutional design extends to broader understandings of economic development, where Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) contend that policymakers should not necessarily view economic growth and greater equality as mutually exclusive goals, a perspective that challenges earlier assumptions about the inherent trade-offs. Kuznets (1973, p.1) similarly highlighted the necessity of adapting institutions and ideologies alongside technological innovation for sustained economic growth, noting that even advancements like steam power necessitate shifts away from practices like slavery and rural lifestyles. Stern (2004, p.1) complicates this picture, however, by questioning the robustness of the Environmental Kuznets Curve, suggesting that simple relationships between economic development and environmental degradation may be overly simplistic. While the initial stages of industrialization may see increased pollution, the long-term trajectory is likely shaped by more complex factors, including technological advancements and evolving environmental regulations (Stern 2004, p.3). Ultimately, these perspectives reveal a nuanced understanding of how institutional arrangements shape labor market dynamics and skill premiums, contributing to regional economic divergence and presenting a complex challenge for policymakers.

The tendency to attribute economic success solely to the establishment of secure property rights, as initially proposed by North and Weingast, overlooks the intricate interplay of exogenous shocks, evolving priorities, and global economic forces that have shaped institutional pathways across diverse regions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further complicate this perspective by demonstrating how urbanization patterns, crucial for economic development, were unevenly distributed across Europe, suggesting that geographically specific factors often outweigh the universal application of institutional models. Simson (2019, p.9) extends this nuance by revealing how postcolonial budget policies in East Africa, often characterized as clientelist practices, were in fact responses to global economic pressures and shifting developmental goals. Peters (2004, p.30) underscores this point by showing that seemingly deliberate land negotiations frequently masked processes of exclusion and class formation, demonstrating how institutional changes can inadvertently reinforce existing inequalities. Ultimately, the historical record reveals that institutional development is not a linear progression towards optimal outcomes, but a contingent process shaped by a complex web of factors, including beliefs, exogenous shocks, and global economic forces.

The historical slave trades, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.3), have left a lasting negative mark on African economic development, further evidenced by Nunn and Wantchekon’s (2011, p.4) findings on trust erosion and Akyeampong and Fofack’s (2014, p.3) analysis of women’s obscured contributions. The slave trades, as Nunn (2008, p.6) details, instigated cycles of violence and instability, disrupting traditional social structures and hindering the development of robust institutions capable of fostering long-term growth. This disruption occurred through the direct loss of human capital and the creation of norms and beliefs that undermined cooperation and trust; Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.16) further explore this mechanism, highlighting the erosion of social cohesion. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) qualify this narrative by suggesting that Sub-Saharan Africa’s economic performance was, prior to 1950, more robust than previously understood, implying that the long-term impact of the slave trades was compounded by subsequent factors. Ultimately, the legacy of the slave trades, combined with subsequent colonial policies and internal dynamics, has created a complex web of challenges that continue to impede economic progress across the continent, demanding a more nuanced understanding of historical trajectories.

The fundamental role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across several strands of scholarship, with initial work establishing the importance of secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) further demonstrated the significant impact of institutions on economic performance, even finding that geographic factors become less relevant when institutional quality is considered, suggesting that Africa’s relative poverty is attributable to institutional deficits rather than inherent geographic disadvantages (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) cautions against overly simplistic narratives of institutional development, noting that focusing solely on secure property rights as the driver of growth neglects the complexities of historical pathways and the adaptation of institutions to specific contexts (Austin 2008, p.2). The literature thus agrees on the foundational importance of institutional frameworks, but diverges on the precise mechanisms through which they operate and the extent to which they can be universally applied (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2).

A key debate revolves around the extent to which institutions are shaped by deliberate design or emerge from distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that pre-industrial European institutions often resulted from power struggles and distributional conflicts, not purely efficiency-seeking behavior (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective contrasts with the view that institutions are primarily designed to foster economic growth, as suggested by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who acknowledge that even extractive institutions can persist if they maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive institutions, even when detrimental to long-run growth, highlights the influence of political considerations and the resistance to change from vested interests (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These differing perspectives underscore the complex interplay between economic incentives and political power in shaping institutional trajectories (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Ultimately, the debate regarding the drivers of institutional development is partly resolved by examining the divergent experiences of colonial contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.15) points out that colonial regimes sometimes supported existing indigenous property rights systems when they proved economically beneficial, demonstrating that institutional choices were not always driven by a desire to impose “best practice” models (Austin 2008, p.15). This contrasts with the “reversal of fortune” thesis proposed by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23), which posits that colonial institutions were shaped by settler mortality and subsequent extractive policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). The contrasting experiences of India and Ghana, where colonial policies adapted to existing property rights systems, provide empirical evidence that challenges the universal application of the reversal of fortune framework and underscores the importance of historical context in understanding institutional development (Austin 2008, p.15).
