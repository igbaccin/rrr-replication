The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central, and often contentious, question in historical and economic scholarship, as divergent paths of development across regions and over time challenge simple explanations rooted in geography or resource endowments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Intellectual debates revolve around the precise mechanisms through which institutional arrangements influence economic trajectories, particularly concerning the timing and nature of their impact on productivity and prosperity (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and limited government as prerequisites for market development and sustained growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), while others highlight the role of culturally embedded beliefs and power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes, even when these outcomes impede economic progress (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Understanding how institutions evolve, persist, or transform is crucial for disentangling the complex interplay of factors that have driven economic divergence and convergence across the globe (van Zanden 1999, p.2). The persistence of seemingly suboptimal institutions, and the difficulty in transitioning to more economically advantageous structures, underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the political and social forces that shape institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This review will explore several streams of scholarship that illuminate the multifaceted relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic performance.

Colonial legacies, specifically, illustrate how political forces have shaped long-run economic trajectories; Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23) found that institutions, not geographic factors, largely explain contemporary income disparities. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborated on this point, arguing that the initial factor endowments in colonial societies, and the institutions that evolved alongside them, created divergent development paths that have proven remarkably persistent. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.43) illustrate how elites may actively block investments that would benefit broader society, fearing political turbulence or the erosion of their own power, thereby perpetuating suboptimal institutional arrangements. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even in the face of technological advancements that require broader participation, underscores the difficulty in transitioning to more economically advantageous structures (North 1989, p.6). These findings collectively suggest that understanding the political motivations underpinning institutional choices is crucial for explaining historical economic outcomes and predicting future development patterns (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The interplay of historical context and political power continues to shape the landscape of economic development across the globe.

The consequences of past events and evolving social structures resonate through time, establishing institutions that either foster or impede long-term economic advancement (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trades, as Nunn argues, extended beyond simply removing individuals from their communities, fostering a climate of mistrust that continues to impede economic progress (Nunn 2008, p.3). This erosion of trust, a shared finding across several scholars (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Simson 2019, p.9), is further evidenced by Simson’s work on post-colonial budget policies in East Africa, which reveals how inherited patterns of instability influenced economic choices (Simson 2019, p.11). Pamuk’s research on the Black Death reinforces this understanding, showing how a sudden demographic shock spurred institutional changes with long-term economic ramifications (Pamuk 2007, p.4). Hornbeck’s study of barbed wire in America provides a contrasting, yet complementary, perspective, highlighting how seemingly minor technological shifts can alter property rights and stimulate agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). Broadberry et al. challenge simplistic narratives of colonial decline by demonstrating that India’s relative economic stagnation predates British rule, suggesting that pre-existing institutional weaknesses played a crucial role (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4). The collective effect of these historical events and subsequent policy decisions underscores the enduring legacy of initial conditions on long-run economic trajectories. Ultimately, understanding these deep roots is essential for formulating effective development strategies.

Pre-industrial European institutions were frequently molded by distributional struggles, complicating established interpretations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further complicate simplistic understandings of institutional development by highlighting the vital, often overlooked, economic contributions of women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, revealing that established power structures frequently excluded significant segments of the population from participating in and benefiting from economic growth. Peters (2004, p.30) complicates this view by showing that formal land negotiation processes often masked underlying dynamics of exclusion and class formation, indicating that apparent flexibility within institutional frameworks could simultaneously reinforce existing inequalities. This dynamic highlights how institutions are often constructed and maintained to protect the interests of dominant groups, not to maximize overall economic welfare, and how pre-existing power dynamics can create self-reinforcing cycles of exclusion. Even seemingly benign processes, such as donor-financed economic development, as Peters (2004, p.35) points out, can inadvertently exacerbate conflict and environmental degradation, demonstrating the unintended consequences of institutional interventions. Ultimately, the interplay of beliefs, power, and exclusion shaped the trajectory of economic development in diverse contexts, laying the groundwork for later patterns of inequality and hindering broader societal advancement. The legacies of these dynamics continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of examining the historical roots of institutional structures.

Sustained economic expansion requires technological advancements and corresponding adjustments in institutions and underlying beliefs (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Stern 2004, p.1). Stern (2004, p.3) challenges this idea, showing that statistical support for the Environmental Kuznets Curve is weak and its prevalence likely results from considering only structural shifts toward less polluting industries and increased environmental awareness, while neglecting other factors. This highlights the crucial point that technological advancement alone is insufficient; the institutional and ideological frameworks must evolve in tandem to ensure that progress is truly sustainable. Kuznets’s own observations regarding the incompatibility of modern technology with practices like slavery and rural lifestyles (Kuznets 1973, p.1) underscore the necessity of broader societal transformation alongside technological adoption. Stern’s analysis further suggests that relying solely on future technological solutions or shifts in industrial structure to address environmental concerns may be a precarious strategy, as the EKC’s empirical validity remains questionable (Stern 2004, p.3). The need for proactive and deliberate institutional design to guide technological innovation and mitigate potential negative externalities is therefore paramount, shaping the course of long-run economic development. The complexities of balancing technological progress with environmental sustainability demonstrate the ongoing need for nuanced analyses of institutional impacts.

Conventional assumptions about the trade-off between economic growth and income equality are questioned by evidence suggesting the possibility of simultaneous progress (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Their analysis highlights the complexities of disentangling the causal relationships between these two factors, particularly when examining historical trends (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). The authors emphasize that pre-industrial inequality is often the result of a confluence of macroeconomic forces impacting factor rewards, making it difficult to isolate specific drivers (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). For example, the surge in wage inequality preceding the American Civil War stemmed largely from extraordinary rates of capital accumulation, which disproportionately benefited skilled workers (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24).

The relationship between growth and inequality is not always straightforward; the historical experience of the United States demonstrates this (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.24). The understanding of these complex relationships is crucial for designing policies that promote inclusive and sustainable development, recognizing that the benefits of growth must be shared more equitably across society. The need to consider these historical precedents informs contemporary debates about the design of equitable growth strategies.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17; 2022, p.10) demonstrate that long-run economic performance in Sub-Saharan Africa, previously characterized by narratives of persistent stagnation, was more nuanced prior to 1950, exhibiting periods of growth punctuated by episodes of contraction. Van Zanden (1999, p.2; 1999, p.13) similarly complicates simplistic narratives of European economic progress, revealing that periods of growth were often interspersed with troughs in real wages and living standards, challenging the notion of continuous upward trajectories. The persistence of these “shrinking” events in Africa, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17), suggests that institutional factors beyond those promoting investment and innovation may be crucial for sustained development. Guilds, as analyzed by van Zanden (2009, p.19), offer a potential parallel, demonstrating how institutions designed to regulate skill transfer and labor markets could simultaneously foster specialization while also potentially restricting broader economic participation. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further illustrate the importance of urbanization and industrial districts for economic advancement, noting that the absence of such concentrated development in parts of Europe mirrored the challenges faced by Sub-Saharan Africa. Understanding the institutional mechanisms that underpin both growth and contraction is therefore essential for formulating effective development strategies.

The foundational role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across historical and contemporary scholarship (North 1989, p.1). Scholars like North (1989, p.1) have challenged neoclassical perspectives that prioritize population and savings, arguing instead for the critical importance of political and economic institutions in fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) further supported this view, demonstrating that institutional quality, not geographic factors, explains the divergent economic trajectories of nations, particularly concerning colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). The establishment of secure property rights, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), is a crucial element in creating an environment conducive to investment and economic expansion, as evidenced by the transformative impact of constitutional design on capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of nuanced debate (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). While Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that institutions can persist even when they are extractive and impede development, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions are often shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs rather than solely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This tension suggests that the incentive structures of powerful groups and the prevailing cultural norms can significantly distort institutional development, even when more economically beneficial alternatives exist (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical record demonstrates that guilds, while potentially facilitating skill development (van Zanden 2009, p.19), also risked creating localized cartels that limited overall economic expansion (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

Despite these disagreements regarding the motivations behind institutional formation, there is broad agreement that institutions shape the skill premium and, consequently, long-run economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Van Zanden (2009, p.27) shows how a low skill premium, indicative of well-functioning labor and capital markets, is linked to long-term economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This measurement, comparing skill premiums across regions and historical periods, provides a valuable empirical benchmark for assessing institutional quality and its impact on economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The relative skill premium in western Europe, consistently lower than in other regions, offers compelling evidence that institutional factors, such as secure property rights and efficient labor markets, played a pivotal role in driving its economic divergence (van Zanden 2009, p.27).
