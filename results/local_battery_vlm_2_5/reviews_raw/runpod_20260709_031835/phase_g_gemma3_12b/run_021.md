The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central, and often contentious, question in economics, with profound implications for understanding historical development and designing policies for contemporary societies. A core debate revolves around the relative importance of formal rules, informal norms, and underlying factors like geography or culture in driving persistent economic disparities. Some scholars emphasize the power of exogenous forces, while others highlight the crucial role of human agency and institutional design in fostering prosperity, leading to disagreements about the very mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained economic advancement. The challenge lies in disentangling the complex interplay of these forces and establishing a robust causal link between institutional structures and long-term economic trajectories, a task complicated by the difficulty of isolating institutional factors from other potential drivers of growth. The persistent puzzle of why some regions have thrived while others have languished underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the institutional foundations of economic success, and the ways in which these foundations are constructed and maintained.

Early contributions established the foundational argument that secure property rights and limited government are essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North’s work, for instance, directly challenges neoclassical perspectives that prioritize population and savings, asserting the primacy of institutional development in fostering economic expansion (North 1989, p.1). Simultaneously, North and Weingast demonstrated how constitutional design, specifically the commitment to secure property rights, could trigger transformative shifts in capital markets and overall economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective suggests that credible commitment, the ability of a government to enforce contracts and protect property, acts as a catalyst for investment and innovation, laying the groundwork for long-run growth. The implications of this line of reasoning are clear: without reliable institutions, economic activity remains constrained, and the potential for sustained prosperity remains unrealized.

Building on this foundation, Acemoglu and colleagues have advanced a theory of “extractive” versus “inclusive” institutions, arguing that colonial legacies continue to shape economic outcomes today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their research highlights how institutions designed to extract rents from the population, rather than to promote broad-based economic participation, can create persistent poverty traps (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). This perspective explains why historically rich colonies often exhibit relatively poor economic performance, challenging purely geographic or cultural explanations (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Ogilvie’s work complicates this narrative by emphasizing the role of distributional conflicts and shared beliefs in shaping institutions; she suggests that even institutions appearing “extractive” can be rooted in complex social dynamics (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This highlights the importance of understanding the motivations and power structures that underpin institutional development, rather than simply viewing institutions as tools for maximizing economic efficiency.

Further refining our understanding of institutional impacts, van Zanden’s research suggests that the skill premium, the relative wage of skilled labor, provides a valuable lens through which to examine long-run economic divergence (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 2009, p.27). This perspective connects institutional quality to specific economic outcomes, demonstrating how institutional arrangements can shape factor markets and ultimately influence economic performance. The observation that guilds, initially intended to protect skilled labor, also influenced capital goods pricing demonstrates the nuanced and often unintended consequences of institutional design.

While institutions are undeniably important, alternative explanations for long-run economic growth cannot be dismissed. Ultimately, a comprehensive understanding of long-run economic growth requires acknowledging the interplay of multiple factors, including institutions, geography, culture, and technological innovation.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) reinforces this perspective, noting that sustained economic growth necessitates not only technological innovation but also corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments, a point underscored by the historical challenges faced when integrating new technologies into societies lacking the necessary frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.7). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate on this dynamic by demonstrating how divergent institutional paths in the New World, stemming from variations in factor endowments and elite control, led to vastly different development outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Hornbeck (2010, p.2) provides a compelling historical example of this process, illustrating how the introduction of barbed wire facilitated agricultural development by enabling the enforcement of property rights and mitigating conflicts arising from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). The interplay between technological advancement and institutional evolution, as highlighted by these scholars, reveals a complex and interdependent relationship essential for sustained prosperity. Ultimately, the historical record demonstrates that economic progress is inextricably linked to the creation and maintenance of a stable and predictable legal environment.

Guilds, intended to standardize training and protect skilled laborers, inadvertently contributed to a system where the transfer of skills was mediated by formal institutions (van Zanden 2009, p.19), a contrast to regions with less structured apprenticeship systems. This institutional arrangement, however, also fostered a degree of market restriction, potentially hindering broader economic advancement (van Zanden 2009, p.19), a point reinforced by Lindert and Williamson’s observation that policymakers often face a trade-off between growth and equality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further contextualize this dynamic by noting that the development of industrial districts, a consequence of concentrated industries, was limited in central and eastern Europe, suggesting a link between institutional frameworks and regional economic disparities. While van Zanden (1999, p.2) initially questioned a direct correlation between economic development and living standards in early modern Europe, the formalized apprenticeship systems and guild structures reveal a more nuanced picture, demonstrating how specific institutional arrangements could both facilitate and constrain economic progress (van Zanden 1999, p.13). Ultimately, the complex interplay of these factors underscores the profound influence of institutional design on long-run economic outcomes. The nuances of these historical developments invite further exploration of how specific institutional arrangements shaped economic realities across different regions.

Austin (2008, p.2) complicates this narrative, however, by arguing that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies the complexities of historical causation, compressing diverse colonial experiences and overlooking the agency of colonized populations. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further emphasizes the importance of understanding long historical processes in shaping contemporary African development, highlighting how colonial rule was often negotiated and influenced by local initiatives. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9) contribute to this understanding by demonstrating that India's relative economic decline began prior to British rule, indicating that pre-existing institutional and productivity factors played a significant role in shaping its subsequent development path. These analyses collectively underscore the need to move beyond simplistic narratives of colonial impact and to consider the intricate interplay of historical forces, local agency, and path-dependent institutional development in understanding long-run economic outcomes. The persistent effects of colonial policies, even when modified by local adaptations, reveal the enduring power of institutional legacies in shaping economic trajectories.

Assessments of long-run economic development frequently underestimate the impact of recurring contractions, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). Their work, alongside Stern’s critique of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (Stern 2004, p.1; Stern 2004, p.3), highlights the limitations of broad, sweeping narratives about institutional progress and economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate how reliance on specific datasets can skew perceptions of historical economic performance, underscoring the need for nuanced analysis that accounts for periods of economic decline. Stern’s (2004, p.1) initial description of the EKC's origins reveals how such models can become entrenched despite lacking empirical validation, a phenomenon mirrored in broader institutional analyses that may overemphasize growth while neglecting contraction. Indeed, the emphasis on incentives for investment and innovation, a common focus in institutional economics, neglects the crucial link between institutions and the prevention of shrinking. The persistence of these contractionary episodes, even in regions exhibiting periods of growth, reveals the fragility of economic progress and the importance of considering factors beyond institutional frameworks. The need to understand these dynamics points to the necessity of examining regional specificities and the complex interplay of factors that shape economic outcomes.

Internal factors have significantly shaped African economic trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The erosion of trust, fostered by the historical slave trades, created a persistent drag on development, a finding supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) who highlight the vital, yet often overlooked, contributions of African women to pre-colonial and colonial growth. Nunn’s work (Nunn 2008, p.3) demonstrates that the production of enslaved people spurred domestic conflict and instability, undermining the potential for long-term economic advancement, while Hopkins (2019, p.3) notes that a full understanding of African economic development requires multiple approaches and techniques. The resulting norms and beliefs, as detailed by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.16), have demonstrably impeded cooperation and investment, accounting for at least half of the slave trade's lasting impact. Pamuk (2007, p.4) further illuminates how exogenous shocks, such as the Black Death in Europe, triggered institutional changes with far-reaching consequences, offering a comparative perspective on the potential for historical events to reshape economic pathways. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.27) caution that assessing whether recent growth rebounds qualitatively differ from past patterns is crucial for determining if African nations have truly entered a phase of sustained development. The complex interplay of historical shocks, internal dynamics, and evolving institutions underscores the need for nuanced analyses that move beyond simplistic explanations of economic trajectories.

Peters (2004, p.35) further complicates narratives of institutional flexibility by demonstrating how reported land negotiations often masked processes of exclusion and class formation, revealing a darker side to seemingly adaptable systems. The prioritization of maintaining existing power structures, as evidenced by Simson (2019, p.9), frequently overshadowed efforts toward genuinely transformative development, particularly in post-colonial East Africa where budget allocations favored industries and military spending over broader societal needs. These analyses, alongside Ogilvie's (2007, p.27) emphasis on the interplay between beliefs and institutional rules, suggest that institutional development is less a story of rational choices and more a reflection of the struggles for control and the persistence of entrenched hierarchies. The prioritization of debt servicing, as Simson (2019, p.11) highlights, further constrained developmental possibilities, demonstrating how global economic forces can shape seemingly local institutional choices. Such patterns reveal that claims of institutional adaptability often obscure the ways in which global forces and internal power dynamics reinforce existing inequalities. The persistence of these patterns underscores the need to critically examine the origins and trajectories of institutions, moving beyond simplistic explanations that emphasize efficiency or inherent flexibility.

The foundational role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across numerous scholarly perspectives (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate how the establishment of secure property rights, fostered by constitutional design limiting arbitrary government action, is crucial for economic development, while Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the tendency to oversimplify the complexities of institutional evolution by emphasizing the political delivery of secure private property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) further highlight the significance of institutions, showing that, even controlling for geographic factors, institutional quality explains substantial differences in income across countries, suggesting that the persistence of poverty in some regions is directly attributable to deficient institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). However, Hopkins (2009, p.8) cautions against simplistic policy prescriptions based on "best practice" institutions, arguing that colonial rule was often negotiated and shaped by African initiatives, complicating the narrative of exogenous institutional imposition (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, therefore, requires careful consideration of both formal structures and the historical processes that shape them, revealing a nuanced picture of how institutions both enable and constrain economic growth.

Despite a general agreement on the importance of institutions, a key point of contention revolves around the degree to which institutions are inherently designed to promote economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that even “equilibrium institutions” can be extractive, serving the interests of politically powerful groups at the expense of broader economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while Ogilvie (2007, p.27) emphasizes the role of distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, in shaping pre-industrial institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). This disagreement highlights the potential for institutions to be shaped by power dynamics and vested interests, rather than solely by a desire for economic advancement, complicating the task of identifying and implementing policies that foster inclusive growth. The historical record, particularly in the context of colonial legacies, provides valuable evidence for disentangling these competing perspectives.

The divergence in interpretations can be resolved by examining the specific historical experiences of former European colonies, as demonstrated through Acemoglu et al.’s (2001, p.23) analysis of settler mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Comparing colonies with high settler mortality, which resulted in extractive institutions, to those with low settler mortality, which fostered more inclusive institutions, reveals a clear correlation between institutional quality and subsequent economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). This empirical comparison, which Austin (2008, p.15) notes has been used to examine the impact of colonial policies on economic development (Austin 2008, p.15), allows for a more nuanced understanding of how institutional choices are shaped by power dynamics and historical circumstances, demonstrating that the pursuit of long-run economic growth is often secondary to the interests of politically dominant groups.
