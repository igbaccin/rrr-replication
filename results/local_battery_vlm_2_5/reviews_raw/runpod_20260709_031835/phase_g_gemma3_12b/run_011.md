The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central, and often contentious, question in economics, with profound implications for understanding historical trajectories and designing effective development policies. A core disagreement revolves around the precise mechanisms through which institutional arrangements influence economic performance, with some scholars emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and contract enforcement while others highlight the role of broader political and social structures. This debate extends to the timing and nature of institutional change, with some arguing for a relatively straightforward path toward economic advancement through institutional reform, while others emphasize the complexities and contingencies of historical development. The persistence of this disagreement underscores the challenges of isolating causal effects and disentangling the interplay of factors that drive long-run economic outcomes. Secure property rights, a cornerstone of economic development, are often presented as a prerequisite for investment and innovation (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Challenges exist for the straightforward connection of institutional reform and economic growth, especially when considering the complex historical backgrounds of development (Austin 2008, p.2). The complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic trajectories demands a deeper exploration of the underlying mechanisms and historical contingencies.

Secure property rights and limitations on arbitrary government action, as emphasized by North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and others (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4), are foundational for long-run prosperity. The continued existence of institutions designed to favor ruling groups over broad growth highlights the difficulties in institutional reform (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further illuminate this point by showing how initial conditions, such as the distribution of land and labor, can lead to divergent institutional paths across societies, perpetuating inequality and limiting development. Hornbeck (2010, p.2) offers a detailed view, showing how seemingly small technological innovations, such as barbed wire, can alter property rights enforcement and encourage agricultural development, which demonstrates that institutional change can follow unforeseen routes. This illustrates that while elite capture and historical legacies can create entrenched extractive institutions, technological and social shifts can also generate opportunities for more inclusive development (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). The complex interplay of these factors reveals that institutional evolution is rarely a linear process, but rather a dynamic interplay of power, technology, and social change.

Sustained economic growth demands technological innovation alongside related institutional and ideological changes, as Kuznets (1973, p.7) proposed; van Zanden’s (1999, p.2) work complicates this view by questioning positive accounts of pre-industrial European economic development. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) further illuminate this point by demonstrating that the pursuit of economic growth need not inherently conflict with greater equality, suggesting that the institutional framework can mediate the relationship between these two objectives. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.10) contribute to this understanding by positing that the “Great Divergence” began earlier than previously thought, highlighting the role of uneven institutional development in shaping disparities between regions. Kuznets’s (1973, p.1) emphasis on the incompatibility of modern technology with existing social structures, such as slavery and rural lifestyles, underscores the transformative power of institutions in enabling long-run prosperity. Van Zanden’s (1999, p.13) observation of a negative correlation between economic development and real wages in early modern Europe further complicates simplistic narratives of progress, indicating that institutional failures can impede the realization of potential gains. These findings collectively emphasize that technological advancement alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; rather, it requires a supportive institutional environment that facilitates its adoption and equitable distribution of its benefits. The historical examples of wage stagnation alongside economic development (van Zanden 1999, p.13) suggest that the benefits of technological progress can be unevenly distributed without appropriate institutional safeguards.

Guilds, as van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights, played a complex role in fostering skill transmission and economic development, offering a more nuanced view than simplistic narratives of institutional failure. These institutions, designed to regulate apprenticeships and ensure fair labor practices, arguably facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills, though their tendency towards cartelization potentially offset these benefits (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Stern (2004, p.1) further contextualizes this discussion by examining the evolution of environmental awareness and regulation alongside economic growth, noting the popularization of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) as a reflection of this dynamic. Stern (2004, p.3) advises against excessive dependence on the Environmental Kuznets Curve, noting that it often lacks strong statistical evidence and may be replaced by more advanced models that consider structural shifts and technological progress. This critique resonates with the recognition that institutional mechanisms, like guilds, can have unintended consequences that complicate their overall contribution to economic progress (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The interplay between formalized structures, market forces, and evolving societal priorities demonstrates the intricate relationship between institutions and long-run economic trajectories. These findings collectively suggest that a more granular understanding of specific institutional mechanisms and their dynamic effects is essential for accurately assessing the drivers of sustained economic growth.

Postcolonial budget policies in East Africa, as Simson (2019, p.9) demonstrates, were largely shaped by global economic forces, complicating explanations based solely on clientelist practices. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight the critical, often overlooked, contributions of women to economic growth across the continent, challenging narratives that center exclusively on male-dominated sectors. Peters (2004, p.35) further complicates simplistic development narratives by revealing how seemingly flexible land negotiation processes often masked underlying patterns of exclusion and class formation. Hopkins (2019, p.3) underscores the ongoing evolution of economic history scholarship in Africa, noting the increased recognition of the continent’s economic complexity and heterogeneity over the past half-century. The prioritization of industrial development through trade taxation and state-led investment, as detailed by Simson (2019, p.11), reveals a concerted effort to diversify economies and reduce dependence on primary commodities, a strategy influenced by prevailing global economic ideologies of the time. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.27) suggest that evaluating the qualitative distinctions between past economic improvements and current growth trends is essential to confirm whether African nations have achieved lasting development. This emphasis on broader global forces and the nuanced roles of various societal groups provides a more comprehensive understanding of economic trajectories in precolonial and colonial Africa. The examination of budgetary shifts and military spending, as shown by Simson (2019, p.9), suggests that external pressures and regional instability played a significant role in shaping economic priorities. A deeper understanding of these dynamics is essential for accurately interpreting the long-run economic development of the continent.

Hopkins (2009, p.8) cautions against the uncritical adoption of “best practice” institutional models in the context of African development, recognizing the negotiated nature of colonial rule (Hopkins 2009, p.17). Austin (2008, p.2) further complicates simplistic narratives by demonstrating how the application of institutionalist theories, particularly those emphasizing secure property rights, often overlooked the adaptive capacity of existing systems and the influence of external demand (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective challenges the widespread assumption that replicating Western institutional frameworks guarantees economic progress, particularly when historical contexts and power dynamics are disregarded, as highlighted by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) underscore this point by noting that a focus on investment incentives often overshadows the critical need to address recurring episodes of economic “shrinking” across the continent. The persistent challenge, therefore, lies in understanding how to foster economic growth while acknowledging the diverse and often contradictory forces that have shaped Africa’s development.

The transatlantic slave trade and mortality events demonstrably shaped long-run economic trajectories across Africa, influencing the evolution of social norms and beliefs (Nunn 2008, p.3; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Pamuk 2007, p.4). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) convincingly argue that the slave trade, beyond its immediate demographic and productive losses, fostered deep-seated mistrust within African societies, a consequence amplified by the disruption of existing social structures and the rise of raiding economies (Nunn 2008, p.6). This erosion of trust, a crucial ingredient for cooperation and investment, subsequently impeded economic development, accounting for a significant portion of Africa’s persistent underperformance (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.16). Pamuk (2007, p.4) similarly demonstrates how the Black Death in Europe spurred institutional changes and wage growth, highlighting the potential for exogenous shocks to trigger long-term economic transformations, though the nature of those transformations depended heavily on pre-existing conditions and subsequent adaptive responses (Pamuk 2007, p.24). Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) further contextualize these impacts by revealing how pre-existing inequalities and institutional weaknesses in some regions exacerbated the long-term consequences of such shocks, preventing them from catalyzing positive economic change. The resulting legacy of diminished trust and disrupted social cohesion continues to influence economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring power of historical events to shape contemporary development pathways. The complex interplay of these factors suggests that understanding the long shadow of the past is essential for formulating effective development strategies.

The enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth is a central theme across diverse scholarly perspectives (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) highlight how institutions shape economic trajectories, demonstrating that factors beyond geography or culture explain disparities in contemporary income levels. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize the critical role of secure property rights and limited government action in fostering economic development, illustrating how these factors underpin the growth of markets. Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the straightforward application of this institutionalist perspective, arguing that it oversimplifies complex historical processes and neglects the nuances of colonial experiences. Despite these differing emphases, a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2) is that institutions are not merely passive reflections of economic activity, but active forces shaping economic outcomes over extended periods. The long shadow of historical events and institutional choices continues to influence contemporary development pathways.

A key disagreement within the literature concerns the extent to which institutions are shaped by exogenous factors versus the degree to which they are endogenous responses to political and economic pressures (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that powerful groups often design institutions to maximize their own rents, even if those institutions hinder broader economic development, while Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that colonial regimes sometimes supported indigenous property rights systems for pragmatic reasons, demonstrating a responsiveness to local conditions. This divergence in perspective highlights the difficulty of isolating the causal impact of institutions when they are themselves products of power dynamics and historical contingencies. The complex interplay of these factors underscores the need for nuanced historical analysis to understand institutional development.

The historical evidence from colonial contexts provides a crucial basis for resolving this disagreement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Austin 2008, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) utilize settler mortality rates as a proxy for institutional quality, demonstrating a strong correlation between mortality rates and subsequent economic outcomes across former colonies, while Austin (2008, p.2) points to examples of colonial policies that actively supported existing property rights, suggesting a more flexible and adaptive institutional landscape. Comparing the economic trajectories of colonies with divergent settler mortality rates, alongside careful examination of colonial policy records, allows for a more refined understanding of the interplay between exogenous shocks, endogenous political responses, and the long-run development paths of these regions.
