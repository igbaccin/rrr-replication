The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central, contested question in economics, with implications for understanding historical development trajectories and designing policies for contemporary growth challenges. A core disagreement revolves around the relative importance of formal rules, informal norms, and broader societal beliefs in fostering economic prosperity, with some scholars emphasizing the primacy of resource endowments or technological innovation. Early perspectives often treated institutions as exogenous, but a growing body of literature now investigates how institutions evolve, interact, and ultimately determine a society’s economic fate (North 1989, p.1). North’s work highlights the critical interdependence of political and economic institutions, arguing that neoclassical theories, which prioritize population and savings, overlook the fundamental role of institutions in shaping economic development (North 1989, p.6). The interplay between institutional development and broader societal beliefs warrants deeper investigation, as it shapes the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) report, “It is important to emphasize that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development.”

Kuznets also notes that sustained economic advancement requires technological innovation alongside adjustments to institutional and ideological frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These scholars, alongside others, underscore that institutions, encompassing both political and economic structures, are crucial for fostering long-run economic development by shaping incentives and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6; Kuznets 1973, p.7; Hornbeck 2010, p.2). The introduction of technologies like steam and electric power, for example, demands a departure from traditional structures such as family enterprises and slavery, requiring the establishment of new institutions to facilitate their adoption (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Hornbeck’s analysis of barbed wire in American agriculture illustrates how institutional development, specifically the clarification and enforcement of property rights, can stimulate economic progress (Hornbeck 2010, p.2). The ability to protect land from livestock damage, facilitated by barbed wire, incentivized investment and ultimately drove agricultural development (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). Ultimately, the successful integration of new technologies depends on the capacity of institutions to adapt and provide a stable framework for investment and innovation, a process that demands ongoing evaluation and refinement.

These theoretical frameworks are complicated by empirical evidence demonstrating that regional disparities persisted even during periods of apparent European economic convergence (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.10). The persistence of these inequalities suggests institutional factors shaped divergent economic trajectories, moving away from a uniform path toward modernization (van Zanden 1999, p.2). Guild systems, for instance, while intended to regulate apprenticeship and skill transmission, may have inadvertently contributed to a low skill premium in Western Europe by restricting entry and artificially maintaining wage levels (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This phenomenon contrasts with regions experiencing lower wages and limited access to skilled labor, indicating that institutional arrangements significantly influenced the distribution of economic benefits (van Zanden 1999, p.13). Broadberry and Gupta further contextualize this divergence by demonstrating that the Great Divergence began well before 1800, suggesting that institutional differences were already shaping economic outcomes centuries prior (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Ultimately, the uneven distribution of skills and resources across Europe underscores the complex interplay between institutions, technology, and long-run economic growth, revealing a more nuanced picture than previously understood (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The implications of these findings extend to contemporary debates about institutional reform and economic development, highlighting the need for careful consideration of regional contexts and historical legacies.

Such historical context suggests that simplistic growth narratives fail to capture the complexities of Sub-Saharan Africa’s economic trajectory (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight the crucial, yet often overlooked, contributions of women to economic activity, demonstrating that traditional accounts of African economic history frequently omit a vital segment of the population. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) further complicate this picture by suggesting that policymakers have historically operated under a false dichotomy between economic growth and equality, a misconception that has often led to counterproductive policies. Stern (2004, p.1) challenges the widely accepted Environmental Kuznets Curve, arguing that its statistical support is tenuous and that more sophisticated models are needed to accurately assess the relationship between economic development and environmental degradation. Simson (2019, p.11) adds complexity, showing that postcolonial budget policies in East Africa were largely shaped by global economic forces and shifting priorities, not solely by clientelist practices. These perspectives collectively underscore the importance of considering the multifaceted and often contradictory factors that have shaped Africa’s economic history, moving beyond simplistic narratives to embrace a more nuanced understanding of the continent’s development challenges. Ultimately, a more complete understanding of Africa’s economic past necessitates a broader examination of the institutional, social, and environmental forces at play, and their complex interplay.

The persistence of these patterns underscores the need to consider the profound impact of historical slave trades on Africa’s long-term economic development (Nunn 2008, p.3; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). The detrimental effects extended beyond the immediate loss of human capital, as the trade fostered internal conflict and instability, disrupting social structures and hindering the development of robust institutions (Nunn 2008, p.6). Further complicating the understanding of these historical legacies, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade eroded trust within African societies, a factor they estimate accounts for a substantial portion of its lasting impact. This erosion of trust, a vital component of economic cooperation and institutional effectiveness, created a self-perpetuating cycle of instability and limited growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.16). Hopkins (2019, p.3) highlights the challenges inherent in reconstructing precolonial African economic systems, acknowledging the progress made in recent decades but emphasizing the ongoing need for nuanced and multifaceted research approaches. While earlier scholarship often focused solely on the impact of external forces, such as colonialism, the recognition of internal factors, like the slave trade’s corrosive effect on social cohesion, has spurred a more complex and comprehensive analysis of Africa’s developmental trajectory (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The long shadow of these historical shocks continues to shape contemporary economic realities, demanding careful consideration of both external pressures and internal vulnerabilities.

These historical legacies emphasize the importance of understanding how distributional conflicts, not just economic efficiency, have shaped European institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges conventional narratives that prioritize rational economic design, highlighting how power dynamics and conflicting interests often diverted institutional development from paths that might have fostered broader prosperity (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Peters 2004, p.35; Pamuk 2007, p.4). Peters (2004, p.30) reinforces this view by demonstrating how seemingly flexible land negotiation processes in Africa often masked and exacerbated underlying patterns of exclusion and class formation, revealing how institutional arrangements can actively perpetuate inequalities. The persistence of inefficient or inequitable institutions, even when alternatives exist, underscores a self-reinforcing dynamic whereby established power structures resist change, as noted by Ogilvie (2007, p.27), who observed how modern less developed countries retain practices despite recognizing their limitations. While Pamuk (2007, p.24) emphasizes the positive long-term consequences of these shifts, Peters (2004, p.35) reveals that similar processes in Africa resulted in destructive outcomes, illustrating the contingent nature of institutional evolution and the profound impact of local contexts. These analyses collectively point to the importance of understanding the political and social forces that shape institutional trajectories, rather than assuming a straightforward path toward optimal economic outcomes. The complex interplay of beliefs, values, and power dynamics continues to demand careful scrutiny when assessing the long-term impact of institutions on economic development.

The continued relevance of colonial legacies necessitates a more nuanced understanding of historical processes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2). The initial framing by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) suggests that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonial powers, could become entrenched even when they hinder long-term development, a dynamic complicated by the resistance of powerful elites fearing a loss of political influence (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) expand on this by demonstrating how divergent initial conditions in colonial societies, particularly the distribution of land and labor, led to vastly different institutional trajectories and persistent inequalities. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) offer a complementary perspective, positing that a commitment to secure property rights is crucial for fostering economic growth and market development, a condition often lacking in colonial contexts. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial regimes sometimes prioritized political control, even when indigenous property rights systems were economically beneficial. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) further underscore the importance of institutions by showing that, after accounting for institutional quality, geographic factors often lose their explanatory power in explaining income disparities across nations.

The foundational role of institutions in fostering long-run economic growth is a central theme across a substantial body of scholarship, with researchers highlighting their influence on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that even seemingly inefficient institutional arrangements can persist due to the political power of groups seeking to maximize their own rents, revealing the complex interplay between economic development and political realities. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore that the commitment to secure property rights, facilitated by constitutional design, is critical for the development of markets and sustained economic progress. Austin (2008, p.2) challenges the idea that institutions alone explain economic trajectories, arguing that the Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson thesis oversimplifies historical processes by compressing timelines and overlooking local contexts. This perspective acknowledges the importance of institutions while also recognizing the limitations of broad-sweeping explanations, suggesting a need for more granular analysis of specific historical paths (Austin 2008, p.15).

While the importance of institutions is widely accepted, disagreement arises concerning the precise drivers of institutional development and their relationship to broader societal beliefs (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.27) argues that distributional conflicts, not purely economic efficiency, have historically shaped core institutions, suggesting that beliefs about legitimacy and rights are crucial determinants. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.43) demonstrate how technological advancements can worsen the incompatibility of extractive institutions, highlighting technology’s potential to reshape power dynamics and necessitate institutional adaptation. van Zanden (2009, p.27) further illuminates this dynamic by showing how a low skill premium, indicative of well-functioning labor and capital markets, is linked to investment in human capital and long-term economic growth, suggesting that institutions can facilitate such virtuous cycles. These diverse perspectives underscore the importance of considering both the political and economic forces shaping institutional evolution.

Ultimately, the debate regarding the primacy of institutions versus other factors is often resolved by examining specific historical comparisons, such as those undertaken by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23). Their analysis of colonial mortality rates and subsequent institutional development demonstrates that geographic factors, frequently invoked as explanations for economic disparities, lose their explanatory power when institutional quality is accounted for (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). The divergent economic paths of former colonies, attributable to differences in institutional development driven by colonial policies and mortality patterns, provide empirical evidence supporting the enduring impact of institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This focus on comparative historical analysis, a methodological approach shared across these scholars, allows for a more nuanced understanding of the complex relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth.
