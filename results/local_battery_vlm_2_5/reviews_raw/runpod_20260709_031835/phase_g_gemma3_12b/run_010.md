The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central, yet contested, question in economics, with implications for understanding historical trajectories and designing policies for development. A persistent debate revolves around the relative importance of formal rules, informal norms, and underlying factors like geography or culture in driving economic prosperity, and whether institutions are primarily instruments of elite control or engines of broader societal advancement. Some scholars emphasize the role of exogenous factors, while others highlight the agency of human choices and the evolution of institutions over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). This review explores how institutions shape economic growth, focusing on streams of scholarship that illuminate the causal mechanisms at play and the complexities of institutional change. The persistence of inequality across regions and over time suggests that understanding the deep-rooted causes of economic divergence requires a nuanced examination of the political and social frameworks that underpin economic activity (van Zanden 2009, p.19).

A foundational stream of scholarship, exemplified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.43), emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial legacies on contemporary economic outcomes. These authors argue that institutions established during colonial periods, often designed to extract resources and maintain control, have created persistent patterns of inequality, even after formal decolonization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The design of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, was contingent on factors like settler mortality and the potential for broad-based economic participation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). This perspective suggests that simply changing formal rules is insufficient to overcome the long-term effects of historically entrenched institutional arrangements (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) similarly underscore the crucial role of credible commitment in fostering economic growth, highlighting how the establishment of secure property rights and limitations on arbitrary government action are essential for creating a stable and predictable environment for investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets demonstrates the transformative effect of institutional changes that reduced uncertainty and fostered trust (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

Another stream, building on the insights of Acemoglu and colleagues, explores the mechanisms through which powerful groups shape institutions to serve their own interests (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.23) argue that elites may actively block investments in new technologies or activities that threaten their political power or economic rents, even if these innovations would ultimately benefit society as a whole (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). This perspective sheds light on why institutional change is often difficult to achieve, as those who benefit from the status quo have a vested interest in maintaining it (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) supports this view by highlighting the role of shared beliefs and values in shaping institutional structures; she suggests that institutions reflect deeply ingrained cultural norms, not simply the product of rational calculations (Ogilvie 2007, p.27). Understanding the interplay between political power, economic interests, and cultural beliefs is crucial for explaining the persistence of extractive institutions and the challenges of promoting inclusive economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.27).

A further line of inquiry focuses on the historical evolution of institutions and the factors that have driven institutional change, moving beyond a static view of institutions as predetermined outcomes (North 1989, p.6). North (1989, p.1) argues that the development of political institutions that specify property rights and enforce contracts is essential for economic growth, and that these institutions evolve alongside increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) connects institutional development to the evolution of labor markets and the formation of human capital, suggesting that a low skill premium, indicative of efficient labor and capital markets, is a key driver of long-term economic prosperity (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The historical record reveals that the path to economic prosperity is rarely straightforward, and that institutional change is often a complex and contingent process.

Constitutional design limiting arbitrary government action, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; Hornbeck 2010, p.2; Kuznets 1973, p.7), establishes secure property rights as a critical foundation for sustained economic advancement. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasized that sustained economic growth requires technological innovation alongside institutional and ideological adjustments, illustrating the interconnectedness of economic and political development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this perspective by arguing that divergent development paths in the New World stemmed from varying institutional frameworks, where elite control restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. Hornbeck (2010, p.2) provides empirical evidence of this dynamic, showing how the introduction of barbed wire facilitated agricultural development by enabling property rights protection and mitigating the detrimental effects of livestock damage. The ability to enforce property rights, therefore, is not merely a prerequisite for economic activity but also a catalyst for broader societal transformation. While institutions can facilitate growth, their effectiveness is contingent on broader societal conditions and the capacity to adapt to changing circumstances, as evidenced by Kuznets’ observations regarding the compatibility of modern technology and social structures (Kuznets 1973, p.7). The historical record suggests that the path to economic prosperity is rarely linear, and requires ongoing adaptation and refinement of institutional frameworks to ensure continued progress.

Europe’s divergence was partly fueled by guild institutions that facilitated labor markets and human capital development, as Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) observed a correlation between wage inequality and macroeconomic forces. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights that western Europe's institutional framework, particularly the guilds, provided a means to guarantee the fair execution of apprentice contracts, fostering the intergenerational transfer of skills. These formalized structures, however, also faced criticism for potentially restricting entry and artificially inflating wages, a tension that complicates the narrative of purely beneficial institutional development (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further contextualize this by noting that urban development, a key component of economic advancement, lagged in certain regions, impacting the ability of laborers to benefit from increased productivity. Stern (2004, p.1) cautions against simplistic interpretations of economic progress and environmental quality, suggesting the Environmental Kuznets Curve may not always hold true, mirroring the complexities of institutional impacts. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) extend this perspective by emphasizing the importance of understanding “shrinking” episodes in Sub-Saharan Africa, which underscores how even seemingly positive institutional developments can be undermined by recurring crises. The interplay of these factors reveals a nuanced picture of long-run economic growth, where institutional effectiveness is inextricably linked to broader societal conditions and the capacity to adapt to unforeseen challenges.

This perspective, shared with their earlier work (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4) and supported by Austin (2008, p.2), posits that colonial legacies continue to exert a powerful influence on contemporary economic disparities, challenging explanations rooted solely in geographic or cultural factors. Austin (2008, p.15), however, cautions against a simplistic narrative of colonial imposition, noting instances where colonial regimes adapted to existing indigenous property rights systems, recognizing their potential to support economic growth within specific contexts. These findings suggest that economic development results from a complex interplay of political power, historical contingencies, and the adaptability of both colonial and indigenous actors, not solely from institutional design. The enduring impact of colonial policies underscores the importance of understanding the historical roots of contemporary economic inequalities.

Distributional conflicts frequently shaped pre-industrial European institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) demonstrates, challenging conventional understandings of institutional development. This view aligns with Peters’ (Peters 2004, p.35) analysis of land negotiations in Africa, which demonstrates how state-led institutional innovations frequently masked processes of exclusion and class formation, suggesting that seemingly flexible systems can reinforce existing power structures. Examining the historical context of European economic development, Pamuk (2007, p.24) argues that the Black Death spurred long-term changes, ultimately contributing to a wage gap that facilitated northwestern Europe’s economic ascendancy, while van Zanden (1999, p.13) complicates this narrative by noting that even periods of apparent economic growth coincided with periods of low real wages. Hopkins (2019, p.3) further underscores the complexity of understanding precolonial economic development by emphasizing the need for diverse methodologies and recognizing the limitations of relying solely on numerical data, a point also echoed by Simson (2019, p.9) who demonstrates how postcolonial budget policies in East Africa were shaped by global economic forces rather than solely by local clientelism. These perspectives collectively suggest that institutional development is rarely a linear process of progress, but rather a dynamic interplay of power, belief systems, and external pressures. The persistence of these dynamics across continents and eras reveals the enduring influence of historical contingencies on contemporary economic outcomes.

Africa’s contemporary economic underperformance is partly attributable to the continent’s historical involvement in the transatlantic slave trades, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.3), a perspective further substantiated by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) who highlight the overlooked economic contributions of women. The slave trades, as Nunn (2008, p.6) details, induced violence, disrupted social structures, and diverted resources away from productive investments, creating a legacy of instability that continues to impede development. This disruption extended beyond the direct loss of human capital; Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade fostered pervasive mistrust within African communities, fundamentally altering social norms and hindering cooperation, a mechanism that accounts for at least half of the trade's lasting impact. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.4) offer a comparative perspective, noting that India’s relative economic decline predated British rule, suggesting that pre-existing institutional weaknesses and labor productivity issues contributed to its later struggles, which complicates straightforward attributions of underdevelopment to external forces. The impact of these pre-colonial factors, such as slavery and gender inequality, highlights the importance of understanding the historical context shaping contemporary economic trajectories.

The fundamental role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme across numerous scholarly investigations (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) demonstrate that institutional quality, rather than geographic factors, explains persistent differences in economic prosperity, a finding reinforced by van Zanden (2009, p.19) who links a low skill premium to the presence of effective institutions for human capital formation. Austin (2008, p.2) cautions against overly simplistic causal narratives, arguing that the “reversal of fortune” thesis often compresses historical processes and overlooks the complexities of institutional evolution. Despite these nuances, the consensus remains that secure property rights and limited arbitrary government action are essential for fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

While the importance of institutions is widely acknowledged, disagreement arises regarding the precise mechanisms through which they influence economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) posit that even extractive institutions can persist due to the political power of vested interests, while Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes the role of shared beliefs and values in shaping institutional structures, sometimes independently of economic efficiency. This divergence in perspective highlights the challenge of disentangling the interplay between formal rules, informal norms, and the distribution of power in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that institutions are often designed to maximize rents for specific groups rather than to promote broad-based development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43).

Ultimately, empirical comparisons of historical trajectories provide valuable insights into the relative importance of institutional factors (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) illustrate how institutional changes in England spurred economic growth, a contrast that underscores the importance of credible commitment and the rule of law, while Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial regimes sometimes supported existing indigenous property rights systems, demonstrating the contingent nature of institutional development. Comparing the experiences of India and Ghana, as Austin (2008, p.15) does, reveals how divergent institutional pathways, shaped by unique historical circumstances and political considerations, have resulted in vastly different economic outcomes, demonstrating the enduring significance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
