The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central and contested question in economics, with implications for understanding historical trajectories and designing effective development policies. A core disagreement revolves around the relative importance of formal rules and informal norms in fostering economic prosperity, and whether institutional development is primarily a consequence of underlying economic factors or a driver of independent growth. Some scholars emphasize the role of secure property rights and contract enforcement in stimulating investment and innovation (North 1989, p.1; North and Weingast 1989, p.2), while others highlight the complexities of institutional change and the potential for powerful groups to manipulate institutions for their own benefit (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). This debate underscores the need to move beyond simplistic models and consider the historical, political, and social contexts that shape institutional evolution. The ongoing challenge is to disentangle the causal relationships between institutions, economic performance, and broader societal factors, moving beyond descriptive accounts to identify mechanisms through which institutions drive growth.

Defining and enforcing property rights through evolving political institutions, as North (1989, p.6) argues, directly facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs. Kuznets (1973, p.7) supports this view, arguing that sustained economic growth requires technological innovation alongside the institutional and ideological adjustments needed to integrate new technologies into society. This shared understanding, that well-defined property rights and legal frameworks are essential for long-run economic development, is further clarified by Hornbeck (2010, p.2), who demonstrates how innovations like barbed wire can spur agricultural development by clarifying land ownership and reducing conflict. Hornbeck (2010, p.41) also cautions that the benefits of institutional clarity depend on broader societal conditions; insecure property rights can impede economic development, especially in frontier regions. The historical example of American agriculture illustrates that the inability to protect land from encroachment can significantly limit growth potential, underscoring the importance of both formal legal structures and the social norms that support them. The interplay between technological advancement and institutional adaptation reveals a complex dynamic that shapes the trajectory of economic growth.

The historical trajectory of economic growth across diverse regions reveals the profound and lasting impact of colonial legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This dynamic was not solely due to geographic or cultural factors, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) found that controlling for institutions reduced the explanatory power of variables like distance from the equator and continental dummies, indicating that institutional quality is a primary driver of economic disparity. The imposition of such institutions, frequently serving to block broader participation in economic activity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43), created a self-perpetuating cycle of inequality and limited economic advancement. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that even seemingly minor institutional changes, like those in France before the Revolution, could have significant economic repercussions, suggesting that the initial colonial choices had long-lasting consequences. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further emphasize that explaining these institutional differences remains a central challenge for economic historians, as the initial conditions set in motion divergent trajectories that proved difficult to reverse. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions emerge and evolve, recognizing that initial colonial choices can have profound and enduring effects on long-run economic development.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) highlights the crucial role of institutions in fostering long-run economic growth, particularly through their impact on labor markets and human capital development (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.10; van Zanden 1999, p.2). Guild structures, for example, provided a framework for the intergenerational transfer of skills and ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts (van Zanden 2009, p.19), contributing to a positive feedback loop of investment and productivity. Van Zanden (1999, p.13) complicates this perspective by noting a historical period where economic development coincided with low real wages in western Europe, suggesting that the benefits of institutional development were not always immediate or universal. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further refine this understanding by demonstrating that urbanization, a key feature of economic development, was uneven across Europe, with central and eastern regions lagging behind in the realization of economies of scale. This unevenness underscores the importance of considering regional variations when assessing the impact of institutions on long-run economic trajectories, as the positive effects of formalized structures may have been spatially constrained. The persistence of these patterns suggests that institutional effectiveness is contingent on a complex interplay of factors beyond mere formal structures. These observations invite further investigation into the nuanced ways institutions interact with other economic forces to shape long-term development outcomes.

Postcolonial budget policies in East Africa, often viewed as clientelistic, reveal the influence of global economic forces and shifting developmental priorities, as Simson (2019, p.9) demonstrates. These policies, which included substantial investment in industrial development and infrastructure, reflect a broader pattern of states responding to perceived vulnerabilities and opportunities within the international system (Simson 2019, p.11). Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) offer a complementary perspective, suggesting that similar pressures, albeit operating over a longer historical timeframe, influenced economic trajectories in Europe, where demand-side factors and resource allocation played a crucial role in shaping regional disparities. Pamuk (2007, p.24) further illuminates this dynamic by highlighting the long-term consequences of exogenous shocks, such as the Black Death, which spurred institutional changes that ultimately contributed to Europe’s economic development. These historical shifts, like the postcolonial budgetary choices in East Africa, underscore how external forces can trigger institutional adaptations with profound and lasting economic ramifications. The observed patterns highlight the interconnectedness of regional economic outcomes and the influence of broader global trends, regardless of the specific historical context (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). Understanding these dynamics necessitates moving beyond simplistic explanations that focus solely on internal factors, acknowledging the complex interplay of global forces and local responses (Simson 2019, p.9).

Austin (2008, p.2) contends that narratives like Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson’s “reversal of fortune” thesis often compress timelines and overlook nuanced local adaptations, challenging the notion of straightforward institutional development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) similarly underscores how development policy frequently overlooks the complexities of historical trajectories, promoting “best practice” institutions without accounting for the specific contexts shaping their implementation and impact in regions like Africa. Peters (2004, p.30) further complicates these narratives by revealing how reported land negotiations frequently mask underlying processes of exclusion and class formation, demonstrating that seemingly flexible institutional arrangements can reinforce existing inequalities. The emphasis on formal institutions can obscure how local actors negotiate and adapt to colonial or post-colonial rule; for example, indigenous property rights systems were sometimes supported for economic efficiency (Austin 2008, p.15). Furthermore, the framing of ethnicity as a primary driver of development challenges simplistic explanations, suggesting that deeper political and economic struggles are at play (Hopkins 2009, p.17). Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) and Peters (2004, p.35) collectively demonstrate that simplistic narratives of institutional change and development often fail to account for the contingent nature of historical processes and the agency of local actors. The persistent influence of historical legacies and local contexts suggests that understanding long-run economic growth requires a more granular and historically informed approach, moving beyond generalized models of institutional development.

Africa’s current economic underdevelopment is significantly shaped by the historical slave trades, as Nunn (2008, p.3) argues, challenging explanations solely reliant on colonial legacies. The transatlantic slave trade, and the preceding intra-African trade, spurred conflict and instability, diverting resources from productive investments and hindering institutional development (Nunn 2008, p.6). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further complicate simplistic narratives by highlighting the vital economic contributions of African women, which were often overlooked in accounts emphasizing solely external forces or colonial structures. This perspective underscores the importance of internal dynamics and agency in shaping African economic trajectories, demonstrating that the continent's history is more complex than narratives focusing solely on external imposition. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) extend this line of inquiry by proposing that the slave trade fostered a climate of mistrust within African communities, which continues to impede economic cooperation and development. The erosion of trust, a key ingredient for collective action and economic growth, resulted from the constant threat of raiding and enslavement, thereby creating a self-perpetuating cycle of instability (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.16). Although institutional explanations often focus on formal structures, the pervasive effects of the slave trade demonstrate the profound and lasting impact of social and psychological factors on long-run economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.27) remind us that understanding these legacies requires careful attention to the nuances of historical processes and the potential for periods of growth even within challenging circumstances. The long shadow of historical shocks, and the ways they affect social capital, demands that we consider a broader range of factors when assessing the drivers of African development.

The fundamental role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central concern within development economics, and scholars agree that the design and enforcement of rules profoundly influence economic trajectories (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of credible commitments and limited government as essential for fostering secure property rights and enabling capital market development, ultimately driving economic expansion. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) extend this argument by demonstrating the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary income levels, suggesting that initial institutional conditions can create persistent divergence across nations. Austin (2008, p.2), however, cautions against overly simplistic narratives of institutional causality, arguing that the "reversal of fortune" thesis overemphasizes the singular importance of institutions and neglects the complexities of historical processes and path dependence. These perspectives demonstrate the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on economic development.

Despite broad agreement on the significance of institutions, disagreement arises regarding the extent to which these institutions are shaped by exogenous factors or reflect deliberate choices by powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically influential groups even if they impede broader economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.17) further emphasizes the role of historical legacies and power dynamics in shaping institutional outcomes, particularly within the African context, where colonial rule often involved negotiated arrangements rather than imposed structures. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) offers a nuanced perspective, suggesting that institutions facilitating the transfer of skills and investment in human capital can contribute to long-term economic growth, but that these institutions are often shaped by underlying factor proportions and market conditions. This highlights the interplay between institutional design, power structures, and economic forces.

The debate surrounding the relative importance of exogenous and endogenous factors in shaping institutions is partially resolved by examining the historical experiences of European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.43) find that European mortality rates, a relatively exogenous factor, significantly shaped colonial institutions, which in turn influenced long-run economic outcomes, demonstrating the potential for external shocks to shape institutional development. Austin (2008, p.15) points to instances where colonial regimes supported existing indigenous property rights systems in India and Ghana, illustrating how local conditions and political considerations can modify the imposition of external institutional models. By comparing the experiences of colonies with varying mortality rates and examining the adaptation of colonial policies to local contexts, we can better understand the complex interplay between external forces and endogenous processes in shaping institutional trajectories.
