The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a central question in economics, with profound implications for understanding historical development trajectories and designing effective policy interventions. A core debate revolves around the relative importance of formal rules, informal norms, and broader societal factors in fostering sustained economic advancement, moving beyond simplistic explanations rooted solely in resource endowments or technological progress. Early perspectives often focused on quantifiable factors, but a growing body of scholarship emphasizes the critical, and often path-dependent, nature of institutional arrangements. The challenge lies in disentangling the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and acknowledging the complex interplay between political, social, and economic forces. This review examines several streams of literature that shed light on this complex relationship, highlighting how institutional structures shape incentives, constrain actions, and ultimately determine a society's capacity for long-run growth. The persistence of colonial legacies underscores the need to understand how institutions, once established, can profoundly and enduringly shape economic trajectories.

The literature examining the historical roots of institutional development frequently highlights the enduring impact of colonial legacies on contemporary economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) demonstrate that factors like settler mortality, which influenced the types of institutions established during colonial rule, continue to predict income levels today, even when controlling for geographic variables. This perspective emphasizes that institutions are not neutral arbiters but are often shaped by the interests of powerful groups, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that even "equilibrium institutions" can be extractive and stifle economic development if designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further illustrates how historical processes, such as the negotiated nature of colonial rule in some regions, continue to shape contemporary governance structures in Africa. The long shadow of colonial history underscores the difficulty of achieving rapid economic convergence and highlights the need to address deeply embedded institutional constraints (Hopkins 2009, p.17). Understanding the historical construction of institutions is therefore essential for explaining persistent economic disparities.

A parallel stream of research focuses on the crucial role of secure property rights and limited government in fostering economic growth, emphasizing the importance of constitutional design (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the development of capital markets is inextricably linked to the degree to which regimes are committed to upholding rules and limiting arbitrary government action. This perspective resonates with North’s broader argument (North 1989, p.1) that institutions, particularly those defining property rights and enforcing contracts, are critical for economic advancement, moving beyond explanations centered on population or savings. The establishment of credible commitments, therefore, appears to be a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for sustained economic growth.

Further complicating the picture, scholars have explored how institutions interact with technological change and broader economic transformations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.23). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.43) suggest that extractive institutions become increasingly problematic as new technologies require broader participation in the economy, as elites may actively block innovations that threaten their power or redistribute gains. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) connects institutional development to the skill premium, arguing that efficient labor and capital markets, underpinned by strong institutions, contribute to lower skill premiums and long-term economic growth, thus revealing a complex interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes. Recognizing this dynamic interaction is crucial for understanding how institutions can either facilitate or hinder technological progress and economic development.

Finally, a nuanced perspective acknowledges the complexities of institutional change and the ways in which historical legacies can constrain development pathways (Hopkins 2009, p.17; van Zanden 2009, p.19). Van Zanden (2009, p.19) illustrates this point by examining the role of guilds in western Europe, noting that while they facilitated the transfer of skills, they also attempted to restrict competition, demonstrating that institutions can have both beneficial and detrimental effects. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and political context when designing institutional reforms and recognizing that institutional change is often a complex and protracted process.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) similarly emphasizes that sustained economic growth requires more than resource exploitation, necessitating accompanying institutional and ideological adjustments, a point reinforced by Hornbeck (2010, p.2)’s examination of how barbed wire facilitated agricultural development by enabling property rights protection. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by illustrating how divergent historical institutions in the New World led to vastly different development trajectories, demonstrating that the specific configuration of institutions, rather than simply their existence, dictates long-run prosperity. This highlights the challenges inherent in replicating institutional successes across diverse contexts, as historical specificity complicates straightforward transferability (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The enduring legacy of institutional choices shapes economic opportunities and constraints, underscoring the need for nuanced analyses that consider the complex interplay of political, economic, and social factors.

Their work, alongside that of Austin (2008, p.2), highlights the persistence of institutional structures established during colonial periods, demonstrating how these structures can either foster or impede long-run economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) further complicates this perspective by suggesting that the imposition of colonial institutions was not always a straightforward process, as colonial regimes sometimes adapted to existing indigenous property rights systems to ensure economic efficiency. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) provide additional context by demonstrating that India's relative economic decline began before British rule, suggesting that pre-existing institutional and productivity factors also contributed to its trajectory. The long shadow of colonial history, therefore, necessitates a nuanced understanding that accounts for both the deliberate actions of colonial powers and the complex interplay of local contexts and pre-existing conditions. Understanding these intricate dynamics is crucial for crafting effective development strategies that address the root causes of persistent inequality.

However, van Zanden’s (1999, p.2) earlier work challenges the notion of continuous economic growth in Europe between 1500 and 1800, a finding that complicates straightforward narratives of progress and suggests periods of stagnation or even decline despite institutional advancements. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) extend this temporal perspective by demonstrating that the Great Divergence began well before 1800, indicating that the institutional factors shaping economic trajectories were at play for a considerable period. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) highlight the complex interplay of macroeconomic forces influencing both growth and inequality, suggesting that policymakers have historically faced difficult trade-offs when attempting to balance these competing objectives. The persistence of these patterns across centuries demonstrates the enduring impact of institutional arrangements on regional economic trajectories, even as those arrangements evolve and adapt over time.

Peters (2004, p.35) further complicates these narratives by demonstrating how reported land negotiations often obscure processes of exclusion and class formation, revealing that institutional development is not always a straightforward path toward equitable growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) contribute to this nuanced understanding by highlighting that Sub-Saharan Africa's economic performance was more robust prior to 1950 than previously thought, suggesting that recurring episodes of "shrinking" have significantly hampered long-term progress. Simson (2019, p.9) contextualizes these patterns by showing that East African postcolonial budget policies, often viewed as clientelist, were instead responses to global economic forces and shifting priorities, demonstrating the influence of external pressures on domestic institutional development. Hopkins (2009, p.17) underscores this complexity by noting the role of ethnicity in shaping development challenges, a factor often overlooked in broader institutional analyses, while Simson (2019, p.11) further reveals how military spending, initially a colonial responsibility, became a significant budgetary burden in the post-independence era. This perspective, shared by Hopkins (2009, p.8); Peters (2004, p.35); Simson (2019, p.9); Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17); and Hopkins (2019, p.13), emphasizes the need to move beyond simplistic models and consider the intricate interplay of historical legacies, local agency, and global forces in understanding African economic trajectories. Ultimately, a deeper investigation into the drivers of economic volatility is necessary to inform more effective development strategies.

Nunn (2008, p.3) demonstrates that Africa’s historical involvement in the transatlantic slave trade continues to impede its economic development. The disruption caused by the slave trades, which involved raiding, warfare, and kidnapping, fostered a climate of insecurity and instability that undermined the development of institutions and infrastructure (Nunn 2008, p.6). This perspective challenges explanations that solely attribute Africa’s underdevelopment to colonial policies, highlighting the role of pre-colonial shocks in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Nunn 2008, p.3; Pamuk 2007, p.4; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The erosion of trust, a crucial element for cooperation and investment, can be traced to the heightened levels of insecurity and the breakdown of social norms induced by the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.16). Ultimately, understanding the long-term effects of historical shocks requires a more complex assessment of their interplay with local agency and institutional development, acknowledging the multifaceted nature of African economic history.

The foundational role of institutions in fostering long-run economic growth is a central theme across numerous scholarly investigations (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate how the establishment of credible commitments, particularly through constitutional design that limits arbitrary government action, is essential for the development of secure property rights and robust capital markets. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) further emphasize the significance of institutions, finding that their influence on economic performance outweighs factors like geographic location or colonial history, suggesting that institutional quality explains observed income disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). However, Austin (2008, p.2) cautions against overly simplistic narratives that attribute economic outcomes solely to institutional frameworks, pointing out that the “reversal of fortune” thesis can compress historical trajectories and overlook the nuances of local agency and adaptation (Austin 2008, p.2). Ultimately, understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic development necessitates a recognition of the historical specificity of institutional change and its interaction with local contexts.

Despite broad agreement on the importance of institutions, scholars diverge on the precise mechanisms through which they shape economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions often evolve to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, even if those institutions are not conducive to broader economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33), while van Zanden (2009, p.27) highlights the role of skill premiums and human capital investment in driving long-term economic growth, linking these factors to institutional development (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Hopkins (2009, p.17) underscores the importance of considering local initiatives and negotiated arrangements in shaping colonial institutions, challenging the notion of externally imposed “best practice” models (Hopkins 2009, p.17). This disagreement regarding the drivers of institutional development reveals the challenges in disentangling cause and effect and the need for more nuanced analyses of historical processes.

The debate concerning the relative importance of institutional design versus other factors can be partially resolved through examining the historical experiences of colonial trade versus settlement colonies (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin (2008, p.15) notes that in colonies of trade, indigenous property rights systems often proved supportive of economic growth, leading colonial regimes to reinforce them, while settlement colonies experienced different patterns (Austin 2008, p.15). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) similarly demonstrate that the persistence of colonial institutions is linked to mortality rates, impacting long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). By comparing these contrasting colonial experiences, scholars can better understand the complex interplay between institutional frameworks, local agency, and historical context in shaping long-run economic growth.
