The enduring puzzle of long-run economic growth centers on the role of institutions, specifically how they shape trajectories of prosperity and poverty across nations and over time. Intellectual debate revolves around the extent to which institutions are endogenous products of societal development or exogenous forces capable of driving it, and whether their impact is constant or contingent on specific historical or technological contexts. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and limited government as foundational pillars of economic advancement, while others highlight the complexities of institutional change and the potential for even seemingly “extractive” institutions to persist or even contribute to growth under certain conditions. This nuanced understanding necessitates a careful examination of how institutions evolve, interact with other factors, and ultimately influence the long-run economic fortunes of societies. The historical record demonstrates that seemingly beneficial institutions can be maintained, or even reinforced, by politically powerful groups seeking to maximize rents, even if those institutions ultimately hinder broader economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The question then becomes whether these entrenched institutional arrangements can be overcome, and under what conditions transformative change is possible (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.43).

The work of Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson has spurred considerable discussion regarding the causal link between colonial legacies and contemporary economic outcomes, prompting critiques that challenge the simplicity of their “reversal of fortune” thesis (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin (2008, p.15) suggests that the framework carries a risk of compressing historical periods and oversimplifying the factors influencing institutional development; colonial regimes sometimes supported existing property rights systems. This perspective highlights the significance of recognizing the agency of local actors and the variable nature of institutional change, because colonial rule was frequently negotiated, not simply imposed (Hopkins 2009, p.17). Hopkins further emphasizes the long-term impact of historical processes, particularly in Africa, suggesting that simplistic policy prescriptions based on idealized institutional models often fail to account for the deep-rooted social and political dynamics that shape economic trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Institutional evolution is a complex process, shaped by power dynamics, historical context, and the interaction of internal and external forces, and it does not follow a linear progression toward ideal results.

North and Weingast’s work provides a foundational argument for the crucial role of constitutional design in fostering economic growth, emphasizing the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis highlights how the ability to limit arbitrary government action through institutional frameworks can unlock investment and fuel market development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The emphasis on formal institutions, while important, may also overlook the significance of informal norms and practices that shape economic behavior and influence the effectiveness of formal rules (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The ongoing debate underscores the need to consider the interplay between formal and informal institutions and the complex mechanisms through which they shape economic outcomes.

Van Zanden’s research offers a valuable counterpoint to narratives of continuous economic progress, challenging the notion that western Europe consistently experienced upward trajectories in living standards during the early modern period (van Zanden 1999, p.2). His work suggests that periods of economic development were often accompanied by periods of stagnation or even decline, complicating the relationship between economic growth and welfare (van Zanden 1999, p.13). This perspective resonates with the broader argument that institutional development is not always synonymous with improved living standards and that the distribution of economic gains is a crucial factor in assessing the overall impact of institutional change (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The interplay between institutional development, factor proportions, and skill premiums reveals a more nuanced picture of long-run economic growth, highlighting the importance of considering the broader social and economic context in which institutions operate.

The literature consistently points to the crucial role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, yet the specific mechanisms and conditions under which institutions exert their influence remain a subject of ongoing investigation.

Establishing secure property rights through constitutional design, as argued by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), provides a foundation for market development and sustained economic expansion (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4; North 1989, p.6). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) further elaborate on this by illustrating how divergent colonial legacies, characterized by varying degrees of elite control and restrictions on competition, resulted in profoundly different developmental trajectories across the New World. The persistence of these institutional differences, initially shaped by colonial circumstances, has demonstrably hampered long-run economic growth in certain regions, even after the formal end of colonial rule (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North (1989, p.1) underscores this point by emphasizing the interdependence of political and economic institutions, arguing that the development of effective institutions is essential for fostering market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. The long shadow of colonial institutions, therefore, continues to shape economic outcomes, revealing the enduring power of initial conditions and the challenges of institutional reform.

The introduction of barbed wire exemplifies how property rights protection can reshape agricultural practices, as demonstrated by Hornbeck (2010, p.2). This seemingly simple technological innovation highlights a broader point: specific institutional shifts, such as clarifying property rights, can spur long-run economic growth by resolving externalities and fostering investment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The consequences of insecure property rights extend beyond agricultural settings, as evidenced by observations from contemporary developing countries, where limitations on land ownership demonstrably impede economic development (Hornbeck 2010, p.41). The evolution of social norms and beliefs surrounding trust, as detailed by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.16), reveals how seemingly distant historical events can have enduring impacts on economic trajectories. Ultimately, the interplay between technological innovation, institutional frameworks, and social dynamics underscores the complex and multifaceted nature of long-run economic development. The legacies of institutional shifts, whether technological or social, continue to shape economic outcomes across time and space.

The adoption of modern technologies, such as steam and electric power, necessitates corresponding shifts in social structures and institutions, as initially observed by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and further supported by Pamuk (2007, p.4). The demographic shock of the pandemic, as Pamuk (2007, p.24) details, created a labor scarcity that led to higher wages and, subsequently, a series of institutional adjustments that fostered economic growth in northwestern Europe. Kuznets (1973, p.7) further clarifies that technological innovation often involves combining known technologies with novel institutional frameworks, highlighting the unpredictable nature of such combinations and the necessity of exploration into the unknown. This perspective emphasizes that simply adopting new technologies is insufficient; the accompanying institutional and ideological changes are crucial for translating technological advancements into sustained economic growth. While the initial impact of a technological shift might be positive, its long-term effects depend on the adaptability of existing institutions and the willingness to embrace new social norms. These transformations, however, were not uniformly beneficial, and the long-term consequences of the Black Death's impact on European institutions continue to be debated (Pamuk 2007, p.24). The interplay between demographic shifts and institutional adaptation underscores the complex and dynamic relationship between population changes and long-run economic trajectories.

The fair execution of apprenticeship contracts depended on institutions that facilitated the transmission of skills across generations (van Zanden 2009, p.19). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) further complicate this picture by demonstrating that policymakers did not necessarily have to choose between economic growth and greater equality, implying that institutional arrangements could be structured to promote both simultaneously. The interplay of these factors underscores the nuanced and often contradictory role of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, demonstrating that their impact is contingent on specific historical circumstances and the complex interplay of social and economic forces.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) emphasize the critical, often overlooked, economic contributions of women in precolonial and colonial Africa. Peters (2004, p.35) stresses that these contributions were integral to production and resource management, not merely supplementary to formal economic structures; state-led initiatives intended for economic development sometimes worsened existing inequalities. Hopkins (2019, p.3) underscores the challenges inherent in reconstructing these historical economic realities, noting the need for diverse methodologies to fully grasp the complexity of African economic development. The persistent emphasis on industrialization and large-scale infrastructure projects during the postcolonial era, as Simson (2019, p.9) demonstrates through an examination of budget policies in East Africa, reveals a prioritization of certain development pathways that may have inadvertently marginalized women’s economic roles and the decentralized systems they often managed. While these policies aimed to reduce economic dependence, their implementation frequently resulted in unintended consequences, such as increased debt burdens and the displacement of capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). Ultimately, understanding the nuances of African economic history requires a more inclusive perspective that acknowledges the significant and multifaceted contributions of women to long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Peters 2004, p.35; Hopkins 2019, p.13; Simson 2019, p.9). The complexities of these interwoven factors suggest that institutional frameworks, even when seemingly designed to promote progress, can inadvertently perpetuate or exacerbate existing inequalities.

Nunn (2008, p.3) pushes this critique further by demonstrating the lasting negative impacts of the transatlantic slave trades on African development, a consequence often overlooked in accounts solely attributing underdevelopment to colonialism. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) contribute to this challenge by arguing that the divergence between Europe and Asia began earlier than previously thought, suggesting that pre-existing conditions and internal factors played a significant role. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.4) then qualify this broader narrative by illustrating how India's relative economic decline predates British rule, indicating that internal factors, such as labor productivity and pre-existing institutional weaknesses, were critical drivers of divergence (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.10; Nunn 2008, p.3; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.9). These works collectively demonstrate that simplistic explanations relying solely on institutional frameworks often fail to capture the complex and contingent nature of long-run economic development. The recognition of these intricate historical forces necessitates a deeper exploration of the specific conditions under which institutions exert their influence.

The enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth is a central theme across historical and contemporary scholarship (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the critical role of constitutional design in fostering secure property rights, which, in turn, incentivizes investment and market development. Austin (2008, p.2) builds upon this foundation by critiquing the oversimplification inherent in some institutionalist accounts, arguing that they often fail to adequately account for the complex interplay of historical contingencies and path dependencies. This perspective underscores the importance of recognizing that institutional change is rarely a straightforward or universally applicable process, and that simplistic explanations often miss crucial nuances (Austin 2008, p.15). The recognition of these intricacies necessitates a deeper appreciation for the specific conditions under which institutions exert their influence.

While scholars generally agree on the importance of institutions, a key point of contention revolves around the precise mechanisms through which they shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.4). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.4) demonstrate the significant impact of colonial institutions on long-run development trajectories, while van Zanden (2009, p.27) emphasizes the role of factor proportions and skill premiums in driving European divergence. Austin (2008, p.2) challenges this view, suggesting that the emphasis on institutional reversals may overstate their importance relative to other factors, such as pre-existing economic structures and local conditions. This disagreement centers on the relative weight of exogenous institutional shocks versus endogenous, historically contingent processes.

The empirical evidence from colonial contexts helps to resolve this debate (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies, even when controlling for geographic factors, which supports the view that institutional reversals have a lasting impact. Hopkins (2009, p.17) advises against using a single institutional framework for varied historical contexts, and emphasizes understanding how local actors negotiated and adapted to colonial rule. Ultimately, the long-run economic performance of former colonies, as measured by sustained income levels and economic diversification, reflects a complex interplay of institutional factors, historical contingencies, and local agency, demonstrating that the impact of institutions is contingent upon a broader range of historical forces (Austin 2008, p.2).
