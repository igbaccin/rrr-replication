The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. The stakes are high because understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One key disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers, influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. This disagreement is evident in the differing views on whether extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were the primary cause of long-run economic underdevelopment. Another area of contention is the role of formal education and the codification of customary law in shaping economic outcomes. These debates highlight the complex interplay between institutions, economic growth, and historical legacies. This interplay is evident in the varied economic outcomes of former colonies, where some thrived while others stagnated. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. The type of institutions implemented depended on European settlers' mortality rates and their intended duration of stay. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic outcomes. However, the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact on economic growth was also mediated by other factors, such as the ability to industrialize.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Austin (2008, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) find that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more beneficial impact on education than other colonial powers, highlighting a nuanced view of colonial impacts. British rule fostered formal education development through its institutional framework, which encouraged African political leaders and families to perceive the benefits of formal education. This institutional framework influenced the demand for missionary education, altering the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. As a result, the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule changed the perceived benefits of formal education, leading to its expansion. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varied across Africa. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations altered their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that the effectiveness of colonial institutions depended on local conditions and the responsiveness of colonial administrations to indigenous initiatives. This indicates that while British rule was more conducive to formal education development, the overall impact on economic growth varied based on local factors and the adaptive nature of colonial institutions.

The early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with significant differences in real incomes observed between European cities and their Asian counterparts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta show that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector was driven by merchant distribution and finance. This suggests that the service sector played a critical role in early modern economic growth. The divergence was underpinned by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity in Europe. Allen adds that European cities maintained high real wages during this period, despite fluctuations in grain prices and other commodities. This indicates that the economic gains observed in Europe reflected deeper structural changes. The uneven distribution of these gains suggests that the benefits of early modern development were not uniformly experienced across different segments of society. These findings underscore the importance of institutional factors in shaping long-run economic growth, as the service sector's role in Europe highlights how institutional changes can drive productivity and economic development.

The economic decline in India and China during the early modern period offers a contrasting perspective on how institutional factors can influence long-run economic growth. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) demonstrate that economic stagnation in both countries was marked by significant institutional weaknesses. Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10) document that India's revenue per square mile reached its lowest point in the mid-eighteenth century, indicating a period of severe economic distress. This decline was exacerbated by the lack of state institutions capable of fostering investment and innovation, which are essential for breaking out of the Malthusian trap where population growth outpaces economic growth. This stagnation was underpinned by a lack of institutional support for labor market integration and productivity growth. However, Broadberry et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16) qualify this perspective by noting that while India's decline continued during the colonial period, it had already begun during the Mughal Empire. This suggests that the institutional weaknesses predated colonial rule, highlighting the enduring impact of pre-colonial institutions on long-run economic growth. This underscores how the lack of robust institutional frameworks can hinder economic development, even in the face of other favorable conditions.

The institutional revolution in factor markets, driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity, led to significant changes in economic structures but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Austin 2007, p.20; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This institutional change was partly driven by the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, which, along with the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour. The removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony further integrated markets, enhancing economic activity. These institutional changes did not directly correlate with long-run economic growth. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) provides a complementary perspective, showing that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by protecting property rights and reducing externalities from livestock damage. This innovation allowed farmers to better manage their land, leading to increased agricultural productivity. The herd law, which regulated livestock movement, played a crucial role in this transformation. However, the long-term economic impact of these institutional changes remains uncertain, as the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped detailing these decisions after 1880. This suggests that while institutional changes in factor markets can drive short-term economic adjustments, their impact on long-run economic growth is less clear.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight the significant roles played by women in economic growth and development, both historically and contemporarily. Women's contributions to economic growth are evident in various contexts, including African societies during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and the U.S. during the 20th century. In the U.S., the increased relative demand for female office workers and the growth of educational institutions significantly altered female labor supply, transforming women's employment and education dynamics. This shift was driven by exogenous changes, including technological advancements in household production and institutional changes that made part-time work more accessible. These factors collectively dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women, thereby increasing their participation in the labor force and contributing to economic growth. However, the codification of customary law under colonial rule in Africa reinforced patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework, which marginalized women's economic contributions, contrasts with the U.S. context, where institutional changes facilitated women's economic participation. Nevertheless, the broader implication is that women's contributions to economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework that governs their access to economic opportunities.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with colonial legacies playing a significant part. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutional frameworks, in turn, influenced economic outcomes by either encouraging investment and economic development or by extracting resources for the benefit of the colonial power. This dynamic is evident in the contrast between colonies like the United States, where inclusive institutions were established, and those like the Congo, where extractive institutions were imposed. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional framework, which marginalized women's economic contributions, contrasts with the U.S. context, where institutional changes facilitated women's economic participation. Nevertheless, the broader implication is that women's contributions to economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework that governs their access to economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. However, the codification of customary law under colonial rule in Africa reinforced patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework, which marginalized women's economic contributions, contrasts with the U.S. context, where institutional changes facilitated women's economic participation. Nevertheless, the broader implication is that women's contributions to economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework that governs their access to economic opportunities. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the region's economic performance was more robust than previously characterized. This finding suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform and that local factors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The broader implication is that women's contributions to economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework that governs their access to economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth. This perspective is supported by the broader implication that women's contributions to economic growth are contingent on the institutional framework that governs their access to economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their long-term effects on economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional frameworks across different colonial contexts. This variation, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), shows that inclusive institutions led to higher levels of economic development, while extractive institutions resulted in stagnation or decline.
