The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of whether the quality of institutions determines a country's economic trajectory over the long term. The disagreement lies in whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights are crucial for economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that other factors, such as colonial policies or local economic conditions, are more important. The debate is further complicated by the challenge of distinguishing between institutions that promote growth and those that extract resources. This tension is evident in the varying interpretations of how colonial institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. Austin, however, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This debate highlights the complexity of understanding the role of institutions in economic growth and the need for a nuanced approach that considers multiple factors. The interplay between institutional quality and economic outcomes is a central theme that runs through the literature, and it is essential to examine how different scholars have approached this relationship. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly shaped long-run economic growth in their former colonies. The type of institutions established was contingent on the mortality rates European settlers faced, which in turn influenced the institutional framework. For example, in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further elaborate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism, and geographic factors. This institutional framework, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, often hindered long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions can be measured using various indicators, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These findings underscore the enduring influence of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the importance of institutional quality in shaping long-run economic growth.

The institutional changes in African economies, particularly in Ghana during 1874-1939, demonstrate how shifts in land use rights and hired labour markets can be pivotal in driving economic transformations (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This period saw a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, driving an institutional transformation in Ghana's factor markets. The shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the transition from coerced to free labour and the abolition of slavery and pawning. This institutional change was influenced by both colonial policies and indigenous economic and political actions. Colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to these local dynamics. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to hypotheses that predicted otherwise (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional evolution in Ghana highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions, shaping the trajectory of economic growth in the region. This dynamic interplay illustrates the role of institutions in facilitating long-run economic growth by adapting to changing economic conditions and local actions.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing in the American Plains, as documented by Hornbeck (2010, p.32), exemplifies how technological advancements can be leveraged by institutions to enhance property rights and stimulate economic development. This technological innovation allowed farmers to demarcate their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated increased investment in agricultural improvements. Hornbeck (2010, p.8; 2010, p.32) shows that herd-law counties, which adopted the barbed wire technology, experienced a notable increase in improved farmland after the introduction of barbed wire. The difference-in-difference estimates indicate a substantial rise in improved land from 1880 to 1890. This technological shift protected farmers' investments and encouraged more intensive land use, as farmers could now safeguard their crops and livestock from trespassing animals. The adoption of barbed wire fencing thus exemplifies how institutional changes, in this case, technological innovations that enhance property rights, can drive agricultural development and long-run economic growth. This pattern suggests that the effectiveness of property rights institutions in fostering economic growth is contingent on the ability of these institutions to adapt to and integrate new technologies.

The literature on African economic history and development explores the complexities of economic growth in Africa without directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). This literature highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, particularly through their role in providing incentives for investment. These institutions are essential for providing the necessary incentives for investment, which is a key driver of economic growth. This perspective is further supported by the recognition that the history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques to be fully understood (Hopkins 2019, p.13). This progress underscores the need for continued research and institutional reforms to address the economic challenges faced by Africa.

The Great Divergence between Europe and Asia, as discussed by Broadberry and Gupta and Allen, underscores the importance of early institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence was driven by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture and manufacturing. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta highlight that "institutional change is usually seen as bringing about investment in agriculture, leading in turn to high agricultural labour productivity" (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This suggests that the Great Divergence was deeply rooted in early modern institutional frameworks that promoted economic growth, and was a product of industrialization. BroadberryEtal note that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, indicating that the lack of state institutions to underpin investment and innovation was a critical factor (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This implies that the absence of robust institutions in India hindered its ability to break out of the Malthusian trap, where population growth was constrained by available resources, and per capita incomes remained stagnant (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that the impact of colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa was not uniformly negative, suggesting a more nuanced role of institutions in economic development. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to Africans. This suggests that the educational policies and economic development fostered under British colonial rule had tangible benefits for African societies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also point out that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability implies that while British colonial rule may have had positive effects on education and economic growth, these effects were not uniform across all regions and were influenced by a range of contextual factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay of historical factors, colonial policies, and local endowments. The extractive institutions established by European colonizers, such as those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions significantly affected current income per capita, illustrating how the colonial legacy continues to influence economic outcomes today. The varying strategies of European colonization, from settlement with rule-of-law institutions to extractive states, underscore the diverse impact of institutional frameworks on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This diversity is further highlighted by the fact that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating a dynamic relationship between local conditions and institutional design (Austin 2008, p.15). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This reversal is deeply rooted in the institutional frameworks that were established during the colonial period, and is a result of geographic factors. The extractive nature of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can inform development strategies and help eliminate poverty. Institutions that provide incentives for investment are crucial for development, and the experience of East Asia offers relevant lessons for Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law and promoting progressive policies, and not solely by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The colonial legacy, particularly the development of formal education under British rule, has had a more benign impact on sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This educational foundation has contributed to the development of human capital, which is essential for long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. The diverse institutional strategies of European colonizers, from settlement with rule-of-law institutions to extractive states, illustrate how institutional frameworks significantly influence economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which these institutions affect economic outcomes. While some argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, others emphasize the importance of local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of per capita income levels in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century, which shows that many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This empirical comparison highlights the nuanced interplay between colonial institutions and local economic conditions in shaping long-run economic growth.
