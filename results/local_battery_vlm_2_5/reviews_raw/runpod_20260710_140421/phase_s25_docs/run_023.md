The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. Institutions are the rules of the game, and their quality can either foster or hinder economic development. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to extract resources from colonies have persisted and significantly affected current economic outcomes. Others contend that the impact of institutions is more nuanced, influenced by historical periods and paths that are often overlooked. Still, others suggest that the development of formal education and real wages in colonies were more conducive to growth than previously thought. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for policymakers seeking to design effective development strategies. The debate highlights the need to consider the complex interplay between institutions, historical contexts, and economic outcomes. This interplay is central to understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth, and the evidence presented in this review underscores the importance of examining the historical and contextual factors that influence institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutions established by European colonizers varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) describe how, in regions with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented. This institutional framework was a direct result of colonial policies, not just a product of geographic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism, underscoring the enduring impact of these policies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the notion that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis accurately captures the economic development of former colonies. They argue that this thesis oversimplifies the complex historical processes involved in shaping long-run economic growth. They contend that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, obscuring the nuances of institutional evolution and economic policy. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the difficulties in quantifying the institutionalist approach and the challenges in explaining why certain institutions favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. He notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is limited in its ability to explain the diverse economic trajectories of former colonies. This limitation is particularly evident in the case of Anglophone African economies, where colonial institutions were not uniformly extractive or exploitative. Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrates that in some regions, colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, leading to outcomes that differed from the extractive model predicted by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. This adaptation highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and the importance of local factors in shaping economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate this point by showing that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century was more complex than previously characterized. They argue that many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, challenging the notion that colonial institutions uniformly impeded economic growth. This finding suggests that the economic trajectory of former colonies was influenced by a variety of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors interacted in complex ways to produce diverse economic outcomes, underscoring the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg, along with Frankema, demonstrate that colonial rule in Africa had varied impacts, influencing historical and educational developments in specific ways (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg's work underscores that African poverty resulted from complex interactions between colonial policies and local conditions, not from structural impediments alone. These interactions influenced the development of formal education, which in turn affected long-run economic growth. Frankema argues that British rule, in particular, fostered a demand for missionary education driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development was intricately linked to the implementation of new social and political institutions, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels across British African colonies reveals that economic development varied significantly over time (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This variation suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, they were not the sole determinants of economic outcomes. The long-term picture of African welfare development was influenced by a combination of historical, geographical, and institutional factors, indicating that the trajectory of economic growth was more complex and multifaceted than often portrayed. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth.

The early modern economic divergence between Europe and Asia was driven by differences in productivity in traded goods sectors, climate effects, and revenue changes under different rulers (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This mechanism is further supported by Allen (2001, p.8), who argues that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. Allen (2001, p.8) notes that prices changed frequently, suggesting that economic conditions were dynamic and that the maintenance of high real wages in certain areas was a decisive break with the past. However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) qualify this by noting that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This implies that the institutional changes and investment in agriculture that Parthasarathi attributes to high levels of economic development in southern India may not have been as effective as initially thought. The decline in revenue and agricultural labour productivity in India during the Mughal Empire further underscores the complex interplay of factors that shaped long-run economic growth in Asia.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates that the introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains significantly enhanced agricultural development by strengthening property rights and mitigating livestock damage. This innovation enabled farmers to more effectively demarcate and protect their land, leading to substantial increases in improved farmland and overall agricultural productivity (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). The mechanism behind this growth was the reduction of externalities from livestock damage, which had previously hindered agricultural development. The introduction of barbed wire allowed farmers to create more secure boundaries, thereby encouraging investment in agricultural improvements. The qualitative shift in agricultural development varied across regions. The Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports reveal that the adoption of herd laws, which were intended to manage livestock and protect farmland, varied significantly across different counties (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This variability suggests that the effectiveness of barbed wire in enhancing agricultural development was contingent on local conditions and the existing legal framework governing land use. Therefore, while barbed wire fencing played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by enhancing property rights and reducing externalities, its impact was moderated by regional differences in land use policies and practices.

Bryant's critique of Eurocentric views of economic progress, shared by Goldin, underscores the importance of recognizing the contributions of non-Western societies and women to economic development (Bryant 2006, p.12; Goldin 2006, p.3). Both authors argue that the focus on Western institutions overlooks the significant contributions of other regions and demographics. One specific mechanism that Bryant identifies is the role of political institutions in facilitating economic development. Bryant contends that European political institutions provided a supportive environment for modernization, a perspective that contrasts with the revisionist view dismissing the uniqueness of European institutions (Bryant 2006, p.12). This institutional framework included the development of democratic regimes and legal systems that supported economic growth. Goldin, meanwhile, emphasizes the role of institutional changes in altering female labor supply and educational opportunities. She cites the growth of educational institutions and changes in household production technology as key factors that increased the supply of potential office workers and made part-time work readily available (Goldin 2006, p.3). These institutional changes transformed women's employment and had broader implications for economic development. This suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, they were also influenced by broader economic and social factors. The implication is that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving interactions with other economic and social dynamics. This complexity underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic development, one that acknowledges the roles of diverse societies and demographics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the colonial strategies of European powers, which established different institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. High mortality rates in colonies like the Congo led to the establishment of extractive institutions, while lower mortality rates in places like the United States resulted in institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional variance is further supported by evidence that European settlers' presence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the responsiveness of colonial administrations to economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The impact of these institutions is profound, as they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the type of institutions established during colonial rule directly influenced the economic trajectories of these regions.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes were deeply rooted in the policies pursued by European powers, not just a function of geographic factors. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional influence is further underscored by the fact that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the economic performance of these regions was not uniformly poor (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule had a lasting impact on economic development, shaping the economic outcomes of these regions in the long run.

The development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule highlights another dimension of institutional impact. British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of education and influencing the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development was part of a broader set of social and political institutions that affected long-run economic growth. The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies further supports this view, showing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This historical evidence indicates that institutional changes introduced by colonial rule had a profound and lasting impact on economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is primarily due to the type of institutions established or the broader economic and social factors that influenced these institutions. The evidence supports a view that both factors are crucial. Institutions shaped by politically powerful groups and the broader economic and social dynamics interact to influence long-run economic growth. For example, the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule shows that institutional changes can alter the perceived benefits of education and influence economic development (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving interactions with other economic and social dynamics.
