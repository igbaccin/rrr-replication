The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergent trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth and those who argue that geographical factors or other exogenous variables play a more decisive role. One position, advocated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), posits that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic outcomes of their former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, were shaped by the mortality rates and settlement patterns of Europeans, leading to persistent differences in income per capita. Another position, represented by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors, arguing that the institutional evolution and economic paths of nations are more nuanced and cannot be reduced to a single causal variable. This nuanced view emphasizes the importance of considering multiple historical periods and the diverse paths of institutional development. The debate underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic growth over the long term.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated through the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates. These institutions, designed to maximize resource extraction for the benefit of the colonizers, did not foster long-term economic development. By contrast, in colonies where Europeans settled in larger numbers, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, leading to better economic outcomes. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on economic growth, as extractive institutions impeded the development of productive activities and the accumulation of human capital. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, as the legacy of extractive institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in many former colonies today.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry et al. (2015) argue that shifts in land abundance, such as those experienced during the colonial period, led to institutional changes that are a specific instance of the broader concept of institutions. Specifically, these shifts triggered an 'institutional revolution' in factor markets, which is a specific instance of the broader concept of institutions. This revolution encompassed widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which were pivotal in shaping economic growth. The institutional revolution in factor markets was driven by the transition from coerced to free labour, which had significant economic ramifications. This transition was influenced by the abolition of slavery and pawning, which, while not directly induced by institutional innovation, responded to changing factor scarcities and played a crucial role in transforming the economic landscape (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2007, p.4; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This transformation was not uniform across regions; in areas where European settlers were absent, indigenous commodity production often aligned with colonial government interests, leading to institutional adaptations that encouraged economic activity (Austin 2008, p.15). These institutional changes, however, did not always lead to economic growth. For instance, in India, the decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This decline was exacerbated by the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation, which are essential for breaking out of the Malthusian trap and achieving sustained economic growth (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16).

Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrate that the introduction of money and new economic institutions into traditional systems can drive significant structural changes. In the case of the Tiv people of Nigeria, the colonial introduction of money and new markets altered the economic landscape, shifting away from a multicentric economy. The introduction of brass rods as a currency within the prestige sphere altered existing economic institutions. This transformation illustrates how new economic institutions can reshape traditional systems, even when those institutions are alien to the local culture. The adoption of new economic institutions can be challenging. Bohannan's work highlights the challenges and potential pain points in this transition, such as the inability of brass rods to fully replace traditional exchange systems. Hornbeck (2010, p.8) illustrates the challenges in the adoption of new economic institutions. The introduction of barbed wire fencing, which was intended to enhance agricultural development by protecting property rights, initially faced administrative hurdles. The Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped including details on herd laws, showing the administrative challenges in implementing new economic institutions. This demonstrates that the introduction of new economic institutions can face significant administrative hurdles, which can slow down or complicate the transition process. Despite these challenges, the introduction of new economic institutions can lead to significant shifts in economic structures and institutions, even when those institutions are alien to the local culture.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Hopkins 2019, p.3) challenge the conventional narrative of African economic performance by revealing that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the significant progress made in African economic history, which has been overlooked due to the limited use of national accounting in African economic history. Hopkins (2019, p.13) adds that a multifaceted approach to economic development history is essential for a comprehensive understanding. The availability of economic data for the colonial era and beyond suggests that African economies were more integrated into global markets than previously thought. This underscores the need for continued research to fully grasp the complexities of African economic development.

The economic divergence between regions, particularly in terms of real wages, reflects broader institutional dynamics that influence economic outcomes over the long run. Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share the finding that regional economic performance varies widely, with some areas experiencing stagnation while others see improvements (Bryant 2006, p.12; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This divergence can be traced to institutional factors that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Bryant (2006, p.12) highlights that European political institutions provided a supportive environment for developmental modernization, whereas Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that in China, real wages stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, indicating a lack of institutional support for economic progress (Bryant 2006, p.1; Allen et al. 2011, p.24). Feinstein (1998, p.28) further supports this view by demonstrating that real wages and the standard of living in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that institutional changes played a crucial role in this economic transformation (Feinstein 1998, p.28). The variation in real wages across regions underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks shape economic trajectories, even if this stream does not directly address the topic of institutions' role in long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Goldin (2006, p.3) show that women have historically played significant roles in economic growth in various regions and periods. Similarly, in the United States, women's increased participation in the labor force, fueled by changes in household production technology and educational opportunities, transformed economic dynamics (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts highlight the importance of institutional changes in enabling women's economic participation. This tension suggests that while institutional changes can facilitate women's economic contributions, they can also create barriers that limit their full participation. The evolution of women's roles in economic growth highlights the necessity of institutions that recognize and actively support women's economic activities.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into institutional reforms that could alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). One specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic outcomes is their role in providing incentives for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that "good" institutions matter because they are essential for creating incentives that drive investment, a key factor in development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who highlight the importance of understanding the long-term economic development of Africa, including income distribution, to assess future growth potential (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The historical persistence of poverty in Africa is not exclusively due to structural impediments, as some literature suggests. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies to show that poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon, suggesting that institutional changes can significantly alter economic trajectories (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This historical analysis implies that targeted institutional reforms could address contemporary economic challenges in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic decisions. Acemoglu and colleagues argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, which often prioritized the extraction of resources over investment and development, created a framework that hindered economic progress. This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who highlights that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further underscored by the historical analysis of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies. Frankema reconstructs these trends to show that poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon, suggesting that institutional changes can significantly alter economic trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.1). This historical analysis implies that targeted institutional reforms could address contemporary economic challenges in Africa. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The experience of East Asia, for example, demonstrates that institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Austin 2008, p.15).

Despite the shared finding that institutions are crucial for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8), there is a remaining disagreement over the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic development. Acemoglu and colleagues emphasize the role of extractive institutions in perpetuating poverty and underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), while Austin argues that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were responsive to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The historical evidence of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies, reconstructed by Frankema, settles this disagreement. This evidence shows that institutional changes can significantly alter economic trajectories, providing a clear measurement of the impact of institutions on economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.1).
