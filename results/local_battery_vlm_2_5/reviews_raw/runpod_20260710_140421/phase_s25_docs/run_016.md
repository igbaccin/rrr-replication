The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the explanatory power of institutions in understanding the divergent economic trajectories of nations. Scholars disagree over the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some arguing that institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, while others contend that geography, culture, or other factors play more significant roles. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that govern economic and political interactions. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, with some scholars arguing that institutions are shaped by economic factors, while others maintain that institutions shape economic outcomes. Another key debate revolves around the specific types of institutions that matter most for economic growth, with some focusing on property rights and the rule of law, while others emphasize the role of political institutions. A third area of contention is the historical context in which institutions matter, with some scholars arguing that institutions have always been crucial, while others maintain that their importance has varied over time. The interaction between institutions and other factors, such as geography and culture, is another critical area of debate. Understanding these issues is essential for developing effective policies to promote economic growth and reduce poverty. The central claim in this literature is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth, but the mechanisms through which they operate remain hotly debated. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of their colonies. The persistence of these institutions over time further entrenched economic disparities, as they were designed to benefit the colonizers and did not foster local economic growth. Acemoglu et al. also document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as the ability to industrialize was closely tied to the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule. The persistence of these institutions over time further entrenched economic disparities, as they were designed to benefit the colonizers and did not foster local economic growth.

In contrast, the educational legacies of colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa reveal a more nuanced picture, where the impact of colonial institutions was not uniformly detrimental (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This is evident in the way British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were closely tied to the implementation of new social and political institutions. This shift in perceived benefits was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education, for instance, was influenced by these new institutional frameworks, which provided incentives for formal education that were not present under other colonial regimes. This institutional difference had a profound impact on the development of formal education, suggesting that the long-run economic growth in these regions was influenced by the quality of education fostered under British colonial rule. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further support this by noting that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty, implying that the institutional frameworks under British rule played a crucial role in shaping long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions thus shaped the long-run economic outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) both examine how shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in factor markets during Ghana's colonial period. This shift in land availability triggered a series of institutional changes that fundamentally altered the economic landscape of colonial Ghana. The institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, was driven by the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity. This transition was a profound economic and social transformation that reshaped the colonial economy. The shift from land abundance to scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional revolution was driven by the transition from coerced to free labour, which had significant economic dimensions. The 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony played a crucial role in this transition, as it drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This legal action, coupled with the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, facilitated the shift from coerced to free labour. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that the colonial administrations' responses to these changes varied. Colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This encouragement was driven by the need for fiscal security and the desire to create a material interest in the status quo among their subjects. This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between local economic and political actions and colonial institutional prescriptions.

This shift highlights the transformative power of new economic practices, which can disrupt traditional systems and create new economic dynamics (Bohannan 1959, p.10) and Hornbeck (2010, p.32) (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). These scholars highlight how economic innovations can reshape traditional systems by introducing new forms of exchange and altering the balance of power. Bohannan (1959, p.10) noted that the introduction of money, such as brass rods, facilitated a shift from a multicentric economy to a more integrated system. This transition was driven by the need for a general-purpose currency that could function across various domains, including marriage and trade. However, the integration was not seamless, as the new currency did not fully replace traditional forms of exchange. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) provides a parallel example from the American Plains, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This technological innovation allowed for the demarcation of land and the establishment of clearer property boundaries. The introduction of barbed wire fencing was a response to the need for better protection of agricultural land, much like the introduction of money in Africa was a response to the need for a more efficient means of exchange. Both innovations illustrate how new technologies and economic practices can fundamentally alter the economic landscape, enabling more efficient and productive use of resources. The integration of these innovations into existing systems is not always straightforward. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) notes that the adoption of barbed wire fencing was not immediate and required significant adjustments in land-use practices (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This highlights the complexity of integrating new economic practices into traditional systems, where existing norms and institutions may resist change. The introduction of money in Africa similarly faced challenges, as traditional forms of exchange and social norms had to be adapted to accommodate the new currency. The integration of new economic practices into existing systems requires a nuanced understanding of the local context and the ability to navigate the complexities of traditional institutions.

By examining these historical transformations, we can gain insights into how institutions adapt and evolve in response to changing economic conditions (Bryant 2006, p.12; Goldin 2006, p.3). These transformations are driven by exogenous changes that alter the demand for labor, the supply of skilled workers, and the availability of new technologies. For instance, the increased demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century and the growth of educational institutions greatly increased the supply of potential office workers in the U.S. (Goldin 2006, p.3). These shifts were part of a broader transformation that included changes in household production technology and the availability of part-time work, which together altered female labor supply. Similarly, in Europe, the rise of the modern state and the development of capitalist institutions facilitated the accumulation of capital and the expansion of markets, which in turn drove economic growth. The impact of these institutional changes varied across regions. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that in early modern China, wage rates may serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labor in the economy as a whole. This suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, the specific outcomes depend on the local context and the nature of the institutional changes. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a range of factors that interact in different ways across regions.
