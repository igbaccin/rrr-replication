The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Scholars debate the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure economic and political interactions, drive economic performance over the long term. Two prominent positions exist: one emphasizes the impact of extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers, while the other highlights the adaptive nature of institutions in response to local economic and political conditions. The former position maintains that the endurance of extractive institutions, crafted to maximize rents for colonizers and not to foster long-term growth, has hindered economic development in many former colonies. The latter position posits that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and incentives, shaping economic outcomes in complex and context-dependent ways. This debate underscores the importance of understanding how institutions are formed, maintained, and changed, and how these processes influence economic growth. The interaction between colonial policies and local responses to these policies, including the adoption of institutional reforms, is a key area of contention. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These policies led to the establishment of varied institutional frameworks, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The differential impact of these institutions on economic outcomes is evident in the stark contrast between the economic trajectories of colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand versus those of the Congo or the Gold Coast. The former colonies, which benefited from institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, experienced higher levels of economic development. Colonies with extractive institutions encountered persistent economic underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further substantiate this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, and rule of law, all correlate strongly with economic outcomes. This institutional framework, shaped by European policies, created a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting the central role of institutions in influencing long-run development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Hopkins (2002) argue that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. The implementation of barbed wire allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated the adoption of more intensive farming practices. This technological innovation helped to mitigate the adverse effects of open-range grazing, where livestock could freely roam and cause damage to crops. The difference-in-difference estimates of the change in land-use outcomes for herd-law counties relative to non-herd law counties highlight the transformative impact of barbed wire. These counties experienced a significant increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890, a period that coincided with the widespread adoption of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period saw a 20 percentage point increase in improved farmland, underscoring the role of property rights in fostering agricultural development. The discontinuation of detailed reporting on herd laws after 1884 suggests that the impact of these laws may have been overshadowed by the more tangible benefits of barbed wire fencing (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). The introduction of barbed wire fencing boosted agricultural productivity and established a foundation for more sustainable land use practices, which were vital for long-term economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets. This transformation is evident in the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour, driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions. This transition was economically motivated as well as a response to legal changes, as chiefs sought to reduce debt by selling land to strangers for cocoa farming. The integration of British rule further facilitated market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes were not always simple. Colonial administrations often adjusted their prescriptions in response to incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions, as seen in the higher production equilibrium opted for by Governor-General Clifford (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between local economic conditions and colonial policies highlights the complex nature of institutional change in African economies. The emergence of these new institutions underscores the role of adaptive responses to economic pressures in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on African economic history highlights significant progress made despite initial skepticism, particularly focusing on precolonial production and the role of governance (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The shift in development policy towards incorporating governance and institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. This shift was driven by the recognition that "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins (2019, p.3) suggests that the history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques if it is to be fully understood. This multidimensional approach is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth in Africa. The literature does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2019, p.13). The multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa requires a nuanced understanding of the institutional frameworks that support growth.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Allen (2001, p.8) and BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) show that economic growth in early modern periods was shaped by factors such as merchant distribution, finance, and the service sector. The Great Divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with north-western Europe experiencing high productivity in its traded goods sector. This productivity was driven by merchant distribution and finance, highlighting the role of the service sector in economic growth. Allen (2001, p.8; 2001, p.23) supports this view, noting that real wages in European cities were maintained through the seventeenth century, indicating sustained economic development. However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10; 2015, p.16) qualify this perspective by emphasizing that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period. This decline was influenced by the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation, as well as colonialism. The absence of these institutions hindered India's ability to break out of the Malthusian trap, where population growth was constrained by available resources. This institutional deficit is a crucial factor in understanding the divergence in economic performance between regions.

The literature on economic performance in sub-Saharan Africa, as seen in the works of Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15), highlights the role of colonial institutions in shaping educational developments. British rule, for instance, fostered a demand for missionary education among African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework, in turn, facilitated the growth of per capita income in many Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role, their impact was not uniform and was contingent on a range of additional factors. The legacy of these institutions continues to shape the economic landscape of sub-Saharan Africa today, with enduring implications for long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping the economic landscape of sub-Saharan Africa. The establishment of extractive institutions by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, significantly impacted the development trajectory of these areas. Colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, experienced different outcomes. This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the enduring implications of colonial policies on long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The legacy of these institutions continues to influence the economic performance of sub-Saharan Africa today, with the impact of colonial policies on economic growth remaining a subject of intense interest. The differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective challenges the notion that geographic factors alone determine economic outcomes. The new economic history of Africa informs institutional reforms and helps eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous and still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The role of institutions is crucial in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa has claimed the attention of development specialists, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and incentives created by colonial rule (Frankema 2012, p.15). The origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa underscore the significance of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.1).
