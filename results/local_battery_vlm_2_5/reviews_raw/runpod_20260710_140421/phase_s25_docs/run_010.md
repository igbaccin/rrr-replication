The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental drivers of a country's prosperity over time. At stake is understanding whether institutions are the primary force behind economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The debate centers around two main positions: one argues that institutions are crucial for economic development, while the other suggests that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth. The disagreement hinges on the causal mechanisms by which institutions affect economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth. This view is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who note that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. This stream of scholarship emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, suggesting that the type of institutions established during colonial rule has long-lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The differential mortality rates experienced by Europeans in various colonies led to the establishment of distinct institutional frameworks. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run economic growth. In contrast, in regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with extractive institutions hindering long-run economic growth. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered a more positive environment for education compared to other colonial powers. They note that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. This institutional shift was driven by the demand for missionary education, which depended on cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels. These calculations were influenced by the new institutions introduced by the British, which created incentives for investing in education. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) extend this argument by reconstructing real wage levels and trends in British African colonies, showing that the long-term economic development in these regions was shaped by the institutional framework established during colonial rule. Their analysis highlights the importance of understanding the distribution of income and the long-term picture of economic development in assessing future growth possibilities. This perspective suggests that the institutional differences shaped by colonial rule had a lasting impact on long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa, influencing not only education but also broader economic outcomes.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) jointly establish. These changes were driven by the need to adapt to new economic realities, such as the decline in land availability and the increasing demand for labour. This adaptation process is evident in the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour, which transformed the economic landscape of regions like Ghana. For instance, the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony, along with subsequent legal actions, facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This transition had profound economic implications, as it altered the dynamics of labour markets and encouraged the development of new economic activities. The institutional changes were not uniform across all African economies. The presence of European settlers, for example, influenced the direction of institutional evolution. Where settlers were absent, colonial governments often supported indigenous commodity production, which in turn shaped the institutional framework in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions in shaping institutional outcomes. The institutional changes driven by the shift from land abundance to scarcity thus had a profound impact on the long-run economic growth of African economies, as they adapted to new economic realities and colonial influences.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) both show that the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. This development allowed farmers to invest more confidently in their land, knowing that their improvements would not be easily destroyed by wandering livestock. The adoption of barbed wire fencing was a critical institutional change that facilitated agricultural growth by addressing a key externality problem. The herd laws, which regulated livestock movement, had limited impact before the introduction of barbed wire. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) notes that herd-law counties experienced insignificant changes in land improvement before 1880, but saw a substantial 20 percentage-point increase from 1880 to 1890, after barbed wire became widely available. This shift underscores the importance of effective property rights enforcement in driving agricultural investment and growth. The impact of these institutional changes was not uniform across regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight that in African colonies, the introduction of similar property rights institutions was often shaped by colonial policies and local economic conditions, leading to varied outcomes. This suggests that while property rights are crucial for agricultural development, their effectiveness depends on the broader institutional and economic context.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Austin and Broadberry, find that African economies had achieved significant growth by the early twentieth century, with levels of per capita income above subsistence in many Anglophone African economies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This growth was tied to the development of foreign trade and export markets, which varied in timing and scale across regions and was influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The development of export production was a key driver of economic growth in many African economies, highlighting the role of trade in shaping long-run economic performance. However, BroadberryEtal argue that the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation hindered the transition out of the Malthusian trap, where population growth is constrained by limited resources (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This suggests that while trade and export markets were crucial for economic growth, the absence of supportive institutions limited the potential for sustained development. The absence of institutions to support investment and innovation was a significant barrier to long-run economic growth in African economies.

Akyeampong and Fofack's work on the role of women in African economies, together with Bryceson's insights on social change (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bryceson 2002, p.2) indicates that the marginalization of women, particularly in relation to land access and legal rights, has significantly shaped economic outcomes in Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack point out that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that privileged men. This patriarchal alliance, as Akyeampong and Fofack note, was a key avenue for the codification of the subordinate status of women in the colonial era. The implication is that the economic contributions of women were systematically undervalued, which in turn limited the overall economic potential of African societies. Bryceson's analysis of depeasantization and social change in rural Africa adds depth to this understanding. Bryceson shows that economic restructuring has led to deep-rooted social changes, including the increased economic activity of women. This is evident in Malawi, where women's non-agricultural activities, such as selling food snacks and brewing beer, became significant income sources. The downstream implication is that the economic restructuring in Africa has led to a shift in gender dynamics, with women taking on more economic roles. However, Bryceson also notes that this shift is often driven by economic necessity rather than empowerment, qualifying the extent to which these changes represent genuine economic progress for women.

The literature on African economic history and development explores the complexities of economic growth in Africa without directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). This focus on institutions is crucial because it underscores their role in fostering economic development. This suggests that the current understanding of institutions' role in economic growth may be incomplete, as it often overlooks precolonial economic structures. Hopkins further illustrates that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation, indicating that other institutional factors are at play. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is not uniformly distributed across Africa, and there may be specific institutional arrangements that are more conducive to growth. Hopkins emphasizes the need for multiple approaches and different techniques to fully understand the history of economic development, suggesting that a comprehensive analysis of institutions' role in long-run economic growth requires a holistic view that includes precolonial economic structures. This highlights the importance of considering the historical context when examining the role of institutions in economic growth.

Institutional arrangements play a crucial role in determining long-run economic growth, with historical context shaping the specific institutions that emerge in different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) show that European colonizers established varying institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settler presence, leading to divergent economic trajectories in former colonies. These institutional differences, such as the presence or absence of secure property rights and rule of law, significantly influence economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, experienced lower income per capita. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic development. Furthermore, the role of institutions in economic growth is not uniform across Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) emphasizes that institutional quality, rather than ethnic fragmentation, is crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. However, the specific institutional arrangements that foster growth may vary, as shown by the differing experiences of former colonies with varying mortality rates and settler presences (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical context, including precolonial economic structures, is essential in understanding how institutions influence economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This calls for a nuanced analysis of the institutional legacies of colonialism and their impact on contemporary economic development. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the measurement of income per capita in former colonies, which shows that those with extractive institutions have lower economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
