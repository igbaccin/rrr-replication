The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure political, economic, and social interactions, are widely acknowledged to influence economic outcomes. The live disagreement revolves around the specific mechanisms through which institutions affect growth. Some scholars emphasize the importance of institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts, arguing that these are crucial for encouraging investment and economic activity. Others focus on the historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve, suggesting that the path dependence and endogenous nature of institutional change are critical factors. Acemoglu et al. argue that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varied institutions in colonies, with extractive institutions in high-mortality areas significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin posits that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). The debate centers on whether institutions are exogenous factors that drive economic growth or endogenous outcomes shaped by historical, political, and social dynamics. This question is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth encompasses both present factors and historical legacies that continue to influence economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, contra geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, suggesting that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have persisted and continue to shape economic outcomes in post-colonial societies. This historical context is crucial for understanding the current economic disparities among former colonies, as it underscores the importance of institutional legacies in shaping long-run economic growth. The enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development is not limited to the economic sphere; it also extends to social and political institutions. This historical perspective is essential for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, as it highlights the need to consider the historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve.

The Europeans' approach to colonization varied widely, leading to the establishment of different institutional frameworks. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, long-run economic growth was robust. This institutional divergence was driven by the Europeans' strategic responses to local conditions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework, characterized by extractive practices, hindered economic development in many former colonies. These findings highlight the enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, underscoring the need to consider the historical context of institutional development when analyzing long-run economic growth.

This historical influence extends to the American Plains, where the introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated long-term investments in agriculture. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates that the adoption of barbed wire led to a notable increase in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a significant rise in the improved fraction of farmland post-1880. This development is crucial because it highlights how changes in property rights institutions can directly influence economic outcomes. Domar (1970, p.4) complements this by showing that the scarcity of labor can drive the development of institutions like serfdom, where land ownership is concentrated in the hands of a non-working class. This institutional setup, while different from the American context, underscores the broader point that property rights and labor scarcity are critical factors in shaping economic development. Domar (1970, p.4) also notes that the increase in population in Western Europe by the end of the thirteenth century coincided with the decline of serfdom. This historical pattern suggests that as labor becomes more abundant, the institutional framework may evolve to accommodate this change, potentially leading to more inclusive economic growth.

Similarly, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2007, p.20) both highlight that this transition was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal in this transition, although they were not direct responses to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) further notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). The extent to which these institutional changes led to long-term economic growth is qualified by the broader context of colonial rule and its market-integrating effects (Austin 2007, p.4). The imposition and extension of British rule in Ghana, for instance, removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade, facilitating market integration (Austin 2007, p.4). This suggests that while localised institutional changes were crucial, the broader colonial framework also played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The interplay between local and colonial institutions highlights the complex nature of institutional change in African economies.

This transformation is further evidenced by the introduction of money into African subsistence economies, which has led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures, highlighting the transformative impact of monetary systems on traditional economic frameworks (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). The integration of the Tiv economy into the broader world market, facilitated by colonial infrastructure, exemplifies this shift (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration disrupted traditional economic institutions, aligning them with global economic systems. This transition presented challenges. The introduction of money as a medium of exchange did not immediately replace existing economic institutions; instead, it coexisted with them, fulfilling specific functions such as payment but not others like marriage or food exchange (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The coexistence of traditional and modern economic systems underscores the complexity of institutional change in African economies. This complexity is further highlighted by the social changes accompanying economic restructuring. In Malawi and Tanzania, for instance, women have increasingly taken on income-earning roles outside the home, reflecting broader social transformations driven by economic necessity (Bryceson 2002, p.8). These changes illustrate how economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social change, including depeasantization, and not just economic shifts.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) jointly establish that the quality of institutions in Africa is not merely a function of ethnic fragmentation or colonial legacies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that "good" institutions provide essential incentives for investment, which is vital for development. Frankema (2012, p.15) elaborates on this by showing that the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities. This suggests that institutional reforms, particularly in education, can drive economic growth by fostering a skilled workforce and enhancing productivity. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by noting that the scale and timing of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This underscores the importance of tailored institutional reforms that consider local contexts and endowments.

The dynamics of technological advance and social change play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, as Abramovitz and Feinstein (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Feinstein 1998, p.28) both show. Technological advance, in particular, drives productivity growth and can alter the relative positions of countries in the global productivity scale. This dynamic is not straightforward; it is influenced by a country's ability to absorb and implement new technologies, which Abramovitz (1986, p.21) refers to as "social capability" (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). Feinstein (1998, p.28) illustrates this point through the Industrial Revolution in Britain, where technological advancements led to significant improvements in real wages and living standards. This period saw substantial economic growth driven by technological innovations, which in turn fostered broader social changes. The relationship between technological advance and economic growth is not linear. Feinstein (1998, p.28) notes that while technological progress is a key driver, other factors such as war and economic policies can significantly impact growth trajectories (Feinstein 1998, p.5). This underscores the multifaceted nature of economic growth, where technological advance is just one piece of a larger puzzle that includes institutional and social factors.

This multifaceted nature is evident in the great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia, which began well before the 1800s, with north-western Europe experiencing significant economic growth during the early modern period. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Allen (2001, p.8) argue that this growth was driven by the service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, which played a crucial role in economic development. This economic growth was not evenly distributed; skilled laborers benefited more than unskilled laborers, indicating that the fruits of early modern development were concentrated among certain segments of society. Allen (2001, p.23) notes that the maintenance of high real wages in European cities after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century was a decisive break with the past. This pattern of economic performance, where certain regions and groups experienced sustained growth while others did not, highlights the role of institutional changes in facilitating investment and productivity gains. The specific mechanisms by which these institutional changes occurred and their impact on different economic sectors remain complex and multifaceted. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) point out that while high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, the service sector's role in economic growth was crucial. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the specific institutional factors that contributed to economic divergence.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, which is consistent with the view that economic growth in these regions was influenced by institutional factors. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied owing to a range of factors, including political institutions. This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) who argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. These strategies included setting up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole, or establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This highlights the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as different institutional frameworks led to varying outcomes in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions were likely designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. This suggests that the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. The precise historical evidence of these institutional changes and their impact on economic growth is shown by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) who note that the patterns of growing and shrinking were tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports. This demonstrates that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
