The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central question in economic history and development studies. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can illuminate why some regions have prospered while others have stagnated. A key disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance, arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and the rule of law are crucial for sustained growth. Others point to the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development, suggesting that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by a complex interplay of political, social, and economic forces. The debate hinges on whether institutions are exogenous determinants of economic growth or endogenous outcomes of deeper historical and structural factors. This interplay is central to understanding the long-run economic trajectories of regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, differed from those in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where settlers implemented the rule of law and fostered investment. These extractive institutions, which aimed to maximize rents for European colonists, had lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that in regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they were less likely to settle permanently and more likely to implement extractive institutions that facilitated rapid resource extraction. This institutional framework, characterized by a lack of property rights and rule of law, hindered long-term economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity, which shaped the institutional landscape of the colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interplay between colonial policies, institutional design, and long-run economic outcomes underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This technological innovation facilitated the enforcement of property rights, which in turn reduced externalities from livestock damage. The mechanism through which barbed wire enhanced agricultural development was by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage. Domar (1970, p.4) complements this by highlighting that the scarcity of labor can drive the development of serfdom, where landowners secure labor by tying workers to the land. This pattern suggests that the effectiveness of property rights institutions is contingent on the availability of labor and the ability to enforce these rights. Domar (1970, p.4) observes that the development of serfdom is not solely an economic phenomenon; government policies that facilitate the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners can also influence it. Therefore, while property rights are crucial for economic development, their effectiveness can be modulated by broader institutional frameworks and labor market conditions. This interplay between property rights, technological innovation, and labor availability underscores the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, a shared finding of Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). These scholars argue that the historical trajectory of African economic development, particularly under colonial rule, provides valuable insights into the institutional factors that can drive growth and poverty reduction. One specific mechanism highlighted is the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Hopkins emphasizes that the quality of public goods and the rule of law are crucial for economic development, regardless of ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who note that the reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies can offer a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This assessment is crucial for understanding the structural impediments that may have hindered economic growth in the past and for identifying the institutional reforms that could facilitate future growth. The implication is that historical data on real wages and economic conditions can guide policy makers in designing effective institutions that promote investment and development. Hopkins underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Therefore, understanding the historical context of African economic development can help in designing institutional reforms that address the root causes of poverty and promote sustainable growth.

Austin's analysis of Ghana during 1874-1939 demonstrates that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to significant transformations in factor markets, including the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The institutional changes in African economies, particularly in Ghana, were driven by the need to adapt to changing factor scarcities, which in turn shaped the development of markets for land and labour. Austin notes that the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and subsequent prohibitions on slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal in transitioning from coerced to free labour. This transition had profound economic implications, as it compelled Akyem chiefs to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming, thereby fostering market integration (Austin 2007, p.20). The imposition and extension of British rule also played a crucial role in market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes highlight the dynamic nature of economic development in African economies, where localised scarcities and colonial policies interacted to shape factor markets. This interaction underscores the importance of understanding historical institutional changes in designing effective policies for sustainable economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that the economic performance of African and Asian economies has been influenced by specific historical and economic factors, including the role of colonial rule and the development of educational institutions. Colonial rule, particularly British rule, played a significant role in shaping the economic trajectories of these regions. This influence extended beyond mere economic exploitation to include the establishment of educational systems. Frankema (2012, p.1) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. These calculations were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule. The impact of these institutional changes was not uniform across regions; the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, owing to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The development of educational institutions under colonial rule had long-term implications for economic growth, as it laid the foundation for a skilled workforce and the dissemination of knowledge. However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) note that while educational development was crucial, the absence of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation limited the economic benefits of these educational gains. The lack of these institutions meant that many regions remained trapped in low productivity and stagnant economic growth, despite the educational advancements made during the colonial period.

Bryant (2006, p.12) and other scholars (Bryant 2006, p.12; Allen 2001, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) examine the historical roots of economic divergence, challenging Eurocentric views and highlighting the complex social formations that influenced global hegemony. They argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with institutional changes playing a crucial role in this process. For instance, Bryant (2006, p.12) critiques the revisionist position that dismisses Eurocentric views, asserting that European political institutions provided an advantageous setting for developmental modernization. As a result, the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled labourers benefiting more than unskilled labourers. Long-term economic growth in Europe was not a smooth, continuous process; it was marked by significant fluctuations and periods of stagnation.

Women played a pivotal role in economic development, both historically and contemporarily, as evidenced in the U.S. and Africa. Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) share the finding that women's contributions to economic growth were significant. Goldin (2006, p.3) describes how shifts in the U.S. economy from the early twentieth century onwards created new opportunities for women, driven by increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. This transformation was further catalyzed by changes in household production technology and institutional changes that facilitated part-time work and removed barriers to the employment of married women. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land for cash crops. This institutional change had a profound impact on women's economic contributions, as it constrained their ability to participate fully in the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast a leading producer of cocoa. Goldin (2006, p.10) further illustrates this pattern in the U.S., noting that women began to pursue higher education in professional and graduate schools around 1970, marking a significant shift in their educational and economic opportunities. This educational advancement paralleled the growth of women's participation in the workforce, suggesting that institutional changes in education and employment policies were crucial in enhancing women's economic contributions. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus evident in the ways they facilitated or hindered women's participation in the economy.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both highlight that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers in regions with low mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.18) underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the ways they facilitated or hindered women's participation in the economy. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that codified the subordinate status of women through customary law, reinforcing their marginalization (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional change highlights how policies can either enhance or constrain economic opportunities. The institutional framework set by colonial powers, as Frankema shows, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This educational advancement paralleled the growth of women's participation in the workforce, suggesting that institutional changes in education and employment policies were crucial in enhancing women's economic contributions.

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) is the difference between the types of institutions established in regions with low versus high mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established in regions with low mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial institutions were shaped by the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The differing impacts of these institutions on long-run economic growth are evident in the varying economic trajectories of former colonies.
