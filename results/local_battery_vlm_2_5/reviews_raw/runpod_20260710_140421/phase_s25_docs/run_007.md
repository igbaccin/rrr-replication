The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the extent to which institutional frameworks drive economic development. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over whether institutions are the primary determinants of economic growth or if they are merely one factor among many. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social and economic interactions, are seen by some as fundamental to economic success, while others argue that geographic factors, cultural norms, or technological advancements play equally significant roles. This debate is crucial because understanding the drivers of economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies, potentially lifting billions out of poverty. One key position in this debate is that institutions designed to protect property rights and enforce contracts are essential for fostering economic growth. This perspective is supported by evidence showing that countries with strong institutional frameworks tend to experience higher levels of economic development. Another perspective posits that institutions' influence on economic growth is complex, with historical and contextual factors significantly shaping institutional effectiveness. This debate highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They show that European colonizers established different institutions depending on their mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions and low mortality encouraging investment-friendly institutions. This institutional divergence is crucial because it directly shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states prioritizing swift resource transfer to the metropole. The implications of these institutional legacies are profound, as they highlight how initial colonial policies can have enduring effects on economic development.

Austin (2007, 2008) argue that shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in Ghana, highlighting how specific historical contexts shaped economic institutions (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu et al.'s formulations. Austin also notes that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. The colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, and tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, which was tied tightly to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This economic growth was facilitated by the integration of markets. Austin notes that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' (Austin 2007, p.4). This suggests that the institutional changes in Ghana were not solely driven by colonial policies but were also influenced by local economic and political dynamics.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows that the adoption of barbed wire led to a substantial increase in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a notable 20 percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890. This change was a direct result of technological innovation enabling more effective property delineation, not merely a continuation of pre-existing trends. This suggests that in regions where labor is scarce, the introduction of technologies that enhance property protection can catalyze significant economic development. Domar (1970, p.4) also notes that the effectiveness of such institutions depends on the broader economic and social context. For instance, the decline of serfdom in Western Europe by the end of the thirteenth century coincided with an increase in population, indicating that demographic changes can influence the sustainability of property rights institutions. This implies that while technological advancements like barbed wire can facilitate economic growth by protecting property rights, the long-term success of these institutions depends on broader economic and demographic factors.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Allen (2001, p.8) both note that the economic growth of northwestern Europe during the early modern period was driven by the service sector, which facilitated the distribution and finance of goods. This dynamic is evident in the high productivity of the traded goods sector, which contributed to increased living standards for skilled labourers. The service sector's role in economic growth during this period mirrors its importance in the modern era. The reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates further reinforced this effect. Parthasarathi's explanation of high productivity in southern Indian agriculture presents logical difficulties. Parthasarathi claims that high levels of investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries led to both high levels of economic development and low silver wages. In northwestern Europe's developing regions, institutional change typically drove agricultural investment, leading to high agricultural labor productivity. This suggests that the maintenance of incomes in European cities was not solely due to institutional changes but was also influenced by broader economic and social factors.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Bryant (2006, p.12) jointly emphasise that economic growth and technological advancement are complex processes influenced by various factors, including social change and technological dynamics. This complexity is further underscored by the role of institutions, which, though not explicitly highlighted as a fundamental cause by Abramovitz and Bryant, are implicitly recognised as part of the broader social and technological dynamics that drive economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) identifies a country's social capability with technical competence and its political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, suggesting that these institutional factors are crucial for mobilising capital and managing large-scale enterprise. The implication is that strong institutional frameworks can facilitate technological advance and social change, thereby promoting economic growth. Bryant (2006, p.12) warns that institutions' role in economic growth is not straightforward. The revisionist position, which challenges Eurocentric views, questions the alleged historical differences in political institutions between the East and West, suggesting that the institutional advantages of Western countries may have been overstated. This perspective qualifies the idea that institutions are a straightforward driver of economic growth, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth. This underscores the importance of considering the broader social and technological context in which institutions operate.

Feinstein (1998, p.28) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) jointly argue that real wages and standards of living improved during and after the Industrial Revolution in Britain, and that wage rates can be a reasonable proxy for average earnings. One specific mechanism for this improvement is the vibrant and active labor market, particularly for short-term labor, which allowed for greater economic mobility and integration between urban and rural areas. This dynamic labor market facilitated the movement of workers to areas of higher productivity, contributing to overall economic growth. The extent of regional migration and the precise proportion of day laborers remain unclear. The existence of a high degree of integration between labor markets in commercialized regions near major urban centers further supports the idea that wage rates can serve as a proxy for average earnings. This integration allowed for a more efficient allocation of labor resources, which in turn contributed to economic growth. This pattern of economic improvement and integration of labor markets highlights the complex interplay between economic factors and institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonies with high mortality rates developed extractive institutions that hindered economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework limited the potential for economic growth in these regions. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional factors were crucial in determining economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories, even in regions that were not initially characterized by high levels of economic development.

The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which had lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continued to influence economic outcomes long after the colonial period, underscoring the enduring impact of institutions on long-run economic growth. Austin presents evidence that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting that institutions evolved in response to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive nature of institutions further emphasizes their role in shaping economic growth.

The historical evidence from Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates the institutional revolution in factor markets marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor (Austin 2007, p.20). This shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to significant changes in the economic landscape, highlighting the dynamic role of institutions in responding to changing economic conditions. This institutional revolution in Ghana shows how institutions can adapt to changing economic conditions and contribute to long-run economic growth. Austin also shows that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, which further supports the idea that institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes (Austin 2007, p.4).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the empirical comparison of European colonies with different institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that colonies with extractive institutions had lower levels of economic development compared to those with institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that institutional factors were crucial in determining economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This historical evidence and empirical comparison settle the disagreement by showing that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment lead to higher levels of economic development.
