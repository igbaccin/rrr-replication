The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it directly addresses the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are widely recognised as critical factors in economic outcomes. A lively debate exists over the causal pathways through which institutions influence growth. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, particularly those that were extractive, have had enduring effects on economic development. Others posit that the impact of institutions is more nuanced, shaped by complex historical and contextual factors that go beyond simple colonial legacies. Furthermore, the role of specific groups, such as women, and the influence of colonial powers like Britain, add layers of complexity to this debate. Understanding these dynamics is essential for formulating effective policies aimed at fostering economic growth in regions that have historically lagged behind. The interplay between institutions and economic growth underscores the need for a comprehensive analysis that considers both the historical context and the contemporary implications of institutional frameworks. This interplay is evident in the arguments presented by Acemoglu et al. and Austin. Acemoglu et al. assert that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, which had lasting impacts on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin, on the other hand, argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans established different types of institutions in their colonies, which had lasting impacts on income per capita. These institutions were shaped by the mortality rates of European settlers; in areas where mortality was high, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where mortality was low, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This difference in institutional design had profound effects on economic development. For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, the establishment of inclusive institutions led to sustained economic growth. In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions impeded long-run economic development. This pattern is further supported by Acemoglu et al.'s finding that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional framework highlights the critical role of colonial policies in shaping economic trajectories, as the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity, which in turn affected the type of institutions established. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impact, suggesting that while colonial institutions were pivotal, their effects were mediated by the specific economic and political contexts of each colony.

The introduction of new institutions, such as barbed wire fencing and money, into traditional subsistence economies led to significant economic and social transformations (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Bohannan 1959, p.10). This institutional change allowed for a more efficient use of land and resources, as farmers could now invest in improvements knowing their property was secure. The new road network and increased trade brought by the introduction of money facilitated a transition towards a market-based economy, altering traditional exchange systems and social structures. This shift highlights how new institutions can drive long-run economic growth by facilitating trade and integrating local economies into broader markets. Bryceson (2002, p.2) qualifies this by showing that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social changes, including depeasantization, which challenges views that focus solely on economic shifts. These social transformations underscore the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic growth, suggesting that while new institutions can drive growth, they also catalyze significant social changes that must be managed for sustainable development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, highlighting the need for deeper understanding of historical economic paths beyond structural impediments. They argue that understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including income distribution, is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This approach underscores the importance of historical context in shaping contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.15) supports this perspective by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic interplay between local agency and colonial policies. This interaction suggests that the colonial experience varied based on local conditions and responses. This variability also points to the complexities of institutional change, as the same policies could yield different outcomes depending on the specific context. The nuanced understanding of these historical paths is essential for comprehending the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economies.

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) and Allen (2001, p.8) examine the economic divergence between Asia and Europe, focusing on the impact of colonialism and pre-colonial economic conditions in India and other regions. They argue that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This divergence can be attributed to the differing economic conditions and institutional changes in these regions. For instance, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. Allen (2001, p.23) supports this by noting that the maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders, such as Britain, was a decisive break with the past. This indicates that institutional changes, such as those in the service sector, were critical in driving economic growth. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) further qualifies this by pointing out that India lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation that allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap. This implies that the absence of such institutions in India contributed to its economic decline relative to Britain.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Goldin (2006, p.3) show that women's economic roles are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth and development. This is evident in both historical African and American contexts. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that the codification of customary law under colonial rule in Africa marginalized women's access to land, which was crucial for cash crop farming. This institutional change had significant economic implications, as it restricted women's ability to participate in the economic revolution driven by cash crops. Goldin (2006, p.3) complements this by noting that in the U.S., shifts in women's employment were driven by exogenous changes such as increased demand for female office workers and growth in educational institutions. These institutional changes altered female labor supply and made part-time work more accessible, thereby integrating women more fully into the economy. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) further elaborate that patriarchal alliances and customary law reinforced women's subordinate status, which had profound effects on their economic contributions. This institutional framework limited women's economic activities and shaped broader economic dynamics. The downstream implication of these institutional changes is that women's economic roles are deeply intertwined with long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the transformations in both Africa and the U.S. These institutional changes underscore the importance of considering gender dynamics in economic development.

Domar (1970, p.4) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) discuss various factors influencing economic growth, such as labor scarcity and social capability, but do not explicitly identify institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Domar's (1970, p.4) analysis of labor scarcity and its impact on economic structures, such as the emergence of slavery or serfdom, highlights the role of economic forces in shaping institutions. This institutional framework can influence broader economic dynamics and growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Abramovitz (1986, p.5) argue that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. These factors encompass social and institutional dynamics in addition to economic capabilities. The institutional changes and social capabilities can act as catalysts for economic growth, as they can mobilize resources and foster technological advancements. This broader view of social capability underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth. The downstream implication of these institutional changes is that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with various factors, including labor scarcity, social capability, and technological advancements.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that institutional changes in Africa, particularly in Ghana during 1874-1939, were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity. The integration of markets within Ghana, facilitated by the imposition and extension of British rule, removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade, further enhancing economic activity (Austin 2007, p.4). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This economic transformation was a result of broader social and political shifts in addition to institutional changes. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutional changes, driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity, led to significant economic transformations in Africa. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is deeply intertwined with various factors, including land scarcity, social capability, and technological advancements.

The literature on African economic history highlights significant progress made despite initial skepticism, particularly focusing on precolonial production and the role of governance (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). The new economic history of Africa has gained traction by demonstrating the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. In particular, the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is crucial for development. This is because institutions that promote good governance and the rule of law are essential for fostering an environment conducive to economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the capability of institutions to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive policies. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.3), who notes that the history of economic development in Africa requires multiple approaches and different techniques to be fully understood. The implication is that a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics is necessary to appreciate the complex interplay between governance and economic growth. This suggests that a comprehensive analysis of African economic history must consider both the historical context and the evolving role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonial rule, where European powers established varying institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that settler colonies, such as the United States and Australia, developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas extractive states in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This differential institutional development had lasting impacts on current income per capita, underscoring the profound influence of colonial institutions on economic trajectories. The persistence of these institutions highlights their role in shaping economic outcomes over the long term, as extractive institutions in former colonies continue to hinder growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) further elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change under colonial rule. This adaptation process reveals that institutional frameworks evolved in response to local conditions and pressures, further emphasizing the role of institutions in economic development.

The institutional legacies of colonialism are also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This institutional perspective is crucial for understanding why some former colonies have experienced sustained economic growth while others have lagged behind. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, showing that institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This underscores the need for institutional reforms that can foster economic growth and eliminate poverty, particularly among the bottom billion people in Africa. The new economic history of Africa, with its focus on institutions, offers valuable insights into the historical processes that have shaped contemporary economic outcomes.

The contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods further illustrate the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) demonstrate that women made significant contributions to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role. This perspective is crucial for understanding the broader impact of institutional frameworks on economic development, as it highlights the role of gender dynamics in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema (2012, p.15; 2012, p.1) adds that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, showing that the institutional legacies of colonialism can have varied impacts on economic development. This varied impact underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the differential impacts of colonial institutions on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, particularly the role of local adaptations and gender dynamics. Austin (2008, p.15) highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, showing that institutional change is dynamic and responsive to local conditions. This dynamic nature of institutional change is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between governance and economic growth. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the adaptation of institutional frameworks in response to local conditions, as shown by the varied impacts of colonial rule on economic development in different regions. This adaptation process reveals that institutional frameworks evolve in response to local pressures, further emphasizing the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.
