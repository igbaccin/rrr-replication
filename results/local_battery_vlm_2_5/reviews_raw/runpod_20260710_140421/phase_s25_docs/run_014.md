Understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal for comprehending the divergent paths of development among nations. At the heart of this debate lies the question: what are the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes over extended periods? Two primary positions vie for attention. One camp posits that institutions, particularly those that enforce secure property rights and the rule of law, are the bedrock of sustained economic growth. These scholars argue that the presence or absence of such institutions can determine whether a society thrives or stagnates. The opposing camp, however, contends that the impact of institutions on long-term economic growth is more nuanced. These scholars suggest that institutions alone do not guarantee economic success; instead, they interact with a myriad of other factors, including geography, culture, and historical contingencies, to shape economic trajectories. This interplay underscores the complexity of economic development and highlights the need for a more holistic understanding of institutional effects. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. This influence was mediated through the type of institutions established by European colonizers, which varied based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic development and persisted long after colonial rule ended (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Where Europeans settled in large numbers, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. This variation in institutional frameworks, driven by initial colonization strategies, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, has been a critical factor in shaping the current economic landscapes of many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by demonstrating that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, consistently show the impact of these colonial legacies. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, remains a key determinant of economic outcomes in many regions today.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This technological innovation allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated the adoption of more intensive farming practices. Specifically, the use of barbed wire enabled farmers to implement the herd law, which required livestock owners to fence their animals rather than allowing them to roam freely. This shift was crucial because it reduced the damage caused by stray livestock, thereby increasing the productivity of agricultural land. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) demonstrates that the adoption of the herd law, facilitated by barbed wire, led to a substantial increase in improved farmland. Domar (1970, p.4) qualifies this by noting that the effectiveness of such institutional changes depends on the broader economic context, particularly the availability of labor. When labor is scarce, as it often was in the American Plains during this period, the benefits of improved property rights are amplified. This interplay between technological innovation and institutional change highlights how effective property rights can drive economic development, even in challenging environmental conditions. The introduction of barbed wire fencing protected individual farmers' investments and laid the groundwork for more sustainable and productive agricultural practices, which were essential for the long-term economic growth of the region.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that the institutional landscape in colonial Africa evolved significantly in response to changes in factor endowments. In Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity triggered an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets for land use rights and hired labour, driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and subsequent prohibitions on slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 played crucial roles in this transition, facilitating the shift from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.20). The colonial administration's removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast further integrated markets, enhancing economic efficiency (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional adaptation was a result of indigenous economic and political actions that influenced colonial policies. The Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis, illustrates how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay between local economic actions and colonial policies highlights the complex nature of institutional change in colonial Africa, where the emergence of new markets and labour practices was driven by both internal and external factors.

The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). One specific mechanism through which historical understanding can inform policy is by identifying the long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. This focus on institutions aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) emphasis on understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which can provide insights into the long-term picture of economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that institutional quality can vary independently of ethnic composition. The implication is that institutional reforms must be tailored to the specific historical and geographical contexts of different African regions to be effective in reducing poverty.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before the 19th century, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gupta and Allen et al. (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This divergence is partially explained by the role of merchant distribution and finance in north-western Europe, which drove productivity in the traded goods sector (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This regional variation in economic growth highlights the importance of service sectors in early modern economic development. The economic landscape of India, for instance, was marked by a lack of state institutions necessary for underpinning investment and innovation, which Broadberry et al. identify as crucial for breaking out of the Malthusian trap (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This institutional void contributed to stagnating real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The implications of these findings suggest that the absence of robust institutional frameworks can constrain economic growth, even in regions with significant agricultural productivity.

Bryant challenges the traditional Eurocentric narrative of historical progress, arguing that the role of women in economic development has been underestimated (Bryant 2006, p.12; Goldin 2006, p.3). This underestimation is particularly evident in the shifts from one phase to the next in women's employment, education, and family dynamics. These shifts were driven by various exogenous changes, such as the increased relative demand for female office workers in the early twentieth century, and the growth of educational institutions at the secondary level from the 1910s to the 1940s. These changes made "nice" jobs available and greatly increased the supply of potential office workers. Changes in household production technology from the 1920s to the 1940s also altered female labor supply, as did institutional changes that made part-time work readily available and dispensed with barriers to the employment of married women. Goldin shows that women also began to further their education in professional and graduate schools around 1970. Whereas in the late 1960s one in 20 entering law school was a woman, two out of five were in 1980, and parity was reached in the 1870s and 1880s. This educational advancement highlights the transformative power of institutional changes in shaping women's economic roles, illustrating the complex interplay between societal progress and institutional frameworks.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and geographical contexts in which they operate. European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to distinct institutional frameworks across colonies. In some cases, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in colonial institutions directly influenced the long-run development trajectories of these regions, with settler colonies generally experiencing more robust economic growth. The persistence of these institutional legacies is evident in current income per capita levels, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These findings are supported by the broader historical context, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional influence is further underscored by the fact that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from the colonial era demonstrates that institutions designed to maximize short-term gains often came at the expense of long-term economic development, illustrating the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes.

The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have had lasting effects on the economic development of African nations. European colonization strategies often led to the establishment of extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic growth. The interaction between colonial institutions and local economic conditions played a crucial role in shaping the long-run development of African colonies. This is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, which was closely linked to industrialization and the institutional changes introduced during the colonial period (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical record shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability in institutional frameworks highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development. The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is influenced by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This underscores the importance of institutional quality in driving economic development and reducing poverty.

The historical divergence in economic development between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. The high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, highlighting the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers in northwestern Europe yielding a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. This economic divergence resulted from high levels of investment in agriculture during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The institutional changes that brought about investment in agriculture led to high agricultural labor productivity, further reinforcing the economic divergence between Europe and Asia. The persistence of poverty in Africa is a consequence of historical and institutional factors that have shaped long-term economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule provides a deeper understanding of the determinants of the real wage divergence across British Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This historical evidence shows that the long-term economic development of African nations is closely tied to the institutional frameworks established during the colonial period.
