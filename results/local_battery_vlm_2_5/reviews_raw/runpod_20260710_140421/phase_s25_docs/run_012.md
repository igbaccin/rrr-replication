The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense debate among economists and historians. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic development over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, with geographic factors or other variables also playing a role. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights, is crucial for sustained economic development. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors and that geographic endowments, cultural factors, or other variables play equally significant roles. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) both address the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, but from different perspectives. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable development.

The impact of colonialism on long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27). They propose that European colonizers established different institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced, leading to a dichotomy between colonies with extractive institutions and those with more inclusive ones. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, did not encourage long-run economic development. This institutional divergence was a critical factor in shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this reversal to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, suggesting that the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is nuanced and multifaceted.

Austin's analysis of Ghana's institutional evolution reveals that shifts in land abundance and scarcity during the 19th and early 20th centuries were pivotal in shaping the country's factor markets. Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) demonstrate that the transition from land abundance to scarcity led to the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, fundamentally altering Ghana's economic landscape. This institutional revolution was driven by the need to adapt to changing factor scarcities, as localised scarcity incentivised chiefs to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming (Austin 2007, p.20). The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 further facilitated this transition, although these acts were not initially driven by institutional innovation (Austin 2007, p.20). The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule also played a crucial role in market integration, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what would become Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) further argues that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, highlighting the adaptive nature of colonial institutions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that colonial institutions responded to local economic and political dynamics, which in turn shaped long-run economic growth. The complexity of these interactions underscores the need for nuanced analyses that consider the specific historical and institutional contexts of different regions. This nuanced approach is necessary for understanding the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) suggest that British colonial rule in Africa was more favorable for the development of formal education compared to other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education in Africa was driven by cost-benefit analyses at both macro and micro levels, influenced by new social and political institutions introduced by colonial rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. This dynamic highlights how the introduction of new institutions can reshape local incentives and behaviors, ultimately impacting long-run economic growth. Poverty in Africa persists due to more than just structural impediments. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon. This perspective challenges recent literature that emphasizes structural impediments as the primary cause of African poverty.

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the economic performance of India and African economies has been characterized by significant periods of growth and decline, closely tied to external factors such as foreign trade and export markets. The economic performance of these regions was influenced by institutional factors, local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) note that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This decline was influenced by a range of factors, including the collapse of the Mughal Empire and the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This suggests that while institutional factors played a role, they were not the sole determinant of economic performance. The economic performance of these regions was also influenced by external factors such as foreign trade and export markets, which highlights the complex interplay between institutional and external factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

The literature on African economic history has made significant strides, particularly in understanding the role of institutions and governance in development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Hopkins (2019, p.3; 2009, p.18); (Hopkins 2019, p.3) share the view that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. One mechanism that has been explored is the relationship between ethnic fragmentation and institutional quality. Hopkins (2009, p.18) cites Englebert, who concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This implies that ethnic heterogeneity does not necessarily hinder the development of progressive institutions. The development of these institutions is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing these incentives (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that while ethnic fragmentation may not be a barrier, the presence of well-functioning institutions is critical for economic development. Hopkins (2019, p.3) notes that the history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques if it is to be fully understood (Hopkins 2019, p.13). This underscores the complexity of institutional development and the need for a multifaceted approach to understanding it.

The papers in this cluster examine specific economic and social changes in Europe and Asia without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argues that the revisionist position dismissing Eurocentric views is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, contra the revisionists who claim that major Eurasian societies were progressing along a comparable modernization path. Bryant's analysis is particularly insightful in highlighting the ideological blinders that can obscure the unique advantages of European political institutions in facilitating developmental modernization. This perspective is mirrored in Feinstein's work, which underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. Feinstein (1998, p.28) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) demonstrates that real wages and the standard of living in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution, suggesting that institutional frameworks played a crucial role in this economic progress. The stagnation of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, as documented by Allen et al. (2011, p.22) (Feinstein 1998, p.5), further illustrates the impact of institutional differences on long-term economic performance. This stagnation highlights the need for robust institutional frameworks to drive sustained economic growth, a point that Bryant's critique of revisionist views indirectly supports.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can catalyze profound shifts in economic structures and social dynamics, challenging the primacy of institutions as the sole determinant of long-run economic growth. Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Domar (1970, p.4) share this posture. Bohannan argues that the introduction of money into an African subsistence economy has led to significant changes, including the shift away from a multicentric economy and the integration of local economic institutions with the global economy. This shift reflects deeper economic transitions, as well as institutional changes. Domar's analysis of historical land-to-labor ratios and the emergence of serfdom underlines the economic forces that shape societal structures, including the development of non-working classes and the compulsory fastening of populations to their occupations. This perspective is crucial for understanding how economic transitions can drive long-run growth, even in the absence of institutional reforms. The economic forces that Domar describes, such as land scarcity and labor shortages, highlight the mechanisms through which economic transitions can reshape societies. The transition to a monetized economy presents its own challenges. Bohannan notes that while the introduction of money can lead to increased trade and a greater number of markets, it also brings potential pain points, such as the disruption of local economic institutions and the integration with global economic systems. These pain points underscore the complex interplay between economic transitions and institutional changes, suggesting that while economic transitions can drive growth, they also require careful management to mitigate their disruptive effects.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue that has been extensively studied. Institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, are pivotal in driving economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita. This perspective underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The impact of institutions varies across different contexts. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and varies based on specific historical and geographical factors.

The interaction between institutions and economic development is further complicated by the role of colonial policies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2002, p.33) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Hopkins (2009, p.8; 2009, p.18) adds that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This shows that understanding the historical role of institutions is crucial for designing effective policies to address contemporary economic challenges. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10; 2022, p.2) provide evidence that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance and highlights the importance of historical data in understanding long-term economic growth.

The precise mechanisms through which institutions shape economic growth remain a subject of ongoing debate. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) show that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15; 2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping economic growth is contingent on a variety of factors, including the political and economic context. The evidence shows that institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Frankema 2012, p.1).
