The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development studies. The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms behind economic growth can inform policy and institutional design. A live disagreement exists between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that geographic factors or other variables play a more significant role. Some scholars emphasize the importance of extractive institutions, while others highlight the role of inclusive institutions in promoting economic development. This tension is evident in debates about the impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions shape economic outcomes through their impact on investment, property rights, and governance. Others suggest that the relationship is more nuanced, with institutions interacting with other factors such as geography, culture, and historical context. The central question remains: how do institutions shape long-run economic growth, and what are the key mechanisms through which this occurs? This question is particularly relevant in the context of colonial legacies, where the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers have had lasting effects on economic development. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth is far from settled, with scholars continuing to explore the complex interplay between institutions and other factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of varying institutions. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other cases, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups, who may have been reluctant to initiate institutional change that would not directly benefit them. This institutional design, focused on maximizing short-term gains, had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. However, the specific mechanisms by which these institutions influenced economic growth varied across colonies, depending on factors such as population density and prosperity. This variation highlights the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes in the context of colonialism.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This improvement was particularly pronounced after 1880, as barbed wire became more widely available. Domar (1970, p.4) further illustrates that the scarcity of labor can drive the development of institutional structures that facilitate land ownership and agricultural development. In the American North, for example, the institutional framework was shaped by economic forces that encouraged land ownership and agricultural expansion. Domar (1970, p.4) qualifies that this process can vary based on the specific economic and social conditions of different regions. Agricultural productivity gains from barbed wire fencing were not uniform across all regions, suggesting that the effectiveness of property rights institutions depends on local context and the availability of complementary technologies.

The transformation of land abundance and factor markets in colonial Africa underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their adaptive responses to changing economic conditions. Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2)(Austin 2007, p.20) show that the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 catalyzed an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This shift was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, particularly the demand for cocoa production. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony, for example, played a pivotal role in this transformation by altering the dynamics of labour and land ownership. The ordinance, followed by the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908, facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, which had significant economic implications. This institutional change was not merely a response to legal reforms but also a result of economic pressures, as local chiefs, driven into debt by the loss of slaves, became more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming. The integration of the Gold Coast colonies under British rule further enhanced market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade. However, Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies the impact of European settlers on colonial economic policies, noting that where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often supported indigenous commodity production to foster fiscal security and stability. This qualification highlights that the institutional changes were not uniform and varied based on local economic and political contexts.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share a finding that colonial rule, particularly British rule, played a significant role in shaping educational development in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift in perceived benefits was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which in turn influenced the demand for missionary education. This long-term perspective is crucial for evaluating the structural effects of historical and geographical conditions on African welfare development. However, the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, particularly regarding the persistence of poverty and the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic outcomes (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This gap in data highlights the need for further research to fully understand the impact of colonial policies on economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms, as seen in the works of Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2019, p.3), provides valuable insights into the historical context and development of institutions in Africa, yet it does not directly address how these institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2019, p.13) argues that understanding Africa's economic development requires multiple approaches and techniques, highlighting the complexity of institutional reforms. This multifaceted approach is essential for addressing the persistent issue of poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. However, the new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes, has gained attention from development specialists by emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This focus on institutional quality and its impact on economic development is a significant step forward in understanding the long-term economic outcomes in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner and BroadberryEtal have shown that by the early 20th century, many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence, while India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). This historical economic performance, particularly in Africa, was intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly across and within countries, reflecting the multifaceted nature of economic development in these regions. The economic trends in India, however, reveal a different pattern: the decline in per capita GDP was not solely attributable to colonialism but had already commenced during the Mughal Empire (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This nuanced understanding of historical economic trends in Africa and India underscores the complexity of factors influencing long-run economic growth, highlighting the need for a comprehensive analysis that considers both institutional and non-institutional determinants.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia, as Bryant and Broadberry and Gupta (Bryant 2006, p.12; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), is often attributed to various factors, including institutional differences, but these papers focus on the historical context and specific conditions that led to this divergence. Bryant (2006, p.12) challenges the revisionist position that dismisses Eurocentric views, arguing that it is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent. One specific mechanism contributing to this divergence was the role of merchant distribution and finance in north-western Europe, which facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector, a factor not as pronounced in other regions. These changes provided a supportive setting for modernization, as Bryant notes that European political institutions offered an advantageous setting for developmental modernization. This institutional support was crucial in enhancing Europe's capacities in coercive and productive power, which in turn facilitated its passage to colonial domination and capitalist modernity (Bryant 2006, p.1). The role of these institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex and multifaceted, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and institutional factors that contributed to the great divergence.

Women have played pivotal roles in shaping economic growth and development, both historically and contemporarily, with significant contributions during pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Goldin 2006, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack show that women were central to the economic revolution in the Gold Coast, driving cocoa production as men migrated in search of farmland. Goldin's analysis of the U.S. labor market highlights exogenous changes that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics. These shifts were driven by increased demand for female office workers, educational expansions, and technological changes in household production. Goldin also notes that institutional changes, such as the availability of part-time work and the removal of barriers to married women's employment, further altered female labor supply. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and women's economic contributions, suggesting that while women have been instrumental in economic growth, their full potential is often constrained by institutional barriers. These barriers can limit women's economic agency and prevent them from fully participating in economic development.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share a view that technological advance and social change are pivotal in shaping economic growth, influencing productivity scales and regional migration patterns (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The dynamics of technological progress involve the broader social and institutional changes that facilitate or hinder their adoption and impact. This process can alter the ranks of countries in the productivity scale, with institutional changes playing a crucial role. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) provide empirical support for this view, showing how real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, a period marked by significant institutional and economic continuities. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic growth and productivity. However, the precise proportion of labor market integration remains elusive, indicating that while technological advances and social changes are pivotal, their impact on economic growth is contingent on the specific institutional context. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) qualifies this by noting that the trouble with absorbing social capability into the catch-up hypothesis is that no one knows just what it means or how to measure it (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This highlights the need for further research to better understand the mechanisms through which technological advance and social change influence economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) and Feinstein (1998, p.28) highlight the significance of accurate economic measurements, asserting that reliable estimates of national accounts are essential for understanding global economic performance. They note that historical economic data are often based on partial information and assumptions, leading to margins of error that can be substantial, especially for more distant historical periods. Feinstein (1998, p.5) builds on this by emphasizing the need for meticulous analysis of wage and price data to accurately assess standards of living. The Industrial Revolution's impact on British economic growth, for instance, has been the subject of extensive quantitative research, with scholars refining estimates over time. Feinstein (1998, p.5) underscores the collaborative nature of this endeavor, where scholars have built upon each other's work to improve the accuracy of historical economic data. This iterative process of refinement is crucial for understanding long-term economic growth patterns. This underscores the need for a holistic approach that integrates various data points and contextual factors to achieve a comprehensive understanding of economic performance. The collaborative efforts in refining economic measurements are pivotal for advancing our knowledge of long-term economic growth, highlighting the importance of continuous scholarly engagement with historical data.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economies, highlighting the deep social transformations that accompany economic restructuring in Africa (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Bryceson (2002, p.2) describe how the influx of new economic institutions and trade networks fundamentally altered traditional economic systems. For example, the integration of the Tiv economy into the broader colonial economy disrupted local economic institutions and markets. This shift was driven by the introduction of alien institutions, such as new currency systems and trade networks, which changed the way economic exchanges occurred. These changes were deeply social, as traditional roles and structures were altered. The need for cash income led to new economic activities, such as food snack sales and beer brewing, which became significant sources of income for rural women. This economic restructuring had profound implications for social dynamics, as men increasingly accepted that their wives and daughters needed to work outside the home to earn money. The shift from subsistence to cash-based economies required not only economic adaptation but also social accommodation, as traditional roles and structures were redefined. This underscores the interconnected nature of economic and social changes in Africa during periods of economic restructuring.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the economic activities and social dynamics of regions. The type of institutions that are established during colonization significantly affects the economic trajectory of colonies. Extractive institutions, which prioritize resource extraction over long-term growth, have been shown to persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The persistence of these institutions is a result of the political power dynamics that favor the interests of the colonizers rather than promoting long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For example, European settlers in colonies like the United States established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to different economic outcomes compared to extractive states like the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, highlighting the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the idea that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This adaptation shows that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the historical experience of African women, who made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women's involvement in economic activities, such as food snack sales and beer brewing, became significant sources of income, demonstrating the interconnected nature of economic and social changes in Africa during periods of economic restructuring (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). These economic changes required not only economic adaptation but also social accommodation, as traditional roles and structures were redefined (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The codification of customary law under colonial rule further marginalized women, but their economic contributions highlight the broader impact of institutional changes on economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by the idea that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical experience of African women and the new economic history of Africa both highlight the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing economic activities and social dynamics, as shown by the persistence of extractive institutions and the role of good institutions in providing incentives for investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which colonial institutions were extractive or conducive to development. This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which shows that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly affects the economic trajectory of colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
