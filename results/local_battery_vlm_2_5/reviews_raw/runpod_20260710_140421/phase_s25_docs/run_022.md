The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. This question is pivotal for understanding why some regions have thrived while others have stagnated. A key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, rather than geographic or cultural factors, drive economic outcomes. The debate is framed by those who argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as colonial policies or local economic conditions, play a more significant role. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic planning. Understanding the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth can inform strategies for development and poverty reduction. The stakes are high, as the effectiveness of institutional reforms depends on a clear understanding of their impact on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth, a pattern evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The differential colonization strategies employed by Europeans resulted in varied institutional frameworks across colonies. In colonies where Europeans settled, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were formed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks was driven by the mortality rates experienced by Europeans, which in turn shaped the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions, particularly the extractive ones, has had lasting effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to these institutional changes, highlighting the profound impact of colonial institutions on economic development. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes, as evidenced by the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the 19th century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

This divergence in economic performance can be traced back to specific institutional factors that shaped economic outcomes during critical periods, such as the 19th century. Broadberry and Gardner's analysis of Anglophone African economies reveals that these economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous narratives about Africa's economic stagnation (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Allen's study of European cities from 1500 to 1750 shows that real incomes fell in most cities during this period, but economic leaders maintained their income levels. This divergence in real incomes set the stage for later economic disparities (Allen 2001, p.8). The mechanisms driving these economic shifts include local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, which influenced the timing and scale of export production in various regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Allen's findings suggest that the maintenance of high real wages in some European cities after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century was a decisive break with the past (Allen 2001, p.23). BroadberryEtal argue that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism, highlighting the role of state institutions in underpinning investment and innovation (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). The lack of state institutions in India, coupled with workers remaining on the land, had negative consequences for agricultural labor productivity and the relative size of the industrial and service sectors (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in these regional studies, which collectively underscore the importance of political and economic structures in driving economic performance.

Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, challenging the formulations of Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). Austin shows this by pointing out that European settlers' presence influenced colonial governments' institutional prescriptions, sometimes encouraging indigenous commodity production and changing institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism is crucial because it highlights how local conditions and responses shaped colonial institutions. Austin (2007, p.20) supports this by showing that the transition from coerced to free labour in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This shift was driven by localised scarcity and economic changes, illustrating how institutional evolution can be induced by changing factor scarcities. Austin (2007, p.20) qualifies this by noting that while the British emancipation ordinance and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti were not examples of induced institutional innovation, they had significant economic dimensions (Austin 2007, p.20). This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional change, where legal actions and economic dimensions intertwine. The integration of British rule in Ghana also had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade (Austin 2007, p.4). This implies that while local conditions drove institutional changes, broader colonial policies also played a role in shaping economic outcomes.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing on the American Plains exemplifies how institutional changes, such as enhanced property rights, can drive agricultural development and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This technological innovation facilitated greater control over land use, as farmers could now delineate their properties more effectively. Barbed wire allowed for the demarcation of boundaries, which in turn enabled more efficient farming practices. For example, the introduction of barbed wire led to a substantial increase in improved farmland in herd-law counties. From 1880 to 1890, these counties experienced a 20-percentage-point increase in improved farmland, a trend that was not evident before the advent of barbed wire (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This shift highlights how institutional changes, driven by technological advancements, can profoundly impact economic development. The role of property rights in shaping economic outcomes is further underscored by Domar's analysis of land-to-labor ratios. Domar argues that when land is abundant and labor is scarce, institutions like slavery or serfdom emerge to manage the distribution of resources (Domar 1970, p.4). This perspective complements the findings on barbed wire, suggesting that institutional frameworks evolve in response to economic conditions. However, Domar also notes that the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners can be facilitated by government intervention, indicating that institutional changes are not solely driven by economic forces but can also be influenced by policy decisions (Domar 1970, p.12). This interplay between economic conditions and policy interventions underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies illustrates how institutional changes can lead to profound shifts away from multicentric economic structures (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Bohannan highlights that the integration of alien institutions, such as the colonial road network, disrupted traditional economic systems and connected local markets to the global economy. This shift was economic and social, as new forms of exchange and value systems emerged. Bryceson elaborates on this by noting that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social change, including depeasantization. This process involves economic shifts and significant social transformations, as rural populations adapt to new economic realities. Bryceson's observations suggest that the introduction of money and market systems has altered traditional gender roles, with women increasingly engaging in income-earning activities outside the home. This economic restructuring has profound implications for social dynamics, as new income-earning roles are accepted by rural men, reflecting broader social changes driven by economic necessity. However, Bryceson also notes that these changes are often accompanied by worsening economic circumstances, highlighting the complex interplay between economic conditions and social adaptation. This interplay underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its impact on long-run economic growth, as social and economic transformations reinforce each other in complex ways.
