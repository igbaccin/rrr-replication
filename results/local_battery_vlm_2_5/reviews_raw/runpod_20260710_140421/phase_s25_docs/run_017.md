Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, but their precise mechanisms and impacts remain subjects of intense debate. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or colonial policies, exert more significant influences. One position argues that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, particularly those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, were pivotal in determining long-term economic outcomes. One perspective suggests that the reversal of fortune among former European colonies is primarily due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, with geographic factors playing a lesser role. This debate is crucial because it informs policies aimed at fostering economic development in regions historically disadvantaged by colonial rule. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can provide insights into how modern policy can address historical inequities and promote sustainable development. This review synthesises the literature on the causal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established institutions that were either extractive or growth-promoting, depending on the settlement's mortality rates. In high-mortality areas, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in low-mortality areas, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This pattern of institutional development had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. further note that equilibrium institutions in colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, leading to significant differences in current income per capita. This institutional legacy highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2007, p.20) argue that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets. This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. In Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift towards localised scarcity of land drove chiefs to sell land to strangers, fostering the development of markets in land use rights. This institutional change was driven by both colonial policies and local economic and political actions. Colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political incentives, as seen in the case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The integration of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region. The removal of these barriers facilitated the development of factor markets, further enhancing economic growth (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes driven by localised scarcity had lasting effects on economic development, highlighting the dynamic relationship between institutional evolution and economic outcomes.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies, as highlighted by Bohannan (1959, p.10) and Bryceson (2002, p.2), led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures, illustrating the transformative impact of monetary systems on traditional economic frameworks. This transformation was driven by the colonial introduction of new institutions and infrastructure, such as roads and trading companies, which facilitated increased trade and the proliferation of markets. The economic institutions of the Tiv were thus brought into contact with the broader world economy, altering the dynamics of exchange within the region. Bryceson (2002, p.2) builds on this by noting that the economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social changes, including depeasantization. This process involves profound social transformations that are often overlooked. This shift involves social adaptations, where rural men have generally accepted that their wives and daughters need to work outside the home to earn money. This dynamic underscores how the introduction of money can reshape economic structures and social roles and expectations within African societies. The profound changes brought about by the introduction of money into African subsistence economies illustrate the deep and lasting impact of monetary systems on traditional economic frameworks, setting the stage for further exploration into the broader implications of economic restructuring and social change.

The new economic history of Africa offers critical insights into institutional reforms that can alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Both Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg emphasize that understanding the role of institutions is crucial for designing effective policies to reduce poverty. Hopkins argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the underlying institutional framework, not solely by ethnic fragmentation. This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's finding that poverty in Africa is a result of specific institutional failures, not an inevitable consequence of historical or geographical conditions. By focusing on real wage levels and trends, they demonstrate that African economies have the potential for growth when institutions are reformed to incentivize investment. Hopkins further elaborates that the prominence of institutions in development policy has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain traction among development specialists. This shift in focus from purely economic factors to institutional ones highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing the necessary incentives for investment and development. Englebert's conclusion underscores the complexity of institutional reform, as ethnic homogeneity does not guarantee functional institutions, nor does heterogeneity preclude the development of progressive ones. This nuanced understanding of institutions suggests that reforms must be tailored to the specific context of each African society, taking into account the unique historical and cultural factors that shape institutional outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that British colonial rule in Africa fostered the development of formal education and economic growth. Frankema shows that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by new social and political institutions. This suggests that the impact of British colonial rule on African education was more positive than that of other colonial powers. The positive impact of British colonial rule on African education and economic development was facilitated by the establishment of new social and political institutions, which altered the benefits of formal education. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This suggests that the positive impact of British colonial rule on African education and economic development was not uniform across all regions and was influenced by a variety of factors.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia had already begun before 1800, with north-western Europe experiencing high productivity in the traded goods sector due to merchant distribution and finance, as well as increased living standards from falling prices of manufactured goods (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This process was unevenly distributed, with skilled labourers benefiting more than unskilled ones. Allen (2001, p.8) argues that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.8). The maintenance of high real wages in these leaders, despite economic development, suggests that institutional changes played a role in sustaining economic growth. The gains from 1815 to 1850 were a minor cycle, not a trend, indicating that the maintenance of real wages was a series of fluctuations (Allen 2001, p.23). This suggests that while institutions may have supported economic growth, they did not do so in a linear or uniform manner. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, therefore, must be understood as part of a complex interplay of factors that includes but is not limited to institutional change.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical context of colonization and the subsequent development of economic policies. Institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional frameworks. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional development highlights how the type of institutions introduced during colonization played a crucial role in determining long-term economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, underscoring the complexity of institutional impacts (Austin 2008, p.2). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interplay is further complicated by the fact that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development is also evident in the differing outcomes of British and other colonial powers in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This educational development, in turn, influenced the economic trajectories of these regions by altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in economic development, as these frameworks directly influenced the economic policies and outcomes of former colonies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, emphasizing the enduring relevance of institutional factors in economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.18).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of institutions is uniform or varies based on historical and contextual factors (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence and empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional frameworks introduced during colonization, as demonstrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), which show that different colonization strategies led to different economic outcomes. This variation highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional impacts on long-run economic development.
