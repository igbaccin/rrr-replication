The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and what factors are pivotal in driving sustained growth. One live disagreement is the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to the growth process. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, shaping economic outcomes by influencing investment, productivity, and technological advancement. These scholars often emphasize the role of institutions in providing credible commitments and enforcing property rights. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing growth, and that geopolitical, demographic, and cultural factors are equally important. This disagreement is crucial because it influences policy recommendations, with some advocating for institutional reform as a panacea for economic underdevelopment. The extent to which colonial institutions have shaped the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies is a key area of contention. This debate is central to understanding the legacies of colonialism and the pathways to economic development in the postcolonial world. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). The policies and institutions established by European colonizers varied widely depending on the mortality rates they faced and the economic potential of the colonies. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions were characterized by a lack of property rights, weak rule of law, and minimal investment in local infrastructure. Where mortality rates were low, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, promoting long-run economic growth. This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. This institutional legacy persisted even after independence, shaping the economic outcomes of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) observe that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was also influenced by the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, indicating that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were not the only determinant of long-run economic growth. Nevertheless, the impact of colonial institutions on economic development remains a critical factor in understanding the divergent economic trajectories of former colonies.

The specific outcomes depended on local conditions and the nature of colonial administration, highlighting the importance of contextual factors in shaping institutional change. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional evolution in African economies, where localised scarcity and colonial policies intersected to drive economic transformation.

This technological innovation allowed farmers to delineate and defend their land more effectively, which in turn fostered investment in agricultural improvements. In the American Plains, the adoption of barbed wire fencing coincided with a marked increase in the improved fraction of farmland, indicating that better property rights protection encouraged farmers to invest in their land. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows that herd-law counties experienced a significant increase in improved land from 1880 to 1890, a period that aligns with the widespread use of barbed wire. This suggests that the ability to protect property rights through fencing was a critical factor in agricultural development. The effectiveness of property rights in fostering economic growth is conditional. This highlights that while property rights are crucial for economic development, their impact can be modulated by factors such as labor availability and social institutions. Therefore, the role of property rights in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of the economic and social environment in which they operate.

This educational focus altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families, creating new incentives for investing in human capital. This educational investment had significant implications for economic development, as it equipped the population with skills that could enhance productivity and foster long-run economic growth. The economic benefits of this educational development varied across regions. The patterns of economic growth in Anglophone African economies were intricately tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors highlight the complex interplay between colonial institutions, educational development, and economic outcomes, suggesting that while British rule facilitated certain educational advancements, the overall economic impact was modulated by a broader set of conditions.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, with both regions experiencing distinct economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) argue that this divergence was driven by differences in the economic structures of Europe and Asia. The service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, played a crucial role in driving economic growth in Europe. This sector fostered productivity in the traded goods sector, which in turn contributed to the rising living standards of north-western Europeans. The high productivity in the traded goods sector, coupled with the service sector's role in economic growth, suggests that the benefits of early modern development were spread unevenly across different regions. This uneven spread of benefits was due to both geographical factors and high levels of investment in agriculture. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) build on this by noting that India, in particular, lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation that allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap. Consequently, India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This implies that the institutional framework in India was not conducive to the same level of economic growth seen in Europe.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the varying impacts of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework influenced the long-term economic development of these colonies. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the importance of institutions in economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings are supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who notes that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Furthermore, the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform across regions. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule varied significantly (Austin 2008, p.2). This is supported by Frankema (2012, p.15), who shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, indicating that the type of colonial institution mattered for economic outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.1). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) add that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that the institutional legacies of colonial rule had already begun to shape economic trajectories (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the empirical evidence on the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence is supported by the finding that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa further illustrates the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as British rule was more conducive to educational development than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1).
