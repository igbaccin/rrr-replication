The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary economic policies. At the heart of this debate is a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutional factors and those who consider geographic and cultural elements to be more influential. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern economic and political interactions, are seen by some as the driving force behind economic growth, while others argue that geography and culture set the stage for institutional development. This disagreement is central to understanding the persistence of economic disparities among regions and countries. The institutional perspective posits that well-designed institutions can foster economic growth by providing incentives for investment and innovation. Extractive institutions can stifle economic development by diverting resources to a privileged few. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at promoting economic growth and reducing poverty. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is essential for designing effective policies that can foster sustainable development. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the complex interplay between institutions, geography, and culture. Some scholars argue that institutions are endogenous to geographic and cultural factors, while others contend that institutions can shape these factors over time. This interplay highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) find that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutions established during colonialism were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. For instance, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, which enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, were more likely to be established in colonies where Europeans could settle safely and invest in long-term development. This pattern is evident in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. However, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to transfer resources quickly, leading to different economic outcomes. This is because these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups that may have obtained fewer rents with institutions of private property or feared losing their political power if there was institutional development. This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, had profound and enduring effects on the economic trajectories of these regions.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) both argue that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets. This transformation was driven by the need to adapt to new economic conditions, which in turn shaped the development of markets for land use rights and hired labour. The institutional revolution in factor markets was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. For instance, in Ghana during 1874-1939, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the need to adapt to new economic conditions (Austin 2007, p.4). The imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, including the removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change was not merely a response to exogenous shocks but was also shaped by endogenous factors, such as the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This endogenous dimension of institutional change highlights the complex interplay between external forces and local dynamics in shaping economic institutions. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism had profound and enduring effects on the economic trajectories of these regions.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to significant economic transitions, altering traditional systems and property rights (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This disruption fundamentally alters the economic landscape. The shift from a multicentric economy to a more integrated system is driven by the need for a general-purpose currency, which brass rods initially fulfilled within the prestige sphere (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This reorganization can have profound implications for property rights and economic development. The herd law, which regulated livestock movement, played a crucial role in this transition. However, the effectiveness of these laws varied over time, and their implementation was not always straightforward. For example, the Kansas State Board of Agriculture Reports stopped including details on herd laws after 1880 and entirely stopped reporting the law status after 1884 (Hornbeck 2010, p.8). This variability in implementation highlights the complexities involved in institutional changes and their impact on economic development. These examples illustrate how the introduction of new economic institutions can lead to significant transitions in property rights and economic activities, with enduring effects on long-run economic growth.

In sub-Saharan Africa, the development of formal education systems was intricately linked to the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that the introduction of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which in turn influenced the demand for missionary education. The implementation of these new institutions was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa, and varied significantly based on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in these factors led to different patterns of economic growth and development across the region. For instance, the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. This variability underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2019, p.3) explore the complexities of economic growth in Africa without directly addressing how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach is consistent with the broader literature on African economic history and development, which has evolved to incorporate the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. "Good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This emphasis on institutions aligns with the idea that credible commitment underpins growth. The new economic history of Africa highlights the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape long-run economic growth, even if it does not directly address this question. This suggests that while the role of institutions in economic growth is recognized, the specific mechanisms through which they operate are still an area of ongoing research. The history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques if it is to be fully understood, and this includes understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) examine specific factors contributing to African economic development and poverty, such as the role of women in economic growth and historical wage trends. They establish that African women made significant contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic is crucial for understanding the broader economic landscape, as it highlights how gender-based land access policies shaped agricultural productivity and, by extension, economic growth. The invention of customary law during colonial rule further entrenched patriarchal alliances, which had profound implications for economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This historical perspective is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This gap in data underscores the importance of continued historical and economic research to elucidate the complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic growth in Africa.

The Great Divergence between regions like Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with factors influencing real incomes in different regions (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Allen 2001, p.8). Broadberry and Gupta, alongside Allen, argue that the economic performance of different regions was significantly influenced by the productivity of the traded goods sector and the role of the service sector in economic growth. Allen notes that the maintenance of high real wages in European cities after 1750 was crucial for economic development. However, the Great Divergence was not solely economic; it also involved institutional change, which was crucial for investment in agriculture and high agricultural labour productivity in Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). Broadberry and Gupta also point out that the decline in India's per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, and India lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation that allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This divergence, therefore, was not merely a result of colonialism but a complex interplay of economic and institutional factors that had already been set in motion before colonial rule. BroadberryEtal argue that India’s relative decline began during the Mughal Empire, indicating that the institutional framework played a significant role in shaping long-term economic outcomes (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the divergent paths of European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence is further supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Both authors highlight that the institutional framework, shaped by European colonization strategies, had lasting effects on economic development. The extractive institutions implemented in colonies with high mortality rates, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, illustrate how institutional design can hinder economic progress (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of African development. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, emphasizing that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, which show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Both perspectives underscore the importance of institutions in fostering economic development. The historical evidence of African women's contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, as documented by Akyeampong and Fofack, further illustrates the impact of institutional changes on economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which marginalized women, highlights how institutional frameworks can either support or hinder economic development.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). The measurement of income per capita and the comparison of institutional frameworks across different colonies provide a clear picture of how institutions shaped economic outcomes. The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which colonial powers, such as Britain, had different impacts on African education systems. Frankema shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, highlighting the variation in institutional impacts across different colonial regimes (Frankema 2012, p.1).
