The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious topic among scholars, with debates centering on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacts long-term economic development. This perspective suggests that institutions designed to extract resources have lasting negative effects on economic performance. Some scholars argue that the impact of institutions is more nuanced, influenced by a variety of factors including historical context, local endowments, and political dynamics. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they interact with other variables in complex ways. Understanding this debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to implement effective economic development strategies. To fully grasp the implications of institutional frameworks, it is essential to examine how different colonial strategies shaped institutional environments and their subsequent economic impacts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) validate this perspective through robust regressions using various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. These measures consistently show that institutional quality is a critical determinant of economic outcomes. The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic trajectories, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary development.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) share a focus on the intricate relationship between land abundance, scarcity, and institutional evolution, arguing that shifts in resource availability drove significant changes in economic institutions. Austin (2007, p.20) specifically highlights the impact of the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony, which, despite not being an example of induced institutional innovation, facilitated a transition from coerced to free labour. This transition had profound economic implications, driving Akyem chiefs into debt and making them more willing to sell land to strangers for cocoa farming. This institutional shift was a response to changing factor scarcities and the integration of markets facilitated by British rule, which removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on the role of colonial administrations in shaping institutional prescriptions, noting that governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This dynamic highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional change, where local economic and political actions interact with colonial policies to shape economic trajectories. The nuanced institutional changes observed in Ghana underscore the importance of considering the specific historical and contextual factors that influence economic growth, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the institutional legacies of colonialism.

This understanding has led to a more nuanced view of how institutions influence economic outcomes in Africa. This understanding is shared by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2019, p.3). The importance of institutional quality is underscored by the fact that it provides incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This institutional framework is crucial for understanding Africa's economic trajectory, as it has been shown that even ethnically heterogeneous societies can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating that the economic challenges faced by African countries are not solely due to ethnic diversity. This perspective is crucial because it highlights that the key to Africa's economic development lies in fostering good institutions that can drive investment and growth. The institutional quality in Africa is influenced by various factors, including governance and the role of the state. By the 1990s, the concept of 'bringing the state back in' had gained prominence, expanding the scope of development policy to incorporate governance. This shift allowed institutions and organizations to come to the fore, emphasizing the role of good institutions in providing incentives for investment. Africa's economic history is complex, requiring multiple approaches and different techniques for a full understanding. The history of economic development in Africa necessitates a nuanced approach that considers various factors, including the role of institutions and governance. This multifaceted understanding is essential for addressing the economic challenges faced by African countries and for fostering sustainable development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both highlight the varied effects of colonial rule on African education and poverty, focusing on British rule's influence on formal education development. They find that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This was because demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level and African families on the micro level (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social status (Frankema 2012, p.15). The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Therefore, the impact of colonial rule on African education and poverty is not straightforward, and the specific historical and geographical conditions of colonial rule played a significant role in shaping long-term African welfare development.

This early achievement sets a baseline for evaluating the subsequent economic performance of African economies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) both focus on specific economic conditions, such as per capita income levels and real wages, without directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner show that the growing and shrinking of African economies were tied to foreign trade and export markets. These economies' growth patterns varied owing to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced growth remain unclear, and the relationship between these factors and long-run economic growth is not straightforward. Allen et al. describe a vibrant labor market in early modern China, with wage rates serving as a reasonable proxy for average earnings. Allen et al. also document that agricultural day laborers were at the lower end but not a marginal fringe of the income ladder. This suggests that the integration of labor markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions near major urban centers was relatively high. The existence of a large and active labor market in early modern China, particularly for short-term or day labor, is well documented. This provides a useful context for understanding the economic conditions of the period, but it does not directly address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The divergence in economic performance between Europe and Asia during the early modern period was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta, along with BroadberryEtal, argue that this divergence was driven by differences in institutional frameworks. Specifically, Broadberry and Gupta highlight that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was facilitated by merchant distribution and finance, which played a crucial role in economic growth. This institutional setup allowed for investment in agriculture and led to high agricultural labour productivity. BroadberryEtal further elaborate that India lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation that allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap. This institutional gap meant that Indian workers remained predominantly on the land, with negative consequences for agricultural labour productivity and the relative size of the industrial and service sectors. BroadberryEtal also note that India's decline began during the Mughal Empire. The implication is that the institutional frameworks in place before colonial rule significantly shaped long-run economic trajectories. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth extends to the early modern period as well.

Institutional frameworks play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the diverse impacts of colonial institutions on former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutions significantly influenced current income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutional changes were actively shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers. The persistence of these institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, has had lasting effects on economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the interaction between colonial policies and indigenous economic actions. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction suggests that institutions evolve in response to local conditions and actions. Furthermore, Hopkins underscores the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Good institutions, as defined by the literature, matter because they create an environment conducive to investment and economic growth.

The specific impact of colonial institutions on economic development varies across regions and periods. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions could support economic growth under certain conditions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding is supported by Frankema, who argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The development of formal education, in turn, contributed to economic growth by enhancing human capital. The historical evidence shows that the interaction between colonial institutions and local conditions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic trajectories.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth, as Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement is the extent to which these institutions were shaped by local conditions and actions, as Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic interaction between colonial policies and indigenous economic actions. This interaction is crucial for understanding how institutions evolve and shape economic development over the long run.
