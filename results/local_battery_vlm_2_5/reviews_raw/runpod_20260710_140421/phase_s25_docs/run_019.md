The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a contentious issue, with scholars debating the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography or culture. One key disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily determined by exogenous factors or if they are endogenous, shaped by historical and political processes. Some argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors. The former position is grounded in the idea that institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on their nature. On the other hand, the latter position acknowledges the importance of institutions but also considers other factors. The debate is not just academic; it has significant implications for policy, as understanding the role of institutions can inform strategies for economic development and poverty reduction. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) both address the role of institutions in shaping economic growth, but they take different approaches to the question of causality. This disagreement highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonialism introduced different institutions to colonies based on European mortality rates, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. This is due to the fact that Europeans adopted distinct colonization strategies. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. For instance, in the Congo and the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional design was influenced by the political and economic interests of the colonizers. The colonizers' primary goal was to extract resources efficiently, which led to the establishment of institutions that prioritized short-term gains over sustainable development. This is because politically powerful groups may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting how historical institutional changes continue to influence contemporary economic development.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that institutional changes in Ghana were driven by shifts in land abundance, leading to the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony, followed by the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908, marked a significant shift in labor dynamics. These legal actions, while not directly induced by institutional innovation, created an economic environment where free labor became more prevalent. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes in factor markets can be a response to economic pressures, leading to the emergence of new markets and labor practices. Austin (2008, p.2) further illustrates that colonial governments, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, opting for higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation underscores the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes, even when they were not initially designed for long-run growth. The interplay between institutional changes and economic pressures in Ghana demonstrates how historical shifts in land abundance can drive institutional innovation, ultimately influencing long-run economic development.

Hopkins and Englebert (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3) share a posture that African economic history provides valuable insights into the historical context and development of institutions in Africa. The historical and institutional contexts of Africa are crucial for understanding the continent's economic trajectory. This evolution emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This insight underscores the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic outcomes. This perspective does not directly address how these institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to significant economic transitions, altering traditional systems and property rights (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This mechanism of enhancing property rights can be seen as a key driver of economic transitions in both contexts. The introduction of money and new property rights protection mechanisms can lead to substantial economic growth, but this process is not without its complexities, as evidenced by the challenges in aligning traditional systems with new economic institutions.

The role of women in economic development is significant, as highlighted by Akyeampong and Fofack and Goldin (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Goldin 2006, p.3). These scholars document women's substantial contributions to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, and also the early 20th century in the US. Women's economic participation was driven by shifts in demand for female office workers and changes in household production technology (Goldin 2006, p.3). This participation was not uniform across all sectors or regions. For instance, in colonial Africa, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their access to land for cash crops was limited by the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between gender, institutions, and economic development.

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) (Allen 2001, p.8) and Allen (2001, p.23) share that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750. In Britain, the maintenance of high real wages after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century was decisive for long-run economic growth. This period was marked by significant population changes and economic shifts in the medieval period. The gains from 1815 to 1850 were a cycle, and not a trend. The real wages and the standard of living in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution (Feinstein 1998, p.5). This improvement was due to the gains from 1870 to the First World War, which were decisive breaks with the past. The importance of the long swings reﬂecting population changes in the medieval period cannot be overstated. The divergence in real incomes was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. However, BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) qualifies this pattern by noting that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This divergence was influenced by the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation. As a result, India remained trapped in a Malthusian cycle, where population growth outpaced economic growth, leading to stagnation.

This interplay highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional impact on economic growth, encompassing both educational and political dimensions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.1) argue that British rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social mobility. This shift in perceived benefits drove demand for missionary education, as African political leaders and families recognized the potential advantages of formal education in the new colonial context. The timing and scale of economic growth in export production varied between and within countries, owing to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The distribution of income and the long-term picture of economic development are crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This nuanced view challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic history and colonial impacts, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a more comprehensive picture of economic development during this period. This approach reveals that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, as recent literature has emphasized.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of colonial legacies. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of institutions that either enforced the rule of law or set up extractive states. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Austin agrees that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This adaptation shows that institutions were not static but evolved in response to local conditions, influencing economic growth trajectories.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the interaction between population density and prosperity, which influenced the policies pursued by Europeans. Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking it to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, emphasizing the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He argues that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective aligns with the view that institutions matter because they shape the incentives and opportunities for economic growth.

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement about the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical evidence, grounded in the measurement of income per capita and the efficiency of the judiciary, provides a clear picture of how institutions shaped economic growth trajectories. Hopkins's argument that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment further supports this view, as it highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
