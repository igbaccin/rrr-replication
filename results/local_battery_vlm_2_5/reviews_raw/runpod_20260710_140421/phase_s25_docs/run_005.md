The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive societal prosperity. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic interactions, are the primary determinants of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play equally significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions are the critical drivers of long-run economic growth, while others contend that the impact of institutions is contingent on historical and geographical contexts. The debate is nuanced, with different studies offering varied perspectives on the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic outcomes. This review synthesizes key findings from the literature, focusing on the causal role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The centrality of institutions in economic growth is evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. They argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. In regions where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and protected property rights, economic growth was more robust. For example, Acemoglu et al. cite the United States, Australia, and New Zealand as cases where colonial institutions fostered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to transfer resources to the metropole quickly, resulting in persistent economic underdevelopment. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, underscoring the enduring influence of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The significance of institutions in economic growth is further supported by Hopkins, who notes that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert that colonialism's institutional legacies significantly shaped current income per capita across former colonies. The authors argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality rates they faced. In regions with high mortality, extractive institutions were implemented to facilitate rapid resource extraction, whereas in areas with lower mortality, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence persisted and had lasting effects on economic development. The impact of these institutions was particularly pronounced during the nineteenth century when the opportunity to industrialize played a crucial role in the long-run development of these colonies. This institutional framework, designed to benefit European colonists, hindered the economic growth of the colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development trajectory of these colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic performance.

Hornbeck (2010, p.32) argues that the advent of barbed wire fencing in the late 19th century was pivotal in transforming agricultural development on the American Plains. This innovation allowed farmers to secure their property rights more effectively and mitigate externalities from livestock damage, thereby fostering a more productive agricultural landscape. The introduction of barbed wire enabled farmers to delineate their land more precisely, which reduced disputes over grazing rights and enhanced the efficiency of land use. This technological advancement was particularly impactful in regions where herd laws were already in place, as it provided a practical means of enforcing these regulations. For instance, in Kansas, the use of barbed wire became prevalent after 1880, coinciding with a significant increase in improved farmland (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period saw a marked shift in land-use patterns, with a notable 20 percentage point increase in improved land from 1880 to 1890, highlighting the transformative effect of barbed wire on agricultural development. The adoption of barbed wire varied across regions. In areas where herd laws were less stringent or not enforced, the impact of barbed wire was less pronounced. This variability underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping the effectiveness of technological innovations. The introduction of barbed wire fencing enhanced agricultural productivity and laid the groundwork for more robust property rights enforcement, which is crucial for long-term economic growth. This development underscores the broader role of institutions in facilitating economic progress by providing the necessary framework for technological advancements to thrive.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2007, p.20; 2008, p.2); (Austin 2007, p.20) argue that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets. This shift was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, which in turn shaped the institutional landscape. For instance, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, leading to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic is evident in the case of Ghana during 1874-1939, where the shift from coerced to free labour had important economic dimensions. The loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. The British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony in 1874 and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal in this transition. These institutional changes were induced by local economic and political actions as well as responses to external pressures. This highlights the unique historical context of African economies, where institutional changes were often a result of localised scarcity and the need to adapt to new economic realities. This adaptive process underscores the broader role of institutions in facilitating economic progress by providing the necessary framework for economic activities to thrive.

Many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This challenges the prevalent narrative of persistent poverty in Africa, which has often been attributed to structural impediments. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic activities and facilitating growth by providing the necessary framework for trade and export markets to thrive. This adaptive process underscores the broader role of institutions in facilitating economic progress. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary for fully grasping the long-term picture of economic development. This includes exploring how these determinants relate to other parts of colonial Africa and assessing the possibilities of future growth. This nuanced view of African economic history suggests that while institutions were pivotal in shaping economic activities, the specific historical and geographical conditions also played a significant role in long-term welfare development.

The literature on economic divergence and colonial impacts explores how historical events and policies have shaped economic trajectories but does not directly address the role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). BroadberryEtal and Allen both consider the Great Divergence, the historical process by which the world's economies began to diverge in terms of productivity and living standards. BroadberryEtal establish that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period, tracing its roots back to the Mughal Empire's collapse. This period saw a significant decrease in revenue and agricultural labour productivity, which hindered India's economic growth. Allen, meanwhile, argues that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. The key mechanism here is the role of institutional change: Broadberry and Gupta show that in north-western Europe, institutional change brought about investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labour productivity. This process varied across regions. Broadberry and Gupta note that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled labourers benefiting more than unskilled labourers. This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, their impact varied significantly based on local conditions and the specific nature of the institutions involved. BroadberryEtal further qualifies this by noting that India lacked the state institutions needed to underpin the investment and innovation which allowed Britain and Holland to break out of the Malthusian trap. This highlights the importance of specific institutional frameworks in driving long-run economic growth, as well as the complex interplay between historical events, institutional changes, and economic outcomes.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms highlights the complexities of development in Africa, emphasizing the role of governance and historical context (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2019, p.3). This insight suggests that effective governance and progressive institutions are crucial for economic development, regardless of ethnic diversity. This emphasis on institutions aligns with broader economic theories that underscore the role of credible commitments in driving long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2019, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the history of economic development requires multiple approaches and different techniques for full understanding (Hopkins 2019, p.13). This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the institutional frameworks that can drive long-run economic growth in Africa.
