The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. It is widely accepted that institutions matter for economic growth, but the mechanisms through which they exert their influence remain contentious. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions are endogenous to economic development, shaped by the very processes they influence. On the other hand, others contend that institutions are exogenous, acting as deep determinants that shape economic outcomes over the long run. This debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and our understanding of economic development trajectories. For instance, if institutions are exogenous, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could be pivotal in spurring economic growth. If institutions are endogenous, such interventions may be less effective, as institutions would naturally evolve in response to economic changes. The disagreement hinges on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, with scholars debating whether institutions drive growth or whether growth shapes institutions. This tension sets the stage for exploring how different historical contexts and theoretical frameworks have informed our understanding of this complex relationship. A key theme connecting these debates is the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, particularly in the context of former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This innovation allowed farmers to demarcate their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated investment in agricultural improvements. Hornbeck (2010, p.32) shows that the adoption of barbed wire led to a substantial increase in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a notable rise in improved land from 1880 to 1890. This period coincides with the widespread use of barbed wire, suggesting that the technology played a crucial role in enhancing agricultural productivity. Domar (1970, p.4) notes that the effectiveness of property rights in stimulating economic growth can be influenced by the broader institutional framework, including government policies. This interaction between technological innovation and institutional support underscores the complex role of property rights in driving economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that varying colonization strategies led to different economic outcomes in former European colonies. These strategies resulted in diverse institutional frameworks, with some colonies benefiting from institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, for instance, significantly impacted current income per capita. This variation in institutional development highlights the role of colonial policies in shaping long-term economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) demonstrate that the nature of colonial institutions was deeply influenced by the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers. The persistence of these institutions, even after decolonization, underscores their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6; 2001, p.27) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic outcomes long after the colonial period has ended. The enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic development underscore the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are formed and their long-term implications for growth.

Austin (2007, 2008) (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20) examine how shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in Ghana, focusing on the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labor. The institutional revolution in factor markets during 1874-1939 in Ghana was driven by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity. This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which was a response to changing factor scarcities. The 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony and the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti were pivotal legal actions that facilitated this transition. These legal changes, coupled with the loss of slaves driving Akyem chiefs into debt, made chiefs more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This institutional change had significant economic implications, as it transformed the labour market from coerced to free labour. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule also had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what later became Ghana. The economic dimension of these institutional changes was profound, as they created new incentives and opportunities for economic activity. The specific institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations varied. Where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. Colonial administrations seeking fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This shows that colonial institutions adapted to local conditions and economic pressures.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) both stress that the economic contributions of women in Africa have been substantial, but the influence of institutions on these contributions remains complex. The colonial era introduced significant institutional changes that often marginalized women's economic roles. During this period, customary law was codified in a way that reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities. This codification was actively shaped by colonial administrations. For instance, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cultivation of cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional bias had profound implications for economic development, as it restricted women's ability to participate fully in the emerging cash crop economy. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is influenced by the effectiveness of institutions. Even ethnically heterogeneous societies can develop progressive institutions and uphold the rule of law, suggesting that the relationship between ethnicity and institutional quality is not deterministic (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view underscores the importance of institutional reforms in addressing the economic marginalization of women and promoting broader economic development. Institutional reforms are crucial for providing the incentives necessary for investment and development, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia predates colonialism, with the roots of this divergence visible by 1800 (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8). The high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector was driven by merchant distribution and finance, highlighting the role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This early divergence suggests that pre-colonial economic conditions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta point out that the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates, such as those in southern India, may have reinforced economic disparities (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This climatic factor, combined with the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation, further exacerbated the divergence (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.16). This historical context underscores the complexity of institutional influences on economic growth, where pre-existing conditions and colonial impacts intertwine to shape long-run economic outcomes.

The role of British colonial rule in shaping education in sub-Saharan Africa was more nuanced than previously thought, with Broadberry and Gardner and Frankema (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) arguing that it facilitated the development of formal education. This process was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African leaders and families. This shift in institutional framework created a new set of cost-benefit calculations that drove demand for missionary education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of these institutional changes on economic growth varied across all African regions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) show that the divergence in real wage levels across British Africa was influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These factors suggest that the economic impact of British colonial rule on Africa was complex and varied, with some regions experiencing more significant economic growth than others. The timing and scale of growth in export production, tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, further highlight the complexity of these institutional impacts (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different historical and geographical contexts leading to varied outcomes. The impact of colonial institutions is particularly significant, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that these institutions were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This is evident in the contrast between settler colonies, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, and extractive states where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting effect on current income per capita, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial policies on economic development.

The complexity of institutional impacts is further illustrated by the varying economic growth experiences across regions under British colonial rule. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is supported by the observation that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, indicating that local conditions and responses played a crucial role in shaping institutional outcomes. This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local contexts underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and geographical factors that influence institutional development and economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This perspective is supported by the findings of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and provide evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions. The emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic development is further highlighted by the role of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. Frankema (2012, p.15; 2012, p.1) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers, suggesting that the type of colonial institution matters significantly for economic outcomes.
