The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some regions thrive while others stagnate, and the mechanisms that drive these disparate trajectories. The debate centers on the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes, with some scholars emphasizing the primacy of institutional frameworks in fostering growth, while others point to the interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography and historical contingencies. The disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and the design of interventions aimed at promoting economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly affected current income per capita. The type of institutions established by European colonizers varied greatly depending on the mortality rates they faced. This pattern is evident in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The long-term impact of these institutional differences is profound; extractive institutions stifled economic development, whereas inclusive institutions fostered growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions over time has had a lasting effect on the economic trajectories of these regions. The institutional framework established during colonialism continues to shape economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional legacies on contemporary economic development.

This institutional change in factor markets highlights how specific technological advancements can bolster economic growth by addressing long-standing issues in property rights and resource management. Barbed wire fencing allowed farmers to delineate their land more effectively, which in turn reduced the incidence of livestock damage and improved agricultural productivity. This technological innovation was particularly impactful in regions where herd laws had previously failed to adequately protect farmers' interests. This shift underscores the importance of institutional changes that facilitate the protection of property rights, as they can catalyze economic development by encouraging investment and efficient resource use. The effectiveness of such institutional changes depends on broader economic and social contexts. Domar (1970, p.4) argues that the emergence of slavery or serfdom is influenced by the land-to-labor ratio, highlighting that institutional frameworks are shaped by underlying economic conditions (Domar 1970, p.4). This suggests that while technological innovations like barbed wire can enhance agricultural development, their impact is moderated by the prevailing economic and social structures. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks thus plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as these factors collectively influence the efficiency and productivity of agricultural and economic activities.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies has led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures, highlighting the transformative impact of monetary systems on traditional economic frameworks (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bryceson 2002, p.2). Bohannan (1959, p.10) observed that the integration of the Tiv economy with the global market through colonial trade networks disrupted local economic institutions. The shift from a multicentric economy, where various forms of exchange coexisted, to a more unified monetary system altered traditional economic practices. This transformation is evident in the Tiv's experience, where brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within specific spheres but struggled to fulfill all functions of money outside these spheres (Bohannan 1959, p.9). Bryceson (2002, p.2) builds on this by noting that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social changes, including depeasantization. This process involves not only economic shifts but also significant social transformations, as rural populations adapt to new economic realities. For instance, in Malawi and Tanzania, women have increasingly taken on income-earning roles outside the home, reflecting broader economic pressures (Bryceson 2002, p.8). However, Bryceson (2002, p.2) also points out that neoliberal policies, while aiming to create an enabling environment, have often overlooked the impact on rural social structures (Bryceson 2002, p.2). This suggests that while monetary systems can drive economic growth, the social implications of such changes must be carefully managed to ensure sustainable development.
