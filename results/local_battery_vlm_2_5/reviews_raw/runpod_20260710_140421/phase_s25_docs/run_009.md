The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has long been a subject of intense debate among economists and historians. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions and economic development strategies. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of extractive institutions by European colonists significantly impacted the economic trajectories of former colonies. Others contend that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex, influenced by a multitude of factors including historical context, indigenous economic and political action, and the interaction between institutions and opportunities for industrialization. The debate ultimately hinges on the extent to which institutions can be credited with long-run economic growth, and how this credit might be shared with other factors. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers or merely one piece of a larger puzzle remains open. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established institutions that varied widely depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns, which in turn shaped economic outcomes in former colonies. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where Europeans settled in large numbers, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional design had profound effects on economic growth, with extractive institutions leading to lower income per capita in the long run. The long-term economic impact of colonial institutions is further highlighted by Acemoglu et al. who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this reversal to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction underscores the critical role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping long-run economic growth.

However, the impact of property rights on economic development can be significantly enhanced by technological innovations, such as the introduction of barbed wire fencing (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This innovation allowed farmers to reduce livestock damage and improve land management, demonstrating how specific institutional changes can drive economic growth. Hornbeck shows that the adoption of barbed wire led to a substantial increase in improved farmland, with herd-law counties experiencing a 20 percentage point increase from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period coincided with the widespread use of barbed wire, suggesting that the technology played a crucial role in facilitating agricultural expansion. Domar’s analysis of agricultural structures supports this view, highlighting that the creation of a non-working class of agricultural owners can significantly alter land-use patterns (Domar 1970, p.4). Domar (1970, p.12) also notes that the development of serfdom in Europe was driven by labor scarcity, indicating that the effectiveness of property rights institutions can vary depending on the labor market conditions. This qualification suggests that while property rights are essential for agricultural development, their impact can be moderated by broader economic factors.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) find that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This focus on institutions has allowed the new economic history of Africa to engage with development specialists, who recognize that institutions matter for long-run economic growth. The historical experience of Africa suggests that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the broader institutional framework that can either uphold the rule of law or lead to dysfunctional outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012, p.28) reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies, which reveals that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The effectiveness of institutional reforms in reducing poverty may be contingent on broader economic factors, such as labor market conditions and the distribution of income, which can moderate the impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

Austin shows that the institutional landscape in Ghana underwent significant changes during the period 1874-1939, driven by shifts in land abundance (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The transition from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, characterised by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional transformation was influenced by indigenous economic and political actions, as well as colonial policies. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by local economic and political dynamics. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial governments would always prioritise lower production to maintain control over labour. This adaptive behaviour of colonial administrations suggests that the institutional changes were shaped by the interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions. This shift highlights how the emancipation ordinance and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti were pivotal in reshaping the economic landscape. The integration of the Gold Coast colonies under British rule also had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region. This institutional transformation facilitated economic growth and laid the groundwork for future development by creating a more integrated and dynamic market system. The effectiveness of these institutional changes in promoting long-term economic growth was contingent on broader economic factors, such as the distribution of income and labour market conditions, which could moderate the impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies led to profound changes and a shift away from multicentric economic structures (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). The economic landscape of Africa was significantly altered by the integration of monetary systems, which disrupted traditional economic institutions and brought African economies into contact with the global market. Bohannan (1959, p.10) describes how the Tiv economy in Northern Nigeria underwent substantial changes with the introduction of new road networks and trade routes, facilitated by colonial rule. This integration increased trade and exposed local economic systems to broader global influences. The shift from a multicentric economy to a more centralized one, driven by the introduction of money, had far-reaching implications for local economic practices and social structures. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by the perceived benefits of formal education, which were tied to new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. This educational transformation was a byproduct of the broader economic changes driven by the introduction of money and the subsequent integration into the global economy. The integration of monetary systems and the associated educational reforms were pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. The effectiveness of these changes was contingent on broader economic factors, such as the distribution of income and labour market conditions, which could moderate the impact of institutions on economic outcomes.

BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that the economic performance of India and Anglophone African economies was intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets. The economic fortunes of these regions were closely linked to the dynamics of global trade, with periods of growth and decline directly correlated with the market for exports. This pattern was driven by various factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The role of institutions in shaping these outcomes remains complex. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, highlighting the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth. This suggests that the institutional framework supporting trade and commerce was pivotal in driving economic development. The impact of institutions was not uniform across regions. BroadberryEtal (2015, p.16) argue that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism, but rather due to the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation. This indicates that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth was contingent on broader economic and political conditions, which could either enhance or hinder their impact.

The comparative analysis of living standards and economic progress between Europe and Asia highlights significant differences in economic divergence. Bryant (2006, p.12) and Allen et al. (Bryant 2006, p.12; Allen et al. 2011, p.22) show that the early modern period witnessed distinct trajectories of economic development. Bryant (2006, p.12) argues that European political institutions provided a supportive framework for modernization. This institutional advantage facilitated the European powers' ascent to global hegemony, driven by enhanced capacities in coercive and productive power. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) support this perspective by demonstrating that real wages in Europe experienced sustained growth during this period, while China experienced stagnation. This divergence in real wages underscores the differential impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Feinstein (1998, p.5) qualifies this picture by noting that while real wages in Britain improved during and after the Industrial Revolution, the extent of this improvement was not uniform across all sectors. This qualification suggests that the benefits of institutional support were not evenly distributed, highlighting the complexity of institutional impacts on economic growth. This nuanced view of economic divergence underscores the importance of considering both the supportive and limiting factors within institutional frameworks.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different historical and geographical contexts yielding varied outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the institutional frameworks established in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, had lasting effects on economic development. For instance, colonies where Europeans settled and established rule-of-law-based institutions, such as the United States and Australia, experienced higher economic growth compared to those with extractive institutions like the Congo (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships between colonial institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). He emphasizes that the institutional impacts on economic growth are nuanced and depend on various historical and political factors, including the presence of European settlers and the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that equilibrium institutions often maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Broadberry and Gardner reveal that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding underscores the importance of considering both the supportive and limiting factors within institutional frameworks in understanding economic divergence.

The complexity of institutional impacts is further highlighted by the varied experiences of different colonial powers. Frankema shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This suggests that the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, aligning with Acemoglu et al.'s thesis that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Hopkins emphasizes the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development, noting that the new economic history of Africa has informed institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18) underscores the enduring significance of institutional frameworks in economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is grounded in the premise that institutions influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives and constraints. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may prioritize short-term rents over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by the evidence that different colonial powers introduced varied institutional frameworks, leading to different economic outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.1). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships between colonial institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate is settled by the historical evidence of per capita income levels in Anglophone African economies, which show that many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This empirical comparison reveals that the specific institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, are central to understanding the long-run development of former colonies.
