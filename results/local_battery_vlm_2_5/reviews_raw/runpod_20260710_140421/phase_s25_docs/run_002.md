The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position emphasizes the importance of institutions in promoting economic development by providing a stable framework for investment and innovation. This perspective highlights how institutions that enforce the rule of law and protect property rights can foster economic growth. Another position argues that institutions are just one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that geography, culture, and historical legacies also play crucial roles. This perspective suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on other factors, and that institutions alone cannot explain the diverse economic trajectories of different regions. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can inform policies aimed at promoting economic development in both developed and developing countries. The literature on this topic has focused on the interplay between institutions and economic growth, with some studies emphasizing the causal relationship between institutional quality and economic performance, while others highlight the complex interplay between institutions and other factors such as geography and culture. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in their former colonies. They note that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework set by the colonizers directly affected the economic trajectories of the colonies. This focus on short-term extraction often resulted in institutions that hindered economic development. The legacy of these colonial institutions persists, shaping the economic outcomes of former colonies to this day. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the industrialization opportunities and the institutions introduced during colonial rule. This highlights the profound and lasting impact of colonial institutions on economic development, underscoring the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks in analyzing long-run economic growth.

The literature on economic divergence and colonial impact explores how historical events and policies have shaped economic outcomes in different regions, but it does not directly address the role of institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (BroadberryEtal 2015, p.10; Allen 2001, p.8). BroadberryEtal (2015, p.10) highlights that India's decline in per capita GDP relative to Britain began during the early modern period and was not solely due to colonialism. This decline is attributed to factors such as the lack of state institutions needed to underpin investment and innovation, which are crucial for breaking out of the Malthusian trap. Allen (2001, p.8) shows that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This divergence suggests that the institutional framework in these economic leaders played a significant role in maintaining their economic status. These findings imply that the institutional environment, including state institutions and economic policies, is a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth. However, Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) caution that estimates of national accounts, particularly for distant historical periods, are subject to margins of error and subjective estimates, which can complicate the analysis of institutional impacts. This underscores the need for robust methodologies in measuring economic performance to accurately assess the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, but does not change the fact that institutions are a key factor in shaping economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2)(Austin 2007, p.20)  argue that the institutional revolution in African economies, particularly in Ghana during 1874-1939, led to significant changes in land use rights and hired labour markets. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana drove an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This transformation was not merely a response to exogenous factors but was also shaped by endogenous processes. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, which in turn influenced institutional prescriptions. This dynamic is illustrated by Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for economic development, as it facilitated the integration of markets and the removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony, later integrated as Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes varied across different colonial administrations. Where European settlers were absent, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, highlighting the variability in institutional responses to economic and political actions. This underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic outcomes in African economies.

The introduction of barbed wire fencing significantly enhanced agricultural development on the American Plains by enabling farmers to better protect their property rights and reduce externalities from livestock damage (Hornbeck 2010, p.32; Domar 1970, p.4). This technological innovation allowed farmers to secure their land more effectively, which in turn facilitated the expansion of cultivation and improved land management practices. The adoption of barbed wire coincided with a shift in land-use patterns, as evidenced by the increase in improved farmland from 1880 to 1890 (Hornbeck 2010, p.32). This period saw a notable 20 percentage point increase in improved land, indicating that the ability to enforce property rights through fencing directly influenced agricultural productivity. The role of property rights in economic development is further illuminated by Domar, who argues that the scarcity of labor can lead to the emergence of institutions like serfdom or slavery (Domar 1970, p.12). However, the introduction of barbed wire suggests that technological advancements can mitigate the need for such coercive institutions by providing alternative means of securing property and labor. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes, driven by technological innovations, can shape long-run economic growth by enhancing productivity and land management.

The economic performance of African economies before 1950 is more complex than previously understood, with evidence showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that the availability of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence challenges characterizations of persistent poverty. The economic growth and development of these regions were intricately tied to foreign trade and export markets, with the timing and scale of export production varying due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) highlight the significance of real wage levels and trends in understanding the long-term economic development of British African colonies. Their reconstruction of real wage levels for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule provides a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and its relation to other parts of colonial Africa. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical data, showing that economic performance in Africa was not uniformly poor before 1950.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Bryant (2006, p.12) (Goldin 2006, p.3) show that women's economic participation has dramatically transformed family dynamics and economic outcomes, offering a distinct perspective on economic development. The increased demand for female office workers in the early 20th century, coupled with the growth of educational institutions, significantly altered female labor supply. This transformation was further driven by changes in household production technology and institutional shifts that facilitated part-time work and removed barriers to married women's employment. These changes reshaped women's educational trajectories; for instance, women began to pursue professional and graduate education in significant numbers around 1970. The influx of women into the workforce and higher education not only changed family structures but also contributed to broader economic growth by expanding the labor pool and enhancing productivity. However, Bryant's (2006) analysis suggests that the impact of these changes may be more complex when viewed through a global lens, as the Eurocentric perspective on modernization does not fully capture the diverse institutional and cultural developments across different regions.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of economic gains and the adoption of policies that enhance productivity. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across regions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional frameworks that were implemented in different colonies and how they interacted with local economic conditions to influence long-run growth trajectories.

The divergence in economic outcomes across former colonies can be attributed to the varying institutional strategies adopted by European powers. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, in turn, shaped the economic trajectories of the colonies by determining the extent to which investment and productivity were encouraged. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This explains why some colonies, despite having natural resources and favorable geographic conditions, failed to achieve sustained economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6). This interaction is crucial for understanding why some colonies were able to industrialize and achieve economic growth while others remained stagnant.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight the complexity of institutional development and its impact on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This complexity is evident in the diverse institutional and cultural developments across different regions, as shown by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic growth is context-dependent (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered, as shown by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). This underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. The historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa is crucial for understanding the economic performance of the region, as shown by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).
