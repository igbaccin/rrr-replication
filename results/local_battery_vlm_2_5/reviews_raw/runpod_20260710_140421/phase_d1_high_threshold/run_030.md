The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics, as institutions are believed to be the fundamental determinants of a society's economic trajectory. This question is at the heart of a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations. The former group posits that the establishment of secure property rights, effective governance, and rule of law by institutions creates a stable environment conducive to investment, innovation, and economic expansion. Rival perspectives emphasize the importance of cultural factors, geographical endowments, and exogenous shocks in shaping economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the most critical factor in economic growth or if other variables play a more significant role. The literature on institutions and economic growth is vast, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some researchers emphasize the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights in fostering economic growth, while others point to the role of cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts in shaping institutional outcomes. The literature also explores the impact of historical events and colonial legacies on the development of institutions and their subsequent effects on economic growth. The central theme that emerges from this debate is the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors, shape long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions matter in shaping long-run economic growth, a claim shared by Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. This institutional setup allowed these colonies to develop robust economies. This institutional design, however, had significant implications for economic development, as it created a framework that did not encourage long-term economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on the importance of institutions by emphasizing that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, were the driving force behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were about resolving conflicts and establishing a framework that could support long-term economic growth. This political commitment was essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that institutions should be viewed as an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of institutions, recognizing their complex role in shaping economic outcomes.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is shared by Kuznets, who notes that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, and that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, have been dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This technological progress is not merely about the invention of new tools and techniques; it also encompasses the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for their efficient use. Mokyr acknowledges that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This is because without institutional progress, technological improvements could be undermined by rent-seeking behavior, limiting long-term economic improvement. McCloskey elaborates on this idea, arguing that ideas and ideologies played a crucial role in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey's argument underscores the importance of a societal environment that fosters experimentation and open dialogue, which in turn facilitates the uptake of new techniques and institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.3). This ideological shift allowed for the acceptance of new ideas and technologies, which were previously met with resistance due to theological and political disapproval. The interplay between technological progress and ideological shifts highlights the complex nature of economic growth, where technological advancements are necessary but not sufficient on their own. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intertwined with the growth of useful knowledge and the ideological conditions that support it.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.23) examine how inequality and structural changes influenced economic development, focusing on how differences in institutions can be traced to variations in factor endowments and initial conditions. The unequal distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies shaped the institutions that evolved, which in turn affected long-term development. This perspective highlights how initial inequality can lock in institutional arrangements that favor elites and restrict competition, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality. The institutional arrangements that evolved in these societies often included policies that protected elite interests, such as limited access to education and political power for the majority of the population. These institutions were actively shaped by the power dynamics within these societies. Pamuk (2007, p.16) further illustrates how structural changes, such as urbanization rates, can be key indicators of economic development and productivity in the pre-modern period. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland enabled rapid responses to new economic opportunities, contrasting with the stagnation in regions like Tuscany where existing monopolies were strengthened. This institutional flexibility allowed for the emergence of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing, contributing to economic growth and development. The interplay between initial inequality, institutional evolution, and structural changes underscores the complex nature of economic development, where institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge the notion that African poverty is a persistent phenomenon rooted in structural impediments. This cluster highlights the dynamic nature of African economic development under colonial rule, where institutional changes were responsive to local economic and political actions. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic activities, fostering commodity production where European settlers were absent. This adaptive behavior contrasts with the rigid institutional frameworks that stifled growth in regions like Tuscany. Colonial administrations that encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This responsiveness suggests that colonial institutions could evolve in ways that supported economic growth. The extent to which these institutional adaptations were sustained and their long-term effects on African development remain subjects of debate. The specific historical and geographical conditions of colonial Africa necessitate a nuanced understanding of how these institutional changes influenced long-term economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is deeply ingrained within the cultural norms of affected populations. This disruption was particularly pronounced in regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, which were better equipped to support trade with Europeans. The long-term effects of the slave trade on economic development extend beyond institutional disruption. These internal norms are at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. The persistence of this mistrust suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape contemporary economic development in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the historical evidence that different colonial strategies led to distinct institutional outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were extractive and did not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence highlights how European colonization strategies directly influenced the economic trajectories of these regions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the importance of understanding the specific institutional contexts of each colony (Austin 2008, p.2).
