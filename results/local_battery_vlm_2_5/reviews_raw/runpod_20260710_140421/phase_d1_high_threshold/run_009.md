The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. Institutions, encompassing laws, regulations, and social norms, can either foster or hinder economic growth by influencing investment, innovation, and productivity. A live disagreement surrounds the causal pathways through which institutions exert their influence. One position emphasizes that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, thereby encouraging investment and long-term planning. Another position argues that the impact of institutions is mediated through other factors, such as colonial policies and geographic endowments, which in turn influence institutional development and economic performance. Additionally, there is a debate on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, with some scholars asserting that institutions are shaped by economic and political forces, while others view them as independent variables that drive economic development. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for formulating effective policies aimed at promoting sustainable economic growth. Institutions matter because they can either propel or stifle economic development, and the mechanisms through which they operate are complex and multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The creation of financial institutions and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This financial revolution was a critical factor in economic growth, as it facilitated long-term capital markets and increased the efficiency of market exchanges. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the success of these institutions was underpinned by political factors, particularly the commitment of the regime to enforce and uphold the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which in turn fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement led to a growing proportion of transactions in the economy, reflecting increased specialization and division of labor. The impact of these institutions on economic growth varied across Europe. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the bureaucratic structure in France, for example, did not hinder economic growth to the extent previously thought, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as initially perceived. This indicates that while financial institutions played a crucial role, their impact varied depending on the broader institutional and political context. The creation of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth.

However, Mokyr (2005, p.49); (Kuznets 1973, p.1) assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements play a more fundamental role than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of knowledge, is seen as the dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This perspective highlights that the development of new technologies and the diffusion of innovations are crucial for economic growth, as they increase productivity and efficiency. This dynamic is evident in the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments, where the latter facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further elaborates on this by noting that the sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional frameworks are necessary to manage the social and political challenges that arise from economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that the growth of economic reasonableness does not follow the same monotonic or irreversible path as technological progress. This implies that while institutions are important, their effectiveness in supporting economic growth can vary and may not always be sustained over the long term.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that institutional inequality is a key factor in shaping long-run economic growth. They argue that the institutions established in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. This institutional framework, which favored the elite, was shaped by the initial factor endowments of these societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor. These conditions led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this framework by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to economic and political actions of the indigenous populations. This indicates that while initial conditions set the stage, the dynamic nature of institutional evolution also plays a crucial role in economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) further qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both historical context and ongoing institutional adaptations. The persistence of inequality in economic growth is a function of both initial endowments and the evolving institutional responses to these conditions.

The slave trade has significantly impacted African development by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, which Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2) argue is a fundamental factor in Africa's underdevelopment. The slave trade's legacy is evident in reduced trust levels, which are at least partially responsible for Africa's economic stagnation. Both the internal norms of mistrust and the external environment, such as the rule of law, play crucial roles in this dynamic. This mistrust is deeply ingrained and affects economic development on multiple levels, including trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. Nunn (2008, p.2) further underscores the enduring impact of the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, by contrasting it with the relatively brief period of colonial rule. This historical perspective highlights the profound and lasting effects of the slave trade on African economic development, suggesting that the legacy of mistrust is a significant barrier to growth. This finding complements Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) assertion that trust is a critical factor in economic development, and that understanding its origins is essential for addressing Africa's underdevelopment.

The literature on institutional changes due to colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) reveals that European colonization strategies significantly shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. posit that high European mortality rates in colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize short-term resource extraction rather than long-term growth. This institutional framework was particularly detrimental to economic development, as it failed to encourage investment and enforce the rule of law. These extractive institutions persisted over time, creating a lasting impact on the economic outcomes of these regions. In the former, settlers created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in the latter, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence, driven by differences in colonization strategies, had profound implications for the economic development of these regions. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, ensured that they were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutions are robust to various alternative measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This robustness underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism, therefore, played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies, with significant implications for their current economic status.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share a focus on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa, highlighting the importance of both endogenous and exogenous factors in driving institutional change and public goods provision. Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods. This finding underscores the complexity of African societies, where ethnic homogeneity does not guarantee functional institutions, nor does heterogeneity preclude the development of progressive institutions. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) build on this by demonstrating that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. Frankema (2012, p.15) contends that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This institutional change highlights the role of colonial powers in shaping educational outcomes, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the patterns of growing and shrinking economies were tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is thus multifaceted, involving both endogenous factors such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation and exogenous factors such as colonial rule and foreign trade.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by the nature of colonial institutions established by European powers. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive institutions that encouraged investment or extractive institutions designed for rapid resource transfer. This institutional legacy persists, significantly affecting current economic outcomes in former colonies. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that the impact of colonial rule varied based on the presence of European settlers and the fiscal interests of colonial governments, which often adapted institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. These findings highlight that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have enduring effects on economic development.

The persistence of these institutional frameworks is further underscored by the work of North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth, as seen in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England. These institutional changes fostered secure property rights, which were crucial for economic growth. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s (2002, p.33) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional legacy is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role.

The multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by endogenous factors such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues that while ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods, it can influence the development of progressive institutions. This highlights that the institutional landscape in Africa is shaped by both exogenous factors, such as colonial rule, and endogenous factors, such as ethnolinguistic fragmentation. The new economic history of Africa, therefore, provides valuable insights into how institutional reforms can help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The historical evidence of institutional changes and their impact on economic growth, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), shows that the legacy of colonial institutions persists. The empirical comparison of economic performance between former colonies with different institutional frameworks, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the nature of colonial institutions is a critical determinant of long-run economic growth.
