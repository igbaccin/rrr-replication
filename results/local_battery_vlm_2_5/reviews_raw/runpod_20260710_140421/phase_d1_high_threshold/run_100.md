The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergence in economic outcomes across countries. At stake is the fundamental issue of whether institutions, defined as the rules and norms that govern human interactions, are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one among several factors. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, determine economic trajectories. Some scholars argue that institutions are the critical variable, shaping economic outcomes through their impact on property rights, contract enforcement, and political stability. This perspective is often linked to the idea of 'credible commitment' underpinning growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Others contend that institutions are important but not the sole determinant, and that their effects are contingent on other factors such as colonial history and population density. The debate also involves the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, with some arguing that they are shaped by economic factors and others seeing them as independent variables that drive economic outcomes. This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The resolution of this debate could provide valuable insights into the design of effective economic policies and the promotion of sustainable development. This debate is not merely academic: the ability of institutions to shape economic growth is a key factor in the economic development of nations.

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional change was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. North (1989, p.9) notes that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years War. This trade set the stage for a more secure economic environment, where property rights were better enforced and transaction costs were lowered. The political factors underpinning economic growth, such as the commitment of the regime to these rules, were crucial in this process. The impact of these institutional changes on economic growth is complex. This qualification highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, indicating that while institutional changes were significant, they were not the sole determinant of economic development. The evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England illustrates how secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms can drive economic growth, albeit in a context-specific manner.

Colonial institutions significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of former colonies by shaping their institutional frameworks and resource allocation policies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework directly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists, often at the expense of long-term economic growth in the colonies. The persistence of these extractive institutions has had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies, as they continue to grapple with the legacies of colonial policies.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the posture that African economic development and colonial impact highlight various factors influencing public goods provision, but do not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. This literature examines the influence of colonial institutions on African economic development, with a particular focus on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who discuss how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system that reinforced the subordinate status of women through customary law. This institutional framework both marginalized women and shaped the economic landscape by influencing land access and agricultural practices. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men, had profound implications for economic development. The de-privileging of women in access to land, particularly for cash crops, was a significant factor in the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast a leading producer of cocoa. Frankema (2012, p.15) further qualifies this by showing that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This educational infrastructure, influenced by colonial institutions, altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions. The broader institutional framework, including the codification of customary law and the marginalization of women, highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth in Africa.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) (and Kuznets (1973, p.1)) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental to economic development than institutional frameworks. The growth of useful knowledge is a critical force driving economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) traces the development of formal institutions designed to reduce access costs to knowledge, highlighting the role of scholarly societies in Europe. This institutional support for knowledge dissemination underscores the interplay between technological progress and economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) cautions that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) emphasizes the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a profound adverse effect on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades have had a lasting impact on the development of Africa by causing a culture of mistrust, which has persisted to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is argued to have contributed to the underdevelopment of Africa by hindering economic growth and development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) estimate that both channels are important, but that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. This suggests that the internal norms of mistrust are more important than the external environment in explaining the effect of the slave trade on trust. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the persistence of inequality in societies is due to both internal norms of mistrust and the institutions that evolved in these societies. These institutions are argued to have restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, which in turn has hindered economic growth and development.

Austin critiques institutional theories of long-run economic growth by arguing that they oversimplify causation and fail to account for distributional conflicts and historical complexities (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The reversal of fortune thesis, for example, compresses different historical periods and paths, which obscures the nuances of institutional evolution and investment patterns. This critique is further supported by Ogilvie, who contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the guild system. The shared beliefs that make up part of the institutional structure of a society are deeply rooted in historical and cultural contexts. Austin's work emphasizes that colonial institutions did not always follow a straightforward path towards efficiency. Instead, colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which challenges the notion that institutions are universally efficient. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium contradicts the hypothesis that colonial institutions always aimed for lower production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). Furthermore, Ogilvie argues that institutions should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian, underscoring the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutions in economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not as straightforward as institutional theories suggest, and that a more complex interplay of historical, cultural, and political factors must be considered.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by multiple streams of scholarship that highlight the critical interplay between historical contexts and institutional frameworks. Institutions, as argued by North, are fundamental to economic growth, as they define the rules of the game that structure economic interactions (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive institutions that European colonizers imposed in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This extractive approach contrasts with the inclusive institutions set up in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where the rule of law and investment were encouraged. The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting impact on current income per capita in these regions, demonstrating the profound influence of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

Austin contends that the "reversal of fortune" thesis, which posits that institutional differences account for the economic disparities between formerly colonized regions, oversimplifies the complex historical processes at play (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, leading to higher production equilibria in some cases. This nuanced view underscores the dynamic nature of institutional change and the importance of considering the specific historical and cultural contexts in which institutions operate (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie further emphasizes the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, suggesting that they should be seen as indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians. She argues that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions, and that shared beliefs and cultural factors play a significant role in institutional development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional dynamics and the need to consider a wide range of factors, including cultural and historical contexts, when analyzing the role of institutions in economic growth.

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the varying effects of colonialism on institutional development. Acemoglu et al. show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were critical to economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). Hopkins adds that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, underscoring the practical implications of understanding institutional dynamics (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.9) shows that institutions are central to economic development and that their impact can be measured through historical and empirical evidence.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonialism, whether extractive or inclusive, has had a lasting impact on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts and cultural factors are significant in shaping institutional frameworks (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of economic outcomes in regions with different types of institutions, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This evidence shows that inclusive institutions, which enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, lead to higher levels of economic development compared to extractive institutions.
