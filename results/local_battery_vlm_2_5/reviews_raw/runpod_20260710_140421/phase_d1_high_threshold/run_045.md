The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. The stakes are high: understanding this role can help explain why some regions thrive while others stagnate. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and rule of law are crucial for economic development. Others posit that the reversal of fortune in economic growth among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Still others suggest that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, explain core economic institutions. The key point is that the impact of institutions on economic growth is about their nature and the historical context in which they emerge. This is evident in the stark differences in economic outcomes between colonies that received extractive institutions and those that did not. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional frameworks established by European colonizers significantly shaped the long-run economic trajectories of their former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The type of institutions introduced by the Europeans varied widely, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast. The divergence in economic outcomes can be traced back to these initial institutional setups, which were influenced by European mortality rates and colonization strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth, a pattern that persisted even after decolonization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy continues to impact the economic performance of former colonies today. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that the influence of these institutions is not uniform across all former colonies, and other factors, such as population density and prosperity, also played a role in shaping the policies pursued by Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast, along with North, establish that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). The Glorious Revolution of 1688 in England marked a significant shift in political institutions, establishing a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the monarch and enhanced the authority of Parliament. This political transformation was crucial in securing property rights, as it created a credible commitment that the government would not arbitrarily confiscate private property. This credible commitment, in turn, fostered economic growth by encouraging investment and innovation. This financial revolution was not just about economic instruments but also about the political institutions that underpinned them, ensuring that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could not be easily changed by the sovereign. North and Weingast assert that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the impact of these institutional changes (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The implication is that the institutional framework in England, which included secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, was a critical factor in driving economic growth. North also notes that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges was a complex process (North 1989, p.6). The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that while secure property rights were essential, the broader economic and institutional environment also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Nunn, assert that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality have been shown to advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This institutional bias toward elites can be seen in the way factor endowments influenced the development of societies. While all New World economies initially had an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, the variation in other aspects of their factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). These differences in inequality were reinforced by institutions that favored the elite, limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. For instance, the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets facilitated trade, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, but these institutions also concentrated power and resources in the hands of a few, further entrenching inequality (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional framework restricted competition and shaped the course of development by influencing the evolution of economic institutions. The persistence of inequality, therefore, is deeply rooted in the institutional structures that emerged from the initial factor endowments and were reinforced over time.

The economic performance of African economies has been characterized by periods of growth, often tied to foreign trade and export markets, and not by persistent poverty (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These institutions played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes, as seen in the historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa, which reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, which often overlooked the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence. The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule further supports the idea that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the long-term picture of economic development unanswered. This highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. A better grasp of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, will help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) find that colonial rule in Africa had varied impacts on education development, with British rule being particularly conducive to formal education. This divergence in educational outcomes can be attributed to the institutional frameworks imposed by different colonial powers. British rule, for instance, introduced a set of social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education, leading to a higher demand for missionary education among African political leaders and families. This institutional framework, which included new cost-benefit calculations, fostered an environment where formal education was seen as a valuable investment. The legacy of colonial education policies extends beyond institutional frameworks. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade, a historical institution, caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and affects economic development. This mistrust, rooted in historical institutions, complicates the straightforward narrative of colonial education's benefits, suggesting that the long-term effects of colonial rule on African development are multifaceted and complex.

African women played a pivotal role in economic development during pre-colonial and colonial periods, significantly contributing to growth despite marginalization (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that further disadvantaged women. This institutional framework, however, often overlooked the contributions of women, who were systematically excluded from land ownership and economic opportunities. The marginalization of women's contributions, despite their significant role in agriculture and economic activities, underscores the need for a more inclusive institutional framework that acknowledges and supports their economic role.

Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective suggests that technological progress and the dissemination of knowledge are pivotal for long-term economic development. Kuznets highlights that technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized within societies. This interplay between technological changes and societal adaptations is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating the growth of useful knowledge. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that institutional progress is essential to complement technological progress, as the sustainability of economic improvement depends on both factors. This suggests that while technological advancements are key drivers of growth, the institutional framework must evolve to support and integrate these advancements effectively. Kuznets further stresses the importance of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, indicating that political stability and effective governance are critical for maintaining economic growth. This underscores the complex interplay between technological progress, institutional development, and political stability in shaping long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie and Austin argue that distributional conflicts are a crucial factor in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). They show that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were not merely products of efficiency considerations. Instead, they were deeply influenced by the power dynamics and political struggles between different social groups. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be understood as a result of landowners' efforts to maintain control over labor. Similarly, craft and merchant guilds served to protect the interests of their members against competition, often at the expense of overall economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions always evolve to maximize economic growth. Austin, for example, observes that colonial administrations sometimes adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, indicating that institutions can be shaped by the incentives created by the actions of various groups (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic has its limits. Ogilvie notes that the institutional framework of pre-industrial Europe was determined by distributional conflicts and broader cultural and political factors. This suggests that while distributional conflicts play a significant role, they are just one piece of a complex puzzle that includes a variety of other influences, such as cultural beliefs and political legitimacy.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic and political actions, thereby determining the trajectory of economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions, both of which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This demonstrates that institutions are continuously shaped by the actions of various groups, which in turn influence economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin highlights the dynamic nature of institutions by noting that colonial administrations often responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which shaped institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and economic actions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North, who argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.9). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is reinforced by Ogilvie, who contends that distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, in turn, influence economic outcomes by determining the distribution of resources and opportunities. Hopkins shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, indicating that the presence of poor institutions is a more critical factor in Africa's economic record (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that institutional reforms are essential for eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter for economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that affect economic and political actions (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. While Acemoglu et al. emphasize the role of colonization strategies and the establishment of extractive institutions, Ogilvie highlights the importance of distributional conflicts in shaping core economic institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
