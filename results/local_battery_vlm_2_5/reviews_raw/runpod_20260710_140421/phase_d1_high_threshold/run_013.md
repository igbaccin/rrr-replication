The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development literature. At stake is the understanding of how and why some nations prosper while others stagnate. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, coordinating behavior, and allocating resources. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions drive economic growth, and whether they do so independently or in conjunction with other factors. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, emphasizing their role in creating a stable environment for investment and innovation. Others posit that institutions interact with geographic, cultural, or technological factors to influence economic outcomes, suggesting a more complex interplay. Still others propose that institutions are merely one of several factors, and that their impact is contingent on other variables. The degree to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth is also a point of contention. This debate is crucial for policy implications, as it informs whether and how institutions should be targeted for reform to promote economic development. Institutions' role in economic growth is not straightforward, as their impact can be mediated by other factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The type of institutions Europeans established in their colonies was not uniform. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The differing institutional frameworks led to divergent economic trajectories. This finding is supported by Acemoglu et al., who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization, highlighting the central role of institutions in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The impact of these institutions varies across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth can vary based on local conditions and the specific policies implemented (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. North and Weingast note that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. The success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, which showed a remarkable commitment to rules and enforcement mechanisms (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework, which specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs. As a result, the costs per transaction fell, but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate grew, becoming a larger proportion of GNP. This growth was driven by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as initially thought, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth can be context-dependent.

The slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) arguing that the slave trades were a significant factor in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation. This temporal scope highlights the enduring impact of the slave trades on Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This observation underscores the importance of institutional factors in facilitating economic development, even in the context of the slave trades. The role of institutions in mitigating the adverse effects of the slave trades is complex. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement recent studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. These findings suggest that the adverse effects of the slave trades on economic development result from both the direct impact of the trade and the erosion of trust and social capital that it engendered.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The economic performance of African economies during colonial times reveals that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges the notion that African economies were uniformly stagnant during the colonial period. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) explain that the patterns of economic growth and decline in these economies were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. This pattern suggests that the economic performance of African economies during colonial times was highly responsive to external market conditions. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries due to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This variability underscores the complex interplay between local conditions and external factors in shaping economic outcomes.

The persistence of inequality in New World societies can be attributed to institutions that favored elites through various government policies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). One key mechanism through which these institutions operated was the restriction of competition, which allowed elites to maintain their dominant positions. This was achieved through policies that limited access to education and economic opportunities for the broader population. The benefits of formal education were not evenly distributed. In societies where elites controlled access to education, the broader population was often excluded from these opportunities, perpetuating the cycle of inequality. This pattern of elite control over education and economic opportunities had significant implications for the long-term development of these societies. It contributed to the persistence of inequality by limiting the ability of the broader population to compete economically and politically, thereby reinforcing the power of the elites.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, while the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests that institutions that were historically beneficial for growth in Europe were not adopted in Africa, and this failure is linked to the continent's poor economic record. This approach has limitations in testing quantitatively and explaining why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others. One specific mechanism that Austin (2008, p.15) identifies is that colonial administrations often altered their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the adoption of institutions is influenced by local dynamics. Hopkins (2009, p.8) supports this view by emphasizing the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The downstream implication of this mechanism is qualified by the observation that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous and still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This qualification suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a variety of factors beyond just institutional design. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is mediated by a range of historical, political, and social factors.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the evolution of property rights and transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that the development of institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs is crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) highlights the significance of the Bank of England's establishment and the financial revolution, which led to a decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights. This institutional framework fostered a long-term capital market and economic growth. Similarly, North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize the critical role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and facilitating market exchanges, which are essential for economic development. These insights underscore the importance of institutional design in creating an environment conducive to economic growth. The development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are key factors in reducing transaction costs and promoting economic development.

The institutional framework established by colonial powers significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional outcomes. In some colonies, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy has had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) adds that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have persisted, influencing the quality of public goods and the rule of law in post-colonial societies. The persistence of these institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic growth and development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted. All scholars agree that institutions matter (North 1989, p.6), but the precise mechanisms and historical factors influencing institutional development remain debated. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. Austin (2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the measurement of institutional quality in former colonies. This measurement shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
