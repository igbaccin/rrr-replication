The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic theory, with significant implications for understanding historical development and contemporary policy. The extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic and social interactions, can propel or hinder economic growth over extended periods is at stake. The live disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic performance, while others highlight the mediating role of institutional changes introduced by exogenous factors such as colonialism. Additionally, there is a debate on whether the efficiency of institutions or the distributional conflicts they address are the primary drivers of economic growth. This discourse is crucial for comprehending why some regions have experienced sustained economic development while others have stagnated. The resolution of these debates could provide valuable insights into the design of effective economic policies aimed at fostering long-term growth. The question of whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors or endogenous processes is pivotal in understanding the dynamics of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were established based on European mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies. In colonies with high European mortality, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This pattern contrasts with colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were established. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive nature of these institutions was designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional design was influenced by the political power dynamics of the time, where powerful groups might resist institutional changes that could threaten their political power or fail to directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework had profound implications for economic development, as it shaped the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast, together with North (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9), argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated the emergence of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The security of property rights, both in terms of ownership and over time, was critical to the development of a long-term capital market and economic growth. This institutional evolution reflects a political model where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues, a development driven by fiscal crises such as the Hundred Years' War. The development of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement mechanisms was crucial for economic growth. This institutional framework was also instrumental in facilitating market exchanges, leading to a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP as specialization and division of labor increased (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic trajectory of the country.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods and institutions in Africa is not solely determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation. He notes that while some African societies may be ethnically homogeneous, they can still be dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional development in Africa, where both internal dynamics and external influences play crucial roles. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further emphasize that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The new economic history of Africa, by focusing on institutions, has enabled a more nuanced understanding of the factors driving economic development in the region. This institutional focus is crucial for informing policy recommendations and institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) examine how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These institutions were shaped by historical factors that led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality was not merely a consequence of factor endowments but was entrenched through institutional mechanisms that favored the elite. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the way in which institutions evolved to protect the interests of the ruling class, thereby restricting economic opportunities for the majority. This institutional framework not only limited competition but also stifled innovation and economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. The uneven distribution of the fruits of early modern development indicates that institutional changes were pivotal in determining economic outcomes. This uneven distribution of economic benefits underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as they determine who benefits from economic development and who is left behind.

Austin critiques the institutional explanations of long-run economic growth by arguing that they oversimplify causation, compressing different historical periods and paths, and fail to account for distributional conflicts and other factors (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not adequately address the complexities of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience over time. He further suggests that colonial administrations, which sought fiscal security, changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic indicates that the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth was influenced by local contexts and power struggles. Ogilvie agrees that institutions are crucial for economic historians and argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). She suggests that these institutions were shaped by the interests of various groups and their struggles over resources. This perspective challenges the efficiency-based explanations that dominate much of the institutional literature. However, Ogilvie notes that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and requires a nuanced understanding of the historical and political contexts in which they operate. The critique of institutional explanations for long-run economic growth underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach that considers the diverse factors influencing institutional change and economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on economic development in Africa, offering an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment solely to institutions or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). One mechanism through which the slave trade impacted economic development was by fostering a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, which persists to this day, has significant implications for economic outcomes. This mistrust can impede economic development by reducing cooperation and hindering the establishment of effective institutions. Moreover, the culture of mistrust can affect various aspects of economic life, including international trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn highlights that the adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development extend beyond cultural factors. This disruption was particularly pronounced in regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, where the slave trade had a more significant impact on economic institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of the slave trade in Africa is complex, encompassing both cultural and institutional dimensions that continue to influence economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to different institutional outcomes in colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, for instance, had lasting effects on income per capita. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter because they determine the security of property rights and the costs of transactions (North 1989, p.9). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England, for example, dramatically reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This shows that the institutional framework set by colonial powers had profound and enduring effects on economic development.

The legacy of colonial institutions persists today, influencing economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonization directly affects current economic performance. This is evident in the significant impact of extractive institutions on income per capita in former colonies. Similarly, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that shared beliefs about property rights and political legitimacy can block the development of institutions that guarantee these rights, further impeding economic growth. The persistence of these institutions underscores their critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial policies on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay between historical periods and paths, and that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) also emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This suggests that the quality of institutions established during colonial rule continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies.

The historical evidence shows that the institutional legacy of colonialism is a key determinant of long-run economic growth.
