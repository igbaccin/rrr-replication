The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development. The stakes are high: understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth can inform policy decisions and economic strategies. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are often seen as either catalysts or constraints on economic development. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions primarily facilitate growth through efficient resource allocation or through power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Some scholars, such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), argue that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which significantly affected current income per capita. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. These conflicting views underscore the complexity of institutional impact on long-run economic growth. The debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes, particularly in the context of historical and contemporary development. The role of political institutions in securing property rights and fostering economic growth is a key area of inquiry, as evidenced by North and Weingast (1989, p.29).

Institutions, particularly financial ones, played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) assert that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and the advent of new financial instruments, were instrumental in fostering economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs reflected an increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) argues that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War exemplifies how political institutions can specify and enforce efficient property rights, thereby facilitating market exchanges and economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) extend this argument by highlighting the role of political institutions in governing public choice, which underpins secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, with institutions playing a secondary role in facilitating this process (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Institutions, then, are crucial for facilitating the efficient use of innovations and resolving conflicts arising from technological changes. This is because technological progress and economic growth are not always compatible with existing social structures and ideologies. Kuznets (1973, p.1) explains that the "modern technology" of the Industrial Revolution required significant institutional and ideological adjustments, such as the shift from rural to urban life and the adoption of new family patterns. This is because rent-seeking behavior can erode the benefits of technological progress over time. This suggests that while institutions are important for facilitating technological progress and economic growth, their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to changing circumstances and resolve conflicts without excessive costs. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, were extractive in nature. Acemoglu et al. note that European colonists established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to quickly transfer resources back to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions persisted over time and had a lasting impact on the economic development of the colonies. This institutional framework, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic growth, led to significant differences in current income per capita among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. also highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today. Acemoglu et al. caution that the persistence of these institutions does not necessarily mean that they are immutable. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, indicating that institutional changes can have profound effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Therefore, understanding the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is crucial for appreciating the enduring impact of historical policies on contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) share the view that the literature on African economic development and colonial legacies explores how institutional changes influenced public goods provision. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa focuses on how institutions affect development, noting that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared focus on institutions and development suggests that institutional reforms can significantly impact economic outcomes. The extent to which these institutional changes can lead to long-run economic growth remains a complex issue. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Broadberry and Gupta's analysis of the economic divergence between Europe and Asia, together with Broadberry and Gardner’s historical national accounting, provides a nuanced understanding of how regional economic growth patterns emerged over time (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This divergence was partly driven by the role of merchant distribution and finance in north-western Europe, which facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector. This highlights the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic growth, as the service sector played a crucial role in early modern development. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The uneven distribution of economic benefits suggests that the fruits of early modern development were not uniformly shared. Broadberry and Gupta argue that the high productivity in north-western Europe resulted from significant institutional changes that drove investment in agriculture. This investment, in turn, led to high agricultural labour productivity, reinforcing the economic divergence between Europe and Asia (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This perspective underscores the complex interplay between institutional factors and economic outcomes, illustrating how institutional changes can significantly impact long-run economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These societies, characterized by extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power, often implemented policies that favored the elite, such as land grants, monopolies, and restrictions on labor mobility. This dynamic was particularly evident in Latin America, where the colonial institutions established by the Spanish and Portuguese often perpetuated inequality by concentrating resources and power in the hands of a small elite. The persistence of these institutions over time reinforced existing inequalities, as they restricted opportunities for social mobility and economic advancement for the majority of the population. The persistence of inequality in Latin America is a result of these institutional arrangements that favored the elite, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the broader population.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) show that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for fostering economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England provides a clear example of how institutional changes can drive economic development. The success of these institutions in England highlights the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights in promoting long-run economic growth. This is further supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. The decline in transaction costs and the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges are direct results of these institutional changes, which have significantly impacted economic growth. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is also crucial. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the belief that these institutions were designed for efficiency and highlights the importance of power dynamics and distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. The shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights, as well as the system of property rights and contract enforcement, have significantly impacted economic outcomes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the persistent effects of colonial institutions in Latin America, the importance of secure property rights and credible commitments in England, and the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The specific historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly impacted long-run economic development.
