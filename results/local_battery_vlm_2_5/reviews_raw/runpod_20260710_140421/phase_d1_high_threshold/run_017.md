The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth of nations. At the heart of this debate lies the tension between institutional determinism and other competing explanations for economic growth. Institutional determinism posits that the quality of institutions, such as property rights, rule of law, and effective governance, is the primary driver of long-term economic performance. This perspective argues that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing investment, innovation, and the efficient allocation of resources. Alternative explanations challenge this view, emphasizing factors such as geography, culture, and the historical legacies of colonialism. These rival causes suggest that economic growth can be driven by forces independent of institutional frameworks, such as the natural endowments of a region or the cultural beliefs of its people. The interplay between these competing explanations highlights the complexity of economic development and underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions matter because they provide the framework within which economic actors operate, influencing their decisions and behaviors in ways that can either foster or hinder growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They contend that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks based on their settlement strategies. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled in large numbers, institutions were designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The implications of these institutions are profound, as they not only influenced economic outcomes but also shaped the political and social landscapes of these regions.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the advent of new financial instruments significantly lowered transaction costs. These institutions reflected an increased security of property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, illustrating how political institutions evolved to support economic growth. This evolution involved more than just economic exchange rules; it also involved the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. A critical political factor in this process is the regime's commitment to these rules, which underpins economic growth and market development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.6) qualifies this by noting that as economies grow, the transaction sector becomes a larger proportion of GNP, reflecting increasing specialization and division of labor. This implies that while financial institutions and secure property rights are crucial, their impact on economic growth is also shaped by the broader economic context and the evolving nature of transactions. The development of efficient property rights and their enforcement through political institutions is a key driver of economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as argued by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges conventional views on serfdom and guilds, suggesting that these institutions were shaped by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. The institutions of serfdom and the community, for instance, were often designed to maintain the status quo and control labor. Similarly, craft guilds and merchant guilds were structured to protect the interests of their members, often at the expense of broader economic efficiency. This focus on distributional conflicts highlights the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions emerge and evolve. The role of distributional conflicts extends beyond pre-industrial Europe. In the New World, Sokoloff and Engerman show that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This pattern suggests that the institutions designed to manage distributional conflicts can have lasting effects on economic development, shaping not only the immediate outcomes but also the long-term trajectory of growth. For example, the persistence of inequality in Latin America can be traced back to the institutions that were put in place during the colonial period, which favored the interests of the elite and restricted economic opportunities for the majority. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the power dynamics and distributional conflicts of the societies in which they operate.

Mokyr and Kuznets both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks, highlighting the critical role of innovation in long-term economic development. The process of technological diffusion and the adoption of new technologies are central to this perspective, where the interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial. Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is necessary to complement technological progress and ensure the sustainability of economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Mokyr also acknowledges that this growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress, indicating that while technological advancements drive growth, institutional stability is vital for maintaining this growth. This perspective underscores that while institutions are important, they are secondary to the technological and intellectual advancements that drive long-term economic growth. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to support and sustain technological progress.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the view that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development was not uniform, as colonial policies were negotiated and imposed in diverse ways (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). One specific mechanism through which colonial rule influenced institutional change was the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This shift in benefits encouraged African families and political leaders to invest in formal education, which in turn shaped the economic landscape. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this implication by noting that the timing and scale of economic growth in African countries varied due to a range of factors, including local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores that while colonial policies played a significant role in shaping institutions, they were not the sole determinant of economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting social trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust channel is at least twice as large as the external channel, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial governments, when not competing for African labor, often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which sometimes aligned with the interests of colonial administrations seeking fiscal security. This suggests that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were responsive to local economic and political actions, which could have mitigated some of the mistrust engendered by the slave trade. The persistence of mistrust indicates that the institutional changes were insufficient to fully overcome the deep-seated cultural impacts of the slave trade.

Institutions, as the structural rules governing interactions, play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions define the rules of the game, influencing incentives, investment decisions, and economic outcomes. North (1989, p.6) argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth. They reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and increasing economic efficiency. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework, by enhancing the security of property rights, fostered long-term capital markets and economic growth. The Bank of England's establishment illustrates how institutions can lower transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) underscores that colonial administrations, when not competing for African labor, often encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning with their fiscal security interests. This institutional response to local economic and political actions mitigated some mistrust, highlighting the adaptability of institutions to local contexts. The persistence of mistrust indicates that institutional changes were insufficient to fully overcome the deep-seated cultural impacts of the slave trade.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by their influence on the distribution of power and resources. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) supports this perspective, arguing that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. The persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies underscores the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes, illustrating the long-term impact of historical institutions on growth. Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development. The new economic history of Africa shows that institutions matter because they are vital for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; North 1989, p.6) is that institutions shape long-run economic growth through their influence on property rights, transaction costs, and investment incentives. The disagreement concerns whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions are often extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Historical evidence from colonial administrations shows that institutions can adapt to local economic and political actions, mitigating some mistrust. The persistence of mistrust indicates that institutional changes were insufficient to fully overcome the deep-seated cultural impacts of the slave trade. The measurement of institutional quality and the comparison of economic outcomes across different historical periods and regions settle the disagreement. The new economic history of Africa provides a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
