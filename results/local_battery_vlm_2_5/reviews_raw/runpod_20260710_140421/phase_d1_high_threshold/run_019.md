The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. The live disagreement revolves around the primacy of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, or economic policy. One position emphasizes the direct and causal role of institutions in economic growth, arguing that institutional quality determines the efficiency of economic interactions and the incentives for investment. This view is often pitted against perspectives that highlight the importance of geographical factors, cultural attributes, or specific economic policies in driving long-run economic performance. The debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs whether institutional reforms or other interventions should be prioritized to foster economic development. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with various scholars offering nuanced arguments that intersect and diverge on key points. The extent to which institutions shape long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with some scholars arguing that institutional quality is the primary determinant of economic performance, while others contend that geographical factors, cultural attributes, or specific economic policies play a more significant role. This debate highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of the role of institutions in economic development, as it informs policy decisions and interventions aimed at fostering growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This institutional evolution in England, which fostered secure property rights, was a critical factor in the economic growth of the colonies, as noted by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments, both of which reflect increased security of property rights, were critical to the English financial revolution. This revolution reduced transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and driving economic growth. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War exemplifies how political institutions can specify and enforce efficient property rights. These institutions were pivotal in transforming the English economy, as evidenced by the striking improvements in capital markets. The development of economic institutions that facilitated market exchanges was a direct result of these political changes, leading to a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as seen in the evidence from capital markets. This success was underpinned by the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange, highlighting the critical role of political factors in economic growth. However, North (1989, p.6) notes that while the costs per transaction were falling, the size of the transactions sector was a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that the benefits of secure property rights and efficient institutions extended beyond immediate economic gains, fostering long-term economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, a shared finding of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions that hindered growth. These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often resulted in extractive policies that deterred economic development. For example, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented institutions that facilitated rapid resource extraction, leading to underdevelopment in the long run. This dynamic is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where extractive institutions persisted and significantly impacted current income per capita. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions is robust across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This robustness suggests that the institutional framework introduced by colonial powers had a profound and lasting effect on economic outcomes, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies for generations.

This institutional framework was deeply influenced by distributional conflicts in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were products of power struggles and elite interests. This perspective highlights the role of social and political power dynamics in shaping economic institutions. The persistence of these institutions can be understood through the lens of elite capture, where dominant groups maintain their privileged positions by controlling the institutional framework. However, the path dependency of these institutions means that once established, they can be difficult to change, even in the face of new economic realities. This is because institutions often have self-reinforcing mechanisms, such as legal frameworks and cultural norms, that support their continued existence. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that to fully understand the role of institutions in economic growth, it is essential to consider the broader social and political contexts in which they operate. This means looking beyond economic efficiency and considering the distributional consequences of institutions. This approach reveals that institutions are not merely tools for economic growth but are deeply embedded in social and political structures, reflecting and reinforcing power dynamics.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) challenge this view, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth was more nuanced than previously thought. This pattern is evident in colonial Nigeria, where Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to hypotheses that predict lower production due to colonial extraction (Austin 2008, p.15). These findings indicate that colonial institutions were not monolithic but rather adapted to local conditions, suggesting that African economies were not universally stunted by colonial rule. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this narrative by showing that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. However, the shifts in expenditure towards military spending and subsidies in the post-independence era highlight the complex and often conflicting priorities of African governments, which had to balance domestic pressures with regional instability (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that while colonial institutions adapted to local conditions, post-independence policies were shaped by a myriad of factors, including regional instability and domestic dissent.

Frankema and van Waijenburg, together with Broadberry and Gardner, reassess African economic history by highlighting periods of growth in per capita income (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This challenges the notion of persistent poverty due to structural impediments. One mechanism through which growth was achieved is the expansion of foreign trade and export production. Broadberry and Gardner show that patterns of economic growth in many African economies were tightly linked to the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This indicates that African economies were integrated into global markets and were capable of responding to international demand, which drove growth in per capita income. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies further supports this, demonstrating that economic development was not uniformly stagnant but varied significantly over time and across regions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). However, the variability in growth patterns across different countries and regions suggests that local conditions, including transport costs and political institutions, played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. This underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and geographical conditions that influenced African economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa have had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development. This mistrust is a key channel through which the slave trades have affected economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) noting that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law. This internalized mistrust has had profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for productive economic activity. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates the economic implications of the slave trades by highlighting the role of centralized governance and well-developed markets in facilitating trade, suggesting that the disruption caused by the slave trades impeded the development of such institutions. This disruption is particularly relevant given that the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This long-term disruption not only impeded the development of economic institutions but also eroded the social fabric necessary for sustained economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both highlight that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological progress, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets underscores that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed "epochal innovations," constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their integration into the economy. Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of not just technological progress but also the institutional frameworks that support and disseminate this knowledge. Mokyr further notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by robust institutions that can manage and disseminate this knowledge effectively. Kuznets complements this view by highlighting the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for maintaining the political fabric of society and ensuring modern economic growth. This implies that the state's role in managing technological progress and institutional adjustments is pivotal for sustained economic development.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development and dissemination of technological advancements. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights that the type of institutions established during colonization had a lasting impact on economic outcomes. North complements this view by emphasizing that institutions matter in economic growth, as they reflect increased security of property rights and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in England exemplify how institutional changes can lead to economic growth by reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9).

The precise remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups versus exogenous cultural factors. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This view contrasts with North's emphasis on the role of institutions in specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, supports North's view that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This evidence settles the disagreement by showing that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups can lead to economic growth, even in the absence of exogenous cultural factors.
