The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue that lies at the heart of debates over the determinants of prosperity. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions and theories of economic development. There are two main positions. One position argues that institutions are central to economic growth. This position holds that institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and ensure secure economic transactions, are crucial for fostering economic development. The opposing position contends that institutions are not the primary drivers of economic growth and instead attributes growth to other factors, such as population density and savings. This position suggests that institutions may be important, but they are not the key determinants of long-run economic growth. For example, Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or if other factors, such as population density and savings, play a more significant role.

The first position, which emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, is supported by several key studies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others received extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This approach suggests that the type of institutions established during colonialism had a lasting impact on the economic development of these regions. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective highlights the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and creating a secure environment for economic activity. One implication of this position is that the type of institutions established in a region can have long-lasting effects on its economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of these regions. In areas where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they implemented extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development. These institutions served to enrich the colonizers at the expense of the colonized populations, while not promoting long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these extractive institutions persisted long after the colonial period, continuing to hinder economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This persistence is crucial because it highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, suggesting that the type of institutions established during colonialism could significantly influence a region's ability to industrialize and grow (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This qualification underscores the complex dynamics at play, where the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers can have lasting effects, but these effects are mediated by subsequent political and economic developments. The legacy of colonial institutions thus remains a critical factor in understanding the long-run economic growth trajectories of former colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 were critical in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional change, driven by fiscal crises, facilitated the emergence of a long-term capital market, which was essential for economic growth. The institutional framework that evolved in England during this period encompassed economic rules and the political structures that enforced and maintained these rules, ensuring that the regime was committed to them (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political commitment to these rules was crucial for economic development, as it provided a stable environment for market exchanges and investment. This stability was reflected in the growth of the transactions sector, which became an increasingly significant proportion of the economy (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast also acknowledge that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as traditionally interpreted. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that understanding African poverty requires a deeper exploration of historical determinants, particularly the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has limited the ability to assess the extent to which historical conditions have influenced African economic development. This gap in data has hindered efforts to understand the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economies, making it difficult to determine whether specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied across African countries due to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations suggest that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by a multitude of factors. The complex interplay between institutions and other determinants highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth in Africa.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were, in part, designed to protect the interests of elites and maintain social hierarchies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to the New World, showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, were structured to advantage elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, which in turn shaped long-run economic growth. The impact of these institutions is not uniform, and their effects can be qualified by local conditions. For instance, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that the persistence of certain institutions can be influenced by the beliefs and values of the society, which can either reinforce or challenge the existing power structures. The interplay between distributional conflicts and local beliefs thus shapes the economic institutions that influence long-run economic growth. This dynamic highlights the complex nature of institutional development and its impact on economic outcomes, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of these factors is crucial for grasping the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a detrimental impact on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This adverse effect extended beyond economic outcomes, affecting the social fabric, as the slave trades disrupted local communities and weakened institutions. The disruption of local communities and the weakening of institutions led to a persistent culture of mistrust, which hindered economic development by making it difficult for individuals to engage in cooperative activities. The persistence of this mistrust is evident in the reduced levels of trust observed in contemporary Africa, highlighting the enduring impact of historical events on current economic outcomes. The slave trades' detrimental effects on economic development suggest that the institutional factors highlighted in other streams may not be the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective highlights that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress are essential for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further explains that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for modern economic growth. This perspective underscores the importance of technological advancements in driving economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on complementary institutional progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and enforcement mechanisms that govern economic activities. The development of secure property rights, for instance, is crucial for fostering economic growth. This is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the financial revolution it sparked, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and encouraged long-term investments, thereby driving economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice, such as those in seventeenth-century England, further underscores the importance of credible commitments and secure property rights in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions provided a stable environment that encouraged investment and innovation, leading to sustained economic growth. The historical evidence from capital markets shows that these institutional changes were instrumental in fostering economic development, highlighting the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise measurement of this impact can be seen in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was pivotal in determining the economic trajectories of these colonies. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's role in reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security, coupled with the impact of colonial institutions on long-run development, settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth.
