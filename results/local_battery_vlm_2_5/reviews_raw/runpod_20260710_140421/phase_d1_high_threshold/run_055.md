The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules (constitutions, laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms, conventions, and self-imposed codes of conduct), are pivotal in economic development. The central debate revolves around the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors such as geography or culture, determine a nation's economic trajectory. One position posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, shaping incentives, reducing uncertainty, and facilitating economic transactions. This view is supported by studies that highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective governance in fostering economic development. A different perspective posits that institutional development is a byproduct of other underlying factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographic endowments. This perspective challenges the primacy of institutions, asserting that they are secondary to these deeper determinants. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the cause or the effect of economic growth, with significant implications for policy and theoretical understanding. The institutions-first view advocates for policy interventions that focus on institutional reforms to spur economic growth, while the rival view suggests that such reforms are less likely to succeed without addressing the underlying cultural or geographic factors. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or merely a reflection of deeper determinants remains unresolved, with significant implications for economic theory and policy. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The English political institutions specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, which should have shown up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. This dynamic would multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges, as increasing specialization and division of labor would result in a growing proportion of GNP being devoted to the transactions sector. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) demonstrate that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor underpinning economic growth and the development of markets. North (1989, p.6) also notes that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP. This suggests that while the institutions governing public choice were crucial, the overall economic impact was also influenced by the increasing complexity and volume of market exchanges.

These institutions, shaped by colonial powers, continue to influence economic trajectories today. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that European colonizers implemented diverse institutional frameworks depending on local mortality rates and settlement patterns. This resulted in either extractive or growth-enhancing institutions. Extractive institutions, designed to maximize resource extraction for the colonizers' benefit, were more likely to be established in regions with high mortality rates. These institutions impeded long-term economic development by failing to encourage investment or enforce property rights. The extractive nature of these institutions was a deliberate design to maximize colonial rents, not merely a function of geography. This pattern of institutional design was evident in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where European colonizers established states primarily to extract resources. Regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework fostered economic growth and development by creating an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. The persistence of these institutional legacies beyond the colonial period means that former colonies with extractive institutions continue to face economic challenges. The long-term effects of these institutions are evident in the current economic disparities among former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) agree that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This growth is about the accumulation of knowledge and the institutional adjustments that facilitate its efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.6) highlights that modern economic growth requires resolving growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs, emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in peacefully managing these conflicts. This institutional framework is crucial for sustaining economic growth driven by technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, institutional adjustments are necessary to harness this progress effectively. The interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is a dynamic process that shapes long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). In pre-industrial Europe, the persistence of serfdom can be attributed to the landowners' interests in maintaining a stable labor force, while craft and merchant guilds were formed to protect the economic interests of their members against competition. This institutional framework, driven by distributional conflicts, had a profound impact on economic growth. The guilds, for example, restricted entry and innovation, which could stifle economic dynamism. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that these institutions also provided a measure of stability and predictability, which could foster long-term economic growth by reducing uncertainty. This perspective underscores the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and economic institutions, where the latter are shaped by the underlying power dynamics and interests of different social groups.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Frankema 2012, p.15) focus on the role of women and educational policies under British rule in African economic development. They highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, often overlooked in traditional narratives. Women were integral to agricultural production, frequently cultivating marginal lands for food farming. This role was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law in ways that often privileged men over women. Despite this, women's economic activities were pivotal, as seen in the cocoa revolution in the Gold Coast, where women's labor propelled the region to become the world's leading cocoa producer by 1911. This underscores the importance of women’s economic contributions, even within systems that marginalized them. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African families and political leaders. This educational development, coupled with women's economic activities, laid a foundation for long-term economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often marginalized women, highlights a tension between institutional changes and their impact on different social groups. This tension suggests that while institutions can foster economic growth, their effects are not uniform and can vary significantly based on social dynamics.

Institutional reforms have been proposed as a powerful tool to alleviate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform these reforms, highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Simson (2019, p.2) elaborates on this by showing that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not as unproductive as commonly thought. Simson (2019, p.2) points out that the growth in military spending and the high debt burden in countries like Kenya and Tanzania were responses to regional instability and domestic dissent. The shift in military spending indicates a strategic reallocation of resources, which can be seen as a form of institutional adaptation to changing security dynamics. This nuanced view of institutional effectiveness suggests that reforms must be tailored to the specific context and challenges of African countries.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and by providing incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. For example, the extractive institutions set up in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas the institutions in places like the United States encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North and Weingast also note that the success of these institutional changes is evident in the capital markets, underscoring the importance of credible commitment in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions are shaped by the political dynamics of the time, which can lead to the creation of institutions that do not necessarily encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie suggests that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She argues that institutions like serfdom and guilds were shaped by the power dynamics and shared beliefs within societies, rather than by efficiency considerations alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of African countries. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He highlights that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The effectiveness of institutions in Africa is determined by their form, function, and the extent to which they can provide incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This nuanced view of institutional effectiveness suggests that reforms must be tailored to the specific context and challenges of African countries. The evidence from capital markets shows that the institutional changes in England were not so revolutionary after all, highlighting the importance of credible commitment in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the remaining disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that this reversal was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic growth and highlights the need for tailored institutional reforms that can provide incentives for investment and promote long-run economic development.
