The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can influence economic outcomes by affecting the incentives and constraints that individuals and firms face. This question is at stake intellectually because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive economic progress over extended periods. One live disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions that promote growth and the causal pathways through which they operate. Some scholars emphasize the importance of inclusive institutions that provide secure property rights and enforce contracts, while others highlight the role of extractive institutions that concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few. Additionally, there is debate over whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they are shaped by cultural beliefs or political struggles. This disagreement is significant because it informs policy recommendations and interventions aimed at fostering economic development. The question of whether institutions matter for long-run economic growth, and if so, how, is thus critical to understanding the dynamics of economic development and the potential for policy to influence it. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is deeply intertwined with the question of how historical legacies shape contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of specific institutions, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were pivotal for long-run economic growth. These institutions facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting the increased security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development was crucial for fostering a long-term capital market, which in turn drove economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and enhance market efficiency. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the success of these institutions relied on the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights and maintaining a stable political environment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework underpinned the growth of capital markets and economic development. This suggests that while specific institutions like the Bank of England were instrumental, the broader institutional environment and political stability played a critical role in economic development. The implications of these findings are significant, as they highlight the importance of institutional design and political commitment in fostering long-run economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements are not just about new inventions; they require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use and diffusion. Kuznets highlights that the pace and impact of technological changes vary across regions and periods, necessitating corresponding adjustments in institutions and ideologies. This interplay is crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth. Mokyr elaborates on this idea by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This underscores the importance of a supportive institutional environment in harnessing the full potential of technological progress. McCloskey (2015, p.3) adds a nuanced perspective by arguing that the growth of useful knowledge involves technological innovation and ideological shifts that permit open experimentation and dialogue. This ideological permissiveness is crucial for the uptake of new techniques and institutions, highlighting that the growth of useful knowledge is intrinsically linked to broader societal changes. This suggests that while technological progress is fundamental, it is the broader ideological and institutional environment that ultimately determines the sustainability and impact of economic growth.

This perspective challenges the notion that institutional design is solely driven by efficiency considerations, offering an alternative view on the determinants of long-run economic growth. Ogilvie explains the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds in pre-industrial Europe through the power struggles between different social groups. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who highlight how the factor endowments of New World societies led to extreme inequality, which in turn shaped institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies. This mechanism of distributional conflict influencing institutional development is particularly evident in the persistence of inequality in societies where elites had the power to shape institutions in their favor. This perspective is qualified by the recognition that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific paths of institutional development can vary significantly based on the initial conditions and power dynamics of each society. For example, the transition from serfdom to free labor in some regions of Europe was influenced by the ability of elites to maintain their power through institutional changes that preserved their economic interests. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “.” Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) report, “These differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recentiy attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally (North, 1988; Engerman and Sokoloff, 1997; Coatsworth 1993, 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, 2000; Engerman, Haber and Sokoloff, 2000).”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) explain that European powers adopted distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In regions with lower mortality rates, Europeans established inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-term growth by failing to promote investment and economic activity. The persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions continuing to impede growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-run development of these regions. This interaction highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth, suggesting that the legacy of colonial policies continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the new economic history of Africa highlights the role of both African initiatives and exogenous factors in driving institutional change, which in turn influenced the quality of public goods provided by African governments. This perspective emphasizes that African economies were active agents in shaping their own development trajectories. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for economic advancement (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional change was not uniform across Africa, as different regions experienced varying levels of economic and social development due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions, while significant, did not uniformly impede economic growth. Rather, the interaction between colonial policies and local initiatives created a complex landscape of economic development, where some regions were able to achieve higher levels of prosperity despite the challenges posed by colonial rule.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that structural changes in Europe, particularly those following the Black Death, played a significant role in shaping long-term economic performance. The Black Death led to a significant shift in economic structures, notably in urbanization rates and the wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk (2007, p.16) shows that the shift in urbanization rates was a key indicator of productivity and economic development, which was influenced by institutional flexibility. This institutional flexibility allowed new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to flourish, particularly in regions like Holland. van Zanden (2009, p.3) supports this by noting that the skill premium, determined by factors such as training costs and institutional barriers, influenced long-term economic performance. The guilds, while beneficial for skill transfer, also formed local cartels that restricted entry and kept wages above market-clearing levels. This dual role of institutions highlights the complexity of their impact on economic growth. This interplay between structural changes and institutional frameworks underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development in Europe.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Simson 2019, p.2) examine various facets of African economic development and policy impacts, highlighting the multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa. They emphasize the importance of understanding the long-term economic development and the distribution of income to assess future growth possibilities. This perspective is crucial for evaluating the structural effects of historical and geographical conditions on African welfare development. Simson (2019, p.2) extends this view by examining the post-independence budget policies in East Africa, revealing that these policies were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly portrayed. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that neopatrimonial tendencies, where government resources are treated as private property, have significant political and economic consequences. This dynamic is evident in countries like Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 per cent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. Furthermore, military spending grew significantly in response to regional instability and domestic dissent, highlighting the complex interplay between political instability and economic policy. This pattern underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how political and economic factors interact to shape economic outcomes in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). One specific mechanism that Austin and Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) jointly establish is the impact of colonial policies on institutional development. Austin highlights that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This dynamic is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This adjustment suggests that colonial institutions evolved in response to local conditions, and that the resulting institutions could either enhance or hinder long-term economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this view by showing that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists and affects contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of this mistrust underscores the long-term impact of historical legacies on institutional development and economic performance. This interplay between colonial policies, institutional evolution, and economic outcomes illustrates the complex and enduring effects of historical legacies on contemporary economic issues.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the economic incentives and constraints that drive development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that the nature of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacts current economic outcomes, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive institutions fostering it. These findings are supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that the creation of institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs is crucial for economic growth. The shared emphasis on the long-term effects of institutional frameworks underscores the enduring influence of historical legacies on contemporary economic performance.

The persistence of institutional legacies is further illustrated by the impact of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective is complemented by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin contends that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic interplay between colonial policies and local conditions. This interplay is evident in the varying economic outcomes of former colonies, where the long-term effects of colonial institutions continue to shape contemporary economic trajectories.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who emphasizes the importance of institutions that specify and enforce property rights in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. The shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.9) that institutions matter in economic growth, even in pre-industrial contexts, underscores the universal relevance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutional legacies is evident in the enduring economic disparities among former colonies, where the long-term effects of colonial institutions continue to shape contemporary economic trajectories.

The historical evidence of extractive institutions in former colonies, such as those established by European settlers in the Congo and the Gold Coast, shows that these institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The measurement of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, further supports the argument that extractive institutions hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This historical evidence settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the long-term effects of colonial institutions are the primary drivers of contemporary economic disparities.
