The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is a pivotal one in economic history and development studies. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. The debate centers around the role of institutions in economic growth, with scholars advocating for different mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. On one side, some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that the quality of institutions determines the level of economic growth. On the other hand, other scholars suggest that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth, and that their effects are often mediated by other variables. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other economic and social factors to drive long-run economic growth. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not just academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic development. By understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, policymakers can design interventions that promote sustainable development and reduce poverty. Institutions can shape economic growth by determining the incentives for investment and innovation, which in turn drive long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the nature of colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, played a pivotal role in determining long-run economic growth in former colonies. European settlers adopted distinct colonization strategies based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This approach fostered economic development and growth. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic development, leading to persistent economic underperformance. This institutional framework, characterized by a focus on resource extraction and the absence of investment incentives, significantly hindered the economic development of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, shaping their current economic landscapes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth. These changes were pivotal because they reduced the cost of transactions, thereby enhancing the security of property rights over time. This institutional framework fostered a robust capital market and drove economic growth. This development was underpinned by political institutions that specified and enforced property rights, creating an environment conducive to market exchanges and economic growth. The Bank of England’s role in managing public debt and providing financial stability was instrumental in this process. The political stability and credible commitment to property rights in England, as opposed to France, played a significant role in this regard. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided the financial infrastructure necessary for long-term economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated economic exchanges and ensured that the rules governing these exchanges were enforced and could be adapted over time. This suggests that while institutions are critical, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent. The institutional developments in England, characterized by secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and McCloskey (2015, p.3) argue that the ideologies of Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were the primary drivers of economic change. The role of ideas in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the changing beliefs about the role of human agency in economic progress. McCloskey (2015, p.3) contends that the Industrial Enlightenment was marked by a shift in societal rhetoric, permitting experimentation and open dialogue about innovations. This ideological change allowed for the uptake of new techniques and institutions, fostering an environment where economic progress could flourish. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) underscores the importance of institutions as indispensable tools for economic historians, suggesting that the transformation of ideas into institutional frameworks was crucial for economic development. The ideological shift varied across Europe. McCloskey (2015, p.4) notes that even religious institutions, such as the Catholic Church, began to favor capitalism over socialism by the late nineteenth century, indicating a broader acceptance of economic liberalism. This shift suggests that the ideological underpinnings of economic growth permeated religious doctrines as well as secular institutions, reinforcing the idea that beliefs and ideologies played a pivotal role in shaping economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of knowledge, is central to sustained economic growth. Technology requires institutional adjustments to be efficiently implemented. Kuznets (1973, p.6) posits that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, thereby facilitating economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress. Without this complementary institutional development, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited.

The slave trade in Africa had a profound negative impact on economic development, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) finding that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development. This historical exploitation fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, significantly impeding economic growth. The slave trade's role in shaping economic development is multifaceted, with Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) showing that the slave trade's effect on trust stems from internal norms of mistrust as well as the external environment. This internal mistrust, at least twice as large as the external channel, highlights the deep-seated psychological and social impacts of the slave trade. Nunn (2008, p.19) elaborates that the economic and social institutions of local coastal societies were too undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade, implying that the slave trade exacerbated pre-existing institutional weaknesses. This historical context underscores the enduring legacy of the slave trade, which continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory.

Colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly British rule, fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). This phenomenon is evident in the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This educational development was tied to broader economic patterns, as the growth and shrinkage of economies were often linked to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

The literature on institutional reforms and African poverty, as presented by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18), explores how historical and contemporary factors influence development, but it does not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This variation suggests that the effectiveness of institutions is contingent on local contexts and historical trajectories. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This implies that understanding the historical context of institutional development is crucial for designing effective reforms. The role of institutions in long-run economic growth remains complex and multifaceted. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic trajectories over the long run.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to the establishment and enforcement of property rights, as well as the broader political and economic frameworks that support them. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both emphasize the critical importance of secure property rights in fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonists established different institutions based on mortality rates, with some colonies receiving extractive institutions that hindered growth, while others received institutions that encouraged investment and development. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. These institutions, by providing security for property rights, facilitated long-term capital markets and economic expansion.

The political dimensions of institutional development are also crucial. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both highlight how political institutions shape economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) posits that political institutions that specify and enforce property rights are essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) explain that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, which can hinder long-run growth. This political dimension underscores the complexity of institutional development, where institutions are deeply intertwined with political power dynamics.

Moreover, the historical context in which institutions are formed plays a significant role in their effectiveness. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both stress the importance of understanding the historical and cultural contexts in which institutions evolve. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complex historical processes that led to the adoption of different institutions in various countries. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions. This historical perspective is essential for understanding why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others, and how these institutions have shaped long-run economic trajectories.

The complexity of institutional development is further highlighted by the role of colonialism and its lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both address the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions, particularly those introduced by Europeans, significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that understanding the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. The legacy of colonial institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, continues to shape the economic outcomes of former colonies today. The historical evidence of colonial institutions shows that they have had a lasting impact on economic growth, with extractive institutions often hindering development and growth-promoting institutions fostering economic progress.
