The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. Institutions are the rules of the game in a society, encompassing formal rules such as laws and informal rules such as norms and conventions. A live disagreement exists on the extent to which institutions determine economic growth. One position asserts that institutions are pivotal in creating the environment that fosters economic development. This perspective emphasises that institutions shape economic outcomes by providing incentives for investment, protecting property rights, and ensuring the rule of law. The opposing position suggests that institutions play a secondary role, with other factors such as culture or geography being more influential. This disagreement is significant because it influences how policymakers approach economic development, particularly in regions striving to escape poverty. Understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth is essential for designing effective policies. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors. The causal relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving various historical, cultural, and economic factors. This review will explore how institutions shape long-run economic growth by examining the historical evidence and theoretical frameworks that support this relationship. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This argument is supported by the evidence that the institutions established in colonies were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is reinforced by North, who highlights that the development of new financial instruments and the creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were critical for economic growth, as they facilitated market exchanges and lowered costs per transaction, thereby fostering a growing economy (North 1989, p.6). Austin adds that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between colonial policies and local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The institutional legacy of colonialism, therefore, played a pivotal role in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies, with the nature of institutions established during this period continuing to influence economic outcomes long after independence.

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions shaped by inequality in New World societies have favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, creating a cycle where elites maintained their power through institutional control. This dynamic is further illuminated by Nunn and Wantchekon, who demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which has persisted and hindered economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The mistrust engendered by the slave trade has had a profound impact on economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and economic growth. The legacy of inequality and mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it creates a self-reinforcing cycle where mistrust inhibits the formation of institutions that promote economic growth, thereby perpetuating the cycle of inequality.

North and Weingast, together with Ogilvie, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Their work underscores that the political factors underpinning economic growth are critical, focusing on how rules governing economic exchange are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective highlights the importance of credible commitment in institutional development. North and Weingast demonstrate that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the performance of capital markets. Ogilvie further elaborates on the institutional structure, emphasizing the role of shared beliefs within a society's institutions. This shared belief structure can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on whether it supports or blocks institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. Furthermore, the evolution of institutions in England fostered a culture of secure property rights, which was instrumental in driving economic growth. Ogilvie's call for institutions to be an indispensable instrument in economic history research underscores the necessity of understanding how these institutions function and evolve over time (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This evolution involves efficiency and distributional conflicts, which shape the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The legacy of these institutional developments in Europe continues to influence economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema (2012, p.15) illustrates how the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework laid the groundwork for economic development by fostering education and skill development, which are crucial for long-term economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further supports this view by noting that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, highlighting the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this pattern by showing that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries due to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of local initiatives and endogenous factors in shaping economic outcomes, even within the broader context of colonial rule. The interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change in Africa thus reveals a nuanced picture of economic development, where both historical legacies and contemporary efforts contribute to long-run economic growth.

The economic divergence between Europe and Asia was marked by distinct productivity and development patterns before 1800, a finding shared by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1). Broadberry and Gupta note that high productivity in Europe's traded goods sector resulted from merchant distribution and finance, highlighting the role of the service sector in economic growth. This sector's contribution to growth was evident in the early modern period, as it is today. The uneven distribution of economic gains is suggested by the larger surplus yielded by skilled laborers' wages over basic food needs, compared to unskilled laborers. This pattern aligns with van Zanden's observation that economic development in Europe between 1500 and 1800 did not uniformly improve the standard of living. Van Zanden's analysis of wage data across European cities reveals that economic development, as measured by urbanization, did not correlate with changes in living standards. This finding challenges the notion of an 'industrious revolution' in Western Europe during this period. Van Zanden further notes that economic development shifted towards North Sea countries, with England becoming the most expensive country after 1740 due to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) question the role of geographical factors versus investment in southern Indian agriculture, suggesting that institutional change typically drives agricultural investment and productivity in Europe. This implies that while productivity patterns differed between Europe and Asia, the role of institutions in driving economic growth varied significantly between these regions. The interplay between productivity, institutional change, and economic development in Europe and Asia thus offers insights into the complex factors shaping long-run economic growth.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are crucial for the efficient use of innovations, which in turn drives long-run economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). He argues that technological progress, particularly epochal innovations, requires societal adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from these changes. For instance, the shift from a rural mode of life to an industrial one necessitates changes in family patterns and societal values. This interplay is not uniform across regions or time periods, highlighting the need for tailored institutional responses to technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) similarly emphasizes the role of institutions in complementing technological progress. He suggests that without institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, underscoring the importance of coordinating technological and institutional changes. The implication is that societies must innovate technologically and adapt institutionally to harness the full potential of these innovations. This underscores the complex and dynamic nature of the relationship between technological advancements and institutional adjustments in driving long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the creation and enforcement of property rights, which in turn affect investment and economic development. North argues that the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges is crucial for economic growth, as these institutions reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacts long-term economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings highlight that the nature of institutions, whether they are designed to maximize rents for a select few or to promote broad-based economic development, determines the trajectory of economic growth.

The historical context of colonialism provides a vivid example of how institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some creating inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic growth, while others implemented extractive institutions that hindered development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional variation, driven by factors such as mortality rates and the presence of European settlers, directly influenced the economic performance of these colonies over the long term (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin challenges the simplistic view that colonial institutions uniformly led to economic decline, arguing that the impact of colonialism varied across different regions and historical periods (Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional arrangements and their contextual factors. Austin also notes that colonial administrations sometimes adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, further illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by examining the financial and political developments that underpin economic progress. North highlights the significance of the English financial revolution, which included the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was pivotal in fostering economic growth by providing a stable financial framework that supported long-term capital markets. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts often drive the creation of core economic institutions, such as serfdom and guilds, in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective complements North's emphasis on the role of institutions in economic development by showing how power dynamics and social structures influence institutional design and, consequently, economic outcomes. The shared belief that institutions matter in economic growth is clear across these sources, but there is a remaining disagreement: whether the primary driver of institutional change is the efficiency of economic outcomes or the distributional conflicts within society. The historical evidence of the English financial revolution shows that the creation of institutions that reduce transaction costs and enhance property rights security is crucial for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This evidence supports the view that efficiency considerations play a significant role in institutional development. The case of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts shaped key economic institutions, underscores the importance of power dynamics in institutional change (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
