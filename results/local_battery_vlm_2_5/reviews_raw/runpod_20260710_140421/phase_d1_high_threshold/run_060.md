The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are primarily a driver of economic growth or merely a facilitator of other underlying factors. One position argues that institutions directly shape economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights, contract enforcement, and market exchanges, which are essential for economic development. Another position posits that institutions are less crucial than other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, in determining economic growth. This debate is significant because it informs policy decisions and economic theories, impacting how societies approach development and growth. North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) are among scholars who argue that institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, while Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) present alternative perspectives. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at fostering sustained economic development.

The extractive institutions established in colonies with high European mortality rates persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) describe how in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources to the metropole. This pattern of institutional development was influenced by the policies and strategies of European colonizers, as well as geographic factors. This institutional legacy continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) identify the creation of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments as pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, thereby driving long-run economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions like the Bank of England, which significantly reduced the cost of transacting and enhanced the security of property rights. The development of these institutions facilitated market exchanges by specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement, which in turn reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework addressed political factors governing how these rules were enforced and changed, in addition to economic exchange. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the regime's commitment to these rules, a critical political factor in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of these institutions on economic growth varies across different contexts. This indicates that while financial institutions and secure property rights are crucial, the specific impact of these institutions can vary depending on the broader political and economic context.

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) argues that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This growth involves institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, in addition to technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by highlighting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while institutions play a role, they are secondary to the growth of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. This complexity means that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional adjustments are necessary to fully realize the economic benefits of new knowledge.

Ogilvie argues that institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups, aiming to preserve their interests and privileges. This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that these institutions were designed to enhance economic efficiency. Austin highlights how colonial governments adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions, showing that institutions can evolve to maintain the status quo rather than drive growth. This evolution suggests that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were mechanisms for managing and mitigating distributional conflicts, in addition to tools for maximizing economic efficiency. Austin notes that the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments were influenced by the presence or absence of European settlers, indicating that external factors also played a role in shaping these institutions. This interplay between internal distributional conflicts and external influences underscores the complexity of institutional development in pre-industrial Europe.

The adverse effects of slave trades on economic development highlight a distinct pathway to underdevelopment that does not directly engage with institutional frameworks. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the long-term impact of slave trades can be seen in the economic underdevelopment of regions that experienced high levels of slave export. This impact is evident in the persistence of economic underdevelopment in regions that were heavily affected by the slave trades. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) elaborate on this by noting that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized many New World societies from their inception was a direct result of their factor endowments. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, leading to a vicious cycle of underdevelopment. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this perspective by highlighting that the economic institutions of regions with centralized governments and well-developed markets, such as the Kongo kingdom, were more conducive to trade and economic development. This suggests that while the slave trades had a detrimental effect on economic development, the presence of certain institutional factors could mitigate these effects. The presence of these factors in regions that experienced high levels of slave export could potentially explain the variation in economic development outcomes across these regions.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the posture that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. They jointly establish that economic development in Africa is shaped by the quality of institutions, which in turn are influenced by historical and geographical conditions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the development of effective institutions, as some ethnically diverse societies have managed to uphold the rule of law and create progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this by arguing that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that historical conditions have had a temporary effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Their implication is that the potential for future growth in Africa is significant, given the right institutional reforms. The new economic history of Africa also highlights that the effectiveness of these institutions depends on their ability to provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of African economic development to inform effective institutional reforms.

The institutional frameworks established by European colonizers in different regions had varying impacts on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, such as those set up in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Extractive states, like those in the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction is crucial for understanding the varying economic outcomes in different regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
