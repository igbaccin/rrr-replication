The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and what factors drive sustained prosperity or stagnation. One position posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in facilitating investment, enforcing property rights, and reducing transaction costs. This view is challenged by those who argue that institutions are merely one among many factors, and that other variables such as geography, culture, or initial conditions play equally significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic outcomes, and whether they are the cause or the effect of economic development. The central dispute revolves around the extent to which institutions can be credited for economic growth or if they are merely a byproduct of other underlying forces. This review synthesizes scholarly streams that address these questions, integrating insights from multiple perspectives to elucidate the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This institutional framework was established through a series of fiscal crises and political negotiations that culminated in the creation of the Bank of England in 1694. This financial revolution significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights and facilitating long-term capital markets. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) note that the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically lowered transaction costs. This decline reflects an increased security of property rights, which is critical for both capital markets and economic growth. The Bank of England, as a financial institution, played a crucial role in this process by providing a stable and secure environment for economic transactions, which in turn fostered economic growth. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by highlighting that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should manifest in falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP. This institutional framework ensured that the regime was committed to enforcing these rules, which North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue is a critical political factor. However, the impact of these institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different countries. This implies that while secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structure and economic policies also play a significant role in economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were shaped by the colonial strategies of European powers, which varied based on mortality rates and the economic potential of the colonies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic growth. In contrast, where mortality rates were lower and colonization was more permanent, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework fostered economic development and sustained growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism, rather than geographic factors. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a pivotal role in the long-run development of these colonies. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both the initial design of these institutions and the political dynamics that shape their evolution over time. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic outcomes in many former colonies today.

The impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) both emphasize that the legacy of colonial institutions in Africa is multifaceted, influenced by both the initial design of these institutions and the local responses they elicited. One specific mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped African economic development was the introduction of formal education systems. This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for subsequent economic development by providing a skilled workforce and fostering a culture of learning and innovation. However, the downstream implications of this educational legacy were not uniformly positive. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that while many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, the economic benefits of colonial education were often limited by persistent colonial economic policies that prioritized resource extraction over local industrial development. This highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on African economic development, shaping both the opportunities and constraints faced by post-colonial societies.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that these institutions were not merely responses to economic efficiency but were deeply rooted in the power dynamics and conflicts among different social groups. This perspective highlights how institutional structures were often designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of dominant elites. In pre-industrial Europe, for instance, craft guilds and merchant guilds were not just about regulating trades but also about controlling access to economic opportunities, thereby preserving the privileges of established groups. This distributional conflict mechanism is evident in the persistence of institutions that limited competition and innovation, which in turn influenced long-run economic growth. The distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions often led to rigid social structures that hindered economic progress. For instance, the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe was not solely due to agrarian efficiency but also because it served the interests of the landed nobility, who benefited from the cheap labor provided by serfs. This institutional rigidity had profound implications for economic development, as it limited the mobility of labor and the adoption of new technologies. However, the impact of these institutions was not uniform across regions. While in some areas, these institutions stifled growth, in others, they might have fostered stability that allowed for gradual economic development. This nuanced view is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who argue that the specific historical contexts and factor endowments in different regions influenced how these institutions evolved and their subsequent impact on economic growth. Thus, the legacy of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions continues to resonate in the long-run economic outcomes of different societies.

Nunn argues that Africa's underdevelopment is primarily due to its slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, rather than institutions (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who argue that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has obscured the long-term trends of economic development in Africa, which is a critical gap for understanding the impact of the slave trades (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). The slave trades, which operated through various mechanisms, had a profound impact on economic development. For example, the slave trades disrupted social and political institutions, leading to a lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets and trading networks. This disruption hindered the ability of African societies to engage in sustained trade with Europeans, which was crucial for economic growth. For instance, the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). However, the impact of the slave trades on African economic development was not uniform across regions. Austin argues that in some areas, the slave trades led to a decline in economic activity, while in others, they might have fostered stability that allowed for gradual economic development (Austin 2008, p.15).

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this point by highlighting the role of coordination failures and the need for institutional progress to complement technological progress. Without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would have been limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements can drive economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt to sustain this growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores the importance of the modern national state in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is crucial for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while institutions may not be the primary driver of economic growth, they play a critical role in sustaining the benefits of technological progress.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that institutions set up by European colonizers significantly influenced current income per capita in their former colonies. The rule of law and secure property rights, as emphasized by North (1989, p.6), are crucial for economic growth. These institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby fostering a more efficient economy. North (1989, p.9) further illustrates this point with the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and supported economic growth. This institutional framework is essential for sustaining the benefits of technological progress, as it provides the stability and security needed for long-term investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. This highlights the importance of institutional design in economic outcomes. The development of institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts is critical for economic growth, as shown by the experience of European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.8) both acknowledge the importance of institutions in economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, while Hopkins (2009, p.8) highlights that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment. This shared finding (Austin 2008, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The historical evidence of the Bank of England's impact on transaction costs and economic growth (North 1989, p.9) shows that institutions designed to enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are essential for economic development. The precise measurement of income per capita in former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) settles the disagreement by demonstrating that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, hinder long-run economic growth.
