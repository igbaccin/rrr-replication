The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Institutions, understood as the rules of the game in a society, shape economic incentives and constrain economic behavior. The intellectual stakes are high: institutions can either foster growth by encouraging investment and innovation or hinder it by creating barriers to economic activity. A live disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. One position emphasizes the role of extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, often at the expense of broader economic development. Another position highlights the importance of inclusive institutions, which distribute political power and economic opportunities more widely, thereby fostering sustained growth. A third position focuses on the path dependence of institutions, suggesting that historical legacies and initial conditions significantly shape the trajectory of institutional development and, consequently, economic growth. These competing explanations underscore the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions operate in different contexts. The question of whether institutions are a primary driver of economic growth or merely a secondary factor influenced by other determinants remains unresolved. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of colonial legacies, which have left enduring imprints on the institutional frameworks of many countries. This interplay is evident in the debate over whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive or adaptive, and how they influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions, which did not encourage investment or enforce the rule of law, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This pattern of institutional design had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) find that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all yield similar results in predicting economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) qualify this by highlighting that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in long-run development. The legacy of colonial institutions, therefore, continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies.

Institutions, by reducing transaction costs and enhancing property rights security, are pivotal in facilitating economic growth, as North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue. This institutional framework was critical for economic growth, as it allowed for more efficient market exchanges and a growing proportion of transactions in the economy. The political model outlined by North (1989, p.9) suggests that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of fiscal crises, indicating that institutional changes can emerge from specific historical contexts and have lasting economic impacts (North 1989, p.9). They argue that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets and the commitment of the regime to enforce these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This qualification implies that while institutions are critical, their impact on economic growth can be influenced by other factors, such as historical context and bureaucratic structures. The interplay between institutions and historical contexts, therefore, is essential for understanding long-run economic growth.

Mokyr contends that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective emphasizes that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental drivers of economic growth than institutional frameworks. Kuznets supports this view, highlighting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets notes that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, are termed epochal innovations. These innovations spread to substantial parts of the world, driving economic growth. This process is not without its challenges, as technological progress often conflicts with existing social structures. Kuznets observes that modern economic growth is possible only if these conflicts are resolved without excessive costs, underscoring the importance of peaceful resolution of growth-induced conflicts. Mokyr further elaborates on this point, recognizing the possibility of coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. He argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie and Pamuk highlight that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Pamuk 2007, p.23). These conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, drove the development of these institutions. For instance, the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This wage gap was a result of institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, which allowed for rapid economic responses to new opportunities. The institutional structures that emerged from these conflicts had significant implications for economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) points out that institutions are indispensable instruments in the toolkit of economic historians. They are crucial for understanding long-term economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.16) qualifies this by noting that urbanisation rates, a key indicator of economic development, varied significantly across Europe. This variation underscores the importance of institutional flexibility in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional landscape of Europe was thus not uniform, and the distributional conflicts that shaped these institutions had profound effects on regional economic trajectories. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland illustrates how economic institutions can facilitate or hinder growth, depending on how they resolve distributional conflicts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa had lasting detrimental impacts on economic development. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impeding economic growth. The adverse effects of the slave trade extend to contemporary economic underdevelopment. The slave trade's impact on economic development is mediated through its influence on social trust, which is a crucial factor for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade's legacy is a persistent force shaping current economic trajectories. This mistrust, which is deeply ingrained in African societies, has hindered economic development by undermining the social cohesion necessary for effective economic institutions and cooperation. The slave trade's impact on social trust is particularly damaging because trust is a fundamental component of economic development, facilitating trade, investment, and the functioning of markets. The long-term effects of the slave trade on economic development continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. This enduring impact of the slave trade challenges the notion that institutions are the sole fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, suggesting that historical events such as the slave trade can have lasting effects on economic development that are independent of institutional factors.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more effective in fostering formal education than other colonial powers. This effectiveness was driven by the unique social and political institutions implemented by the British, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. The British approach to education was an active policy that reshaped local incentives. The implementation of these institutions changed the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, thereby increasing the demand for missionary education. The British colonial administration's policies, which included the establishment of schools and the training of local teachers, created a more conducive environment for education. This institutional framework supported formal education and laid the groundwork for long-term economic development. The extent to which these educational institutions translated into economic growth is a subject of debate. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) qualify this point by noting that the persistence of inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power can hinder the translation of educational investments into economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner show that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies was more robust than previously thought, with many regions achieving per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This narrative is shared by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. The key mechanism driving this divergence was the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, underscoring the critical role of the service sector in economic growth. This institutional framework supported economic activity and laid the groundwork for sustained development. However, the benefits of this growth were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers experiencing a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) further elaborate that the growth patterns in these economies were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This institutional support for trade and economic activity suggests that the foundations for long-term economic development were being laid, even if the benefits were not uniformly shared.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) suggest that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth. They argue that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic was influenced by the colonial administrations' need for fiscal security and their responsiveness to local economic and political actions. This institutional adaptation highlights how colonial policies could be modified to align with local interests, suggesting that institutional frameworks were not static but responsive to economic incentives. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this idea by emphasizing the role of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) also acknowledges the complexity of institutional effectiveness, noting that ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity alone does not determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view challenges the notion that institutional quality is solely determined by ethnic composition, suggesting that other factors, such as political will and economic incentives, play crucial roles. Overall, this perspective underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of institutional development and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to influence the distribution of political and economic power, thereby shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers in their colonies had significant and lasting effects on economic development. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, long-run economic growth was more robust. In colonies where extractive institutions were imposed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, economic stagnation persisted. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they reduce transaction costs and provide secure property rights, which are critical for a long-term capital market and economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) shows that institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs are crucial for sustained economic development.
