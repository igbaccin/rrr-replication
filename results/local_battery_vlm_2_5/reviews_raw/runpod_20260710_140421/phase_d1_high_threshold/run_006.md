The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. One live disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, directly impacts economic growth by providing incentives for investment and innovation. Others posit that the efficiency of institutions is less critical than the distributional conflicts that shape them. Additionally, there are competing views on whether the impact of institutions is universal or contingent on specific historical and cultural contexts. The debate ultimately hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they operate in conjunction with other factors, such as cultural beliefs and geographical conditions. This review will explore these positions through the lens of different scholarly streams, focusing on the causal mechanisms proposed by various authors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, emphasizing that the type of institutions established in a society directly influences its economic trajectory (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional impact is evident in the colonization strategies adopted by Europeans, which led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks in their colonies. The enduring effects of these institutions are evident in the current economic performance of these regions, illustrating the profound and lasting impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. Moreover, North (1989, p.9) highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects the increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. This implies that the benefits of institutions on economic growth are contingent on the incentives and power dynamics within the society.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the historical institutional changes introduced by colonialism shaped economic outcomes in different contexts. Their work highlights the role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth, a mechanism that was contingent on the political dynamics of the time. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the success of these institutions in England was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, suggesting that the effectiveness of these institutions was not uniform across different contexts. This implies that the impact of institutional changes on economic growth was mediated by local dynamics and the responsiveness of colonial administrations to these dynamics.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education systems that were more advanced than those in other colonial territories. The benefits of formal education were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived advantages of education. This is further evidenced by the fact that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied between countries owing to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This highlights the importance of local dynamics in shaping the effectiveness of colonial institutions.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, a perspective that challenges conventional views that marginalize their contributions, which is shared by Hopkins (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). One specific mechanism through which women's economic influence was exerted is through land use and agricultural production. Akyeampong and Fofack detail how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, but this did not prevent them from making significant economic contributions. For instance, the migration of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast in search of farmland for cocoa production highlights women's role in driving economic growth. This migration was a key factor in the Gold Coast becoming the world's leading producer of cocoa by 1911. Hopkins notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, suggesting that understanding the historical contributions of women can provide valuable insights for contemporary policy-making. Hopkins also notes that the effectiveness of institutions is influenced by the quality of governance and the rule of law, not just by ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity. This underscores the importance of institutional quality in leveraging historical contributions for economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. They identify a culture of mistrust as a key mechanism through which the slave trade impeded economic growth. This mistrust, they posit, persists to this day and significantly impacts intergroup trust, which is crucial for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) underscores the enduring legacy of the slave trade, noting that it spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade likely had profound and lasting effects on African societies, shaping their economic institutions and trajectories in ways that continue to influence development outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further emphasize the importance of trust in various aspects of economic and political life, suggesting that the erosion of trust due to the slave trade has had far-reaching implications for Africa's economic development. The specific pathways through which this mistrust operates are complex, involving both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors. Nunn (2008, p.19) highlights the importance of centralized governance and well-developed markets in facilitating trade, suggesting that the disruption caused by the slave trade may have hindered the development of such institutions. This implies that the adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development included institutional disruptions that impeded trade and economic growth, in addition to cultural factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts are the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are primarily the result of shared beliefs or cultural values (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This institutional framework, characterized by extreme inequality, persisted over time and significantly impacted the course of economic development. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be attributed to the institutional arrangements that reinforced the power of elites, limiting opportunities for broader economic growth. For instance, the concentration of wealth and political power in the hands of a few led to policies that favored the elite's interests, such as restrictive land ownership laws and limited access to education. This institutional setup hindered economic growth and created a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite's control over resources and institutions ensured their continued dominance. This factor endowment created a context where institutions evolved to protect the interests of those who controlled these resources, further entrenching inequality and shaping the long-run economic trajectory of these societies.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they define property rights and facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights, as seen in the establishment of the Bank of England, led to a financial revolution that significantly lowered transaction costs and supported economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in fostering economic development over the long term.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the interaction between factor endowments and institutional development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the unique factor endowments of the New World, such as an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created a context where institutions evolved to protect the interests of those who controlled these resources (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional response, shaped by the interests of the politically powerful, often resulted in extractive institutions that maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The institutional responses in Europe were distinct from those in the New World, where the factor endowments and the interests of the politically powerful led to the establishment of extractive institutions that entrench inequality and shape the long-run economic trajectory of these societies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This interaction between factor endowments and institutional development is crucial for understanding the long-term economic impact of institutions.

The precise remaining disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutional changes introduced by colonialism drove the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, compared to geographic factors. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which are measured by the type of institutions established in colonies and their impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, compared to geographic factors.
