The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. It is a question of profound intellectual significance because it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity and poverty over extended periods. Scholars have long debated whether institutions are the primary engine of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more decisive role. One prominent disagreement centers on the extent to which institutions, particularly those governing property rights and political stability, are essential for sustained economic development. Another debate revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, with some arguing that institutions primarily affect growth through their impact on investment and technological progress, while others contend that they operate through more complex channels involving political dynamics and social norms. The question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth is also a contentious issue, with implications for policy and theoretical understanding. Understanding the causal role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at fostering development in both developed and developing economies. Acemoglu et al. These colonial institutions, often modeled after European systems, were instrumental in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) note. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that European settlers established institutions that were designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The nature of these institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, was largely determined by the settler mortality rate. Where mortality was low, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. However, in high-mortality regions, the institutions were extractive, focusing on resource exploitation rather than sustainable development. This institutional legacy persists and continues to shape economic outcomes in these former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional quality in shaping economic development trajectories.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that the emergence of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced the cost of transacting, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. This institutional development was particularly evident in seventeenth-century England, where the political institutions specified and enforced efficient property rights, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only lowered transaction costs but also expanded the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP, reflecting the increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The development of capital markets provides compelling evidence of this institutional impact, as seen in the remarkable success of England's economic performance (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were not merely about economic exchange but also about the political factors underpinning market development and the enforcement of rules.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, such as serfdom, the community, and guilds, were shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, rather than by a desire for economic efficiency. Ogilvie highlights that these institutions were often designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the elite, even if this meant sacrificing economic growth. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed to maximize efficiency. For instance, the craft and merchant guilds, rather than being efficient structures, were often used to restrict competition and maintain the power of established elites. This institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had significant implications for economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to the New World, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional inequality, rooted in distributional conflicts, had profound effects on long-run economic growth, often stifling innovation and productivity gains.

Austin argues that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Austin 2008, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg concur that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). One specific mechanism through which this is evident is the role of colonial administrations in shaping economic outcomes. Austin demonstrates that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, leading to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that colonial institutions were responsive to local economic and political actions, contrary to the notion that they uniformly impeded growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies supports this view, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). However, the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered, indicating that while colonial institutions played a role, their impact was not uniform or universally detrimental (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view of colonial institutions challenges the idea that they were solely obstacles to economic growth, suggesting instead that their effects were context-dependent and varied across different regions and time periods.

The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists and accounts for reduced trust levels, impacting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade created a legacy of mistrust that continues to influence social and economic interactions. This mistrust is deeply rooted and manifests in various aspects of African societies, including reduced trust levels among individuals. Nunn further elaborates that the slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are not limited to the historical period but have long-lasting consequences that shape contemporary African economies. These effects are evident in the underdevelopment of economic institutions and the persistent mistrust that hinders economic growth.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The interplay between technological progress and economic growth is complex, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This dynamic is evident in the institutional and ideological adjustments that societies must make to accommodate technological changes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress often conflicts with traditional modes of life and requires significant adjustments. The role of institutions in this context is crucial, as they facilitate the coordination and diffusion of technological knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This institutional framework is essential for harnessing the benefits of technological progress and translating them into sustained economic growth. The implications of this dynamic are profound, as the successful integration of technological advancements into societal structures can lead to sustained economic growth and development.

The integration of technological advancements into societal structures, facilitated by institutions, is pivotal for sustained economic growth. Institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes by influencing the adoption and diffusion of technology. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some colonies adopting institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others established extractive states. This variation in institutional frameworks directly impacted long-term economic development. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) both highlight the importance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for securing property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further elaborates on how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth. These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in creating an environment conducive to technological progress and economic development. The historical evidence from capital markets, as cited by North and Weingast (1989, p.2), shows that institutional changes were pivotal in fostering economic growth. This historical evidence provides a clear indication of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
