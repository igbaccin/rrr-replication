The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. It is a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play more decisive roles. The stakes are high because the answer to this question informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development. For instance, if institutions are the key, then policy should focus on institutional reform. If other factors are more important, then resources should be directed elsewhere. The debate highlights the tension between institutional determinism and alternative explanations for economic growth. This disagreement is evident in the contrasting views on the impact of colonial institutions and the significance of secure property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This institutional variation is crucial for understanding the divergent economic trajectories of colonies, as highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and North (1989, p.9). In the context of colonialism, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) highlight that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly based on their settlement strategies. In other instances, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which hindered economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9) complements this view by demonstrating that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This underscores how institutional changes can lower the costs of economic transactions, thereby facilitating market exchanges and fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists did not necessarily promote long-run growth.

This institutional framework was pivotal in creating an environment conducive to economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue. They focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth, emphasizing that the regime's commitment to enforcing rules is crucial (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This political stability allowed for the evolution of institutions that protected property rights, which were essential for economic development. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by highlighting how different factor endowments in New World societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. They note that societies with extreme inequality often had institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This dynamic illustrates how the initial conditions of a society can influence the development of institutions, which then shape long-run economic growth. This underscores the complex interplay between initial conditions, institutional development, and economic outcomes.

This interplay between knowledge, technology, and institutions is essential for understanding the drivers of economic growth, as emphasized by Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1). The development of useful knowledge is pivotal in driving economic growth, as it fosters technological advancements that enhance productivity and efficiency. Mokyr (2005, p.49) suggests that formal institutions, such as scholarly societies, play a crucial role in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating its dissemination and application. This institutional support for knowledge dissemination is essential for sustaining economic growth, as it ensures that technological advancements are widely adopted and integrated into the economy. Institutions that support the growth of useful knowledge, such as guilds, can also influence the skill premium, which is a predictor of long-term economic performance. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that guilds facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills and acted as local cartels, restricting entry and maintaining higher wages. This dual role of institutions in supporting knowledge growth while also creating barriers highlights the complex interplay between institutional structures and economic outcomes. The beneficial effects of institutions on knowledge dissemination and skill development must be balanced against their potential to create monopolies and restrict competition. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of their impact on knowledge dissemination and market dynamics.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in Africa's underdevelopment. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Nunn (2008, p.2) posits that the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on African economic development. This long-term disruption impeded the development of stable institutions and social structures necessary for sustained economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the colonial period itself did not uniformly impose harmful institutions. In regions where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often supported indigenous commodity production, altering institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political dynamics. This supports the idea that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development varied based on local conditions and colonial policies. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This suggests that the type of colonial administration played a crucial role in shaping educational outcomes, which in turn influenced long-term economic development. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa is complex, with both the duration of slave trades and the specific policies of colonial administrations playing significant parts.

This historical context is crucial for understanding the distinct economic trajectories of Europe and Africa, as Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight. These patterns are evident in Africa's colonial era, where economic data reveals levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges previous characterizations of Africa's economic performance and suggests that African economies were more robust than often portrayed. The growth in export production, tied to foreign trade, varied significantly across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further illuminate this by noting that the economic development in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, underscoring the role of the service sector in economic growth. This mechanism of economic growth through the service sector is a critical factor in understanding the divergence between Europe and Africa, as it highlights the importance of institutional changes in facilitating investment and productivity. The uneven distribution of the fruits of early modern development indicates that the benefits of economic growth were not uniformly spread. This qualification underscores the complexity of economic divergence, where institutional factors play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) jointly establish that institutional reforms in Africa, particularly those aimed at reducing poverty, focus on specific interventions rather than addressing long-run economic growth. The new economic history of Africa has emphasized the role of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation but rather by the broader institutional framework (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective underscores the importance of progressive institutions in upholding the rule of law and facilitating economic development. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) point out that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the long-term picture of economic development in Africa is influenced by specific historical or geographical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This qualification implies that while institutional reforms can mitigate poverty, the historical and geographical context of Africa plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) identify that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced current income levels. The extractive institutions imposed in colonies with high European mortality rates have persisted and continue to affect economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) elaborates on this by showing that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for long-term economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 1694 exemplify how institutional changes can lead to economic growth by providing secure property rights. This historical evidence shows that institutions matter in economic growth, as they provide the necessary framework for economic activities to thrive.

Colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes have been central to the long-run development of these regions. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) provides further insight into how institutions can shape economic outcomes by highlighting the role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) complements this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by various historical and geographical factors. For instance, the presence of European settlers and the fiscal policies of colonial governments played a significant role in shaping institutional prescriptions. The case of Governor-General Clifford opting for a higher production equilibrium in response to indigenous economic and political action illustrates how institutional changes can be influenced by local conditions.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the importance of credible commitment. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth. Their analysis of seventeenth-century England shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets and the remarkable economic performance of England during this period. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) adds that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Englebert's work shows that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily hinder the development of progressive institutions. This suggests that institutions can be designed to maximize rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from England and the empirical comparisons from Africa show that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for economic activities to thrive.
