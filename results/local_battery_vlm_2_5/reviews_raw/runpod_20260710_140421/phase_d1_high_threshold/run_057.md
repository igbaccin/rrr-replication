The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, addressing how social, political, and economic structures influence sustained prosperity. This question is central to understanding why some regions thrive while others stagnate, and it has sparked a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of growth and those who propose alternative explanations. Some scholars emphasize the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic growth, while others highlight the impact of colonial institutions and the persistence of extractive practices. Additionally, there are debates about the role of cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions. The interplay between these factors, whether institutions are the cause or the effect of economic growth, remains a contentious issue, with scholars seeking to unpack the complex relationships between institutions, economic performance, and historical contexts. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is thus both multifaceted and crucial for understanding the dynamics of economic development.

Austin and North both acknowledge the significance of institutions in economic growth, but they diverge in their approaches to explaining this relationship. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the institutionalist approach needs to account for the complexity of historical contexts. Austin highlights that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan. Meanwhile, North posits that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North argues that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. This perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in fostering economic growth, aligning with the broader view that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. North and Austin's arguments converge on the idea that institutions are central to economic growth, but they differ in their emphasis on the complexity of historical contexts and the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework was driven by the political factors underlying economic growth. This security was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament, in return for revenues, was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) emphasize that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the degree to which the regime was committed to enforcing these rules was crucial. The institutional changes in England, which included the development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England, significantly enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework fostered economic growth by reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) notes that while the costs per transaction were falling, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate was a growing proportion of GNP. This suggests that the increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges, further highlighting the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth.

Building on this, Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that technological advancements and knowledge growth are fundamental drivers of economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that technological progress has historically been incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, such as the development of the modern national state (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that the economic growth driven by technological advancements necessitates a supportive institutional framework. The implication is that institutions play a crucial role in facilitating the diffusion of knowledge and technological advancements. The need for institutional progress to complement technological progress is further illustrated by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby enabling modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that institutions are not just a facilitator of economic growth, but also a mediator of the conflicts that arise from technological advancements.

Nunn and Wantchekon's research, alongside Nunn's broader work, shows that the slave trade had a profound impact on African economic development by shaping institutions in ways that hindered growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is not merely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others but is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. This perspective challenges the notion that long-run economic growth is solely determined by institutional factors, suggesting instead that historical events like the slave trade can have lasting institutional effects that impede growth. Furthermore, the impact of the slave trade on African institutions extends beyond economic factors to social and political institutions as well. Nunn's analysis of the Kongo kingdom illustrates that centralized governance and well-developed markets facilitated trade, suggesting that the absence of such institutions in other regions may have been a direct result of the slave trade's disruptive influence (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional disruption hindered economic growth and created a lasting legacy of mistrust that continues to affect African societies today.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, as both Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) assert. This institutional divergence is crucial because it shaped the long-term economic trajectories of the colonies. For example, colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, which adopted institutions that enforced the rule of law, experienced significant economic growth. Colonies such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, which were subjected to extractive institutions, faced persistent economic underdevelopment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborates that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth. This institutional design, rooted in the colonial era, continues to influence the economic performance of these regions today. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) note that the impact of these institutions is not uniform across all measures; various indicators of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and the efficiency of the judiciary, all show similar results. This consistency underscores the pervasive and enduring influence of colonial institutions on economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) re-evaluated historical economic performance by examining specific instances of commodity production and colonial governance, rather than focusing on institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. These scholars argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights that colonial administrations often responded to local economic and political actions, adjusting their institutional prescriptions accordingly. This mechanism is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium in response to local dynamics. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this pattern, showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, tied closely to foreign trade and export markets. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this picture by noting that the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive under British rule than under other colonial powers, indicating that the impacts of colonial governance varied significantly across different domains of economic and social development. This variation suggests that the influence of colonial institutions on economic growth depended on specific historical and contextual factors.

Distributional conflicts are a pivotal factor in shaping economic institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) shared finding. They contend that these conflicts offer a more compelling explanation for the economic structures of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency-based theories. These conflicts arise from the unequal distribution of resources and power, which in turn influence the development of economic institutions that either reinforce or mitigate these inequalities. For instance, the persistence of serfdom can be seen as a mechanism by which elites maintained their control over the labor force, ensuring their economic dominance. The craft guilds, which regulated production and limited competition, similarly served to protect the interests of existing artisans against new entrants. The distributional conflicts thus shaped institutions in a way that perpetuated the existing social and economic order. These institutions, in turn, influenced long-run economic growth by either fostering or hindering economic activity. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this analysis to New World societies, showing how institutions in societies with greater inequality were structured to advantage elites. This dynamic is evident in government policies that restricted competition and maintained the status quo, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is not uniform. While they may have stifled growth in the short term by limiting competition and innovation, they could also have provided stability and predictability that facilitated long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of European colonization. Institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were often established. These institutions prioritized the rapid transfer of resources to the metropole, rather than fostering local economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional structures had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with extractive institutions stifling growth and inclusive institutions fostering development.

The impact of these institutional differences is further highlighted by the role of credible commitment in economic growth. Credible commitment underpins growth by providing stability and predictability, which are crucial for long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is evident in the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were pivotal in facilitating economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and market exchanges.

The relationship between institutions and economic growth is not straightforward. Distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions, as seen in pre-industrial Europe. Serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were also products of distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are always designed for efficiency and growth. Instead, it shows that institutions can be shaped by the interests of powerful groups, which may not always align with long-term economic development.

The persistence of inequality in societies with extractive institutions is a key factor in understanding their impact on economic growth. These institutions often restrict competition and maintain the status quo, thereby contributing to the persistence of inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). However, while they may stifle growth in the short term, they can also provide stability and predictability that facilitate long-term economic development. The historical evidence from European colonization shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights the complex relationship between institutions, inequality, and economic growth, where institutions can both hinder and facilitate growth depending on the historical and political context.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth comes from the comparison of colonies with different institutional structures. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial in determining long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as seen in the divergent paths of colonies with extractive versus inclusive institutions.
