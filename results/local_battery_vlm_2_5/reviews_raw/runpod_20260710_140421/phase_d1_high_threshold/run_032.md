The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in the study of economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate, and whether the quality of institutions can be a decisive factor in this trajectory. One live disagreement centres around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are critical because they determine the security of property rights and the enforcement of contracts, which in turn foster investment and growth. This perspective emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes. Some scholars suggest that institutions are one factor among many, and their influence is often mediated through other variables such as population density and prosperity. Some argue that institutions themselves are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, and are not exogenous determinants of growth. The debate thus hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutions created by Europeans in their colonies were highly dependent on the mortality rates that Europeans experienced in these regions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropolis. These institutions were not conducive to long-run economic growth and development. This pattern is exemplified by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.19) noting that the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, led to vastly different economic outcomes. These institutions fostered economic growth and development by providing a stable environment for investment and economic activity. The persistence of these institutions over time had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is directly related to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This reversal highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, as the economic policies and institutions established during colonial rule continued to shape the economic development of these regions long after independence.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions, particularly those governing property rights and financial systems, was pivotal for long-run economic growth. This institutional development reflects a broader pattern where political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms drive economic growth by facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The emergence of these institutions in England was part of a broader political and economic transformation that fostered secure property rights, which in turn stimulated economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective challenges neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the primary drivers of economic growth, instead highlighting the crucial role of institutional developments in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This nuance is important because it indicates that while institutions are critical, their effects can vary based on historical and political contexts, which may temper the universal applicability of institutional theories.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective posits that the accumulation of knowledge and technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional factors in promoting economic growth. This is evidenced by the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further supports this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. He observes that modern technology is not compatible with traditional modes of life and family patterns, necessitating significant institutional changes to resolve conflicts arising from technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes that these conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to ensure sustained economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, the institutional environment plays a supporting role in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements for long-term economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had a profound adverse effect on economic development, alongside Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This mistrust developed because the slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered an environment where individuals were less likely to trust others, including those from different ethnic groups. The internal norms of mistrust, which are at least twice as large as the external channel, are a significant barrier to economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). These findings are supported by Nunn (2008, p.2), who argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, contrary to those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2). This variation in impact suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is complex and multifaceted, with different regions experiencing different outcomes depending on their specific historical and institutional contexts (Nunn 2008, p.19). This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as the impact of the slave trade on economic development was not uniform across Africa.

This gap in the literature underscores the need for further research into the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, but the literature does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that other institutional factors play a crucial role. This perspective is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who emphasize the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. They argue that the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth. This focus on real wages and income distribution highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) also point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, indicating that more research is needed to fully understand the historical and institutional contexts that have shaped African economic development. This underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, as the impact of institutions on economic development is not uniform and depends on specific historical and institutional contexts.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) examine specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. This thesis posits that the institutional legacies of European colonialism have shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and that colonial administrations encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective highlights the importance of considering the broader cultural and political context in which institutions operate. Austin (2008, p.15) and Ogilvie (2007, p.3) both argue that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. The implication is that a deeper understanding of the specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe can provide valuable insights into the broader role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by a shared emphasis on the importance of property rights and contract enforcement. Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment while others set up extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Similarly, North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore that secure property rights and effective contract enforcement are foundational for sustainable economic development. Austin further elaborates that European settlers' presence or absence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments, indicating a direct link between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts, and not efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations, suggesting that power dynamics and rent-seeking behaviors also play crucial roles. Acemoglu et al. support this view by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional frameworks across European colonies, where the presence of European settlers led to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This empirical comparison shows that the institutional arrangements introduced by colonial powers had a direct impact on economic outcomes, supporting the view that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups are less conducive to long-run economic growth.
