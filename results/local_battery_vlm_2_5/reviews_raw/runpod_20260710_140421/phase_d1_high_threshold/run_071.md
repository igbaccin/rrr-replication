The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At the heart of the debate lies a fundamental question: are institutions the primary drivers of economic growth, or are they merely one factor among many? The stakes are high, as the answer has profound implications for policy and theory. One side argues that institutions are critical for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for investment and economic activity. This view is supported by scholars who emphasize the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights. On the other side, some scholars contend that institutions are not the sole determinant of economic growth and that other factors, such as geography and culture, also play significant roles. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the driving force behind economic growth or if they are influenced by other factors. The institutions-first perspective is supported by North's argument that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Some scholars contend that institutions are molded by politically influential groups, which may secure fewer rents with private property institutions or worry about losing political power if institutions develop (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The debate continues as scholars grapple with the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping economic growth.

The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in the 17th century exemplifies how institutions can facilitate market exchanges and support long-term capital markets. North and Weingast highlight that these institutional changes are both economic and political, involving a credible commitment from the regime to enforce rules and property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutional development and political dynamics, suggesting that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their effectiveness depends on the political context and the interests of those in power. The political economy of institutional change is therefore a critical factor in understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin both show that colonial institutions, shaped by European mortality rates, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2). These institutions varied based on the settlers' ability to survive in the new environments. In places where Europeans could settle and establish institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States and Australia, economic growth was fostered. In regions with high mortality rates, like the Congo, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, hindering long-term economic development. This divergence in institutional outcomes was driven by the differential mortality experiences of European settlers, which in turn shaped the types of institutions they implemented. The resulting institutions persisted over time, influencing economic trajectories long after the colonial period ended. Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which suggests that poor countries were once rich and vice versa, oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs. The persistence of colonial institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth highlights the enduring influence of historical factors on contemporary economic outcomes.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that these institutions were designed to maximize economic efficiency. Rather, Ogilvie posits that these institutions were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups, each seeking to protect or enhance their economic interests. This mechanism highlights how institutional design can be influenced by the underlying power dynamics within society. Specifically, these institutions often served to entrench the economic privileges of the ruling class, thereby perpetuating inequality. The persistence of these institutions over time indicates that their impact on economic growth is complex. For instance, the continued existence of these institutions may have stifled innovation and economic dynamism, as they often hindered the entry of new competitors and the adoption of new technologies. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional design, distributional conflicts, and long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets' work, for instance, highlights how technological progress is central to economic growth and requires institutional adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) identifies epochal innovations as dominant sources of sustained growth, suggesting that the interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is crucial for long-run economic performance. This indicates that the creation and dissemination of useful knowledge are essential for technological progress and require supportive institutions to ensure sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also acknowledges the possibility of coordination failures, indicating that while technological progress is generally irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness can be more variable. This implies that the institutional environment must adapt to technological changes to prevent rent-seeking behavior and ensure that economic improvements are sustained over the long run. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes that modern economic growth is contingent on the peaceful resolution of growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the importance of a strong national state in managing these conflicts. This underscores the complex interplay between technological progress, institutional design, and the political fabric of society in shaping long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) demonstrate that the historical impacts of the slave trade on economic development suggest that the legacy of mistrust and institutional breakdowns caused by the slave trade may have hindered long-run economic growth in Africa. The slave trade disrupted social and economic institutions, creating a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the very foundations of cooperation and trust that are essential for economic growth. This internal mistrust, which is at least twice as large as the external mistrust, significantly impairs economic development by eroding social capital and hindering collective action. The slave trade also led to institutional breakdowns, as the forced migration of people disrupted traditional governance structures and weakened the rule of law. This breakdown of institutions further exacerbated the negative effects of mistrust on economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the impact of the slave trade on economic development was not uniform across Africa, as some regions were able to maintain or even strengthen their institutions despite the disruptions caused by the slave trade. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trade on economic development is complex and multifaceted, with both direct and indirect effects on economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) share a posture that African economic development is influenced by the role of women and the impact of colonial rule on institutional changes. The authors highlight that the marginalization of women in access to land, reinforced by customary law under colonial rule, significantly impacted economic development. This was evident in the Gold Coast, where men dominated the codification of customary law, which in turn affected women's access to land for cash crops. This suggests that even ethnically homogeneous societies can be dysfunctional if they lack progressive institutions.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the claim that colonial powers' impacts on African economies were more nuanced than previously thought. They argue that the influence of colonial institutions on economic outcomes varied significantly across regions and over time. One specific mechanism through which this variation played out was through the provision of public goods such as education. British colonial rule, for example, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This was partly due to the demand for missionary education, which depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. New social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social mobility. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This variation in the provision of public goods and the resulting economic outcomes underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role in shaping economic outcomes, they were not the sole determinant of long-run economic growth.

Simson asserts that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly believed (Simson 2019, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services, which is often used to explain African poverty. The analysis of budgetary shifts reveals that military spending and debt servicing absorbed significant portions of government expenditure. This dynamic shifted resources away from capital and recurrent spending, potentially impacting long-term economic development (Simson 2019, p.11). The rise in military spending was driven by regional instability and domestic dissent, highlighting the complex interplay between security concerns and economic policy. Frankema and van Waijenburg, however, argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, suggesting that historical and geographical conditions may have only temporary effects on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view of economic policy and poverty dynamics underscores the importance of contextual factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

Van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) share a focus on the measurement of economic performance and the determinants of the skill premium, rather than directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth. They jointly establish that the skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance. The skill premium is determined by the costs of training, the chance that the apprentice will successfully finish their training period, the interest rate linking future earnings to the present, and the number of years the skilled worker can earn the higher wage. This mechanism is evident in the apprentice system of Western Europe, where guilds played a crucial role in training and transferring skills across generations. The guilds' role in economic development has been reassessed by recent authors who studied their functioning in detail. The guilds have also been criticized for monopolizing these skills and restricting entry into their trade, which could keep their relative pay above market-clearing levels. This counterbalancing effect may have limited the guilds' overall contribution to economic growth.

Peters (2004, p.19) and Moore (1986) argue that the apparent flexibility of land systems in Africa often masks deeper processes of exclusion and class formation. This phenomenon is evident in the way that "custom" is perceived in official circles, where the idea that custom is static can be strategically used by certain groups to obscure their activities and changes. This dynamic is particularly pronounced in the final decade of colonial rule, where agricultural officers pushed for modernization and productivity through "improved" farming practices. These efforts were often met with dissatisfaction, as they clashed with traditional landholding practices seen as communal and inhibiting individual investment. The reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries in Africa represents a significant shift from the traditional dependence on decaying state institutions for political resources. This shift underscores the complex interplay between political, economic, and social factors in state-making processes. The role of private commercial transactions in Africa highlights how these processes can both facilitate and hinder economic growth. These findings suggest that land systems in Africa are deeply intertwined with broader political and economic dynamics that shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, with scholars highlighting the significance of different institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, which enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, were more likely to foster economic growth, whereas extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to the metropole, hindered long-term development. This variation in institutional outcomes underscores the importance of historical context in shaping economic trajectories. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in England significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework highlights how secure property rights and efficient market exchanges are essential for long-term economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between political and economic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, which were often extractive in nature, maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights how institutions can both facilitate and hinder economic growth, depending on the political and economic context. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) shows that poor institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. Good institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

Institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and political power dynamics. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by conflicts over resource allocation and political power. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9), who shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in England significantly reduced transaction costs, facilitating economic growth. This institutional framework highlights how secure property rights and efficient market exchanges are essential for long-term economic development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with historical context, political power dynamics, and distributional conflicts all playing crucial roles. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
