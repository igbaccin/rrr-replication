The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. This question is live because of a disagreement between those who see institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who attribute growth to other factors such as geography, culture, or technological advancements. The debate centers on whether institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are essential for sustained economic development or if they are merely one among many factors. The core of the disagreement lies in the extent to which institutions can be seen as causal determinants of economic growth, as opposed to being influenced by other underlying factors. This question is pivotal for understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth. Institutions matter in economic growth because they provide the framework for economic activity, influencing how resources are allocated and how risks are managed. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established varied institutions based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive institutions that encouraged investment or extractive ones that aimed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. For example, in colonies such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources to the European colonizers quickly. This institutional divergence had profound implications for long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional design, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction suggests that while initial institutions were crucial, subsequent economic opportunities and policies also influenced long-term economic outcomes.

The importance of secure property rights in fostering economic growth is highlighted by the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, which North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue were pivotal for economic development. A key mechanism was the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, which introduced new financial instruments and dramatically reduced transaction costs. This reduction in transaction costs was a direct result of increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which was critical for both the development of a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. The political model of the time involved trading certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, a product of the fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change was part of a broader trend in which political institutions specified 'efficient' property rights and provided increasingly effective enforcement. As a result, costs per transaction fell while the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). This development underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic growth, as the regime's adherence to rules governing economic exchange was crucial for market development. North and Weingast's (1989, p.29) argument that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought, given France's comparable economic performance, suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be more nuanced than initially assumed.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional impacts, contrasting with the formulations of Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique is shared by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's work highlights the importance of understanding the institutional evolution and policy experience of countries over extended periods, arguing that economic historians should consider the political delivery of secure private property rights and the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional change and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time. This implies that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not straightforward and can be influenced by a variety of factors, including political and social dynamics.

Frankema shows that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The British colonial administration's emphasis on indirect rule and their collaboration with local elites created a demand for educated Africans who could serve as intermediaries. This demand was met by missionary schools, which provided formal education and trained a new class of African elites. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social mobility. This dynamic was evident in both the macro-level decisions of African political leaders and the micro-level calculations of African families. The British colonial administration's policies, which included the establishment of a legal framework that protected property rights and promoted trade, also contributed to the development of formal education. These policies created an environment that was conducive to investment in human capital, which in turn led to the development of a skilled workforce that could support economic growth. However, the persistence of inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power limited the benefits of formal education to a small elite, and the majority of the population remained excluded from the economic opportunities that formal education provided.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that economic performance in Africa and Europe varied significantly over time. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is significant because it challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, which often portrayed Africa as uniformly poor. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also highlight the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence, which has enabled more nuanced comparative historical work on economic growth and development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate that the patterns of growing and shrinking in these economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These patterns suggest that economic growth in Africa was shaped by endogenous factors, including local endowments and political institutions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800. This implies that the economic performance of Africa and Europe cannot be understood in isolation from broader global economic trends. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further highlight that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe may be seen as arising through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, as it does in the modern period. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The availability of economic data and the influence of local endowments and political institutions suggest that institutions played a role in shaping economic performance in Africa and Europe. The great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 suggests that institutions alone cannot explain long-run economic growth.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.19) analysis of European economic development between 1500 and 1800 reveals that factors beyond institutions, such as economic focus and grain prices, played crucial roles. van Zanden (1999, p.9) argues that the economic development of England after 1700 led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, confirming the view that economic development influenced the price level of basic foodstuffs. van Zanden (2009, p.3) argues that the skill premium, which is the remuneration for investing in human capital, was determined by the costs of training, the chance that the apprentice will successfully finish their training period, the interest rate, and the number of years the skilled worker can earn the higher wage. This suggests that economic development in Europe was influenced by the skill premium, which was in turn influenced by the costs of training and the interest rate. van Zanden (2009, p.19) also notes that the beneficial effects of the guilds on the intergenerational transfer of skills may have been counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels. This qualification highlights the complex relationship between institutions and economic development in Europe during this period.

Peters' examination of land systems in Africa reveals that the complexities of land tenure and class formation are intricately linked to broader economic dynamics (Peters 2004, p.19). This perspective is shared by Bolt and van Zanden, who highlight the subjective nature of economic measurements and the challenges in estimating margins of error in historical economic data (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). These challenges are particularly pronounced when examining long-run economic growth, where data is often incomplete or based on assumptions that may not hold true over extended periods. The subjective nature of these estimates underscores the difficulty in accurately measuring economic performance, especially in regions where historical data is scarce or unreliable. Peters notes that the perception of 'custom' as static can advantage groups seeking to keep officials unaware of their activities (Peters 2004, p.5). This dynamic is crucial because it illustrates how land systems and class formation can influence economic outcomes by shaping the distribution of resources and opportunities. The role of institutions in mediating these dynamics is less clear.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2) perspective on the slave trade's impact on Africa's economic development challenges the notion that institutions alone can explain underdevelopment. The slave trade's adverse effects are multifaceted, with one key mechanism being the erosion of trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust, which continues to impact economic outcomes today. This mistrust is deeply ingrained as an internal norm. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn also notes that the development of trade networks and economic institutions was contingent on the presence of centralized governments and well-established markets. The Kongo kingdom, for instance, with its centralized government and developed trading networks, was able to support trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while the slave trade had a detrimental effect, the presence of robust institutions could have mitigated some of its adverse impacts.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) argue that specific institutional changes in Africa can drive long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that African economic development is influenced by the capacity of societies to develop progressive institutions. These institutions can foster economic growth by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Simson (2019, p.11) provides a nuanced view of post-independence budget policies, challenging the narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services. Simson's work suggests that the growth in military spending and the shift in expenditure patterns were responses to regional instability and domestic dissent. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) add that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This perspective underscores the importance of specific historical or geographical conditions that have influenced long-term African welfare development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the finding that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This technological progress requires institutional adjustments to be efficiently used. Economic growth is driven by advancing technology, and this requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further explains that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which is essential for modern economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) cautions that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological progress is the primary driver, institutional adjustments are necessary to sustain long-term economic growth.

McCloskey (2015, p.3) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that ideas and ideologies drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey explains that the key factor was a shift in ideology, which permitted experimentation and open dialogue about new techniques and institutions, ultimately fostering economic growth. Pamuk illustrates this with the example of late medieval Holland, whose institutional flexibility allowed it to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities. This ideological shift enabled societies to embrace technological advancements and institutional changes. Pamuk (2007, p.16) further supports this by highlighting the divergence in urbanisation rates between Northwestern and Southern Europe, indicating different trajectories of productivity and economic development. McCloskey (2015, p.4) qualifies this by noting that even religious authorities, such as Pope Pius IX, eventually began to favor economic progress, demonstrating the pervasive influence of new ideas on economic change. This suggests that the ideological environment was crucial for sustaining long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and structures that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that institutions determine the distribution of economic resources and opportunities, with extractive institutions that prioritize resource extraction over investment hindering growth. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) supports this by arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs are crucial for economic growth. These findings are reinforced by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who highlights that institutions that encourage economic activity and investment are key to sustained growth. This indicates that the institutional framework is essential for long-term economic development.

The historical evidence from European colonies further underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) adds that distributional conflicts and shared beliefs also shape core economic institutions, influencing long-run growth trajectories. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) shows that institutions are central to economic growth, as they determine the distribution of economic resources and opportunities, and shape the incentives for investment and economic activity.

The debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on the underlying mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. North (1989, p.6) shows that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement reduce transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who demonstrate that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes remain a subject of debate. The historical evidence from European colonies shows that institutions that encourage economic activity and investment are key to sustained growth. This is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who shows that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The measurement of institutional quality and the comparison of economic outcomes across different institutional frameworks provide a clear picture of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
