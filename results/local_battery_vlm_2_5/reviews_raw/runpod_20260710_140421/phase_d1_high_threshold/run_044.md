The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economics, with significant implications for understanding the wealth of nations. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. The debate centres around two main positions: one argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for markets to function efficiently, while the other posits that institutions are less critical, with economic growth being driven primarily by other factors such as geography, culture, or technological innovation. Some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by historical and political factors and that their impact on economic growth is mediated through these factors. Others maintain that institutions are endogenous to economic growth, meaning that they evolve in response to economic changes and do not necessarily drive growth. The disagreement hinges on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors. This review explores these positions by examining how institutions shape long-run economic growth, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions, historical contexts, and economic outcomes. The central question remains: how do institutions influence economic growth over the long term? Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

In this context, North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework facilitated a long-term capital market and economic growth, as falling transaction costs enabled more efficient market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The evolution of these institutions involved both economic rules and the political mechanisms that enforced and adapted these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This political commitment to property rights was crucial for economic development, as evidenced by the success of capital markets in England. France's economic performance was nearly equivalent to that of other countries, despite having a different institutional structure (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while specific institutional arrangements were important, they were not the sole determinants of economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress and the growth of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that modern technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts that arise from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). These adjustments are crucial for sustaining economic growth, as they ensure that the benefits of technological progress are not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors or social conflicts. This interplay between technological and institutional progress is evident in the historical record, where societies that successfully managed these adjustments experienced sustained economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also notes that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that support technological progress, is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress itself (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological advancements are a necessary condition for economic growth, the institutional framework that supports and sustains these advancements is equally important.

Distributional conflicts significantly shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions that advantaged elites were maintained through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These policies were effective in maintaining the status quo and protecting the interests of the ruling elite, even if they were not necessarily efficient. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is through the control of resources and labor. In pre-industrial Europe, the control of land and labor was a critical factor in the power dynamics between different social groups. The institutions of serfdom and the guilds were designed to maintain the control of these resources by the elite, ensuring that they could extract surplus labor and maintain their wealth and power. This control was effective in maintaining the social and economic status of the elite, even if it was not efficient in the sense of maximizing overall economic output. The downstream implications of this mechanism are significant. For instance, the persistence of these institutions in pre-industrial Europe had long-lasting effects on economic development. The control of resources and labor by the elite limited the opportunities for economic growth and innovation, as the majority of the population was excluded from participating in the economy. This exclusion had a lasting impact on economic development, as it limited the pool of potential innovators and entrepreneurs. The authors argue that the specific historical and political contexts of different societies played a crucial role in shaping the institutions that evolved. In some cases, the institutions that evolved were more inclusive and allowed for greater economic participation, leading to more rapid economic growth and development. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions highlights the importance of understanding the power dynamics between different social groups in shaping economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) show that the impact of colonialism on economic growth was mediated through the institutions it introduced. These institutions varied widely depending on European mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run economic growth. Colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that these institutions, not geographic factors, are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to shape economic outcomes in these regions today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context in which these institutions were established.

The literature on colonial rule in Africa highlights various impacts such as educational development and governance issues but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is crucial for development. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial legacies, has enduring effects on Africa's economic trajectory. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this by noting that the demand for missionary education was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This suggests that while colonial institutions set the stage, local actors also played a role in shaping educational outcomes. The complexity of these interactions underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional impact on economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), together with Nunn (2008, p.2), highlight that historical factors such as the economic contributions of African women and the impacts of slave trades have shaped economic development. This involvement was crucial for economic growth, as women's labor contributed significantly to agricultural production. Their access to land for cash crops was limited by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule. This codification reinforced the subordinate status of women, illustrating how institutional changes during colonial rule could alter economic outcomes. Nunn demonstrates that the slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had profound effects on Africa's economic development. The slave trades disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to long-term adverse effects on growth. The Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, provides a counterpoint. Its ability to support trade with Europeans highlights the importance of institutional structures in facilitating economic development. This suggests that while historical factors like slave trades and women's economic contributions are significant, the institutional framework plays a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner reassess the economic performance of African economies by highlighting the importance of historical national accounting and post-independence budget policies (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). Their work, alongside Simson's, demonstrates that African economies were more developed than previously thought, with per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This reassessment is grounded in the availability of extensive economic data from the colonial era and beyond. Broadberry and Gardner show that the timing and scale of economic growth in Africa were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Simson elaborates on this by noting that post-independence budget policies were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. They were shaped by regional instability and domestic dissent, leading to shifts in expenditure towards military spending and interest payments. This pattern of expenditure highlights the complex interplay between political and economic factors in shaping African economies. However, Simson qualifies this by noting that the neopatrimonial tendencies in Africa, where government resources are treated as private property of political patrons, can result in policy-making focused on consumption rather than public goods. This suggests that while post-independence policies were not uniformly detrimental, the institutional framework and governance structures played a crucial role in determining economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1) highlight that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 was driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance, as well as climate, which influenced economic growth through the service sector. The role of merchant distribution and finance in economic growth is evident in the high productivity of the traded goods sector in north-western Europe. This productivity arose from effective merchant distribution and financial systems, in addition to production efficiencies. These systems facilitated the service sector's crucial role in economic growth, both in the early modern period and in contemporary times. The service sector's contribution to economic growth is underscored by the uneven distribution of its benefits, where skilled laborers enjoyed a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. This disparity suggests that the fruits of early modern development were not uniformly distributed, a pattern that continues to influence economic structures today. van Zanden (1999, p.9) qualifies this by noting that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, particularly in England after 1740. This shift in economic focus to the North Sea countries, including Holland and England, indicates that institutional changes, such as those in agriculture, were pivotal in driving economic development and high agricultural labor productivity. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic growth, where institutions play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty is not solely a result of persistent structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional view that African economies have been locked into poverty by long-standing structural constraints. Instead, Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight the need for a deeper understanding of the historical economic paths that shaped African development, focusing on the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This approach underscores the importance of examining specific historical and geographical conditions that have influenced long-term African welfare development. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this perspective by noting that colonial governments often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, rather than imposing a uniform set of structural impediments (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, thereby influencing economic outcomes in complex ways.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Peters (2004, p.19) both demonstrate that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to significant economic and social transformations. Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows that by 1450, these changes were evident in urbanization rates and wage gaps between the northwest and the rest of Europe. The Black Death's impact on Europe was not uniform; the different trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe were shaped by institutional flexibility. For instance, Holland's ability to rapidly respond to new economic opportunities was facilitated by weak monopolies and the political authorities' support for new towns and rural manufacturing. This institutional flexibility allowed Holland to become one of the most advanced, urbanized, and commercialized regions of the continent (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The institutional landscape in Tuscany, however, was marked by strong monopolies and political authorities that worked to strengthen existing powers, leading to stagnation and a decline in urban ranks. Peters (2004, p.19) examines the role of land systems in Africa, highlighting how reports of negotiable land systems conceal processes of exclusion and class formation. This finding underscores the importance of understanding the broader institutional context in which economic changes occur. Peters (2004, p.5) also suggests that the final decade of colonial rule saw a push for agricultural development, driven by a perception that African landholding practices were communal and inhibited individual investment. This push for modernization highlights the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes, as well as the role of external influences in shaping these outcomes.

The skill premium serves as a predictor of long-term economic performance, highlighting the importance of human capital and labor market dynamics in shaping economic outcomes over extended periods (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). One specific mechanism through which this occurs is the role of guilds in Western Europe. These institutions facilitated the intergenerational transfer of skills and ensured the fair execution of apprentice contracts, thereby reducing barriers to entry and fostering a more competitive labor market. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) notes that while guilds have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry, their beneficial effects on skill development are substantial. This dynamic is further qualified by the observation that the skill premium is influenced by various factors, including the costs of training, the success rate of apprenticeship completion, and the interest rate linking future earnings to present costs. These factors collectively determine the 'costs' of producing skilled workers, which in turn affects the skill premium and, by extension, long-term economic performance. This underscores the intricate relationship between institutional frameworks, human capital investment, and economic growth, suggesting that effective skill development policies can significantly enhance a nation's economic trajectory.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and McCloskey (2015, p.3) both argue that cultural factors, shaped by historical events such as the slave trade, significantly influence contemporary economic outcomes. The slave trade in Africa fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust, which persists today and impacts economic performance by reducing trust levels. This mistrust affects various aspects of economic life, including trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The historical legacy of the slave trade has left an enduring mark on African societies, creating an environment where trust is scarce and economic interactions are hindered. This cultural mistrust is deeply rooted in individuals' internal norms, making it a persistent barrier to economic development. The impact of this mistrust is evident in the reduced trustworthiness of others, which in turn affects the rule of law and overall economic performance. This dynamic underscores the importance of addressing cultural factors in economic development strategies, as they can significantly influence the effectiveness of institutional frameworks and economic policies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the historical and political contexts in which they develop. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income per capita. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, were shaped by the strategies adopted by European colonizers, which in turn were influenced by factors such as mortality rates and the presence of settlers. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He points out that European colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional development. This dynamic is further underscored by the work of North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement, which reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges. This institutional framework is crucial for long-term economic development, as it creates an environment conducive to investment and growth.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also influenced by cultural and distributional factors. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency alone. This perspective underscores the role of shared beliefs and political power in shaping institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) further illustrate this by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth. This highlights the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that underpin institutional development. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) extends this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty, emphasizing the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment.

The underlying point of agreement across these sources is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the creation of the Bank of England (North 1989, p.9). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income per capita, while Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century.
