The question of what role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high, as the answer influences policy prescriptions for developing economies and our understanding of historical economic trajectories. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One key position emphasizes the causal impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that the quality of institutions determines the efficiency of resource allocation and the incentives for investment and innovation. A rival position posits that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and cultural beliefs being equally or more influential. Another position suggests that institutions are endogenous, shaped by deeper historical and cultural factors. This review will explore these debates, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. The discussion begins with the foundational arguments that institutions matter for economic growth, and how these arguments have evolved over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The settlers' mortality rates in the colonies dictated the type of institutions they established, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence was a critical factor in determining the economic trajectories of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial conditions and the extractive strategies of the colonizers, had profound effects on the economic development of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this reversal to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced economic outcomes remain complex. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) emphasize the robustness of their findings across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) share a perspective that the creation of financial institutions and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework is evident in the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694. As a result, transaction costs per exchange decline, but the aggregate volume of exchanges increases, driving economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This pattern is consistent with the broader institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, which North and Weingast (1989, p.29) link to the success of capital markets and the commitment of the regime to enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implication is that while institutions play a crucial role, their impact is nuanced and interacts with other factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, challenging the notion that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were instead shaped by the power dynamics and conflicts between different social groups. Ogilvie contends that the prevailing beliefs within a society influence its institutions, but these beliefs are not institutions themselves. These beliefs can be seen as part of the institutional structure, as North and his co-authors argue, and can significantly impact economic outcomes. For instance, the shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights in Latin America have been linked to economic failures due to the lack of institutions that guarantee property rights and contract enforcement. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who highlight that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. These policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutions in ways that favor certain groups. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is nuanced. Ogilvie acknowledges that the role of institutions in economic growth is complex and interacts with other factors, suggesting that institutions alone may not be the sole determinant of economic growth. This interaction underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that shape long-run economic outcomes.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) jointly assert. This body of literature suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental to economic growth than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This indicates that technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, and that institutions play a supporting role in facilitating this progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, institutions still play a significant role in ensuring that these advancements are sustained and effectively utilized. The interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is complex, with each influencing the other in ways that shape long-run economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that Africa's underdevelopment can be attributed to a culture of mistrust, which was exacerbated by the slave trade (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, still evident today, is a significant factor in Africa's economic stagnation. The slave trade's impact on trust is profound, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn and Wantchekon suggest that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust is a critical mechanism through which the slave trade's legacy persists, shaping long-run economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.2) further supports this by highlighting that the slave trade spanned nearly 500 years, significantly longer than the period of colonial rule, which suggests that the adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are deeply entrenched (Nunn 2008, p.2). This long duration implies that the institutional frameworks, which are often cited as crucial for economic growth, may have been fundamentally altered by the slave trade. The implication is that the legacy of the slave trade, through the creation of a culture of mistrust, has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interactions between historical periods, economic paths, and institutional frameworks. These scholars emphasise that the economic outcomes of African countries cannot be reduced to a single narrative of colonial exploitation or institutional failure. Austin's critique highlights that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography, which focuses on secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth, faces challenges in quantitative testing and explanatory sufficiency. This implies that the institutional frameworks in Africa were dynamically shaped by local economic and political actions. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic activities, such as when Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further support this by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more nuanced economic landscape than previously characterised. This suggests that the institutional impacts on African economic development were not uniform and were influenced by a variety of factors, including local endowments and transport costs. Thus, the legacy of colonial rule and the slave trade, while significant, cannot fully account for the diverse economic trajectories observed in Africa today.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the historical interplay between political and economic factors. Institutions, such as secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, are crucial for economic development. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges. This institutional framework is essential for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast further illustrate this point by highlighting how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, which in turn supported economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was crucial for the development of capital markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were pivotal in shaping the long-run economic growth of England. This underscores the importance of credible commitments in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional impacts on economic development, as seen in the diverse trajectories of African economies. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous economic and political action, which influenced institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures. The legacy of colonial rule and the slave trade, while significant, cannot fully account for the diverse economic trajectories observed in Africa today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This nuanced view of institutional impacts is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and the community. This perspective underscores the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes by mediating conflicts and allocating resources (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This highlights the importance of understanding the historical and political context in which institutions operate. The historical evidence from capital markets in seventeenth-century England shows that institutional changes, such as the creation of the Bank of England, were crucial for economic growth. This evidence settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutions per se, and in particular, the institutional changes described, were revolutionary and had a significant impact on economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
