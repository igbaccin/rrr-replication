The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is whether the institutional framework of a society can catalyze or hinder economic development over extended periods. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one factor among many. One position posits that institutions are the most crucial factor in determining economic outcomes, while another argues that institutions are important but secondary to other factors like geography or culture. A third perspective suggests that institutions are shaped by other factors, such as colonial policies, and thus are not independent variables. The interplay between these views shapes the current discourse on economic development. Institutions are not just rules and regulations but the underlying frameworks that govern economic interactions and influence long-term growth prospects. Institutions matter because they provide the incentives and constraints that shape economic behavior over time. This is evident in the historical development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The significance of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the fact that they are not static but evolve in response to political and economic pressures, as seen in the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This evolution reflects the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic outcomes over the long run. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

The evolution of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This financial revolution was a product of fiscal crises and political trade-offs, where certain rights were exchanged for revenues, ultimately facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. The emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement was critical. These institutions facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This dynamic highlights the interplay between political and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long run. This institutional development was not just about the rules governing economic exchange but also about the enforcement and change of these rules, underpinning the commitment to secure property rights. This commitment was crucial for economic growth, as evidenced by the success of capital markets in England.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) identify distributional conflicts as a driving force behind economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and Africa. Ogilvie's analysis highlights that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, and the craft and merchant guilds, were shaped by conflicts over distribution rather than efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency. Ogilvie argues that these institutions were shaped by distributional conflicts, which were influenced by the power dynamics and interests of different social groups. These groups often had conflicting interests, and the resulting institutions reflected the outcomes of these conflicts. The merchant guilds, for example, were shaped by the interests of merchants who sought to control markets and exclude competitors. This dynamic was not unique to Europe. In Africa, Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women were significantly marginalized in access to land and resources, which was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule. This marginalization had profound implications for economic development, as women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The codification of customary law under colonial rule further entrenched the subordinate status of women, limiting their economic contributions. This pattern of marginalization was not limited to Africa but was also evident in Europe, where women were often excluded from economic institutions and activities. This perspective highlights that economic institutions are not just about efficiency but also about the distribution of power and resources. The downstream implication of this is that the role of marginalized groups in economic institutions is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. This is evident in the case of African women, who significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).

Colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, which in turn affected the economic trajectories of their colonies. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in areas with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound and lasting effects on economic development. The extractive institutions in high-mortality regions created persistent barriers to economic growth, while the more inclusive institutions in low-mortality regions fostered long-term economic prosperity. This institutional divergence is crucial for understanding the varying economic outcomes observed in former colonies today. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This highlights the role of power dynamics in shaping economic institutions, with politically powerful groups often prioritizing their own interests over broader economic development. The downstream implication of this is that the distribution of power and resources within colonial institutions played a critical role in determining long-run economic growth. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is complex. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was central to the long-run development of former colonies. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was mediated by other factors, such as the availability of industrial opportunities. Therefore, while colonial institutions were a significant factor in shaping long-run economic growth, they were not the only determinant.

The historical legacy of slavery has shaped economic development through the persistence of institutional and cultural impacts, which highlight how historical inequalities have enduring effects over time, as seen in the case of African women. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the institutions established in New World societies with greater inequality, such as those shaped by slavery, advantaged elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality. This pattern is evident in the differential paths of development in Latin America and other regions, where institutional differences have had profound effects on economic growth. These institutional legacies are not isolated to the New World; Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that Africa's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a lasting adverse effect on economic development. This enduring impact is not merely a result of colonial rule or recent events, but a direct consequence of the historical slave trades. Therefore, the institutional and cultural impacts of slavery have created a lasting legacy that continues to influence economic development.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9); (Pamuk 2007, p.23) argue that early modern economic divergence between regions was driven by demographic shifts and structural changes, rather than institutional factors. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence was not merely a result of institutional differences but was significantly influenced by demographic shifts and the flexibility of institutions in response to these changes. For instance, the economic trajectory of Tuscany, which stagnated after the Black Death, contrasts sharply with that of Holland, which rapidly adapted to new economic opportunities due to its institutional flexibility. This institutional flexibility allowed for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing under weak monopolies, fostering economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further elaborate on this by noting that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe can be attributed to merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. The uneven distribution of the fruits of early modern development is evident in the larger surplus over basic food needs that skilled labourers enjoyed compared to unskilled labourers. However, the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warmer climates may have reinforced this effect, but this is not the message that 'world historians' would wish to draw (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This indicates that while demographic shifts and structural changes were pivotal, the role of institutions in mediating these changes cannot be overlooked.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by showing that long-run economic growth is shaped by complex interactions between institutions and historical contexts, rather than by a single, overarching institutional framework. This responsiveness is evident in cases where colonial administrations adjusted their policies in response to indigenous economic activities. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium contradicts Nunn's hypothesis that colonial institutions uniformly aimed to suppress African economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). This variation implies that the long-term effects of institutions on economic development are contingent on specific historical and geographical factors, which complicates the notion of a straightforward institutional determinant of growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is crucial for sustained economic growth. This perspective highlights that technological advancements are not merely tools for economic development but are the mainspring of it. Mokyr’s emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge as the primary driver of economic growth aligns with Kuznets’s observation that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks is essential for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring that economic growth is sustainable. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these conflicts, underscoring the importance of political stability in facilitating economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is fundamental, the institutional environment must adapt to harness its full potential. Mokyr acknowledges that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, highlighting the need for coordination between technological and institutional developments. This coordination is vital for preventing the erosion of economic gains through rent-seeking behaviors. Mokyr's recognition of coordination failures and the policies to rectify them underscores the intricate relationship between technological progress and institutional frameworks in driving economic growth. The growth of economic reasonableness, while less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is nonetheless a critical component in sustaining long-term economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the finding that the quality of public goods in African economies was shaped by both endogenous and exogenous factors during the colonial era, influencing the development of educational systems (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). African political leaders and families made complex cost-benefit calculations regarding formal education, which were influenced by the institutional changes introduced by colonial rule. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions altered the perceived benefits of education, leading to varied outcomes across different regions. The implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule was not uniform and was often contingent on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the economic performance of these regions was more nuanced than previously characterized. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development was not uniformly negative, and that local factors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The interplay between colonial institutions and local conditions underscores the complex nature of institutional change in African colonies and its long-term effects on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the divergence of economic trajectories between colonies with different colonial institutions. The institutional frameworks established by European colonizers had profound and lasting effects on the economic development of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, where Europeans did not settle and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law, significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding aligns with North’s argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify efficient property rights and provide enforcement, which in turn reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). The economic performance of African colonies was influenced by the interplay between colonial institutions and local conditions, underscoring the complex nature of institutional change and its long-term effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9). Local factors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, as seen in the case of commodity production in African colonies where European settlers were not present (Austin 2008, p.15).

The institutional legacies of colonialism are further supported by the evidence that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is reinforced by the observation that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The new economic history of Africa highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on the economic trajectories of African colonies.

The shared finding among these sources is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). There remains a disagreement on the extent to which local factors, such as ethno-linguistic fragmentation, influenced the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Hopkins argues that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of the development experience of the former colonies, which shows that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18).
