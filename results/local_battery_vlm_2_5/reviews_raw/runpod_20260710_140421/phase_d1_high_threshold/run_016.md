The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over time. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who emphasize institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who argue for other factors, such as geographic determinants or cultural beliefs. Proponents of the institutional perspective argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure secure contracts, is crucial for fostering economic development. This view is often contrasted with those who posit that geographic factors, such as climate and disease prevalence, or cultural beliefs and values, play a more significant role in determining economic outcomes. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the fundamental cause of economic growth or if they are merely one of many contributing factors. This question is central to understanding why some countries thrive economically while others stagnate, and it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies. The literature reveals a nuanced interplay between institutions and other factors, highlighting the complex nature of economic growth and the need for a holistic approach to understanding its determinants. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This institutional framework, which secured property rights, was a critical factor in the economic growth of seventeenth-century England, as argued by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during this period led to a significant decline in transaction costs. This decline reflects increased security in property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. This institutional evolution was both an economic and a political phenomenon. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore that the success of these institutions depended on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and the mechanisms for enforcing and changing these rules. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and economic growth. These changes fostered a environment where property rights were secure, and economic transactions were efficient, leading to sustained economic growth. The development of these institutions was a response to fiscal crises, which prompted the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This trade set the stage for the English financial revolution, which in turn facilitated economic growth.

In contrast to the efficiency-driven perspective, distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were designed to protect the interests of the elite and maintain the existing social order. For example, serfdom and guilds were institutions that restricted the economic opportunities of the lower classes and ensured that the elite maintained their dominant position. The elite had the power to shape institutions in their favor, leading to a society where economic institutions were driven by the need to maintain social control. This perspective challenges the conventional view that economic institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) also note that the specific factor endowments of New World societies led to extreme inequality, which in turn influenced the development of institutions that perpetuated this inequality. This highlights the complex interplay between distributional conflicts and factor endowments in shaping economic institutions.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. highlight that European settlers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates, leading to a dichotomy between settler colonies with inclusive institutions and extractive states in high-mortality regions. This institutional divergence was driven by the European colonizers' objectives: in low-mortality areas, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in high-mortality regions, they implemented extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources back to Europe. These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, had long-lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these colonial institutions were shaped by political and economic interests of the colonizers. The persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era significantly impacted long-run economic growth. The relationship between colonial institutions and economic outcomes is complex. Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by other factors such as industrialization opportunities. The long-term effects of colonial institutions on economic growth underscore the importance of understanding the historical context in which these institutions were established.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Frankema and van Waijenburg, examine African economic performance and the impact of colonial rule. They focus on historical data and real wage levels, highlighting that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective provides a nuanced understanding of African economic development, challenging the notion that the region has always been economically stagnant. The analysis of real wage levels in British African colonies, for instance, shows that many economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This suggests that while colonial institutions and foreign trade played a significant role in economic fluctuations, local endowments and political institutions also influenced economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage trends over the colonial period reveals that economic conditions varied significantly across different regions and time periods. This variability underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and geographical context when examining the long-term effects of colonial rule on economic development. The timing and scale of growth in export production, tied closely to foreign trade, varied both between and within countries. This variability suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was mediated by a range of factors, including local endowments and transport costs (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

This variability is further explored by Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), who examine how historical factors, such as colonial policies and the slave trade, have influenced African economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial governments often had incentives to encourage indigenous commodity production, even in the absence of European settlers. This is because colonial administrations sought fiscal security and wanted to ensure that their subjects had a material interest in maintaining the status quo. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this point by highlighting Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This suggests that the impact of colonial policies on economic development was not uniform and could vary based on local conditions and administrative priorities. This mistrust is rooted in both internal norms and external environmental factors, such as the rule of law, and is at least twice as large as the external channel. One downstream implication of this mistrust is its effect on trustworthiness and economic development. This implies that the legacy of the slave trade, through its impact on trust, has had lasting effects on Africa's economic trajectory. Understanding these historical factors is essential for comprehending the long-term economic growth patterns in Africa, as they highlight the complex interplay between historical events, institutional changes, and economic outcomes.

This divergence is analyzed by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23), who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia began before 1800, attributing this to structural economic changes. They suggest that the rise of merchant distribution and finance in Europe played a crucial role in economic growth, driving up productivity in the traded goods sector. This mechanism is supported by Pamuk's (2007, p.16) analysis of urbanisation rates, which indicates that the economic growth in northwest Europe was marked by structural change, including a shift towards urbanisation and commercialisation. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly spread, with skilled labourers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled labourers. This pattern suggests that the economic divergence was about the distribution of its benefits. The role of institutions in this process is complex. Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland enabled rapid responses to new economic opportunities, while regions with stronger monopolies, such as Tuscany, lagged behind. This institutional flexibility was a key factor in the economic divergence, showing that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) underscores that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological change and institutional adjustments is crucial for economic growth, as it allows societies to resolve growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological advancements provide a robust foundation for economic growth, the institutional framework must adapt and evolve to sustain this growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with different studies highlighting various mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Institutions matter in economic growth, as they help to create a stable environment where economic activities can flourish. For instance, North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is because falling transaction costs, which result from secure property rights, facilitate market exchanges and economic growth. Similarly, the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and contributing to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This supports the idea that institutions that enforce property rights and reduce transaction costs are essential for long-term economic growth. Furthermore, the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which the regime is committed to enforcing rules and property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, which provide a striking indication of their effectiveness. This evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that support economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and effective enforcement of rules.

The specific types of institutions that promote economic growth can vary significantly across different historical and geographical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions in some colonies and more inclusive institutions in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These different institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic outcomes, with inclusive institutions promoting long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin highlights that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialisation of Europe. This shows that the specific institutional frameworks that promote economic growth can vary across different historical contexts, and that understanding these variations is crucial for comprehending long-run economic growth. Furthermore, the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) document the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies and show that it was related to industrialization, providing evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This highlights the importance of understanding the specific institutional frameworks that promote economic growth in different historical and geographical contexts.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the fact that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may have conflicting interests. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains. This highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape institutions and their impact on economic growth. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between these perspectives is the long-term development of former colonies, which shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in their economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence underscores the importance of understanding the specific institutional frameworks that promote economic growth in different historical and geographical contexts, as well as the political dynamics that shape these institutions.
