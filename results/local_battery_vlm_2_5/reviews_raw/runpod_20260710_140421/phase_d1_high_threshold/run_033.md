The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains a contentious issue among economic historians and economists. At the heart of this debate lie differing views on the causal mechanisms that drive economic development. One position emphasizes the significance of institutions in fostering secure property rights and facilitating economic exchanges, thereby promoting growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.6). Another perspective argues that distributional conflicts and political power dynamics are more critical in determining the nature of institutions and their impact on economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). A third view attributes the reversal of fortunes among former European colonies to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as well as geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These differing positions highlight the complexity of institutional analysis and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic growth.

The role of institutions in facilitating economic growth has been extensively studied, with particular focus on how they reduce transaction costs and enforce property rights. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and ensured that transaction costs were falling, thereby promoting economic growth. The interaction between institutions and economic development is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al., who argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The establishment of extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole highlights the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by fostering secure property rights. Their work challenges neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. This security was crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. This view is supported by their finding that the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and knowledge-driven factors are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This knowledge-driven growth involves the invention of new technologies and the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Economic reasonableness refers to the ability of societies to coordinate their efforts and resolve conflicts that arise from technological and economic changes. This implies that while technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own. The sustainability of economic improvement also depends on the ability of societies to make the necessary institutional adjustments to complement technological progress.

Distributional conflicts explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe better than efficiency considerations, challenging the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Their argument is that the extremely different environments in which the Europeans established their colonies led to societies with very different degrees of inequality, and these differences persisted over time, affecting the course of development through their impact on the institutions that evolved (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This means that the institutions that evolved in these societies were those that benefited the elites. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) proposes that institutions should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that institutions are a key driver of economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also argues that the game-theoretic view, according to which 'beliefs are institutions', does not apply to the study of institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This means that institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including distributional conflicts. This complexity suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is more nuanced than previously thought.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades in Africa had a significant adverse effect on economic development, a finding supported by Nunn's work which shows that Africa's slave trades lasted nearly 500 years, dwarfing the duration of colonial rule (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by eroding social capital and impeding cooperation. The mechanism through which this occurs is multifaceted: both external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, and internal norms of mistrust play crucial roles (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon also note that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the deep-seated cultural impact of the slave trade is a primary barrier to economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding is particularly salient given the broader literature on the importance of trust for economic development, highlighting that the origins of mistrust are deeply rooted in historical events like the slave trade. The long-term effects of the slave trade on African economic development are a result of immediate economic disruptions and enduring cultural and institutional legacies. The economic impact of these legacies is profound, as they continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes and policy effectiveness.

High mortality rates in colonies, such as those in Africa and parts of Asia, led to the establishment of extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence is pivotal for understanding the long-term economic trajectories of these regions. The extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers in high-mortality areas persisted post-independence, continuing to hinder economic growth. The institutional legacy of colonialism is a crucial factor in understanding the persistent income disparities between former colonies with different institutional histories.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Austin 2008, p.2). They challenge the conventional view that African poverty is a result of long-standing structural impediments and argue for a deeper understanding of the historical determinants of economic development in Africa. Their work underscores the importance of examining the real wage divergence across British Africa to understand the broader economic trajectories of the continent. This perspective is further supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more dynamic relationship between colonial powers and local economies than previously thought. This dynamic suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism was not monolithic and that local actors played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. Austin also points out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, highlighting the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the long-term economic development of Africa. The complexity of these interactions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shaped economic growth in Africa.

This reassessment suggests that the economic trajectories of Africa and Asia were more dynamic and varied than previously thought, challenging the notion of uniform underdevelopment. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that these regions had higher levels of economic performance than previously recognized. They show that the availability of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence has enabled a more nuanced understanding of economic growth in these regions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) also note that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was due to geographical factors and high levels of investment, which raises questions about the role of institutional factors in economic development.

Long-term structural changes in Europe between 1500 and 1800 led to significant economic divergence, as van Zanden (1999, p.1) (Pamuk 2007, p.23) argue in their studies. The wage data across European cities and regions reveal that economic development, measured by urbanization, did not necessarily correlate with improvements in the standard of living (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This economic shift was not uniform across Europe; for instance, Tuscany's economy stagnated while Holland rapidly responded to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The institutional flexibility in Holland, which allowed for the growth of new towns and rural manufacturing under weak monopolies, contrasts with the strengthening of existing monopolies in Tuscany (Pamuk 2007, p.23). The economic divergence was already evident by 1450, as Pamuk (2007, p.16) highlights, suggesting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe. This divergence was marked by differing urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The implications of these structural changes are profound, as they highlight the complex interplay between economic development and institutional factors over the long run.

Institutional frameworks, by defining the rules that govern economic interactions, play a fundamental role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies, with extractive institutions inhibiting growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is echoed by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying and enforcing property rights, thereby reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments illustrate how secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms can drive economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin builds on this by highlighting that the institutional evolution of older industrial economies, including the political delivery of secure property rights, is crucial for understanding long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence from these studies shows that the presence of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is a key driver of economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial policies on the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, as well as geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provide a striking indication of their effectiveness (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Ogilvie adds that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, highlighting the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from these studies shows that the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities plays a central role in long-run economic development.

The precise role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is that institutions define the rules that govern economic interactions, and their effectiveness in enforcing property rights and facilitating market exchanges is a key driver of economic development.
