The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure economic and political interactions, are central to this debate. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions, rather than other factors such as geography or culture, drive economic growth. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance foster investment and productivity. Another position argues that institutions are merely one among several factors, and that their impact is contingent on historical and cultural contexts. A third position contends that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shape core economic institutions. The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth. Institutions are not static; they evolve and adapt, influenced by political, economic, and social dynamics. Understanding this evolution is crucial for grasping how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) traces the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement, which should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). He argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, which reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this view by examining how different colonization strategies led to the establishment of varying institutions, with some colonies setting up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional variation had significant implications for current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate on this by highlighting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective underscores the enduring impact of institutions shaped by colonial policies on long-run economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They posit that the political factors underpinning economic growth included the commitment of the regime to rules governing economic exchange and how these rules are enforced and changed. This perspective highlights how institutions that ensure secure property rights and enforce contracts can facilitate economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) supports this view by emphasizing the need to transform institutions into indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian, thereby underscoring their role in fostering long-term economic development. This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on the broader social and cultural context in which they operate.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine how inequality influenced economic performance in New World societies. They focus on the specific historical contexts and policies that shaped development paths, arguing that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in the New World, where differences in factor endowments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) highlight that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This suggests that the institutional frameworks in these societies were not static but evolved in response to economic and political pressures. The persistence of inequality indicates that these institutional changes did not always translate into more inclusive economic growth. The specific focus on how environmental factors led to societies with different degrees of inequality, and how these differences might have persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on the institutions that evolved, underscores the complex interplay between inequality, institutions, and economic performance.

This perspective shifts the focus to technological and intellectual advancements as fundamental drivers of long-term economic growth, rather than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) explains that technological progress demands institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This is because the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods, spread to a substantial part of the world. These breakthroughs can be termed epochal innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) underscores the importance of the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. He notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited and might ultimately be eliminated by the relentless erosion of rent-seeking (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional framework must adapt to sustain economic growth, even if this adaptation is not always straightforward. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and on a community of feeling, in short, the modern national state, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary levels of trust. This mistrust is not merely a response to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained as an internal norm. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings align with Nunn's broader argument that Africa's slave trades had a lasting adverse effect on economic development. This internal mistrust hinders economic development by undermining the trust necessary for productive social and economic interactions. The slave trade's impact on Africa's economic trajectory is profound, and its legacy of mistrust continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) identify that the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule was a distinct phenomenon, separate from the broader discussion on institutions and long-run economic growth. This development was driven by the cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive. The British colonial administration played a significant role in this process, as it created an environment where formal education could flourish. This is evident in the demand for missionary education, which was closely tied to the new institutions introduced by the British. The British colonial rule's impact on African education was not uniform across the continent. For instance, the British colonial administration in some areas actively promoted education as a means of creating a local elite that could assist in governance. This approach differed from other colonial powers, which often viewed education as a means of control rather than development. The British administration's approach to education in Africa had long-lasting effects on the continent's economic development. The real wage levels and trends in British African colonies over the period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) provide insights into the economic development of these regions. This period saw significant changes in the economic landscape of Africa, with formal education playing a crucial role in shaping these changes. Understanding the determinants of the real wage divergence across British Africa and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, highlighting the need for further research in this area. The long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, is crucial for understanding the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development.

This understanding of African welfare development highlights the need to consider both historical paths and African initiatives in shaping institutional change (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is echoed in the work of Hopkins (2009, p.18), who suggests that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Specifically, Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic is illustrated by Austin (2008, p.2), who shows that Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This indicates that African initiatives played a significant role in shaping institutional change. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies the downstream implication of this mechanism, noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when analyzing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue. In the context of colonialism, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) argument that institutions matter in economic growth. North shows that institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs, which in turn facilitate market exchanges and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are examples of institutional changes that reflect increased security of property rights, critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth.

The role of institutions is not limited to colonial contexts. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is supported by the evidence that colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production and changed their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political action. This highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and its interaction with local conditions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) further underscores the importance of institutions by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This suggests that the design and function of institutions are shaped by the interests and power dynamics of the groups involved.

These findings underscore the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on economic outcomes.
