The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of why some regions thrive economically over the long term while others stagnate, and whether institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules, informal constraints, and their enforcement characteristics, are the primary drivers of this divergence. The debate centers on whether institutions are the key determinant of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or colonial policies, play more significant roles. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional differences, particularly those stemming from colonization strategies, significantly influence current income per capita, while Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This disagreement underscores the complexity of identifying the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. The debate extends to the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate, such as the enforcement of property rights and the development of financial institutions. The central tension lies in whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or if they are merely one of many interacting factors. The answer to this question has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. North and Weingast highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth, suggesting that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is crucial. This sets the stage for examining how different scholars have approached the role of institutions in economic growth, and whether their arguments converge or diverge on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) elaborate on this by showing that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, often maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). As a result, colonies with extractive institutions experienced slower economic development, illustrating the profound impact of institutional design on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring effects of these institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores that the long-run economic growth is fundamentally shaped by the institutions established during critical historical periods, which can either foster or hinder development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their work, along with Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), underscores that the institutional changes in England, specifically those that ensured secure property rights, were pivotal in driving long-term economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the success of these institutions was marked by their ability to enforce rules and commit to them, which in turn fostered a stable environment for economic activity. Austin extends this by showing that the institutional evolution in England was part of a broader pattern of institutional development across Europe. Austin's work highlights that the path to economic growth was influenced by the interplay between colonial administrations and indigenous economic actions, illustrating how institutional changes could be adaptive and responsive to local conditions. This adaptability was crucial in shaping the economic trajectories of different regions, as seen in the colonial contexts where fiscal security and material incentives played a significant role in institutional design. Austin's analysis suggests that the institutional frameworks that supported economic growth were not static but evolved in response to the incentives created by economic and political actions, a finding that underscores the dynamic nature of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run economic growth.

The role of women in economic development during colonial periods highlights how gender dynamics influenced agricultural practices and land access, which were shaped by customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). This legal framework, which privileged men, limited women's access to land and resources crucial for economic activities. This shift in educational benefits likely exacerbated gender disparities, as women faced additional barriers to accessing these new opportunities. The intersection of gender dynamics and colonial institutions thus shaped economic trajectories by limiting women's participation in profitable agricultural sectors and formal education. This highlights how colonial institutions, by entrenching patriarchal norms, constrained the economic potential of African societies.

Technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development, and the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of this process (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from technological changes and to ensure that modern economic growth is possible. Such conflicts can be resolved peacefully by the sovereign state, which plays a crucial role in maintaining political stability during periods of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that these conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to avoid weakening the political fabric of society. This underscores that the institutional environment must facilitate the integration of new technologies and knowledge, thereby enabling sustained economic growth. Institutions that support the dissemination and application of new knowledge are essential for leveraging technological progress to drive long-term economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa had profound and enduring effects on economic development, shaping contemporary economic outcomes in ways that challenge the view that Africa's underdevelopment can be attributed solely to colonialism or recent events. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and significantly impedes economic development. This mistrust operates through both internal norms and external environmental factors, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external one. Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrates that the slave trade's duration, spanning nearly 500 years, is far longer than the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This temporal perspective highlights the enduring influence of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory. This suggests that while the slave trade had adverse effects, the presence of robust institutions could mitigate these impacts and promote economic development.

This perspective shifts the focus from efficiency to the political and social dynamics that underpin institutional design and economic performance. Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman  argue that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective challenges the prevailing view that these institutions were primarily driven by efficiency considerations. Instead, Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman  contend that these institutions were designed to serve the interests of elites, who sought to maintain their power and wealth by restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the lower classes. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influenced institutions is through the regulation of labor markets. For instance, serfdom was a system that bound laborers to the land, preventing them from moving to areas where they could command higher wages. This system was maintained by elites who benefited from a steady supply of cheap labor. This system also had downstream implications for economic development. The restriction of labor mobility hindered the development of a dynamic labor market, which in turn limited the growth of industries that required a flexible and skilled workforce. This pattern is further illustrated by the persistence of inequality in New World societies, where institutions were shaped by the interests of elites to maintain their power and wealth. The result was a system that perpetuated inequality and hindered economic growth, as seen in the case of Latin America. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “.” Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) report, “These differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recentiy attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally (North, 1988; Engerman and Sokoloff, 1997; Coatsworth 1993, 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, 2000; Engerman, Haber and Sokoloff, 2000).”

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions and property rights. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established in colonies by European settlers significantly influenced current income per capita. These institutions were shaped by the initial conditions and strategies of colonization, with extractive institutions in some colonies leading to persistent economic underdevelopment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North and Weingast also highlight the importance of institutions in economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and credible commitment by the regime are critical for fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, for instance, was crucial for securing property rights and promoting economic growth. This shows that institutions that enforce property rights and provide a stable environment for economic activities are essential for long-term economic development.

The historical context of colonization and the establishment of institutions further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify and enforce property rights, which are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is also evident in the context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by North's argument that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe shows that institutions that maximize the rents to powerful groups, rather than promoting long-run growth, are likely to be established.

Institutions matter for economic growth because they provide the rules that govern economic interactions and enforce property rights. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions are critical for long-term economic development, as shown by Acemoglu et al. with the example of European colonization strategies and their impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific disagreement is whether the type of institutions established in colonies were extractive or growth-promoting, as shown by North and Weingast with the example of seventeenth-century England and the evolution of institutions governing public choice (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the difference in economic performance between colonies with extractive institutions and those with growth-promoting institutions, as measured by current income per capita.
