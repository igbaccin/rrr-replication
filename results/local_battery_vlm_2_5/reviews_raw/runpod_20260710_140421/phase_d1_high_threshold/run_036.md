The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods, and the mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. The central disagreement revolves around the nature of institutions and their causal impact on growth. Some scholars posit that institutions are the primary drivers, emphasizing their role in securing property rights and enforcing contracts. Others argue that institutions are merely one among several factors, with geographical and cultural elements playing equally significant roles. Still, others contend that the institutional framework is shaped by underlying social structures and power dynamics, which in turn influence economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are independent variables that shape growth, or if they are themselves shaped by broader historical and social contexts. This tension underpins the scholarly discourse on economic growth, as evidenced by the varied perspectives on the role of institutions in historical and contemporary development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European colonizers implemented different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In areas with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences, rooted in colonial policies, persisted over time and had profound effects on economic development. The extractive nature of these institutions hindered economic development in the colonies, as they were not conducive to investment and growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) support this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all correlate with current income per capita. These findings underscore the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The implications of these findings are clear: the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the critical role of institutions in long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that institutional reforms are pivotal for African economic development, as they address systemic issues that hinder growth and poverty reduction. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that "good" institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law and fostering progressive development is crucial for addressing the systemic issues that have historically hindered African economic development. The quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the effectiveness of institutions in upholding the rule of law and fostering progressive development (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that institutional reforms can mitigate these impediments and promote long-term economic growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of African economic development in order to design effective institutional reforms that can promote sustainable growth and poverty reduction.

The creation of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 marked a significant milestone, enabling a decline in transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights. This financial revolution was both an economic and a political phenomenon, as it involved the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. This trade was a response to fiscal crises, highlighting the interconnected nature of political and economic institutions in shaping economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which demonstrates the critical role of credible commitment in economic development (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Recent research has upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, contesting the notion that institutional changes were revolutionary. This perspective challenges the idea that specific institutional changes were the sole drivers of economic growth, indicating that broader economic and political factors also played significant roles.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe and the New World was profoundly shaped by distributional conflicts. These authors highlight that institutions often emerged as tools for elites to consolidate power and maintain social hierarchies. In pre-industrial Europe, institutions such as serfdom and guilds were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply rooted in the power dynamics between different social classes. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions should be viewed as indispensable instruments in economic history, emphasizing their role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Similarly, in the New World, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that the extreme inequality in wealth and power led to the development of institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency concerns and instead points to the critical role of power dynamics in institutional development. However, the downstream implications of these institutional arrangements are qualified by the broader economic and political factors that also played significant roles in shaping economic growth, thus suggesting that a multifaceted approach is necessary to fully understand the role of institutions in long-run economic development.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, a view shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that technological advancements and the diffusion of knowledge are the primary drivers of economic growth. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing that technological progress requires institutional adjustments to be efficiently utilized. Institutional progress complements technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. Mokyr further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, and economic gains might be eroded by rent-seeking behavior (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutional adaptations in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements for long-term economic growth. However, Kuznets qualifies this by noting that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that the political fabric of society is also a significant factor in long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and the timing of the great divergence, arguing that previous characterizations oversimplify causation and misinterpret historical periods. They posit that the economic trajectories of different regions cannot be compressed into a single narrative, and that the timing of economic divergence was more complex than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) qualify this by noting that the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies was not as dire as previously characterized, with levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This qualification underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic development in Africa, one that takes into account the diversity of experiences and the role of local factors in shaping economic outcomes. The implication is that the historical economic divergences were more complex than previously thought, and that a more granular analysis of regional economic trajectories is necessary to fully understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that European colonization strategies in different regions led to the establishment of extractive institutions that persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The role of institutions in economic growth is further highlighted by North (1989, p.9), who demonstrates that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. These findings underscore that institutions, by defining and enforcing property rights, are critical in reducing transaction costs and fostering long-term economic development.

The diversity of institutional outcomes across regions is evident in the different colonization strategies employed by Europeans. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the variation in European mortality rates influenced the types of institutions established in colonies. In some cases, Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in others, they established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This diversity in institutional outcomes highlights the complex interplay between historical factors and institutional development, which in turn shapes long-run economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates on this by showing that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous economic and political action, altering institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives. This interaction between colonial policies and local factors underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and impact economic growth.

The institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have had enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.6), who shows that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement leads to falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate. These findings highlight that institutions, by reducing transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges, play a crucial role in long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who argues that good institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This underscores the importance of institutional quality in fostering economic growth and development. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency considerations. This highlights the importance of understanding the historical context and the political dynamics that shape institutional development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a shared finding (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes remain a subject of debate. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This debate is settled by the historical evidence of colonial administration policies, which show that institutions that facilitated economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This historical evidence, combined with the empirical comparison of institutional outcomes across regions, demonstrates the complex interplay between historical factors and institutional development, which in turn shapes long-run economic growth.
