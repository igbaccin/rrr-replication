The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with scholars debating the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they are the primary drivers of economic development. One position argues that institutions are the key determinant of economic growth, while another posits that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper economic or cultural factors. This disagreement underscores the importance of understanding the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, as it has implications for policy and our understanding of historical economic development. A key point of contention is the extent to which institutions can be changed to promote economic growth, or whether they are merely the result of underlying economic forces. The implications of this debate are significant, as it informs our understanding of how to foster economic development in both historical and contemporary contexts. A deeper understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic development. The debate over the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes is particularly relevant to the study of colonial legacies and their impact on contemporary economic development. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, driven by European policies, played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in former colonies. European settlers established institutions that varied significantly depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This approach fostered economic development by creating an environment conducive to private property and investment. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth, had lasting negative effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutional differences persisted over time, significantly influencing current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. The institutional framework established during colonial times continues to affect economic performance, illustrating the profound and lasting influence of historical institutional choices on long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the development of secure property rights is a critical factor in fostering long-run economic growth. They demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in establishing a framework that ensured the security of property rights. North (1989, p.9) elaborates on this by highlighting the role of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. This institutional development reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional transformation, as certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North (1989, p.6) further explains that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement led to falling costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, which in turn supported economic growth through increased specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) underscore the importance of credible commitment in this process, emphasizing that the regime's commitment to enforcing property rights was a critical political factor. This commitment ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were established and reliably enforced, adaptable to changing circumstances. These institutional developments in England contrast with traditional interpretations of economic history, which often attribute economic development primarily to other factors.

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. This process involves the creation of new knowledge and the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for its efficient use. These adjustments are crucial because technological progress often conflicts with existing social structures, such as rural modes of life and large family patterns. For instance, Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that modern technology is incompatible with traditional lifestyles, requiring significant institutional changes to integrate new innovations effectively. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological advancements are fundamental, they must be accompanied by institutional changes that facilitate their adoption and diffusion. This highlights the importance of technological advancements and the institutional framework that supports their implementation.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) examine the lasting impacts of historical factors on African economic development, focusing on how colonial policies and the slave trade have shaped contemporary economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that when European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged local commodity production, indicating that colonial policies could be responsive to local conditions. This points to a more nuanced view of colonial institutions, where local contexts and agency influenced institutional outcomes. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) argue that this mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activities. This implies that while colonial institutions might have been adaptive in some contexts, the legacy of the slave trade has had a profound and lasting negative impact on African economic development.

Colonial policies exerted varying influences on educational outcomes across Africa, shaping the continent's development trajectory in ways that are often overlooked in discussions about institutions and economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) examine how colonial policies impacted educational outcomes, arguing that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers. This perspective challenges the notion that all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts on African education systems. This dynamic highlights how colonial policies could foster conditions that either encouraged or hindered educational development, with long-term implications for economic growth. The specific mechanisms through which these policies influenced institutional outcomes and economic growth remain complex. Frankema (2012, p.1) underscores the importance of understanding these nuances, suggesting that the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa are deeply intertwined with the broader institutional and economic legacies of colonial rule. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial policies shaped educational outcomes and, by extension, institutional development in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and incentives that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies led to different institutional outcomes, with some colonies adopting extractive institutions that hindered growth. This finding highlights that the specific type of institutions established during colonial rule had lasting impacts on economic development. Similarly, North (1989, p.6) argues that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for long-term economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) complement this by emphasizing that credible commitment underpins growth, showing that the development of institutions that enforce rules and bind the regime are crucial for fostering economic growth. Together, these sources illustrate that the institutional framework established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial policies on educational outcomes and institutional development in Africa. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the institutional evolution in older industrial economies can be understood within the context of patterns and paths of institutional development. This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.9) argument that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) also highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, showing that the interaction between colonial policies and local conditions shaped institutional outcomes. This interaction is crucial for understanding how colonial policies influenced educational outcomes and, by extension, institutional development in Africa. The precise mechanisms through which these policies influenced institutional outcomes and economic growth remain complex, as shown by the diverse strategies adopted by European colonizers and the varying responses of colonial administrations to local conditions.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth are complex and multifaceted. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This finding highlights that historical events and policies can have long-lasting impacts on institutional development and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) also emphasize that institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.6) argument that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for long-term economic growth. Together, these sources illustrate that the institutional framework established during colonial rule significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes, highlighting the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth.
