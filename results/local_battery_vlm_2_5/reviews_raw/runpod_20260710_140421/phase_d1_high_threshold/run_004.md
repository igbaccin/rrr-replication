The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among scholars, with the debate revolving around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes. One live disagreement centers on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth. On one hand, some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective governance. On the other hand, there are those who contend that economic growth is influenced by a variety of factors, including geography and colonial policies. This disagreement highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that contribute to long-run economic growth. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping economic growth is crucial for understanding the historical and contemporary factors that influence economic development. Some scholars, such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), argue that institutions matter in economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by merging different historical periods and paths.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that long-run economic growth is crucially dependent on the development of specific institutions, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, which reduce transaction costs and enhance property rights security. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) also observe that the success of these institutions relies on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This qualification underscores that while institutional development is necessary for economic growth, the effectiveness of these institutions depends on the political commitment to uphold them. This underscores the complex interplay between political stability and institutional effectiveness in driving long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The European settlers' mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies led to the establishment of institutions that either encouraged investment or were extractive in nature. The persistence of these institutions has had a lasting impact on the economic trajectories of former colonies. This institutional legacy underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on the economic growth of former colonies, illustrating how the initial conditions set by colonial institutions can have profound and lasting effects on economic outcomes.

The institutional impacts of slavery and inequality highlight how historical inequalities shaped by institutions have persisted over time. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the differences in institutions across New World societies can be traced back to the varying degrees of inequality that existed from the outset. This inequality was reinforced by institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and limiting opportunities for the broader population. The persistence of inequality is evident in the enduring effects of these institutions, which continue to shape economic outcomes long after their initial implementation.

One specific mechanism through which this inequality persisted is the concentration of wealth and power in the hands of a few, which was facilitated by institutions that protected elite interests. These institutions often included policies that restricted land ownership, limited access to education, and maintained a rigid social hierarchy. For example, in societies with high levels of inequality, land redistribution was often restricted to prevent the rise of a competitive middle class, thereby maintaining the dominance of the elite. The result was a society where economic opportunities were limited for the majority, perpetuating a cycle of poverty and inequality. This institutional framework impeded economic growth and fostered a societal structure that resisted change, as seen in the enduring legacy of colonial institutions in former colonies. The persistence of inequality was further exacerbated by the lack of social mobility, which prevented individuals from lower socio-economic backgrounds from advancing economically or socially. This lack of mobility was a direct result of institutions that were designed to maintain the status quo, ensuring that the elite remained in power and the majority remained economically disadvantaged. The downstream implication of this institutional framework is that it created a self-reinforcing cycle of inequality, where the institutions that perpetuated inequality were themselves sustained by the very inequality they created.

Institutional change, driven by African initiatives and exogenous causes, significantly impacts the quality of public goods provided by African governments (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that African initiatives to reform institutions can lead to better public goods provision. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) illustrate that women's contributions to economic growth were often marginalized by colonial institutions that codified customary law to reinforce patriarchal structures. This underscores that the quality of institutions is crucial in determining who benefits from economic growth, and how. Frankema (2012, p.15) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This implies that the type of colonial institution matters for the development of human capital, which is a key determinant of long-run economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) also notes that the demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, suggesting that exogenous factors can also influence the impact of institutions on economic outcomes. This highlights the complex interplay between institutions, exogenous factors, and economic growth in Africa.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Simson (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2), argue that post-colonial economic policies in Africa are more nuanced than the prevailing narrative of clientelism and rent-seeking. For example, the budget policies of East Africa during the post-independence period were not as unproductive as often portrayed. Instead, these policies were marked by significant shifts in expenditure patterns, including increased military spending and debt servicing. This pattern of expenditure changes was driven by regional instability and growing domestic dissent, which required substantial military investment. Simson (2019, p.11) elaborates on this by noting that Kenya's subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 percent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending. Similarly, Tanzania experienced a high debt burden with interest payments peaking at 18 percent of expenditure. This indicates that the economic policies addressed immediate challenges and were not merely reflective of clientelist tendencies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries due to a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability underscores the importance of context-specific factors in shaping economic outcomes, even within the broader framework of post-colonial policies.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty results from a complex interplay of historical factors. One specific mechanism through which these historical factors operated was the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which could lead to higher production equilibria. For instance, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production. This adaptation suggests that colonial institutions were influenced by local conditions and actions (Austin 2008, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the long-term picture of economic development in Africa. This underscores the need for more detailed historical data to fully understand the determinants of Africa's economic trajectory. The implications of this finding are significant, as they suggest that African poverty is not an inevitable outcome of historical circumstances but rather a result of specific institutional and economic policies that could be changed.

Institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth, as they create the framework within which economic activities occur. Institutions facilitate economic growth by establishing property rights and enforcing contracts, thereby reducing transaction costs and promoting investment. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because institutions specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement, which in turn reduces costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). Similarly, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and supporting economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a direct result of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement. This implies that the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the fact that "good" institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism. European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks had significant long-term effects on income per capita. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that institutions designed to extract resources can persist and significantly affect current economic outcomes. The institutions established by colonial powers were not always conducive to long-run economic growth, as they were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote economic development in the colonies themselves (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
