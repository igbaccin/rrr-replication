The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy challenges. At the heart of the debate lies a live disagreement over the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. Some scholars assert that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by providing the framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and stable governance structures. Others contend that the impact of institutions is mediated through complex interactions with cultural beliefs, historical trajectories, and distributional conflicts. This debate is crucial for disentangling the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic growth, thereby informing both historical analysis and policy interventions. The stakes are high, as understanding these mechanisms can help identify the most effective strategies for fostering sustainable economic development. Institutions are not mere backdrop; they are active agents that can either facilitate or hinder economic progress, and their influence is deeply intertwined with the broader socio-political landscape. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England highlights the critical role of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the success of England's economic development was underpinned by political factors that ensured the regime's commitment to enforceable rules. This commitment was crucial for the development of capital markets and the overall economic performance of the country. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reflect the increased security of property rights, which is a critical dimension of economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that they are crucial for economic growth. They emphasize the significance of institutions, focusing on population and savings (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in 17th-century England is an example of institutions facilitating economic growth. These institutions provided increased security of property rights, which was critical for the development of a long-term capital market (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This security enabled the emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, leading to a decline in transaction costs and facilitating market exchanges (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, underscoring the need for nuanced analysis.

North and Weingast's (1989, p.29) (Austin 2008, p.2) analysis of seventeenth-century England reveals that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth highlights how the institutional framework can significantly influence economic outcomes. This institutional evolution was marked by a shift towards more secure property rights and a commitment to enforceable rules, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that this institutional change was about economic rules and the political mechanisms that enforced and adapted these rules. Austin (2008, p.15) builds on this by illustrating how colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation underscores the dynamic nature of institutional change and its responsiveness to local conditions. However, Austin (2008, p.15) notes that the effectiveness of these institutional changes varied, with some colonial governments opting for higher production equilibria while others did not. This variation suggests that the impact of institutional changes on economic growth is not uniform and depends on the specific context and the nature of the institutional framework implemented. The nuanced interplay between political factors and institutional frameworks thus emerges as a critical determinant of long-run economic growth.

This divergence was not solely due to institutional differences but also reflected the varying impacts of global economic forces on different regions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that the early modern period saw significant economic growth in north-western Europe, driven by high productivity in the traded goods sector and the crucial role of the service sector. This growth was facilitated by merchant distribution and finance, which enhanced the productivity of the traded goods sector. This economic development did not uniformly translate into improved standards of living for all segments of the population. This pattern of uneven economic growth and its impact on living standards is further supported by van Zanden's (1999, p.9) analysis, which shows that economic development in Europe led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, making essential goods more expensive. However, van Zanden (1999, p.1) also highlights that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living, complicating the notion of a straightforward link between economic growth and improvements in living standards. This suggests that while economic development can drive productivity and growth, the benefits are not always evenly distributed, and the relationship between economic growth and living standards is complex and multifaceted.

These factors have created a complex web of economic challenges that continue to influence contemporary development trajectories. Simson (2019, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that the impact of colonial legacies on African economic development is more complex than a simple narrative of unproductive budget policies. Simson shows that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed, challenging the notion that these policies were solely driven by rent-seeking and bloated payrolls. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.2) finding that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating a more nuanced economic landscape than previously thought. These findings suggest that the economic policies implemented post-independence were influenced by a variety of factors, including regional instability and the need to address domestic dissent. The role of trust and social cohesion in economic development cannot be overlooked. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27; 2011, p.4) highlight that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and affects economic outcomes. This mistrust is not solely an internal norm but is also influenced by external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This implies that while economic policies play a crucial role, the social and institutional fabric of a society is equally important in shaping long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that British rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This institutional legacy was shaped by the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development. The development of formal education systems under British rule highlights that colonial powers played a role in shaping the institutional frameworks that influenced Africa's development trajectories. Despite this, the broader impact of colonial rule on African development is nuanced. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This indicates that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The historical factors and their long-term effects on African economic development suggest that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, challenging those who attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, significantly outweighs the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years from 1885 to 1960. This extended period of exploitation disrupted African societies, depleting human capital and undermining institutional development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates hinders a comprehensive understanding of Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates that the economic and social institutions of local coastal societies were too undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade, which was only sustained when the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom. The Kongo kingdom's centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks enabled it to support trade with the Europeans. This historical context underscores the importance of institutional development in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trades continues to influence contemporary economic conditions.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts and inequality are critical factors in shaping the core economic institutions of pre-industrial societies (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They propose that these conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, are central to understanding economic development. The mechanism by which distributional conflicts influence institutions is through the political power of elites, who can manipulate institutions to maintain their advantage. The most unequal societies in the New World, for example, developed institutions that restricted competition and entrenched inequality, ensuring that elites retained their political and economic dominance (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where institutions like serfdom and guilds were maintained not for efficiency, but because they served the interests of powerful groups. The role of these conflicts is not always straightforward. This suggests that the impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is complex and multifaceted, with both positive and negative effects on economic development.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are central drivers of long-run economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets both assert (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. This mechanism is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where technological breakthroughs have been pivotal. For instance, Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns, necessitating institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This implies that technological progress alone is insufficient; it must be complemented by institutional changes that facilitate its integration into society. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that support technological progress, is essential for sustaining economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets points out that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs is crucial for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This qualification underscores the importance of institutional stability and the peaceful resolution of conflicts, which are necessary for the sustained application of technological advancements. Institutions play a critical role in managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress, ensuring that economic growth is not hindered by internal strife.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by determining the distribution of resources and opportunities. They influence economic outcomes by setting the rules for economic interactions and providing incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that institutions established by European colonizers in their colonies significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin explains that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, for example, led to persistent economic underdevelopment. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which institutions evolve.

The role of institutions in managing technological progress and economic growth is crucial. Institutions provide the framework for the peaceful resolution of conflicts and the stable application of technological advancements. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, which reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress. Ogilvie also shows that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth lies in the comparative analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term economic impacts. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction shows that institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists led to persistent economic underdevelopment in many former colonies. Austin shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which institutions evolve. The comparative analysis of colonial institutions and their long-term economic impacts provides a clear understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
