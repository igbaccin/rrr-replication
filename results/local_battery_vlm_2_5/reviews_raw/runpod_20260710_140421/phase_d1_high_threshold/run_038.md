The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the potential pathways for future growth. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic outcomes and those who contend that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural attributes, play a more decisive role. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world consequences for policy-making and economic development strategies. For instance, if institutions are indeed the primary drivers of economic growth, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could be crucial for fostering development in underdeveloped regions. If geographic or cultural factors exert more influence, institutional change efforts may yield less effective results. The debate centres on whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they are shaped by historical contingencies or by more fundamental economic forces. This literature review will explore the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and the historical contexts in which these mechanisms operate. The debate between institutionalist and non-institutionalist views is particularly pronounced in the context of colonial legacies and their enduring effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly based on mortality rates and settler motivations. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans often established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-run economic growth. In colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions fostered economic development and have had lasting positive effects on income per capita. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to these institutional differences, not geographic factors. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, has had a profound impact on the long-run development trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. observe that the design of these institutions often aimed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional framework established during colonial times has had enduring effects on economic performance, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions for centuries.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) share the finding that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, which reflects increased security of property rights over time. The fiscal crises during the Hundred Years' War prompted the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which initiated a process of institutional change that facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. This process is evident in the emergence of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement, leading to a decline in transaction costs per exchange. As the economy grew, the transactions sector became a larger proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that the success of these institutions depended on the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights, which underpinned economic growth. The institutional changes in England were not merely about the rules of economic exchange but also about the political factors that ensured these rules were enforced and could be changed when necessary. This commitment to rules was a critical factor in the long-run economic growth of England, highlighting the importance of credible commitment in institutional development. The political factors underpinning economic growth in seventeenth-century England illustrate how the evolution of institutions can foster secure property rights and drive economic growth over the long run.

Similarly, the impact of colonial rule on African economic development highlights the complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives in shaping institutional change, as shown by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. This is because the scope of development policy widened to incorporate governance and policy recommendations allowed institutions and organizations to come to the fore. The prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. 'Good' institutions, as the literature calls them, matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic development varies across Africa. The demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level and African families on the micro level. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and challenges presented by colonial rule. The colonial experience varied significantly across different regions and countries, leading to diverse outcomes in terms of institutional development and economic growth. The presence of good institutions, which are crucial to providing incentives for investment, is essential for development. In the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This is despite the central importance of national accounting in the modern economic history of most regions. This has had the unfortunate consequence of limiting the extent to which Africa appears in comparative historical work on economic growth and development. Recent work in African economic history has demonstrated the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. This suggests that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development is a complex and multifaceted issue, with both positive and negative effects depending on the specific context and local initiatives.

In the New World, Sokoloff and Engerman's work (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2) argues that the institutions in societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. They attribute this to the specific focus on how the extremely different environments in which the Europeans established their colonies may have led to societies with very different degrees of inequality. This inequality persisted over time and affected the course of development through its impact on the institutions that evolved. The institutions in these societies were shaped in a way that advantaged elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This is further substantiated by Nunn's analysis of how the slave trades in Africa shaped subsequent economic development. Nunn notes that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This underscores the importance of institutional structures in facilitating economic growth, as the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets enabled the Kongo kingdom to support trade with the Europeans. This institutional advantage did not result in sustained economic development for Africa as a whole. The slave trades, which lasted for nearly 500 years, had a profound and adverse effect on Africa's economic development, highlighting the lasting impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth. The persistence of inequality in the New World societies and the adverse effects of the slave trades in Africa both illustrate the deep and enduring influence of institutions on economic development.

Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon, together with Austin, show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting social and economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade involved more than just the extraction of labor; it fostered an environment of deep mistrust among African communities. This mistrust developed as a result of the violent and exploitative nature of the slave trade, which disrupted social cohesion and eroded trust between different ethnic groups. The legacy of this mistrust continues to affect contemporary African societies, influencing economic development by undermining cooperative behavior and social capital. The slave trade's impact on trust is a persistent factor that shapes current social and economic interactions. The slave trade's effect on trust is multifaceted, working through both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust. This dual impact underscores the complexity of the slave trade's legacy, highlighting how historical institutions can have enduring effects on societal attitudes and behaviors. Austin further elaborates that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49), (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets jointly underscore that technological progress is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Mokyr elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge is essential for sustained economic development. Mokyr's emphasis on the role of knowledge aligns with Kuznets' assertion that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets notes that technological progress has historically necessitated significant institutional changes to resolve conflicts and ensure the sustainability of economic growth. Kuznets argues that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. The sustainability of economic growth depends on institutional progress complementing technological advancements. Mokyr's work suggests that without institutional progress to support technological progress, economic improvements may be limited and ultimately eroded by rent-seeking behavior. This suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, it must be supported by institutional frameworks that facilitate its efficient use and sustainability.

Institutional frameworks are crucial for sustaining the long-term benefits of technological advancements. Institutions facilitate the efficient use of technology by providing the necessary legal and regulatory environments. For example, the Bank of England's establishment and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Credible commitment underpins growth by ensuring that the regime or sovereign is bound by rules, thereby fostering a stable environment for economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This interaction shows that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This interaction shows that institutional progress is essential for supporting technological progress and ensuring that economic improvements are not limited or eroded by rent-seeking behavior.
