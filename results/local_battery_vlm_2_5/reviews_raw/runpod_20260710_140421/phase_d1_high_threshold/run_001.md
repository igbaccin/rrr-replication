The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship, with scholars disagreeing on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. The debate centers on whether institutions directly drive economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, play a more significant role. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in fostering economic development, arguing that the establishment of secure property rights and effective governance structures are crucial for sustainable growth. This view is supported by evidence showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups can hinder long-term economic development. The opposing position contends that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic growth, with geographic factors and cultural traits also playing significant roles. This perspective is bolstered by studies highlighting the complexities of institutional change and the multifaceted nature of economic development. The live disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography and culture, are equally or more important. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at promoting economic development. This institutional framework, which included the establishment of the Bank of England, was instrumental in fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, as highlighted by North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29). This institutional development was a product of fiscal crises and the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which highlights the political underpinnings of economic growth (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, and that a critical political factor was the degree to which the regime was committed to enforcing these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The evolution of financial institutions in Europe, therefore, underscores the importance of secure property rights and reduced transaction costs in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) maintain that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. The settler mortality rate drove the type of institutions Europeans established in their colonies. When mortality rates were low, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which fostered long-run economic growth. When mortality rates were high, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, which hindered economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) bolster this finding by showing that different measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, all support the same conclusion. These institutional differences persisted, with the former extractive colonies experiencing lower economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this finding by noting that the persistence of extractive institutions involves more than just economic development. Rather, it is about the political power of groups who benefit from these institutions and are reluctant to change them. This suggests that the long-run economic outcomes of former colonies are a function of both the initial institutions established and the enduring political dynamics that maintain them.

Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who highlights that technological progress and the spread of innovations are pivotal for sustained economic growth. Kuznets' analysis underscores that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, which he terms "epochal innovations, " have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Kuznets also notes that these technological changes necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr elaborates on this point, recognizing the possibility of coordination failures and suggesting policies to rectify them. He emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that although technological advancements are essential, they need to be aligned with institutional frameworks to avoid the erosion of economic gains due to rent-seeking behaviors. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is therefore critical for long-term economic growth.

The historical impacts of the slave trade on Africa's economic development highlight that the adverse effects of slavery are a significant rival explanation to institutional factors in understanding long-run economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) document how the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is not merely an external environmental factor; it is deeply internalized, affecting individuals' norms and behaviors. Nunn (2008, p.2) emphasizes that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, far outlasts the period of colonial rule, indicating that the economic underdevelopment in Africa may be more deeply rooted in the legacy of slavery than in more recent historical events. This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who highlight the broader implications of trust for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices, suggesting that the erosion of trust due to the slave trade has had far-reaching consequences. Nunn (2008, p.19) further nuances the complexity of Africa's economic development by pointing out that the lack of centralized governance and well-developed trading networks in certain African regions impeded their ability to engage in sustained trade with Europeans, even before the onset of colonialism. This indicates that while the slave trade had a profound impact, the absence of robust institutional frameworks also played a critical role in Africa's economic trajectory.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. This shift in perception was driven by cost-benefit calculations that took into account the changing institutional landscape. The development of formal education, in turn, had significant implications for economic growth, as it enhanced the skills and productivity of the African workforce. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of economic growth varied significantly between and within countries, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic outcomes, suggesting that while British rule may have been more conducive to education development, its impact on economic growth was nuanced and context-dependent. The complexity of these interactions highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Austin's reevaluation of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenges simplistic interpretations of colonial rule's impact on African economic development, arguing that the thesis' compression of different historical periods and paths obscures the nuanced interactions between colonial institutions and local economic trajectories. Austin's critique highlights the importance of considering the diverse institutional legacies left by colonial rule, which shaped African economies in complex ways. For instance, where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning with local economic interests (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic; they adapted in response to indigenous economic and political actions. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies further illustrates this adaptability, showing that economic development varied significantly across regions and periods (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This variability underscores the need for a more granular understanding of colonial institutions' impact on African economies.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that influence economic actors. North and Weingast's work on seventeenth-century England highlights how credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This is because secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are institutional features, reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly influenced their economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is because the extractive institutions that Europeans established in many colonies were designed to maximize rents for colonists rather than to promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic development.

The institutional legacy of colonialism is evident in the varied economic outcomes across different regions. Nunn and Wantchekon's research shows that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust in Africa, which continues to affect economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact is complemented by the institutional frameworks that were established during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate this point by demonstrating that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes, which included the establishment of extractive institutions, have had profound and enduring effects on economic growth. Austin underscores the complexity of these institutional impacts by noting that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability shows that while colonial institutions were often extractive, they were not monolithic and could vary significantly across regions and periods.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between political and economic factors. North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The political factors underpinning economic growth include the rules governing economic exchange as well as the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed. Similarly, North argues that institutions matter in economic growth because they determine the security of property rights and the efficiency of market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This is because the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between the importance of institutions and the role of other factors is the variation in economic outcomes across regions with different institutional frameworks. This variation is evident in the adaptability of colonial institutions and the enduring effects of extractive institutions on economic development.
