The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the contemporary challenges of economic policy. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, influence economic outcomes by shaping incentives, enforcing property rights, and facilitating market exchanges. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic development, while others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that factors like population density, prosperity, and colonial policies are more influential. The debate is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts and the persistence of extractive institutions in shaping economic outcomes. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors play a more significant role is central to understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the contemporary challenges of economic policy. The debate extends to the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, including the enforcement of property rights and the facilitation of market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast argue that specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, are key examples of how institutions can enhance economic growth. The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is crucial for economic development. North and Weingast highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, demonstrating that the rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement are critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of institutions on economic growth varies across different contexts. Thus, while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic growth, their effectiveness depends on the specific historical and political context in which they operate.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both emphasize that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights the importance of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, suggesting that the development of scholarly societies and other formal institutions facilitated the dissemination of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.49). This institutional support for knowledge dissemination is crucial because technological progress alone is not sufficient for economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates on this point, noting that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from technological changes and to maintain the political fabric of society. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic and irreversible as technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This qualification suggests that while institutions play a vital role in supporting technological advancements, their effectiveness can be influenced by various factors, including the political and social context. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not straightforward and depends on the specific historical and political context in which they operate.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) both (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15) find that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa had a more positive impact on educational development and economic performance than other colonial powers. This is evident in the development of formal education systems and the growth of export production, which were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational development was closely tied to the growth of foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, while these institutions supported economic growth, their effectiveness was contingent on the specific historical and political context in which they operated. This suggests that the impact of colonial rule on long-run economic growth was not uniform and depended on the interplay between institutional changes and local conditions.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers implemented vastly different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These divergent institutional frameworks had profound long-term economic implications. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered economic development, while the more inclusive institutions in low-mortality colonies fostered growth. Acemoglu et al. further argue that the persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, has led to a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence current economic outcomes, with former colonies that had extractive institutions lagging behind those with more inclusive frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The long-term economic impact of colonial institutions underscores the enduring significance of historical institutional frameworks in shaping contemporary economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge the oversimplification of African economic development paths, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses diverse historical processes into a single causal narrative. This cluster highlights that colonial institutions did not uniformly impede African economic growth. Instead, the interaction between colonial administrations and indigenous economic activities shaped institutional outcomes, leading to varied development paths. In cases where European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, aligning their interests with those of their subjects. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's policy, which opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations. Such adaptive institutional responses indicate that colonial administrations were not monolithic in their approach, and their policies could be influenced by local economic and political actions. The absence of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the long-term effects of these institutional adaptations. The complexity of these historical processes underscores the need for a deeper analysis of the determinants of real wage divergence across colonial Africa.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were influenced by the power dynamics and interests of elites. Ogilvie notes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. In New World societies, Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This pattern suggests that the institutional frameworks in these societies were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the ruling classes. These institutions had downstream implications for economic growth. Ogilvie argues that the efficiency of these institutions was often compromised by the need to maintain social order and protect elite interests. This tension between efficiency and distributional conflicts highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The persistence of inequality in these societies underscores the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was crucial for fostering economic growth by providing a stable environment for long-term capital markets. Similarly, North and Weingast highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for economic development. Their success is evident in the capital markets, where the commitment to secure property rights and enforceable rules underpinned economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These findings illustrate that institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights are essential for economic development, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage investment.

Ogilvie provides a complementary perspective by arguing that distributional conflicts often shape core economic institutions. She contends that institutions such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe were designed to maintain social order and protect elite interests (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional design, where the need to maintain social order and protect elite interests can compromise economic efficiency. Ogilvie's analysis aligns with North's view that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize rent extraction over long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often hindered long-run growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding (North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) highlights the tension between institutional design aimed at maintaining social order and those that promote economic growth. The persistence of inequality in societies with extractive institutions underscores the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He contends that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's analysis suggests that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, needs to be refined to account for the complexity of institutional evolution and the diverse paths of economic development. Austin's critique aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, which often involved the establishment of extractive institutions, had lasting effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin's analysis of colonial administrations that encouraged indigenous economic and political action further illustrates how institutions can evolve in response to local incentives and actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) highlights the importance of understanding the diverse paths of institutional evolution and their impact on economic growth.
