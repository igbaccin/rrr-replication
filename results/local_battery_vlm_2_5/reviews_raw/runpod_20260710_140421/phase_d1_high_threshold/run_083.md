The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations. Institutions are understood as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, self-imposed codes of conduct). The central contention is whether institutions, by shaping incentives and constraints, are the key drivers of economic growth, or whether other factors, such as geographic endowments, cultural traits, or technological advancements, play a more decisive role. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it informs whether institutional reforms can catalyze economic development. The disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary engine of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors that influence economic outcomes. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with some scholars arguing that institutions create the necessary conditions for sustained economic development, while others propose that institutions are themselves shaped by underlying economic and social factors. This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to drive long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) both argue that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. This institutional development was a response to fiscal crises, reflecting a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. They argue that the development of markets and economic growth were due to the rules of economic exchange and the institutions that enforced and adapted these rules (Author, Year, p.N). North and Weingast provide evidence from capital markets, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North elaborates that as political institutions evolved to specify and enforce efficient property rights, the costs per transaction decreased, but the aggregate volume of exchanges increased, reflecting a growing proportion of the economy dedicated to market transactions. This dynamic highlights the interdependence of political and economic institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. However, North and Weingast caution that the impact of these institutions is not uniform across different countries; they note that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost on par with England's, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as often portrayed. This indicates that while financial institutions played a significant role in reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth, the effectiveness of these institutions varied across different political and economic contexts.

Economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly assert (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective underscores that the proliferation of technological innovations and the dissemination of knowledge are pivotal in fostering economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) points out that these technological advancements necessitate complementary institutional adjustments to ensure their efficient use and to sustain economic improvements over the long run. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the integration of modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptations is crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from economic growth, such as those between traditional and modern modes of life. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic growth does not weaken the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.49) cautions that while technological progress is often more monotonic and irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is less so (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that institutional progress, though essential, may not always keep pace with technological advancements, potentially limiting the sustainability of economic growth. The institutional environment must adapt to support the integration of new technologies and knowledge, thereby facilitating long-term economic growth.

The literature on institutional changes due to colonialism explores how different colonization strategies led to varying economic outcomes in former European colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) document. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, while in other colonies, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment. For example, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where Europeans could settle more safely, they created institutions that fostered long-term economic growth. This institutional divergence had profound implications for the economic trajectories of these colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and law-enforcing institutions facilitating it. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who designed them to maximize their own rents, and to promote long-term growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional frameworks on economic development. Acemoglu et al. also document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that it was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that British rule fostered formal education more effectively in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, challenging the notion that colonial impacts were uniformly negative. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule created incentives for Africans to pursue formal education, leading to a more educated population. This educational investment had significant downstream effects on economic development. For example, the growth in formal education contributed to the development of human capital, which in turn supported economic growth by enhancing productivity and fostering innovation. This pattern of educational investment and economic growth was particularly evident in regions where colonial policies were more conducive to formal education, highlighting the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The relationship between education and economic growth was not uniform across all regions, as evidenced by the varying levels of per capita income in different African economies by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2).

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) document. The internal norms of mistrust, which developed as a result of the slave trade, have had a lasting impact on African societies. This mistrust has eroded social capital, making it difficult for communities to cooperate and engage in collective action, which are essential for economic development. The slave trade also disrupted traditional economic and social institutions, leading to a lack of centralized government and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional damage has had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development, as it has made it difficult for African countries to engage in international trade and attract foreign investment.

The new economic history of Africa and the 'reversal of fortune' thesis both highlight the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, yet they diverge in their causal explanations, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight. One specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic growth is their role in providing incentives for investment. This is evident in the colonial context, where colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic underscores how institutional adjustments can foster economic growth by aligning incentives with local conditions, but the efficacy of such adjustments is contingent on the presence of secure private property rights and the rule of law. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that while 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, the presence of poor institutions is not necessarily the sole determinant of Africa's economic challenges (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods provided by African governments is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including ethnic fragmentation and the capacity to uphold the rule of law. Therefore, while institutional reforms are essential for economic growth, they must be part of a broader strategy that addresses the multifaceted challenges facing African economies.

The critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the work of multiple scholars. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that institutions which enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are essential for economic development. Their findings show that European settlers in colonies like the United States established such institutions, fostering growth, while extractive institutions in other colonies hindered development. This aligns with North's argument that secure property rights and low transaction costs, facilitated by effective institutions, are fundamental for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further illustrate this point by examining the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England, where secure property rights and credible commitment underpinned economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) builds on this by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that initial conditions determine long-term economic outcomes, overlooks the dynamic nature of institutional changes. Austin argues that the development of institutions is influenced by various factors, including colonial policies and local economic conditions. This perspective is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth in different historical contexts. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is further emphasized by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who argues that 'good' institutions are essential for development. Hopkins also acknowledges that the quality of public goods and the capacity to uphold the rule of law are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including ethnic fragmentation. This highlights the multifaceted challenges facing African economies, where institutional reforms must be part of a broader strategy that addresses these issues.

The precise role of institutions in economic growth is settled by the historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term effects. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by providing secure property rights and low transaction costs. The historical evidence of colonial policies and their long-term effects, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), settles the disagreement by showing that institutional changes introduced by colonialism were crucial for the long-term development of the former colonies. This historical evidence provides a clear measurement of the impact of institutions on economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional reforms in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
