The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or initial conditions, play a more significant role. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions shape economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, emphasizing their role in providing secure property rights and fostering investment. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing economic development, with geography and culture playing equally significant roles. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at promoting economic growth in developing countries. Understanding the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth can help policymakers design effective interventions. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth remains unresolved, with scholars continuing to debate the relative importance of institutions versus other factors. This debate is particularly relevant in the context of colonial legacies and their enduring impact on economic development. European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Frankema 2012, p.15).

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that fostered investment and the rule of law, leading to sustained economic development. In contrast, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to quickly transfer resources back to the European metropole, hindering long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely linked to the industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and economic development during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast argue that the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). This institutional change was driven by the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. North and Weingast further highlight that the success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their effectiveness (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also ensured that the regime was committed to these rules, thereby fostering economic growth. The extent to which these institutions were revolutionary is debated. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, indicating that while secure property rights are crucial, the revolutionary nature of English institutions can be nuanced by comparative economic performance.

Frankema argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was more conducive to the development of formal education than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities and costs that emerged from colonial policies. This perspective is supported by the work of Sokoloff and Engerman, who highlight how differentials in development paths are often attributed to institutional variations, such as the security of property rights and the prevalence of corruption, which in turn influence growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for broader economic development by enhancing human capital. The extent to which these institutions were revolutionary is debated. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, indicating that while secure property rights are crucial, the revolutionary nature of English institutions can be nuanced by comparative economic performance.

The slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting economic development by reducing trust within societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This internal norm of mistrust, shaped by the historical trauma of the slave trade, has profound implications for contemporary economic interactions. The internal channel of mistrust, which operates through individuals' internal norms, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which operates through the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This finding underscores the deep-seated nature of the mistrust, which is not easily mitigated by external factors alone. The slave trade's adverse effect on economic development is not merely a historical artifact but a persistent force that continues to shape Africa's economic landscape. The long-term effects of the slave trade on trust and economic development highlight the need for policies that address both the internal norms of mistrust and the external environment to foster economic growth.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight a lack of a clear relationship between economic development and changes in living standards. This is evident in the complex interplay between economic development and living standards in Europe between 1500 and 1800, as well as in the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner note that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, contrary to previous characterizations. This pattern is tied to factors such as foreign trade and export markets, which influenced the timing and scale of growth in export production. The economic development in Europe during the seventeenth century shifted to countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift confirms that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. However, this relationship is not straightforward, as differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760. The abundant supply of Baltic grain and generous British export subsidies also played a significant role in maintaining lower wheat prices in Amsterdam compared to England and France. The economic development in Anglophone African economies and in early modern Europe demonstrates that the relationship between economic growth and living standards is multifaceted and influenced by various factors, including political institutions, local endowments, and transport costs. The complex nature of this relationship underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic performance and living standards over the long run.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlight that the accumulation of technological knowledge spurs economic growth by enabling innovation and enhancing productivity. This process requires not just technical advancements but also the dissemination and application of knowledge across society. Mokyr notes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, emphasizing that technological progress alone is not sufficient for sustained economic improvement. This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. The interplay between technological changes and institutional frameworks is crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from economic growth. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. However, Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, implying that institutional progress may face more significant challenges and setbacks. This underscores the importance of not just technological advancements but also the consistent and effective implementation of institutional frameworks that support economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the diverse ways in which they facilitate economic transactions and secure property rights. Institutions reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. North explains that the emergence of institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing enforcement mechanisms leads to falling costs per transaction, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by North and Weingast, who argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the political factors that foster secure property rights and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, illustrates how institutional changes can dramatically reduce transaction costs and support economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the divergent paths taken by European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the long-run economic outcomes of their colonies. Colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, experienced poorer economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is complemented by Acemoglu et al. 2002, who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the enduring effects of institutional frameworks on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutional effects is a testament to the power of institutions in shaping economic trajectories over the long term.

The historical evidence from colonial rule in Africa further underscores the importance of institutions. Frankema shows that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, illustrating how different institutional frameworks can lead to varying educational outcomes (Frankema 2012, p.15). This is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for economic growth and development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific historical evidence of colonial rule in Africa and the enduring effects of mistrust highlight the profound and lasting impact of institutions on economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shows that the type of institutions established by colonizers had long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. The remaining disagreement centers on the reversibility of institutional progress. Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, implying that institutional progress may face more significant challenges and setbacks (North 1989, p.9). The specific historical evidence of the English financial revolution and the development of new financial instruments illustrates how institutional changes can dramatically reduce transaction costs and support economic growth (North 1989, p.9).
