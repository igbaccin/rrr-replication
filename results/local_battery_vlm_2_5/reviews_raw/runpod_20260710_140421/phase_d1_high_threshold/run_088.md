The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some societies prosper while others stagnate, and whether these trajectories can be influenced by institutional design and reform. The live disagreement centers around how institutions matter, whether they are the primary drivers of growth, and if so, how they interact with other factors. One camp argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they shape incentives, enforce property rights, and facilitate market exchanges. Another camp emphasizes that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and social factors, and that their impact on growth is mediated through these factors. A third camp proposes that the role of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological progress, are more important. The question of whether institutions drive economic growth, or whether they are epiphenomenal, is central to debates about policy and development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore a practical question with significant implications for policy and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-promoting, had significant impacts on current income per capita. This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.9) (North 1989, p.6) view that institutions are critical for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and specify property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further emphasize that credible commitment underpins growth, highlighting the importance of institutions that enforce rules and ensure property rights. The shared finding is that institutions matter because they shape incentives, enforce property rights, and facilitate market exchanges.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended has had lasting impacts on economic outcomes. The long-term implications of these institutions are evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, which Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) attribute to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these regions, illustrating how colonial institutions continue to influence economic growth today.

This historical context is further enriched by North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting the increased security of property rights. This financial revolution was a direct result of the political and fiscal crises of the time, which necessitated the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The resulting institutions facilitated market exchanges and economic growth by lowering costs per transaction and increasing the volume of exchanges. This dynamic underscores the critical role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes, as they determine how rules are enforced and changed. The development of these institutions was about the broader political commitment to these rules, which was essential for sustained economic growth. These institutions, in turn, would reduce transaction costs and increase the aggregate volume of exchanges, further fueling economic growth. They note that while England's institutional changes were pivotal, France's economic performance before the French Revolution was similarly robust, suggesting that institutional changes alone do not account for all economic outcomes. This qualification highlights the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, where the interplay of political and economic factors must be considered.

In this vein, institutional reforms are a crucial mechanism for addressing poverty in Africa, as they can foster economic growth by enhancing governance and policy frameworks. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the view that institutional reforms can eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This institutional framework can provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa highlights the role of institutions in providing these incentives (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The effectiveness of these reforms depends on the historical and geographical conditions that may have cast a structural effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) emphasize that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is crucial for assessing future growth possibilities. This understanding can help in designing institutional reforms that are tailored to the specific historical and geographical conditions of African countries, ensuring that these reforms are effective in addressing poverty.

The historical context of Europe reveals that distributional conflicts shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlights that these institutions were designed to maintain the status quo and redistribute resources in a way that favored the ruling classes. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to the development of institutions that perpetuated these inequalities. This pattern of institutional design, driven by distributional conflicts, had a profound impact on long-term economic growth. It is important to consider that the persistence of these institutions, and the distributional conflicts that shaped them, also influenced the path of development in these societies.

This historical perspective is complemented by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) (Pamuk 2007, p.23) who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. This narrative challenges the conventional emphasis on institutions as the primary driver of long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This productivity was further reinforced by the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in a warm climate. Pamuk (2007, p.16) supports this perspective by noting that structural change, including the shift in urbanisation rates, was a key characteristic of economic growth in the early modern era. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) qualifies the impact of these structural changes by arguing that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. This suggests that while structural changes were significant, their effects on living standards were not straightforward. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between structural changes and economic growth, indicating that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth may be more nuanced than previously thought.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets elaborates on this by highlighting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This implies a dynamic interplay between technological advancements and the institutional environment. Mokyr further supports this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Kuznets qualifies this by emphasizing the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that while technological progress is crucial, institutional factors play a significant role in managing the social and political dimensions of economic growth. This underscores that although technological progress is a primary driver, institutional frameworks are essential for harnessing its benefits effectively.

North, on the other hand, highlights how the development of financial institutions and the security of property rights in England facilitated economic growth, showing that transaction costs decreased as property rights became more secure (North 1989, p.9). Both perspectives underscore the importance of institutions in economic development, though they focus on different historical contexts and institutional mechanisms. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.9) shows that effective institutions are indispensable for sustaining economic growth over the long term.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illuminate this point by examining the political factors that underpin economic growth. North and Weingast argue that the commitment of the regime to secure property rights was crucial for economic development in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this idea by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Both studies highlight the importance of institutional frameworks in economic growth, particularly in how they shape property rights and economic incentives. This shared finding (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) demonstrates that the nature of institutions, whether they are extractive or inclusive, fundamentally determines economic outcomes.
