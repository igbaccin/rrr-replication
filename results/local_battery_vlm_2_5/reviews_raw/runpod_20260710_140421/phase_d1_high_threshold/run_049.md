The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic progress over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, directly influences economic outcomes. Others contend that the efficiency of institutions is less critical than the underlying cultural and social norms that shape economic behavior. Additionally, there are debates about the extent to which colonial legacies and specific historical events have molded the institutional landscapes of different regions, thereby influencing their economic trajectories. The interplay between these factors highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics. This understanding is crucial for policymakers seeking to design effective strategies for sustainable growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting. This financial revolution reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) find that the institutions governing public choice in England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The English financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years- War, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. North (1989, p.6) further argues that the emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of this. The institutional changes in England were not so revolutionary after all, as the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism significantly shaped long-term economic trajectories by introducing institutional changes that either fostered or hindered growth. They argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates and the ease of settlement. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states focused on rapid resource extraction. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States and Australia, institutions were established that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences had profound and lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) provide empirical support for this argument by demonstrating that the quality of institutions, measured through various indices, is strongly correlated with current income per capita. They also highlight that these institutional legacies persist, influencing economic outcomes long after the colonial period has ended. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions may not always encourage economic development. They may be designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories, their impact was not uniform and depended on the specific institutional arrangements established. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that the quality of institutions, as measured by constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, are all strongly correlated with economic outcomes. This underscores the importance of institutional quality in driving long-term economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman share a view that distributional conflicts were pivotal in shaping economic institutions, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were designed to maintain existing power structures and redistribute resources in ways that favored the elite, which often led to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in the development of institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds in pre-industrial Europe. These institutions were deeply rooted in the power struggles and wealth distribution patterns of the time. The persistence of such institutions highlights how distributional conflicts can lock in economic structures that hinder long-term growth. For instance, the craft guilds, while ostensibly serving to regulate quality and standards, often acted as barriers to entry for new competitors, thereby maintaining the economic dominance of established elites. This institutional rigidity, driven by distributional conflicts, can stifle innovation and economic dynamism. The impact of distributional conflicts on economic institutions is not uniform. Sokoloff and Engerman note that the specific factor endowments of New World societies also played a role in shaping these institutions, suggesting that while distributional conflicts are crucial, they interact with other economic factors to determine institutional outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This interaction underscores the complexity of institutional development and the need for a multifaceted approach to understanding long-run economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight that the role of institutional reforms in Africa is crucial for poverty reduction, although these reforms do not directly address long-run economic growth. This shared perspective underscores the importance of institutional reforms in addressing immediate poverty issues, even if these reforms do not directly target long-run economic growth. One specific mechanism through which institutional reforms can impact poverty is by improving the provision of public goods. Hopkins notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily impede the quality of public goods provided by African governments, suggesting that effective institutions can overcome ethnic divisions to deliver essential services (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The long-term impact of these reforms on economic growth remains uncertain, as the historical data on African GDP per capita is limited. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, highlighting the need for more comprehensive historical data to fully understand the long-term effects of institutional reforms (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This data gap suggests that while institutional reforms are essential for poverty reduction, their role in shaping long-run economic growth is still not fully understood.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development. The slave trades created a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that these slave trades occurred over nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, significantly longer than the period of official colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This historical context underscores the profound and enduring impact of the slave trades on African economic development. The slave trades disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a legacy of mistrust that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn (2008, p.2; 2008, p.19) suggests that the slave trades also hindered the development of centralized governance and well-developed markets, which are crucial for supporting trade and economic growth. This institutional disruption is a key mechanism through which the slave trades adversely affected long-run economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further emphasize that trust is a critical factor for economic development, complementing recent studies that highlight its importance for various economic and political outcomes. The enduring legacy of mistrust caused by the slave trades poses a significant challenge to Africa's economic development.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments. These institutions play a critical role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological progress translates into sustainable economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is less so, suggesting that institutional progress is crucial for maintaining the benefits of technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex dynamics of long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework secured property rights and fostered a long-term capital market essential for sustained economic development. North and Weingast further illustrate this point by highlighting how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Their analysis shows that the success of these institutions was evident in the functioning of capital markets, underscoring the importance of credible commitments in economic development.

Institutions also play a critical role in resolving conflicts and ensuring that technological progress translates into sustainable economic growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, with some promoting investment and the rule of law while others created extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). These varying institutional setups significantly affected the long-run economic outcomes of these colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional design on economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Hopkins, who argues that institutional reforms in Africa can inform policy changes that help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). By providing incentives for investment, good institutions are crucial for development, as they ensure that economic growth is both sustainable and inclusive.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex dynamics of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that institutional progress is crucial for maintaining the benefits of technological advancements. Ogilvie supports this view by emphasizing the need to transform institutions from mere fashion accessories into indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes and the need for institutional reforms that promote sustainable growth.

The consensus is that institutions are pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, Acemoglu et al. point out that institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for powerful groups rather than promote growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from European colonization strategies and the long-run economic outcomes of former colonies settle this disagreement. The extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, illustrate the enduring impact of institutional design on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
