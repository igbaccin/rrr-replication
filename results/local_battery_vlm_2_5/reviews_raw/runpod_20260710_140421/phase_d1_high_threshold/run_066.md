The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental drivers behind sustained economic performance over extended periods. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are central to this debate because they influence the incentives and constraints that affect economic behavior. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are essential for economic growth, while others contend that institutions are shaped by underlying cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. Additionally, there is a debate on whether the impact of institutions on economic growth is direct or mediated through other factors. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable development. The literature on this topic is extensive and multifaceted, with various streams of scholarship offering different perspectives on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. The debate between Acemoglu et al. and Austin on the 'reversal of fortune' thesis is a key area of contention. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin, however, contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, as well as the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and contextual factors that shape this relationship.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies by introducing varied institutional frameworks that persisted over time (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In places with high European mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In regions with lower mortality, such as the United States and Australia, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development, as extractive institutions hindered long-run growth by maximizing rents for European colonists rather than fostering economic development. The impact of these institutions varied across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. also note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, highlighting the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic opportunities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The development of secure property rights in England during the seventeenth century illustrates how institutional changes can foster economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that institutions matter in economic growth, showing that the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional change facilitated economic growth by fostering a long-term capital market. North (1989, p.6) extends this argument by noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying and enforcing efficient property rights led to falling transaction costs, thereby increasing the volume of market exchanges and economic specialization. The institutional framework in England, which specified and enforced property rights, was pivotal in driving economic growth. This suggests that while institutions were critical, their impact was contingent on broader economic and political contexts.

Technological and intellectual advancements are the primary drivers of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This technological progress is not merely about innovation but also about the institutional frameworks that support and disseminate these innovations. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in the historical record, where technological breakthroughs have driven economic growth in tandem with institutional adaptations. The sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving these growth-induced conflicts. The downstream implication of this is that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by institutional progress to ensure that economic growth is sustainable.

The slave trades in Africa contributed to a culture of mistrust that persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. The slave trades disrupted African societies, leading to a loss of social cohesion and institutional capacity. This disruption had long-lasting effects on the economic trajectory of African societies. The slave trades also led to a loss of human capital, as many of the most able-bodied and skilled individuals were forcibly removed from their communities. The resulting labor shortages and skill deficits further impeded economic development. The slave trades' legacy of mistrust and institutional weakness continues to affect economic outcomes in Africa, as trust is a critical factor in economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The impact of the slave trades on economic development varied across Africa. The presence of centralized institutions, such as those found in the Kongo kingdom, facilitated trade and economic development, even in the face of the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). This highlights the importance of institutional factors in shaping economic outcomes, even in the context of severe external shocks such as the slave trades.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) find that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality shaped economic outcomes by advantaging elites through various government policies. In societies with high inequality, institutions were structured to maintain the political and economic power of the elite, often at the expense of broader economic development. This dynamic is evident in the way institutions in these societies restricted competition, thereby limiting economic opportunities for the majority of the population. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. In these societies, the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth, reinforcing the idea that institutional structures can significantly influence economic outcomes. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) highlight that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was not solely due to geographical factors but also resulted from high levels of investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This suggests that while institutional factors are crucial, they interact with other variables such as investment and geographical conditions to shape economic development. The interaction between institutional structures and other economic factors underscores the complexity of economic growth and the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions.

Frankema (2012, p.15); (Simson 2019, p.2) examine the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa under British rule as a distinct historical phenomenon. The implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new opportunities for political and economic advancement. Frankema (2012, p.15) notes that the demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, highlighting the role of local agency in shaping educational outcomes. This perspective underscores how colonial institutions interacted with local dynamics to influence educational development. Simson (2019, p.11) further elaborates on the post-independence budget policies in East Africa, showing that military spending grew significantly in response to regional instability and domestic dissent. This shift in expenditure patterns indicates how post-colonial institutions adapted to emerging challenges, even as they grappled with the legacies of colonial rule. The interplay between colonial institutions and local adaptations reveals a complex picture of educational and economic development in sub-Saharan Africa, where the impact of institutions is mediated by a range of factors, including political stability and economic priorities.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the notion that African economies were uniformly poor before the mid-twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding underscores the significance of historical national accounting in understanding Africa's economic development. Broadberry and Gardner note that recent work in African economic history has revealed large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, highlighting the role of export production in economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. These variations suggest that economic performance in Africa was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including political stability and economic priorities. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies over the period of colonial rule (ca. 1880–1965) supports this view, indicating that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This perspective aligns with the broader theme of institutional adaptation, where local factors mediate the impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the long-run economic growth in Africa, as they highlight the need for a nuanced approach that considers both historical data and the specific conditions of different regions.

Austin argues that institutional theories of long-run economic growth oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, a critique shared by Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This emphasis on the complexities of institutional change and the need for a more nuanced approach to understanding economic growth suggests that future research should focus on the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development, rather than relying on broad generalizations.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. Institutions matter in economic growth, as they shape the costs of transactions and the security of property rights (North 1989, p.6). The creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which reflects increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is crucial for long-term economic growth, as it facilitates market exchanges and reduces costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). The political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are essential for economic development (North 1989, p.6).

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provides a striking indication of their effectiveness (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional changes in England, including the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, were critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutions reduced transaction costs and increased the security of property rights, which were essential for long-term economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The political factors underpinning economic growth include the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

A shared finding is that institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape the costs of transactions and the security of property rights (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic development remain a point of disagreement. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and not necessarily encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
