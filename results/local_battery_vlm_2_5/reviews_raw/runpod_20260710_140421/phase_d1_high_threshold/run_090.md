The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the historical trajectories of nations and the effectiveness of contemporary policy interventions. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: some scholars assert that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, with other variables such as geography, culture, or technology playing equally, if not more, significant roles. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development, particularly in regions that have historically lagged behind. The question of whether institutions can be deliberately crafted to promote growth, or whether they are merely symptoms of deeper underlying forces, remains unresolved. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) assert that the creation of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The Bank of England's establishment in 1694, for example, facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This security was critical for the development of long-term capital markets and for sustained economic growth. The political model outlined by North highlights that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change underpins the secure property rights that are essential for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) build on this by emphasizing the role of institutions in enforcing rules and ensuring that the regime is committed to these rules. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provide a striking indication of their effectiveness. The commitment to these rules is a critical political factor that underpins economic growth and the development of markets. The extent of these institutional changes' revolutionary nature is debated. This nuance is further highlighted by the observation that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost at parity with England's, implying that institutions alone may not fully account for economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Their work demonstrates that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence, driven by colonial strategies, had lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further emphasize the robustness of this finding by using various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. Their results consistently show that the type of institutions established during colonial times significantly influenced current economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, even if they do not promote economic development. This is because these institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize their own rents over long-term growth. In the context of former colonies, this means that the institutions were likely designed to maximize the benefits for European colonists. This perspective is crucial for understanding the long-term economic impact of colonial institutions. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), is closely tied to the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial institutional legacies on economic growth.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) jointly assert. This knowledge-driven growth is facilitated by institutional and ideological adjustments that enable the efficient use of technological innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress requires societal adaptations, such as shifts in family patterns and ideological changes, to integrate new technologies into existing economic structures (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates on this point, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustained economic growth. The sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, plays a vital role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby ensuring that technological advancements contribute to long-run economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Institutional progress is therefore a necessary condition for the sustained impact of technological advancements on economic growth.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both assert that African economic development is heavily dependent on institutional reforms. They propose that governance structures can be targeted to produce significant economic improvements. This perspective highlights the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, with a particular focus on the incentives they provide for investment. This is demonstrated by the emphasis on 'good' institutions, which are crucial for fostering the investment essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates has left many questions unanswered, making it difficult to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Austin argues that the impact of colonialism on African economic development varied across different regions and periods (Austin 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon similarly show that the slave trade left a lasting cultural imprint on African societies, shaping contemporary levels of trust and cooperation (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The shared posture of these authors is that historical and cultural impacts have shaped African economic development in ways that defy simplistic explanations. A key mechanism through which these impacts have manifested is the enduring influence of colonial policies on local institutions. Austin highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which could lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that colonial institutions were responsive to local contexts, a point that complicates the view of colonial rule as uniformly extractive. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings on the slave trade's legacy of mistrust indicate that the cultural impacts of historical events can have profound and lasting effects on economic behavior, even after the formal end of colonial rule. Austin's emphasis on the varied responses of colonial administrations to local conditions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical and cultural factors have shaped African economic development.

This perspective challenges the notion that economic institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations, instead highlighting the role of power dynamics and distributional conflicts in their formation. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that the institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies were shaped by distributional conflicts over resources and power. These institutions include serfdom and community structures in Europe, while in the New World, they include policies that restricted competition and advantaged elites. The mechanism by which distributional conflicts shaped institutions is through the political and economic power of elites. These elites had the power to influence institutional design in their favor, leading to institutions that restricted competition and maintained their privileged status. This perspective has qualifications. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) notes that institutions are an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are important, they are not the only factor shaping institutions. Institutions also play a role in shaping economic outcomes, and understanding this role requires a nuanced approach that considers multiple factors.

Institutions are fundamental in shaping long-run economic growth, as they provide the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate this through their analysis of colonial institutions, showing that the extractive institutions established in high-mortality colonies significantly impacted current income per capita. This finding underscores the enduring influence of historical institutions on economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) supports this by highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic paths, emphasizing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions, particularly those that specify efficient property rights and provide enforcement, are crucial for economic growth. This is because they reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for a growing economy. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) adds that institutions are about distributional conflicts, which can shape core economic institutions. This perspective is crucial for understanding why certain institutions persist despite their apparent inefficiency.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) further illustrate this point by showing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups. This highlights the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional changes. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) reinforces this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty, emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth. Institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the framework within which economic activities occur (North 1989, p.6). There is a precise remaining disagreement over the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of colonial institutions, which shows that the extractive institutions established in high-mortality colonies significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence demonstrates that institutions shaped by distributional conflicts can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes.
