The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a contentious issue in economic scholarship, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are necessary for sustained economic development. Proponents of the institutionalist perspective argue that well-functioning institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they provide the necessary framework for investment and innovation. Skeptics argue that institutions are just one of many factors influencing growth, and that geographical endowments or cultural factors may be more determinative. Some scholars suggest that institutions are endogenous to economic development, shaped by underlying economic and social forces. The debate hinges on the causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, with implications for policy and development strategies. If institutions are indeed the primary drivers of growth, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could yield significant economic benefits. If institutions are merely a byproduct of deeper economic and social forces, then efforts to reform institutions in isolation may be misguided. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth remains unresolved, with scholars continuing to grapple with the complex interplay between institutions and other factors influencing economic development. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This security was critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The evolution of these institutions was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, which led to the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues. This institutional change facilitated market exchanges, as political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement were established. The result was a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which shows that the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules is a critical political factor. Recent research has significantly upgraded France's economic performance before the French Revolution, leading to an overhauling of traditional interpretations of British as well as French economic history. This implies that the institutional changes in England were indeed revolutionary and played a crucial role in fostering economic growth.

This influence of colonial institutions extends beyond just economic outcomes, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that they significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional structures established by European colonizers varied widely, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences were driven by European mortality rates and the resulting colonization strategies, which had enduring effects on economic development. For instance, colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were more likely to develop extractive institutions that hindered long-term economic growth. This pattern is evident in the persistent disparities in current income per capita among former colonies, highlighting the lasting impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions is further supported by the fact that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that institutions and gender dynamics significantly influenced economic development. The role of women in economic growth has been systematically overlooked in many historical contexts. In particular, women's contributions to economic development were marginalized by institutions that favored men. This marginalization was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to restrict women's access to land and other resources. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that colonial rule created a dual legal system, with European courts based on common and civil law and native courts that enforced customary law. This system further entrenched the subordinate status of women, as customary law often reflected patriarchal norms that limited women's economic opportunities. This institutional framework, which favored elite men, persisted over time and had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions highlights the importance of considering gender dynamics in understanding economic growth. The impact of these institutions varied across all societies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) show that in some cases, women were able to navigate these institutional constraints and make significant contributions to economic growth. Women's economic activities, such as food farming and migration in search of farmland, played a crucial role in the economic development of pre-colonial and colonial Africa. This highlights the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional contexts in which women's economic activities took place. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, for example, was driven in part by women's migration in search of farmland for cocoa production. This suggests that, despite institutional constraints, women were able to find ways to contribute to economic growth.

The negotiation and imposition of colonial rule in Africa, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18), shaped institutional change through both negotiation and imposition, with African initiatives playing a significant role alongside exogenous factors (Frankema 2012, p.15). Frankema (2012, p.15) adds that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This indicates that colonial rule, particularly British rule, could be more conducive to the development of formal education than previously thought. The role of African initiatives in shaping institutional change is further supported by the fact that African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations regarding formal education, highlighting the agency of African actors in the colonial context. The downstream implication of this agency is that African societies were not passive recipients of colonial rule but actively engaged in shaping their institutional landscape. The extent to which these institutions were progressive or dysfunctional varied, indicating that the impact of colonial rule on African economic development was complex and multifaceted. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, underscoring the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment and development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) propose a nuanced understanding of pre-industrial Europe's economic structures, asserting that distributional conflicts were the primary drivers of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. Austin (2008, p.2) supports this view, highlighting that colonial administrators often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that institutional outcomes were influenced by the bargaining power of local actors. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are the fundamental drivers of economic growth, suggesting instead that the distribution of power and resources within society plays a crucial role in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) notes that institutions are deeply embedded in the social and political dynamics of a society, which can lead to outcomes that are not always economically efficient. This insight underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions operate, as it can significantly influence their impact on economic development.

This profound impact of the slave trade, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2), had a profound impact on Africa's economic development by fostering a culture of mistrust that persists today. This cultural shift is a direct result of the social and psychological trauma inflicted on African societies. The slave trade's legacy is evident in the low levels of interpersonal trust, which hinder economic development by reducing cooperation and social capital. Nunn further highlights that the slave trades, which lasted nearly 500 years, had a more enduring impact on Africa's economic development than the relatively shorter period of colonial rule. The long-term effects of the slave trade include the erosion of social institutions and the degradation of human capital. The adverse effects of the slave trade are compounded by the fact that it targeted specific regions and ethnic groups, leading to persistent regional inequalities and ethnic tensions. This historical trauma has left a lasting imprint on African societies, shaping their economic and social institutions in ways that continue to impede development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological progress. This process involves the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for the efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic growth over long periods. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential, the sustainability of economic growth also depends on the effectiveness of institutions in mitigating rent-seeking and ensuring that technological progress translates into economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the adoption and effectiveness of economic policies. The establishment of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is evident in the historical context of European colonization, where different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. For instance, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law, which in turn encouraged investment and economic growth. Regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast developed extractive states that prioritized resource transfer to the metropole, hindering long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further highlighted by the development of financial institutions. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, enhancing the security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development reflects a broader trend where political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement lead to falling transaction costs and increased economic exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from Europe shows that the evolution of institutions is closely tied to economic growth, as seen in the development of financial markets and the reduction of transaction costs.

The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond financial developments. The design of institutions is often influenced by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize rent-seeking behaviors over long-term economic development. This is evident in the context of former European colonies, where institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes.

The specific historical evidence of the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments shows that institutions that reduce transaction costs and enhance the security of property rights are critical for economic growth. This is consistent with the broader finding that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants.
