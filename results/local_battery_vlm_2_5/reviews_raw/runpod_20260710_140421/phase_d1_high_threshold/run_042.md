The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy debates. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing social interactions, are widely acknowledged to influence economic outcomes. The precise mechanisms through which they operate and their relative importance compared to other factors are subjects of intense debate. One key disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions shape economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights, contract enforcement, and market exchanges. Others contend that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographical determinants. This debate highlights the need to understand the causal pathways through which institutions influence economic growth and the extent to which they can be manipulated to promote development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of financial institutions played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This financial revolution was instrumental in establishing a long-term capital market and driving economic growth. The Bank of England's establishment was a response to fiscal crises, such as those during the Hundred Years' War. The Bank's role in reducing transaction costs was part of a broader trend: as economies grow, the volume of exchanges increases, making the transactions sector a larger proportion of the overall economy (North 1989, p.6). This growth in the transactions sector is facilitated by political institutions that specify and enforce property rights, thereby lowering costs per transaction. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the success of these institutions depends on the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and the enforcement of these rules. This commitment was evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the institutional changes' impact on economic growth. The evidence from capital markets shows that the political factors underpinning economic growth are as critical as the rules governing economic exchange.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes in former colonies. European settlers in colonies with low mortality rates established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in high-mortality regions, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development. The persistence of these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, resulted in differences in economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established during colonial times continue to shape current economic conditions. The mechanisms of these institutions in shaping economic outcomes are also linked to the opportunity for industrialization. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on the economic trajectories of former colonies. The influence of these institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, underscoring the critical role of institutional frameworks in economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) both argue that distributional conflicts are the primary drivers of pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures such as serfdom and guilds. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. For instance, the decline of older industrial economies is a result of complex interactions between institutions and distributional conflicts. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) and Austin (2008, p.15) both find that the adoption of institutions that fostered economic growth was contingent on the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The distributional conflicts among social groups often led to the establishment of institutions that served to maintain the status quo. For example, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights the importance of understanding the broader social and political context in which institutions operate, as these contexts can significantly influence the adoption and effectiveness of institutions. The complexity of these interactions suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not straightforward and requires a nuanced understanding of the underlying distributional conflicts.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This effect is partly mediated by the erosion of trust within African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the legacy of the slave trade is a pervasive culture of mistrust, which continues to hinder economic development. This mistrust is deeply ingrained as an internal norm. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, dwarfing the period of colonial rule, and thus had a profound and lasting impact on African societies. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a persistent underdevelopment that cannot be solely attributed to colonialism or more recent events. The specific institutional mechanisms through which this mistrust manifests and its interaction with other historical factors are complex. Nunn (2008, p.2; 2008, p.19)  suggests that the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African regions further exacerbated the adverse effects of the slave trade. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how historical institutions shape long-run economic outcomes, particularly in the context of Africa's unique historical experiences.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Frankema, show that the role of women and education in African economic development was significant (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Women's contributions to economic growth were substantial during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and their access to land and resources was crucial for agricultural productivity. Formal education became a key avenue for social mobility, influencing the economic trajectory of African societies. The impact of education on long-run economic growth was not uniform, as the benefits were often constrained by the broader institutional framework and colonial policies that shaped access to resources and opportunities. The legacy of colonial education systems continues to influence contemporary African economies, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic development.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). This claim is supported by the argument that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). A specific mechanism through which this can occur is the provision of public goods and services. Simson (2019, p.2) highlights that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, as the prevailing narrative might suggest. Instead, these policies were shaped by regional instability and growing domestic dissent, which influenced military spending and other budgetary allocations (Simson 2019, p.11). This pattern of expenditure suggests that institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty can have downstream implications for long-run economic growth. The effectiveness of these reforms is contingent on the broader institutional framework and the political context in which they are implemented. For instance, the growth in military spending in response to regional instability and domestic dissent highlights the need for institutional reforms that address security concerns while also promoting economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce contracts, which in turn lower transaction costs. North shows that the creation of institutions such as the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework is critical for economic development, as it provides the necessary security for long-term capital markets and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Hopkins argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is reinforced by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes, which include the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, have had a lasting impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes.

The historical context of institutional development is also crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The institutional structures that emerged in Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by these conflicts and had significant implications for economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This historical complexity is further highlighted by the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations, which were often influenced by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions are formed and how they evolve over time.

The historical evidence and empirical comparisons show that institutions matter for economic growth. North argues that institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand had a significant impact on their economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence from these colonies highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The case of the English financial revolution, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, provides a clear example of how institutional changes can foster economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The measurement of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, further supports the argument that good institutions are crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical evidence from former European colonies and the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations provide a clear understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence and empirical comparisons used by the cited sources show that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is significant and enduring.
