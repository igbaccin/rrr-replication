The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic development, while others contend that institutions are merely one factor among many, and that other variables such as geography, culture, or colonial legacies may play a more decisive role. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth, and whether they are the cause or the effect of development. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and the design of development strategies. Institutions are seen as a powerful tool for fostering economic growth, but their effectiveness depends on their ability to adapt to changing circumstances and to promote the rule of law and secure property rights. The question of whether institutions can be deliberately designed to promote economic growth, or whether they must evolve organically over time, remains unresolved. This is a debate about the causal role of institutions in economic growth, and it hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of development or merely one factor among many. The stakes are high, as the answer to this question will determine the effectiveness of policies aimed at promoting economic growth. The debate is further complicated by institutions being dynamic and adaptive systems that evolve over time in response to changing circumstances. This dynamic nature of institutions makes it difficult to pinpoint their exact role in shaping economic growth. However, the consensus is that institutions play a crucial role in economic development, and that their effectiveness depends on their ability to promote the rule of law and secure property rights. The question of whether institutions can be deliberately designed to promote economic growth, or whether they must evolve organically over time, remains unresolved. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) and Austin (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.2) argue that institutions are critical drivers of long-run economic growth, with their design and enforcement shaping economic outcomes. One key mechanism through which institutions influence growth is by specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn lowers transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). This suggests that the growth-enhancing effects of institutions depend on their inclusiveness and the distribution of political power.

North and Weingast, together with Hopkins, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice is pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This is evident in the political factors underpinning economic growth: the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment is crucial for the development of markets, as it ensures that the rules are enforced and can be changed in a predictable manner. The success of this institutional evolution is starkly illustrated by the evidence from capital markets, which shows that secure property rights encourage investment and economic growth. The implications of this institutional evolution vary across different contexts. Hopkins qualifies this by noting that while secure property rights are crucial, the presence of poor institutions can still hinder economic development, as seen in many African countries where despite ethnic homogeneity, the quality of public goods is poor. This suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with the quality of institutions playing a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional framework that fosters secure property rights and economic growth is not a one-size-fits-all solution; it requires a nuanced understanding of the local context and the specific challenges faced by different societies.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective highlights that technological advancements require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets's work underscores that the pace of technological progress and economic growth, as well as the institutional and ideological adjustments, have varied across centuries and regions. This variability suggests that the interplay between technological change and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic growth. Mokyr further elaborates that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. He recognises that coordination failures can impede technological progress, and suggests policies to rectify them. This implies that while technological advancements are essential, they must be supported by institutional frameworks that facilitate their implementation and diffusion. Mokyr acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness, which underpins institutional progress, is not monotonic or irreversible like technological progress. This qualification suggests that while technological advancements drive economic growth, the institutional context in which they occur can either enhance or hinder their impact. The complexity of this relationship underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions and technology interact to shape long-run economic growth.

The role of women in economic development during colonial periods highlights how gender dynamics influenced agricultural practices and land access, which were shaped by customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). The marginalization of women in land access was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This process often relegated women to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cultivation of cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where it became the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911, illustrates this dynamic. The migration of farmers in search of land for cocoa cultivation was driven by men, who benefited from the codification of customary law that privileged their access to land. The impact of colonial rule on economic development varied across African regions. British rule, for instance, was more conducive to the development of formal education compared to other colonial powers. This educational development altered the perceived benefits of formal education, influencing cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule thus had complex and varied effects on economic development, extending beyond the immediate impact on land access and agricultural practices.

Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and is a significant factor in long-run economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). They argue that this mistrust affects both internal norms and the external environment, including the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings complement other studies that document the importance of trust for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates on the enduring effects of the slave trade, noting that it spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, and had a profound impact on Africa's economic development. This prolonged period of slave trade shaped Africa's social and economic institutions in ways that continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The adverse effects of the slave trade extend beyond economic development to social institutions, which are crucial for fostering trust and cooperation. Nunn highlights that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the presence of strong institutions can mitigate some of the negative effects of the slave trade. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, is not just economic but also institutional, shaping the very foundations of African societies and their capacity for long-run economic growth. The slave trade's impact on African economies underscores the deep and enduring effects of historical institutions on contemporary economic development.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) share the view that institutions are deeply influenced by the power dynamics and conflicts within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues for a more nuanced understanding of institutions, urging economic historians to move beyond superficial analyses and integrate institutions into their long-term research programs. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), who highlight that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies shaped the institutions that evolved, often to the advantage of elites. These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality, illustrating how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that hinder economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates that shared beliefs can be part of the institutional structure of a society, and these beliefs are often shaped by the prevailing power dynamics. The implications of these findings are profound, suggesting that the long-run economic growth of societies may be significantly influenced by the distributional conflicts that shape their institutions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between political power dynamics, distributional conflicts, and the evolution of economic systems. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) outline that institutions are actively shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. These groups often design institutions to maximize their rents, which can hinder long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is echoed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that shared beliefs within a society are part of its institutional structure and are often influenced by prevailing power dynamics. These beliefs can, in turn, shape economic institutions in ways that perpetuate inequality (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence from colonial settings further illustrates this point. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonists established extractive institutions in areas with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is also evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, where institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The institutional framework that underpins economic growth evolves over time. North (1989, p.9) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution is also influenced by broader political and economic contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve in response to economic and political incentives (Austin 2008, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) further elaborates that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the presence of progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding is that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise mechanism by which institutions shape long-run economic growth remains a subject of scholarly inquiry. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which distributional conflicts and power dynamics shape these institutions. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, illustrating how institutions can evolve in response to local contexts (Austin 2008, p.15). This finding is consistent with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of institutional changes in colonial settings and the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies settles this disagreement, showing that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups can indeed hinder long-run economic growth.
