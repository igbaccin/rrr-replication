The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive growth and the mechanisms through which they operate. One live disagreement centers on the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geographic determinants or cultural influences. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. Others contend that while institutions matter, their impact is mediated by other factors, such as the initial conditions of a society or the actions of key actors within it. This debate is crucial for understanding the fundamental processes that underlie economic development and for designing effective policies to promote growth. The nature of institutions themselves is also a point of contention, with some scholars emphasizing the role of formal institutions, such as legal and political frameworks, while others highlight the importance of informal institutions, such as norms and cultural practices. This debate is central to understanding how institutions shape economic outcomes and how they can be harnessed to promote sustainable development. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as geography and culture, is complex and multifaceted, and understanding this interplay is essential for developing a comprehensive theory of economic growth. Institutions are not static entities; they evolve over time in response to changing economic, political, and social conditions, and this dynamic nature is a key factor in their impact on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and instruments, such as the Bank of England, played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. This, in turn, drove long-run economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and encouraging investment. The security of property rights, both in terms of their enforcement and their time dimension, was crucial for the development of a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. This perspective aligns with the broader argument that institutions governing public choice and economic exchange are fundamental to economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize the importance of political institutions that enforce these rules and bind the regime to them, highlighting the critical role of credible commitment in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of these institutions varies across different contexts. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, where the interplay between political and economic institutions shapes the trajectory of development.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap by 1450, suggesting an alternative pathway to economic divergence independent of institutional factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This perspective underscores the role of demographic shifts and their economic consequences, which Pamuk (2007, p.16) further elaborates through urbanisation rates. The Black Death's impact on population dynamics led to significant variations in urban growth and economic development across Europe. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) supplement this perspective by noting that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) also note that the high productivity of southern Indian agriculture was due to geographical factors and high levels of investment. This investment, in turn, was driven by institutional changes that facilitated economic development. The interplay between demographic shifts, institutional flexibility, and economic productivity thus offers a multifaceted explanation for the early modern economic divergence, illustrating how various factors can shape long-run economic growth beyond institutional arrangements.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective highlights that technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress and economic growth require institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them, indicating that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in managing the challenges posed by economic growth. Consequently, the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological progress are essential drivers of economic growth, but they must be accompanied by robust institutional frameworks to ensure sustainable development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) challenge the notion that African economies were uniformly poor before 1950. They demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. The growth of these economies was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets, influenced by factors such as local resources, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) reinforce this perspective by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies, showing that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon. This evidence suggests that economic growth in Africa was driven by the ability of these economies to integrate into global markets and benefit from export-oriented growth. The variability in growth patterns across different regions and colonies shows that local factors, including political institutions and infrastructure, significantly shaped economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight the need for a deeper understanding of these determinants to assess the potential for future growth and the impact of historical conditions on long-term welfare development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share a posture that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. They jointly establish that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth in Africa was mediated through the development of formal education systems. The British colonial system, in particular, fostered a more robust educational infrastructure in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This educational development was crucial as it altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were linked to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This finding suggests that the legacy of colonial education policies continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, as higher levels of education are associated with improved economic performance and institutional development. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this implication by showing that the impact of education on economic growth is complex, as post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, despite neopatrimonial tendencies. East African countries experienced significant shifts in expenditure patterns, particularly in military spending and subsidies, which were influenced by regional instability and domestic dissent (Simson 2019, p.11). This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutional reforms, education, and economic growth in Africa, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and contemporary factors that shape economic development.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that colonialism resulted in different economic outcomes in former European colonies due to varying colonization strategies. These strategies were shaped by European mortality rates, which in turn determined the type of institutions established. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole. Colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound and lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that these institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions significantly impacted current income per capita in former colonies. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring influence of historical institutional changes on contemporary economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) highlight that institutions and gender dynamics played crucial roles in shaping economic development in New World societies and African colonial contexts. The institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. Similarly, in African colonial contexts, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their access to land for cash crops was limited by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This institutional framework reinforced gender inequalities, with patriarchal alliances and customary law further marginalizing women's economic contributions. The de-privileging of women in access to land was exacerbated by colonial rule, which introduced a dual legal system that reinforced these inequalities. This institutional framework limited women's economic opportunities and shaped broader social structures, perpetuating economic disparities. The impact of these institutions was profound, as seen in the economic revolution driven by cocoa production in the Gold Coast, where men dominated the industry. However, this dominance did not translate into broader economic growth but rather entrenched existing inequalities, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of economic opportunities and the broader social structures that support or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, long-term economic growth was fostered. In regions where extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, economic growth was stunted. This dynamic is further illustrated by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework reduced the costs of economic exchanges and ensured that property rights were secure over time, which is crucial for sustained economic development.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonial Africa, where the interplay between institutions and gender dynamics significantly influenced economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) demonstrate that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework entrenched economic inequalities, as men dominated the cocoa industry in the Gold Coast, which became a major economic driver. However, this dominance did not translate into broader economic growth but rather perpetuated existing disparities. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) reinforces this point, arguing that the quality of institutions is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa highlights that good institutions matter because they are key to fostering an environment conducive to investment and economic growth.

The historical evidence from colonial Africa and the broader institutional frameworks established during this period show that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than promoting long-run growth, had a profound impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, rather than encouraging economic development. This is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies, which was driven by institutional changes introduced by colonialism. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) also emphasize the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for economic development. The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France during this period illustrates that institutions governing how rules are enforced and changed are critical for economic growth. The specific institutional frameworks established during colonization in Africa and the credible commitment to property rights in seventeenth-century England provide clear historical evidence that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups hinder economic growth, while those that enforce the rule of law and secure property rights foster long-term economic development.
