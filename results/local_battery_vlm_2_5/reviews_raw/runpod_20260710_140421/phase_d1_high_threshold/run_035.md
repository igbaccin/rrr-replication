The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. The stakes are high: if institutions are indeed pivotal, then understanding their mechanics can guide policy to foster sustained growth. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are essential for economic growth. This perspective posits that institutions create an environment conducive to investment and innovation, thereby driving long-term economic development. Another position suggests that geography and culture are more influential, shaping institutions in ways that either facilitate or hinder growth. Some scholars propose that distributional conflicts are key determinants of core economic institutions, with efficiency being a secondary factor. This debate is far from settled, and the resolution has profound implications for economic policy and development strategies. The interplay between institutions, geography, and culture in shaping economic growth remains a central theme in the literature. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-term economic growth in former colonies. In the case of colonies where Europeans settled, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions fostered economic growth by creating an environment conducive to investment and economic activity. Colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, developed extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, with geographic factors being a secondary consideration. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, with long-run growth being a secondary consideration (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutional legacies. The development experience of these colonies underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes.

The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They note that the success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where the commitment to enforce and maintain rules governing economic exchange fostered a stable environment for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and ensured that the costs per transaction were falling, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.6).

The Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap by 1450, suggesting an alternative pathway to economic divergence independent of institutional factors (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This divergence was driven by the differential impact of the Black Death on labor supply and demand across regions. In northwestern Europe, the demographic shock of the Black Death led to a scarcity of labor, driving up wages and encouraging investment in labor-saving technologies. This process was further amplified by the region's institutional flexibility, which allowed for the rapid emergence of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Regions like Tuscany, which had stronger monopolies and less institutional flexibility, struggled to adapt to the new economic opportunities presented by the post-Black Death environment. This shift varied across Europe. In southern India, for instance, high agricultural productivity was maintained through significant investment, but this did not translate into similarly high wages or economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This regional variation underscores the complex interplay between demographic shocks, institutional structures, and economic outcomes, highlighting that the Black Death's impact on economic divergence was mediated by pre-existing institutional and geographical factors.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This progress, however, requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family patterns, necessitating significant social and institutional changes. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and maintaining political stability, which in turn supports economic growth. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) terms it, is essential for preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking behaviors. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both knowledge accumulation and institutional frameworks play interconnected roles.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that the institutions shaping long-run economic growth in the New World are fundamentally linked to the inequality-advantaging policies that emerged in these societies. These policies, which favored elites, restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved, affecting long-run economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this perspective by noting that certain African kingdoms, such as the Kongo, had developed centralized governments and trading networks that facilitated foreign trade. This suggests that, despite the adverse effects of slave trades, some African societies had the institutional capacity to engage in economic activities that could support development.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights various factors influencing public goods provision, including institutional change driven by both African initiatives and exogenous causes (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes that the institutional quality of African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation. Instead, African societies can develop progressive institutions despite ethnic diversity. This argument is reinforced by Frankema (2012, p.15), who shows that the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions under British rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby fostering its expansion. This institutional framework, shaped by both endogenous and exogenous factors, is crucial for understanding the provision of public goods in Africa. Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this perspective by noting that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exclusively clientelist or unproductive. Instead, these policies were shaped by regional instability and domestic dissent, leading to significant shifts in expenditure patterns, particularly in military spending. This suggests that the institutional landscape of post-colonial Africa was complex and multifaceted, with various factors influencing the provision of public goods and the overall economic trajectory.

Broadberry and Gardner along with Frankema and van Waijenburg have challenged the narrative of persistent poverty in Africa by demonstrating that many African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. They show that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been underutilized, limiting comparative historical work on economic growth and development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). These factors underscore the dynamic nature of African economies, which responded to various internal and external stimuli. Frankema and van Waijenburg further emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which is crucial for assessing future growth prospects and the long-term impact of historical conditions on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective highlights the importance of considering the distribution of income and the specific historical and geographical conditions that have influenced economic development in Africa. This integration suggests that African economies were part of a broader global economic system, which had implications for their growth and development.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, and argues that this oversimplifies causation, shared with Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin's critique highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. This perspective contrasts with the view that secure private property rights alone are sufficient for long-term economic growth. Austin further illustrates that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour (Austin 2008, p.15). This shows that institutional prescriptions could change in response to local economic and political actions, rather than being uniformly imposed. Ogilvie's work complements this view by arguing that distributional conflicts better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). Ogilvie emphasizes the importance of incorporating institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that a deeper understanding of these conflicts can provide insights into institutional changes and their economic implications. Ogilvie also notes that exogenous cultural theories, which attribute economic performance to shared beliefs and values, can oversimplify the complex interplay between culture and institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This qualification underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach that considers both institutional and cultural factors in explaining economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between historical contexts and institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional structures in colonies, which significantly influenced current economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, whether inclusive or extractive, shaped the economic trajectories of colonies by determining the extent to which property rights and rule of law were enforced (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). North argues that the development of institutions, such as the Bank of England, reflects increased security of property rights, which is crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, thereby promoting economic development (North 1989, p.6). Austin highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, with geographic factors being a secondary consideration (Austin 2008, p.15).

The historical evidence from Acemoglu et al. shows that the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This finding is reinforced by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which are essential for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie notes that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism were pivotal in shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies, with geographic factors being a secondary consideration (Austin 2008, p.15). The new economic history of Africa, as argued by Hopkins, can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This approach underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development in Africa and its implications for economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. through the establishment of diverse institutional structures in colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, as highlighted by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This evidence demonstrates the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies on economic growth.
