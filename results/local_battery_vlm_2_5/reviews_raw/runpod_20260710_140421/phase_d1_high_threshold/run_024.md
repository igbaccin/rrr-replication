The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies progress over extended periods. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that the establishment of effective institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and encourage investment, is crucial for sustained economic development. Others posit that the efficiency of institutions is less important than the distribution of power and the resolution of conflicts within societies. The tension between these views highlights the need to understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. The question of whether institutions are a cause or a consequence of economic growth is central to this debate. The answer has profound implications for policy-making and economic theory. The debate about the role of institutions in economic growth is not merely academic; it directly informs how governments and international organizations approach development strategies. This question is particularly pertinent in the context of colonial legacies and the persistent economic disparities observed in former colonies. This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both long-term capital markets and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of this growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutional developments imply that economic growth is driven by institutional frameworks supporting secure property rights and efficient market exchanges, in addition to population and savings, as neoclassical theories suggest (Author, Year, p.N). This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that attributes economic development primarily to exogenous factors, highlighting instead the endogenous role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective posits that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets elaborates on the significance of technological progress, noting that it is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures. This incompatibility necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for the efficient use of innovations. The pace and sectors affected by technological progress vary over time and across regions, influencing the nature of these adjustments. Mokyr supports this idea, emphasizing that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements might be unsustainable. He highlights the role of coordination failures and policies to rectify them, suggesting that economic reasonableness, though less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, is crucial for sustained growth. However, Mokyr acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness is not as linear as technological progress, indicating that while technological advancements are critical, they must be accompanied by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) share a finding that British colonial rule had a more positive impact on the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This institutional change was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, where the implementation of British institutions created new incentives for education (Frankema 2012, p.15). However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also note that the timing and scale of economic growth in these countries were influenced by a range of factors, including political institutions, which varied both between and within countries (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while British rule fostered educational development, the specific impact of these institutions on economic growth depended on local conditions and the effectiveness of these institutions in promoting education and economic activity. The effectiveness of colonial institutions in promoting economic growth highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes, even in the face of varying local conditions.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers adopted different strategies based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The persistence of these extractive institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutional legacies. These findings underscore the importance of initial institutional frameworks in shaping long-term economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) also emphasize that the effectiveness of these institutions varied depending on the specific measures used to assess them, such as constraints on the executive or property rights indices. This variability suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by both the nature of the institutions themselves and the local conditions in which they operated.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share a finding that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms that reduce poverty, but these studies do not directly engage with the broader causal mechanisms of long-run economic growth through institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) posits that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This perspective is aligned with the idea that institutions can provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa has gained attention from development specialists because of its emphasis on the role of 'good' institutions. This approach helps to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered. The new economic history of Africa does not directly engage with the broader causal mechanisms of long-run economic growth through institutions, but it does highlight specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty.

The institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, in turn, reinforced the existing power dynamics by restricting access to resources and opportunities, thereby perpetuating inequality. For instance, the establishment of plantations in Latin America led to the concentration of wealth and power in the hands of a few, who then used their influence to shape institutions that protected their interests. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) adds that these institutions were not static but evolved over time in response to the changing economic and political conditions, further entrenching the advantages of the elites. This dynamic is evident in the evolution of guilds in Europe, where the rules and regulations were designed to protect the interests of the craftsmanship elites. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that the persistence of these institutions was not inevitable, and that changes in beliefs and attitudes could lead to institutional reforms that would challenge the existing power dynamics. The persistence of inequality in these societies is thus a result of the interplay between institutions and the interests of the powerful elites who control them.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between the interests of powerful elites and the broader economic and social contexts in which institutions operate. Institutions are dynamic entities that evolve in response to changes in beliefs, attitudes, and power dynamics. This evolution is evident in the development of financial institutions during the English financial revolution, which led to a significant decline in transaction costs and increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of the Bank of England and new financial instruments facilitated market exchanges and fostered economic growth by providing a secure framework for long-term investments (North 1989, p.9). Similarly, the evolution of political institutions in seventeenth-century England underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic development. The political factors underpinning economic growth, such as the degree to which the regime is committed to enforcing rules, are crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment to enforceable rules and secure property rights is a shared finding among scholars who emphasize the role of institutions in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.9). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further highlighted by the persistence of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, illustrate how institutions can be shaped by politically powerful groups to serve their interests rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This evidence illustrates how institutional changes can profoundly impact economic outcomes, underscoring the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
