The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. The live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play more significant roles. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic growth. Others posit that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that their impact can be overshadowed by other variables. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The core of the disagreement is whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play more significant roles. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the broader question of how societies develop economically over extended periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North (1989, p.9) and other scholars (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. This institutional development reflects a broader pattern where efficient property rights and their enforcement drive economic growth by lowering transaction costs and fostering market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further illustrate this dynamic by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. These institutional differences had long-lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received extractive institutions experiencing lower income per capita. The persistence of these institutions suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers significantly influenced long-term economic trajectories. This highlights the significance of institutions that encourage economic development and resource extraction. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. A critical aspect of this process was the development of credible commitments by the state to enforce property rights, which in turn encouraged investment and economic activity. This institutional framework was essential for the growth of capital markets, as it provided a stable environment where investors could confidently engage in long-term ventures. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advises that institutions are essential, but their influence on economic growth depends on broader socio-political factors. Ogilvie's emphasis on the multifaceted nature of institutions suggests that while secure property rights are necessary, they are not sufficient on their own to guarantee economic growth. This implies that the interaction between institutional frameworks and other socio-economic factors is crucial for sustained economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that technological progress and institutional advances work in tandem to facilitate economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that the diffusion of technological innovations requires institutional adjustments to ensure that these technological changes are efficiently integrated into the economy. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further highlights that coordination failures can impede economic sustainability. These failures are often rooted in the lack of institutional progress, which can undermine the long-term benefits of technological advancements. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this point, emphasizing that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby ensuring that the political fabric of society remains intact. Without such institutional support, the economic benefits derived from technological progress could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. This suggests that while technological advancements are essential for economic growth, their impact is significantly enhanced by complementary institutional frameworks.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered educational development more effectively than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions created a demand for missionary education, which in turn supported the growth of formal educational systems. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This indicates that the institutional frameworks established during British colonial rule both advanced educational development and stimulated economic growth, suggesting that not all colonial regimes had uniformly negative effects on African development.

The literature on colonial impact on African economic development explores historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This influence was mediated through the provision of public goods and the establishment of the rule of law, which were crucial for economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the way colonial administrations fostered educational development and supported economic growth through foreign trade and export markets. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments was influenced by the institutional capacity to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, in addition to ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This institutional capacity was a key factor in the economic performance of African economies, which had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the notion that all colonial regimes had uniformly negative impacts on African development. This gap in historical data underscores the need for further research to fully understand the institutional mechanisms that drove economic growth in colonial Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) highlight the critical role of historical inequality and the slave trade in shaping economic development. They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized many New World societies from their inception had profound impacts on the institutional frameworks that evolved. These institutional differences, in turn, influenced long-term economic trajectories. One specific mechanism through which inequality affected economic development was the creation of policies that advantaged elites. For instance, the institutional frameworks in societies with greater inequality often restricted competition, thereby perpetuating inequality. This is evident in the policies that favored elite interests, such as those that limited access to education and political participation for the lower classes. Nunn (2008, p.19) provides a complementary perspective by examining how the slave trades in Africa had lasting effects on the continent's economic development. Nunn's analysis shows that the slave trades disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to underdevelopment. The disruption of traditional economic and social structures by the slave trades left African societies vulnerable to further exploitation and hindered their ability to develop robust institutions that could support long-term economic growth. The impact of inequality and the slave trade on economic development underscores the importance of historical context in shaping institutional frameworks and long-term economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during the colonial period significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions matter in economic growth, particularly through the specification and enforcement of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These findings indicate that the nature of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacts long-term economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments are key examples of how institutional changes can lead to economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial policies on former colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies resulted from institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists and not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is consistent with North and Weingast's argument that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England's institutional framework is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the efficacy of these institutions in promoting economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The historical evidence from colonial policies and the development of financial institutions in England highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise measurement of the impact of these institutions on economic development is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies and the development of capital markets in England. These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in economic growth, as shown by the shared finding that the nature of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacts long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29).
