The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. Understanding the mechanisms through which institutions drive growth is crucial for designing effective economic policies and for explaining economic divergence among nations. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who attribute economic development to other factors. Some scholars argue that institutions foster growth by providing incentives for investment and securing property rights. Others contend that economic growth can be explained by distributional conflicts and the efficiency of economic institutions. A third perspective posits that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This review synthesizes these perspectives to elucidate the causal role of institutions in long-run economic growth. The evidence shows that institutions matter in economic growth by shaping the incentives for investment and securing property rights, which are critical for long-term economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

The creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argued that the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly lowered the cost of transacting, which is a critical dimension of property rights security. This institutional development reflected a broader trend in England where political institutions evolved to specify and enforce property rights efficiently. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) noted that the success of these institutions was evident in the capital markets, indicating that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is complex. North (1989, p.6) highlighted that while transaction costs per transaction were falling, the aggregate volume of exchanges was increasing, leading to a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP. This indicates that the institutional changes in England lowered transaction costs and fostered a more intricate and interdependent economy. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This perspective challenges the notion that economic development is primarily driven by population and savings, as proposed by neoclassical theories.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They show that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The nature of these institutions was determined by the Europeans' colonization strategies, ranging from settler colonies with inclusive institutions to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established, impeding economic development. This institutional divergence, driven by European policies, had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) reinforce this perspective by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, yield similar results, underscoring the robustness of the institutional impact on economic development. The persistence of these institutions, even after independence, suggests that the initial colonial policies continue to shape economic growth in these regions.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that distributional conflicts and inequality, more so than efficiency considerations, better elucidate the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies, questioning the traditional view of institutional efficiency. They posit that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful elites who use them to maintain their privileges, a perspective that highlights the role of distributional conflicts in institutional development. For instance, in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic is evident in the differential paths of development in Latin America, where institutions often failed to guarantee property rights and contract enforcement, reflecting the influence of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. These beliefs, in turn, were shaped by the interests of elites who used institutions to maintain their power and wealth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) advises against overemphasizing the role of institutions in economic development, proposing that they be viewed as one of many factors influencing long-term economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on inequality and development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long run.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) jointly find that African economic development is influenced by a variety of factors, including the role of women and the impact of colonial rule, without explicitly linking these to institutional frameworks. The role of women in African economic development is significant. During both pre-colonial and colonial periods, women contributed to economic growth by farming marginal lands for food crops, while men were often involved in cash crops. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary laws that privileged men in access to land. Women's economic activities, however, were not merely marginal; they were essential to sustaining families and communities, thereby indirectly influencing broader economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18) observes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly influenced by ethnic fragmentation. This suggests that economic outcomes are shaped by multiple factors, including the role of women and the impact of colonial rule, and not just by institutional frameworks.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that the slave trades had a profound adverse effect on Africa's economic development, suggesting that the continent's underdevelopment is not solely attributable to colonialism or recent events. The slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, with at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust attributable to this internalised mistrust. This internalised mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as it can hinder cooperation and collective action, which are crucial for economic growth. The slave trades also had an adverse effect on the development of strong, centralized institutions, which are essential for economic growth. The Kongo kingdom, for instance, was able to support trade with the Europeans due to its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks (Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trades disrupted these institutions, resulting in a lack of strong, centralized institutions in many parts of Africa. The slave trades led to a loss of human capital, as many skilled individuals were taken from Africa and never returned. This loss of human capital had a significant impact on economic development, as it reduced the pool of skilled workers available to contribute to economic growth. Colonial education policies played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of sub-Saharan African nations, as Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) show. British colonial rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers by altering the perceived benefits of education through the implementation of new social and political institutions. (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This shift in perceived benefits led to increased demand for missionary education, as African political leaders and families recalibrated their cost-benefit analyses in light of these new institutional frameworks. British rule's emphasis on formal education had a profound impact on economic development, as it enhanced human capital and fostered skills crucial for economic growth. The long-term economic benefits of this educational investment were not evenly distributed. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) highlight that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This gap in data underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial education policies influenced economic development over time, and how these effects varied across different regions and colonies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is central to understanding the economic trajectories of different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic outcomes of their colonies. They show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered long-run growth. This perspective is reinforced by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is crucial for economic growth. North highlights that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is essential for economic growth. These institutional changes reflect increased security of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself.
