The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. At stake intellectually is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate, and what factors drive sustained economic progress. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions matter primarily because they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights and enforceable contracts, which in turn foster investment and economic activity. Institutions are seen as the cornerstone of economic development, shaping the incentives and constraints that guide individual and collective behavior. Other scholars suggest that institutions are just one component of a complex system, with factors like geography, culture, and historical legacies also playing significant roles. This debate highlights the need to disentangle the specific ways in which institutions operate to promote or hinder economic growth, and to assess the relative importance of institutions compared to other explanatory variables. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, with institutions both shaping and being shaped by economic outcomes. This interplay is evident in the historical evolution of economic systems, where changes in institutions have often coincided with periods of significant economic growth or decline. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the creation of financial institutions and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments in facilitating economic activity. This institutional change reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) provide evidence from capital markets that underscores the significance of these institutional developments. They contend that political factors driving economic growth and market development are vital, including the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions that enforce and modify these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment to property rights and secure enforcement mechanisms is essential for fostering economic growth. This qualification implies that while the creation of financial institutions and secure property rights is important, the specific institutional details and historical context also play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; McCloskey 2015, p.3). This perspective highlights the importance of innovation in fostering long-run economic growth, suggesting that ideas and innovations are more fundamental than institutions in this process. For example, technological progress is often incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating significant institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This dynamic is evident in the historical context of early industrialization, where technological advancements required complementary institutional progress to ensure sustainable economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.1) adds that resolving growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs is essential for modern economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is essential, the institutional framework must also adapt to support and sustain this progress.

This perspective is further complicated by Nunn and Austin (Nunn 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.2), who posit that Africa's slave trades, rather than institutions, were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, highlighting the importance of historical context. They posit that the adverse effects of these slave trades were rooted in the destruction of social capital and the undermining of political institutions. The slave trades disrupted local communities, leading to a loss of knowledge and skills, and created a climate of mistrust that impeded collective action and economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) provides empirical evidence showing that regions more heavily affected by slave trades experienced lower economic growth and development. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could have mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trades. The effectiveness of these policies varied, and the broader impact of the slave trades on economic development remained substantial. This suggests that while colonial policies could provide some economic benefits, they were not sufficient to overcome the deep-seated institutional and social damage caused by the slave trades.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World. These institutions, in turn, influenced long-run economic growth by determining the distribution of resources and opportunities. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) illustrates that institutions are not merely fashion accessories but indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian. This perspective is crucial for understanding how the institutional landscape of pre-industrial Europe and the New World was shaped by the interests of different groups, particularly elites. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) elaborate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in the New World societies led to the establishment of institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and hindering economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality and the restricted opportunities for economic advancement for the majority of the population.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the finding that the institutions established under colonial rule shaped the provision of public goods and education in Africa. This institutional impact on education highlights a broader pattern: colonial institutions shaped not only educational outcomes but also the economic trajectories of African societies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies was more intricate than previously thought, with levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role, other factors such as local endowments and transport costs also influenced economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The intricate interplay between colonial institutions and economic development in Africa underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) further emphasize the significant contributions of African women to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods, as well as the enduring effects of the slave trade on trust within Africa. These factors underscore the multifaceted role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the Gold Coast, where women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cash crop economy. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men's access to land for cash crops. This codification of customary law under colonial rule is a mechanism that Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) show was instrumental in shaping economic development by reinforcing patriarchal alliances. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that trust is crucial for economic development, and the slave trade's impact on trust has lasting effects. The slave trade disrupted social structures and fostered a culture of mistrust that endures today. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activities. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as large as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust has profound implications for economic growth, as it erodes the social fabric necessary for sustainable development. The enduring legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa by perpetuating a culture of mistrust that hinders economic development.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) provide additional insight by asserting that budget policies and land systems in Africa have been shaped by institutional dynamics that, while not directly addressing long-run economic growth, have significant implications for resource allocation and economic development. This institutional framework, where resources are channeled towards political patronage rather than productive investments, has profound implications for economic development. The allocation of resources towards subsidies, interest payments, and military spending, as detailed by Simson (2019, p.2), exemplifies how institutional dynamics can divert funds away from capital and recurrent spending, potentially hindering long-term economic growth (Simson 2019, p.11). Peters (2004, p.19) notes that the perception of static customary land practices can sometimes benefit groups seeking to evade official scrutiny, thereby influencing economic outcomes in nuanced ways (Peters 2004, p.5). The long-term effects of these institutional arrangements, therefore, are complex and multifaceted, influencing not only economic growth but also the broader socio-political landscape of African economies.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established during colonialism significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies. The European colonists adopted varying strategies depending on mortality rates and the potential for resource extraction, which led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. In extractive states, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, the primary goal was rapid resource transfer to the metropole, resulting in institutions that maximized rents for the colonists rather than promoting long-run growth. This extractive framework hindered the establishment of institutions that could encourage investment and enforce the rule of law, which are crucial for sustained economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further illustrate this point by demonstrating the robustness of their findings across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, and property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This suggests that while extractive institutions initially hindered growth, the potential for industrialization could mitigate some of these negative effects over time.

Long-term economic performance is shaped by structural changes that are influenced by various factors, including collaborative research efforts and historical events. van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) together argue that a key factor in long-term economic performance is the skill premium, which is determined by the costs of training and institutional barriers to entry. The skill premium in the long term is determined by the costs of training, including unearned income during the training period and other related expenses, and by the chance that the apprentice will successfully finish their training period and acquire a skilled job (van Zanden 2009, p.3). This mechanism is supported by the role of guilds in western Europe, which took care of intergenerational skill transfers (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The guilds have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into their trade, which could offset the beneficial effects of the guilds on the intergenerational transfer of skills. The collaborative research efforts of scholars working on long-term economic development have been essential in producing a more detailed overview of economic growth, as seen in the work of Angus Maddison and his successors (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). These efforts have led to a more nuanced understanding of long-term economic performance and structural changes. Pamuk (2007, p.23) further qualifies this by showing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, indicating that historical events can significantly impact long-term economic performance (Pamuk 2007, p.23).

Institutional frameworks, including property rights, governance structures, and legal systems, play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that Europeans established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North argues that institutions, by specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement, reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes the importance of understanding institutions as part of the broader economic and social framework. She argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and shared beliefs, which influence their effectiveness in promoting economic development (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The historical context of institutional development is critical in understanding long-term economic performance. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He contends that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). This aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-term development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, as noted by North, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and supporting economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating long-term economic development.

Institutions matter in economic growth because they provide the framework for market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. Austin shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This aligns with North's argument that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). These findings are supported by Hopkins, who argues that good institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa, as highlighted by Hopkins, can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This underscores the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions are fundamental to long-run economic growth. North shows that institutions specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions shape economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth had a significant impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence of colonial policies and their enduring impact on economic development, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27).
