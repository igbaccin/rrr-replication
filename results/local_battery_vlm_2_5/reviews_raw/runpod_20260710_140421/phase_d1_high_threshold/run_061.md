The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergent paths of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who attribute growth primarily to geographic factors, cultural beliefs, or political structures. One side argues that institutions, by defining property rights, enforcing contracts, and ensuring political stability, create an environment conducive to economic development. The opposing side posits that geographic endowments, such as climate and natural resources, or cultural factors, like shared beliefs and values, are more determinant. This tension underscores the need to understand how institutions interact with these other factors to influence economic outcomes. The debate is further complicated by the role of colonialism and its legacy on institutional development. Institutions are not static; they evolve over time, shaped by political and economic forces, and in turn, shape the trajectory of economic growth. This evolution is crucial for understanding how different institutional frameworks can lead to divergent economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments significantly enhanced the security of property rights, which is crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) notes that the Bank of England's establishment in 1694 marked a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights. This financial revolution was instrumental in developing a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows that the political factors underpinning economic growth were crucial. This includes the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing and changing these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) emphasize that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime is committed to these rules. This commitment to secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is what drove economic growth in England. This process leads to falling costs per transaction, which in turn increases the aggregate volume of exchanges, driving economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus deeply intertwined with the security and enforcement of property rights, which are foundational for economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita, a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The institutions established during the colonial period were shaped by European settlers' mortality rates, which led to the development of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional divergence has had lasting effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions has significantly impacted current income per capita, with former colonies that had extractive institutions experiencing lower levels of economic development. The role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus evident in the enduring effects of these institutional legacies.

In pre-industrial Europe, the development of institutions such as serfdom and guilds was driven by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency, as highlighted by Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are purely efficiency-driven, instead highlighting how power dynamics and conflicts over resource distribution influence institutional design. The mechanism through which distributional conflicts shape institutions involves the strategic actions of elites who impose institutions that secure their economic and political power. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, serfdom and guilds were maintained to consolidate elite control over labor and markets. This dynamic is further supported by the observation that in New World societies, institutional structures that advantaged elites through various government policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The implications of this distributional conflict perspective are significant, as it suggests that institutions are not neutral tools for economic efficiency but are deeply embedded in social and political power structures. The impact of distributional conflicts on institutional development varies. In Latin America, for instance, the institutional legacies of colonialism have been particularly durable, with shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights blocking the development of institutions that could guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.3).

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) jointly argue. They establish that technological progress is not merely a driver of economic growth but also necessitates complementary institutional changes. Mokyr (2005, p.54) elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may not be sustainable. Therefore, the interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is vital for long-term economic growth. This qualification suggests that while technological progress is a significant driver, the institutional adjustments required for sustained growth can be complex and uncertain. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving conflicts induced by technological changes without excessive costs, underscoring the role of institutions in maintaining social cohesion. The sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts, highlighting the importance of political stability in fostering economic growth.

The Black Death's long-term structural changes, as argued by Pamuk (2007, p.23) and van Zanden (1999, p.1), played a crucial role in shaping economic divergence in Europe, challenging views that attribute this divergence solely to institutional developments. This divergence was further exacerbated by the varying degrees of institutional flexibility in different regions. For instance, Holland's institutional flexibility allowed for the rapid emergence of new towns and rural manufacturing, while Tuscany's institutions strengthened existing monopolies, hindering economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) elaborates on this by noting that economic development shifted to the North Sea countries, particularly Holland and England, by the 17th and 18th centuries. This shift was marked by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating a significant change in economic dynamics. Van Zanden (1999, p.9) also points out that while England became the most expensive country after 1740, differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760. This suggests that while structural changes were indeed pivotal, the institutional framework played a significant role in mediating these changes and shaping long-term economic outcomes. The relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living was complex, with no clear linear relationship emerging from the data.

The economic performance of early twentieth-century Anglophone African economies, as demonstrated by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), was more robust than previously thought, with per capita income levels above subsistence. This finding is supported by the availability of extensive economic data from the colonial era, which has enabled recent historical national accounting efforts. This data reveals that the growth patterns in these economies were intricately linked to foreign trade and export markets, with variations in timing and scale influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) further argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe. This productivity was facilitated by merchant distribution and finance, highlighting the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period. The uneven distribution of the fruits of early modern development, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled ones, underscores the complex interplay between economic growth and social stratification. The high productivity of southern Indian agriculture, as noted by Parthasarathi, presents a counterpoint. This productivity is attributed to high levels of investment during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, suggesting that institutional change and investment in agriculture could lead to high agricultural labor productivity. This highlights the potential for institutional frameworks to mediate economic outcomes, even in the face of significant structural changes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of historical economic paths beyond structural impediments. They argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique underscores the importance of contextualizing economic development within specific historical and geographical frameworks. Austin's analysis of colonial governance in British Africa demonstrates that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive behavior suggests that institutional change was influenced by local dynamics. However, Austin also notes that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, indicating that the incentives created by colonial rule could sometimes align with local economic interests. This alignment could potentially mitigate some of the negative impacts of colonial institutions on economic growth. Nevertheless, the complexity of these interactions underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of colonial rule.

The slave trade in Africa, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (Nunn 2008, p.2) and (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27), led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development. This mistrust manifests in both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as large as the external channel. This mistrust impedes economic growth by reducing cooperation and hindering the development of robust economic institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the presence of strong institutions can mitigate some of the negative impacts of the slave trade. This pervasive mistrust continues to shape economic interactions and development trajectories in Africa today.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented in historical and economic literature. Austin (2008, p.2) and North (1989, p.9) show that the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) explains that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.6). The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, as described by North and Weingast (1989, p.29), underscore the importance of credible commitment in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment is evident in the successful development of capital markets, which required a regime bound by rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France highlights the transformative role of institutions in economic development, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the institutional changes in England were pivotal in achieving economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional legacy of the English financial revolution, with its secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, continues to influence economic interactions and development trajectories today.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of distinct institutions in colonies, significantly affecting current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that in colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans adopted extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in other colonies, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutional differences, introduced by colonialism, are the primary cause of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, has had long-lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from colonies with different colonization strategies underscores the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic outcomes. The shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth is evident in the historical experiences of both European and colonial economies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of institutions on economic growth is uniform across different historical contexts. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, indicating a more nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
