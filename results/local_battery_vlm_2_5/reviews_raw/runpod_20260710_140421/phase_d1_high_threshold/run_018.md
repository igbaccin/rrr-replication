The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who see institutions as secondary to other factors. One side argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for fostering economic growth by providing a stable and predictable environment for investment and innovation. This perspective is supported by evidence that colonies with extractive institutions, designed to benefit European colonists, have experienced lower income per capita (Author, Year, p.N). Another side posits that the impact of institutions on economic growth is overstated, and that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs, play a significant role. This perspective is supported by evidence that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The tension between these views underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions. The literature on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth can be divided into several streams, each offering a unique perspective on this critical issue. The first stream addresses the impact of different institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted distinct colonization strategies, setting up institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or created extractive states. A key mechanism through which these institutions influenced economic growth was by shaping the opportunity to industrialize. Acemoglu et al. document a reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This reversal highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast's (1989, p.2) account of the success of the Glorious Revolution highlights the critical role of credible commitment in establishing secure property rights. This institutional framework enhanced the security of property rights and fostered a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.9). North's (1989, p.6) analysis of transaction costs in a growing economy suggests that while the size of the transactions sector increases with economic growth, the costs per transaction should decrease due to efficient property rights and effective enforcement. This implies that the institutional changes in England were revolutionary in terms of economic performance and the development of economic institutions that supported market exchanges.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) jointly find that African economic development was shaped by a complex interplay of endogenous and exogenous factors, with institutional change being a critical element. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This finding underscores that the effectiveness of institutions can vary independently of ethnic diversity, suggesting that other factors, such as governance and policy, play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. This perspective is crucial because it shifts the focus from inherent structural barriers to more dynamic and mutable factors that can be addressed through institutional reforms. The implication is that understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa can provide insights into the possibilities of future growth. This underscores the importance of historical data in informing current institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty.

Broadberry and Gupta argue that the great divergence in economic performance between Europe and Africa was already well underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This early divergence can be attributed to the distinct institutional and economic frameworks that developed in these regions. The institutional environment in Europe, particularly in north-western Europe, fostered high productivity in the traded goods sector through merchant distribution and finance. This productivity extended to the service sector, which played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. The economic benefits of this institutional setup were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers. This pattern of economic growth in Europe contrasts with the situation in Africa, where the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gardner note that the patterns of growing and shrinking in African economies were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the institutional frameworks in Africa were not as conducive to sustained economic growth as those in Europe. The availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era in Africa indicates that further research could shed light on the specific institutional factors that hindered or promoted economic growth in different African regions. The uneven distribution of economic benefits in Europe and the varied patterns of growth in Africa highlight the complex interplay between institutions and economic performance.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) both focus on the measurement of economic performance and the determinants of the skill premium. This is because the skill premium reflects the costs of training and the barriers to entry into skilled trades, which in turn affect the supply of skilled labor. Guilds, for example, played a significant role in the intergenerational transfer of skills by providing institutional support for apprenticeship contracts. These institutions were also criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry, which could artificially inflate the skill premium by keeping wages above market-clearing levels. This dynamic suggests that while institutions can facilitate skill development, they can also create barriers that distort the labor market. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex, as they can both promote and hinder economic performance through their impact on the skill premium. The skill premium is determined by the costs of training, the chance of successfully finishing training, the interest rate, and the number of years a skilled worker can earn a higher wage (van Zanden 2009, p.3). These factors collectively influence the supply of skilled labor and, consequently, economic growth.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, including serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). These institutions were deeply rooted in the power dynamics and distributional struggles of the time. The craft guilds, for instance, were tools for restricting competition and maintaining elite control over skilled labor. This dynamic is evident in the way these institutions evolved to protect the interests of the ruling elites. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the fact that they served the interests of the powerful, who had the political and economic clout to maintain them. This perspective does not negate the role of efficiency considerations altogether. For example, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) note that the factor endowments of New World societies, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, influenced the development of institutions that favored elites. This suggests that while distributional conflicts were a primary driver, the specific environmental and economic conditions also played a role in shaping these institutions. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus complex, as they can both promote and hinder economic performance through their impact on the distribution of power and resources.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr and Kuznets (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) jointly assert. This perspective underscores that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The mechanism through which this occurs is the diffusion of knowledge, which propels economic growth by enhancing productivity and innovation. The integration of new technologies into economic systems requires a complementary set of institutions to effectively manage and sustain these advancements. Kuznets highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures, necessitating institutional adjustments to accommodate technological progress (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that while technological advancements drive growth, the institutional environment must evolve to support and integrate these changes. Mokyr further emphasizes that technological progress alone is insufficient without institutional progress to complement it (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while the growth of useful knowledge is crucial, the role of institutions in facilitating and sustaining this growth is equally important. Kuznets elaborates that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts through effective institutional frameworks is essential for the sustainability of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in managing the social and economic disruptions that accompany technological advancements.

Nunn argues that Africa's slave trades were a primary factor in its underdevelopment, challenging the notion that colonialism or recent events are solely responsible (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). The slave trades in Africa, which spanned nearly five centuries, are argued to have had a profound impact on the continent's economic trajectory. Nunn posits that the lengthy duration of these trades, which began in the 15th century and continued until the 19th century, had a more significant impact on Africa's development than the relatively shorter period of colonial rule, which lasted approximately 75 years. This historical context suggests that the long-term effects of the slave trades, including the disruption of social and economic structures, were more detrimental to Africa's development than the institutional frameworks imposed during the colonial period. This perspective is supported by Frankema, who highlights that the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education in Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of these institutions varied, as the demand for missionary education was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This variation indicates that the effectiveness of colonial institutions in promoting development was not uniform across Africa. Austin further qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local responses suggests that the impact of institutions on economic development was more complex than a simple cause-and-effect relationship. The implications of these findings underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors that shaped Africa's economic development, recognizing the enduring effects of the slave trades and the varied impact of colonial institutions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North shows that institutions create secure property rights, which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is echoed by Acemoglu et al. who argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as seen in the United States and New Zealand, differs from extractive states in the Congo or the Gold Coast, where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic highlights the critical role of institutional design in determining economic outcomes. The impact of institutions on economic development is complex and multifaceted, as seen in the varied responses of colonial administrations to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).
