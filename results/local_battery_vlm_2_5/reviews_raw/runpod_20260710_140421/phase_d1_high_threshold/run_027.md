The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of how and why some societies thrive economically while others lag behind. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions drive economic growth, with some scholars emphasizing the primacy of institutional factors over other determinants such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, while others posit that institutions are merely one of many factors that influence economic outcomes. Some scholars propose that institutions evolve in response to economic needs, while others suggest that institutions are shaped by political and social forces. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars offering varying perspectives on the causal mechanisms at work.

The causal role of institutions in economic growth is evident in the historical context of European colonization. Acemoglu et al. argue that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, which led to the establishment of distinct institutions in their colonies. In some cases, Europeans settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other cases, they established extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole. These different institutional frameworks had significant and lasting effects on the economic development of the colonies. Austin offers a complementary perspective, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin highlights that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27)(Austin 2008, p.2) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England, which lowered transaction costs and fostered a long-term capital market. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may prioritize short-term gains over long-term growth. This implies that even if institutions are efficient in the short term, they may not always promote sustained economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies developing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others established extractive states.

Institutional reforms in Africa are crucial for alleviating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Institutional reforms in Africa are pivotal for addressing poverty, driven by both endogenous and exogenous factors, and can be informed by the new economic history of Africa. By examining the colonial experience, Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Instead, societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, or ethnically heterogeneous but capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight underscores the importance of institutional quality over ethnic composition in driving economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) complement this view by demonstrating that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their reconstruction of real wage levels and trends across British African colonies reveals that long-term economic development in Africa has been influenced by specific historical and geographical conditions, rather than being permanently constrained by structural factors (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This perspective suggests that targeted institutional reforms can overcome historical barriers to growth, paving the way for future economic development. The effectiveness of these reforms is contingent on the specific context and the ability of institutions to adapt to changing circumstances.

The institutional framework governing public choice in seventeenth-century England played a pivotal role in securing property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework encompassed economic rules and the political factors that ensured these rules were enforced and could be adapted over time. The commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical factor in underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They note that societies with greater inequality often develop institutions that advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of New World societies, where initial conditions of inequality led to the development of institutions that favored the elite, thereby hindering broader economic growth. The implication is that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness in promoting economic growth depends on their adaptability and the specific historical and geographical conditions in which they operate.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie's analysis of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, shows that these institutions can be better understood as products of power struggles between different social groups. This perspective highlights the role of political and social dynamics in shaping economic institutions. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts influence institutions is through the political and economic power of elites. These elites often have the ability to shape institutions in ways that maintain their own power and wealth, even if these institutions are not the most efficient for economic growth. For instance, Austin's examination of African colonial institutions shows that colonial governments tailored their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, thereby creating a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on the broader political and social context in which they operate. The contextual nature of institutions means that their impact on economic growth can vary significantly across different historical and geographical settings. For example, the institutional evolution in pre-industrial Europe was shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups, which led to the development of institutions that were not necessarily efficient but served the interests of the dominant groups. This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding the political and social context in which institutions evolve, as it provides insights into how these institutions can shape long-run economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade created a legacy of mistrust that has endured through generations. This mistrust manifests in various forms, including reduced intergroup trust and a diminished sense of trustworthiness within communities. The mechanism through which the slave trade influenced trust involves both internal and external channels. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel, which involves individuals' internal norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors like the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internalization of mistrust has significant implications for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) argues that the slave trades in Africa had an adverse effect on economic development, highlighting that these trades spanned nearly 500 years, far longer than the period of colonial rule. This extended duration suggests that the impacts of the slave trade on economic development are deeply ingrained and persistent. The legacy of mistrust created by the slave trade continues to affect contemporary economic and social outcomes, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that British colonial rule fostered educational development in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers, providing a rival explanation to the long-run economic growth of the region. The implementation of these institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, driving its development in the region (Frankema 2012, p.15). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, the educational development fostered during British colonial rule had a lasting impact on the economic growth of the region, as it provided the necessary human capital for industrialization and modernization.

Technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr both argue that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic growth, but they also acknowledge the necessity of institutional adjustments. Kuznets highlights that modern technology requires significant institutional and ideological adaptations, as it is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family structures. Mokyr further elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited. He emphasizes that institutional progress is essential to prevent the erosion of rent-seeking behaviors that could undermine economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets (1973, p.6) points out that the sovereign state, with its authority based on loyalty and a shared sense of community, plays a crucial role in resolving these growth-induced conflicts peacefully. This underscores the importance of political stability and institutional frameworks in supporting technological progress and, by extension, economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to provide a stable framework for economic activity. Acemoglu et al. show how the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This is consistent with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth because they create a stable framework for property rights and market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic growth. North further illustrates this with the example of the Bank of England, whose establishment led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). These findings emphasize that stable institutions are essential for fostering economic growth by providing a predictable environment for investment and economic activity. This is crucial because institutions that maximize long-term growth are often not extractive (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical context of institutional development provides further insight into their role in economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s thesis that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes highlight how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, which in turn influenced economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic trajectories. In this context, the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The prominence attached to the role of institutions enables the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. This is because 'good' institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shows that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has contemporary relevance.
