A central question in economic development is the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The stakes are high because understanding this role can inform policies aimed at fostering sustained economic development. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for economic growth. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors, and that geographic endowments or cultural factors may be more determinative. Still others propose that institutions themselves are shaped by broader historical and political contexts, making their impact on economic growth contingent on these contexts.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) argue that the type of institutions established during colonialism significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. They propose that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, hindered economic development in many colonies. Settler colonies, where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, experienced more robust economic growth. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of historical institutional choices on economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth by facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. He argues that the development of political institutions that specify and enforce efficient property rights is critical for economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) further elaborate on this by showing how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and economic growth. These scholars underscore the importance of credible commitments and effective enforcement mechanisms in sustaining economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They contend that Europeans implemented different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, leading to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality rates, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) document that these institutional differences persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This suggests that while initial institutional frameworks were crucial, subsequent economic policies and industrialization opportunities played a complementary role in shaping long-run economic growth.

This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories. This financial revolution reflects the increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets and economic growth. North (1989, p.9) argues that the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change was instrumental in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering economic growth. They argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment underpins the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. This suggests that while institutional changes were significant, the specific bureaucratic structures in place did not necessarily impede economic development.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that long-run economic growth is primarily driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological progress. Mokyr posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting that the accumulation of technological progress and knowledge is the key driver of economic development. Kuznets identifies the role of technological progress in driving economic growth, noting that technological changes require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. These adjustments are necessary to resolve conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies. For instance, Kuznets points out that the introduction of modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, requiring significant institutional changes to facilitate economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver, institutional adjustments are crucial for sustaining this growth. Mokyr, however, qualifies this by noting that institutional progress must complement technological progress to ensure the sustainability of economic improvement. He argues that without such institutional progress, economic gains could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, underscoring the importance of both technological and institutional advancements in driving long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments highlights the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors must work in tandem to achieve sustained development.

The slave trade has engendered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which has persisted and significantly impacted long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, stemming from the historical exploitation and violence of the slave trade, has eroded social cohesion and hindered the development of cooperative institutions essential for economic prosperity. The mistrust operates through both external and internal channels, with the internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as impactful as the external environment (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalized mistrust manifests in reduced trust levels among individuals and communities, creating barriers to economic cooperation and development. This enduring legacy of mistrust and economic underdevelopment underscores the profound and enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, showing how the legacy of mistrust can hinder the development of cooperative institutions essential for economic prosperity.

The literature on colonial education and African development highlights how colonial rule influenced institutional change in Africa, with Hopkins and Frankema noting that these changes were both negotiated and imposed (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that the type of colonial governance played a crucial role in the development of educational institutions, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. The demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). This highlights how colonial institutions, particularly those related to education, were negotiated by local actors, shaping the institutional landscape in ways that continue to influence contemporary African economies. The prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial education systems on African development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The economic performance of Africa and Asia, as revealed by Broadberry and Gardner, along with Broadberry and Gupta, underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of these regions' historical economic trajectories, challenging previous characterizations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that economic growth in both regions was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, driven by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. The role of the service sector in economic growth is crucial, as it facilitated merchant distribution and finance, thereby contributing to the productivity of the traded goods sector. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation underscores the complex interplay between economic factors and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe may be seen as arising through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled laborers. This uneven distribution of economic benefits highlights the importance of considering the distributional effects of economic growth when examining the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic performance.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They explore the determinants of real wage divergence and how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa. They seek to understand the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. Austin (2008, p.2) further emphasizes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security, and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that colonial policies could adapt to local conditions, influencing long-run economic outcomes. This indicates that the impact of colonial policies on economic growth was contingent on the presence of European settlers and their competition for local labor.

Colonial institutions' impact on long-run economic growth hinges on their nature and the local context in which they were implemented. North and Weingast show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Similarly, Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies where they went to settle (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin notes that colonial policies could adapt to local conditions, influencing long-run economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that the impact of colonial policies on economic growth was contingent on the presence of European settlers and their competition for local labor (Austin 2008, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by the interplay between political and economic factors. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). This is supported by Hopkins, who highlights that good institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and contributed to economic growth. The historical evidence from capital markets shows that institutional changes were pivotal in fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29).

The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).
