The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergent economic trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between those who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who propose alternative causal factors. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that govern economic and political interactions. The institutional perspective posits that well-designed institutions, such as those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for fostering investment, innovation, and overall economic development. This view is challenged by scholars who argue that other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural attributes, play a more decisive role in economic growth. The debate is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and these alternative factors, raising questions about the extent to which institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic development. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for policy, as it informs whether institutional reforms can catalyze economic growth or if other interventions are more effective. The central issue is whether the causal arrow runs from institutions to economic growth, or if other factors mediate this relationship. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutions established by European colonizers significantly shaped the long-run economic growth trajectories of their former colonies. The type of institutions set up by Europeans varied greatly depending on the mortality rates they faced in these regions. In colonies where Europeans experienced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions were not conducive to long-run economic growth, as they were primarily aimed at maximizing the rents for European colonists rather than fostering economic development. In contrast, in colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these colonial institutions into the post-colonial era has had a profound impact on the economic outcomes of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further substantiate this by showing that various measures of institutional quality, such as constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with current income per capita. The long-run effects of these institutions vary across former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) highlight that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This interaction suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, their impact was mediated by other factors, such as the ability to industrialize, which could either amplify or mitigate their effects on economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) highlight the pivotal role of financial institutions in driving long-run economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were crucial in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. This financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should be reflected in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP (North 1989, p.6). This perspective underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic growth, as the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange is critical. This commitment is essential for the development of markets and the enforcement of these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets and Mokyr share the view that economic growth is driven by technological progress and the spread of knowledge. Kuznets's notion of "epochal innovations" is consistent with Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth. These innovations are not merely technical advancements but also encompass institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of new technologies. Kuznets points out that these technological breakthroughs require societal adaptation, including changes in family structures and community values, to integrate new technologies without disrupting societal stability. Mokyr adds that institutions play a crucial role in reducing access costs to knowledge and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements. This perspective is echoed in Mokyr's observation that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements could be limited and eventually eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. However, Mokyr also acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress. This qualification suggests that while institutions are important, their role is more complex and less straightforward than that of technological advancements.

The complex interplay between exogenous factors and local initiatives is evident in the impact of colonial rule on African economic development, as discussed by Hopkins (2009, p.18), Frankema (2012, p.15), and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnic fragmentation but rather by the broader institutional framework. This perspective is supported by Frankema (2012, p.15), who shows that the demand for missionary education in Africa was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This suggests that colonial institutions, by introducing new frameworks, could catalyze local initiatives that drive institutional development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further illustrate this dynamic by noting that the growth in export production in African countries was tightly linked to political institutions, local endowments, and transport costs. However, the specific mechanisms by which these institutions interact with local initiatives remain complex and multifaceted. This complexity is underscored by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who highlights that while institutions matter for providing incentives for investment and development, the relationship is not straightforward. The interplay between exogenous factors introduced by colonial powers and endogenous local initiatives creates a nuanced picture of institutional change in Africa, where the outcomes are not merely a product of colonial imposition but a result of dynamic interactions.

This dynamic interaction is further illustrated by the findings of Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who show how institutions in societies with greater inequality have favored elites through various government policies. The institutional frameworks that emerged in New World societies were heavily influenced by the initial factor endowments, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor. This abundance led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset, setting the stage for institutional development that reinforced these inequalities. The specific focus on how these different environments led to societies with varying degrees of inequality is crucial. In particular, the extreme inequality in wealth and human capital created a political landscape where elites could secure policies that maintained their dominance. This dynamic is evident in the policies that emerged, which often restricted competition and limited the opportunities for broader economic development. For example, the lack of investment in public infrastructure and social capital, as well as the prevalence of corruption, were direct outcomes of these institutional frameworks. These policies not only sustained inequality but also hindered the overall economic growth of these societies. The persistence of poverty in Africa, as shown by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), suggests that structural impediments to growth may not be as pervasive as previously thought. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks interact with local initiatives and exogenous factors, such as those introduced by colonial powers.

The legacy of the slave trade, as argued by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2), created a lasting culture of mistrust within Africa, which significantly impedes contemporary economic development. This mistrust is rooted in the historical exploitation and betrayal experienced during the slave trade, which has left deep-seated psychological scars. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that this mistrust manifests in both internal norms and external environments, with the internal channel being at least twice as impactful as the external one. This internal mistrust erodes social capital, making it difficult for individuals to cooperate and engage in productive economic activities. Austin (2008, p.15) further illustrates that colonial administrations often responded to economic and political actions by indigenous populations, suggesting that the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers were not monolithic but adaptable to local contexts. This adaptability, however, did not mitigate the deep-seated mistrust fostered by the slave trade. The persistence of this mistrust highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the need for policies that address both the structural and psychological legacies of the slave trade.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme that emerges from the literature. Institutions matter because they shape economic outcomes by defining the rules that govern economic interactions and the enforcement of property rights. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that credible commitment underpins growth because it ensures that economic rules are enforced and can be changed only through agreed-upon processes. This credible commitment is crucial for fostering secure property rights, which in turn encourages investment and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often responded to economic and political actions by indigenous populations, suggesting that institutional frameworks were adaptable to local contexts. This adaptability, however, did not mitigate the deep-seated mistrust fostered by historical events such as the slave trade. The persistence of this mistrust highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes. The literature shows that the institutional legacies of colonialism, including extractive institutions, continue to influence economic development in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of extractive states in some colonies and institutions that enforced the rule of law in others. These institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita. The empirical evidence from capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.29) shows that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.
