Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the precise nature of this role remains a subject of intense debate. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as cultural norms or geographic conditions, are more influential. Some scholars emphasize the role of secure property rights and effective governance in fostering economic development, while others highlight the importance of cultural factors such as trust and social cohesion. Additionally, there is a rival perspective that suggests the influence of institutions on economic growth is overstated, and that other factors, such as natural resources or technological advancements, are more critical. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions in economic growth has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies. This literature review will explore the various positions on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. The debate is nuanced, but a common theme across the streams is the enduring impact of institutions on economic performance over the long term. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, contrasting with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and reflected increased security of property rights, both critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, which in turn stimulated economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further elucidate this mechanism by showing how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional setups. This institutional framework fostered economic development by providing a stable environment for investment and growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this pattern by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design, as institutions that benefit powerful groups may not necessarily drive economic growth.

This institutional evolution in England, as detailed by North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18), provides a historical context for understanding how secure property rights can foster economic growth. They emphasize that the success of economic growth in England hinged on the political factors underpinning the enforcement of economic rules, particularly the commitment of the regime to these rules. This institutional framework was crucial for providing a stable environment for investment and growth. The evidence from capital markets supports this claim, showing that the development of secure property rights was instrumental in driving economic growth. This institutional framework provided a stable environment for investment and growth, which was essential for economic development. This perspective is further supported by the new economic history of Africa, which highlights the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.8) notes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions in development policy widened the scope of development policy to incorporate governance. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the broader political and economic context in which institutions operate, as the role of institutions in economic growth can be influenced by various factors, including the political dynamics of the regime.

This perspective, as articulated by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49), underscores the fundamental role of knowledge accumulation in driving economic growth. This perspective highlights the role of technological advancements in shaping long-run economic growth. Technological progress, as Mokyr explains, is the mainspring of economic growth, driving societies forward through the accumulation of knowledge and innovations. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustained economic growth. For instance, Kuznets observes that the major breakthroughs in technological progress, termed "epochal innovations," have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world. This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is about providing incentives and adapting to and facilitating the efficient use of technological advancements. Mokyr notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This implies that while technological advancements are a primary driver of economic growth, institutions play a critical role in ensuring that these advancements are sustainable and beneficial in the long run.

This multifaceted impact of the slave trades, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2), highlights the enduring effects of historical injustices on economic development. The slave trades were a critical factor in shaping Africa’s economic trajectory, and the legacy of these trades persists through mechanisms such as a culture of mistrust. This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of the slave trade’s impact on the trustworthiness of others but is deeply rooted in the internal norms of mistrust that developed during this period. The persistence of this mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as it affects both interpersonal and institutional trust, which are crucial for economic growth. For example, trust is essential for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the adverse effects of the slave trades on economic development are multifaceted and deeply entrenched, impacting not only immediate economic conditions but also long-term institutional and social structures.

This re-evaluation by Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15), underscores the nuanced economic performance of African economies under colonial rule. This re-evaluation challenges the prevalent narrative that colonial rule uniformly impeded African economic development. A key mechanism through which this occurred was the development of formal education. Broadberry and Gardner note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation suggests that the impact of colonial rule on economic development depended on specific institutional and economic contexts. Frankema adds that the demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level and African families on the micro level (Frankema 2012, p.15). This indicates that the development of formal education was influenced by both top-down institutional changes and bottom-up socio-economic dynamics. The extent to which these educational developments translated into long-term economic growth remains a subject of ongoing debate.

This debate, as challenged by Austin, highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the historical processes that shaped African development trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share a view that colonial legacies have shaped African development trajectories in complex ways, not merely through a singular 'reversal of fortune' event. Colonial institutions influenced economic growth by shaping the incentives and opportunities available to African producers and colonial governments. Austin argues that in certain colonial contexts, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, particularly where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This dynamic created a scenario where colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism suggests that colonial institutions were not static but evolved in response to local conditions and actions, thereby influencing long-term economic development. Frankema and van Waijenburg caution that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty and the long-term effects of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the need for more detailed historical data to fully understand the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-term economic growth in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as they determine the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights, which were crucial for economic development. This is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework provided the foundation for sustained economic growth, demonstrating the profound impact of institutions on long-term economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic growth is also significant. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of extractive institutions in some colonies and growth-promoting institutions in others (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). They show that the institutions Europeans adopted varied significantly depending on local conditions, such as mortality rates. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic development of these colonies, highlighting the importance of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes. Austin supports this by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He argues that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, thereby changing their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This underscores the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to local conditions and actions.

The persistence of poverty in many former colonies can be attributed to the enduring effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was related to industrialization, and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes, as poor institutions hinder investment and economic growth. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He shows that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This highlights the need for institutional reforms that promote economic growth and reduce poverty in former colonies.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the variation in institutional design across colonies, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). This variation demonstrates that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions and actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth.
