The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is central to understanding the divergent economic trajectories of societies. The stakes are high: identifying the causal mechanisms by which institutions influence growth can inform policy, shed light on historical economic development, and address persistent global inequalities. Two competing perspectives dominate the debate. One perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth by providing secure property rights, enforcing contracts, and encouraging investment. These scholars argue that institutions that promote economic growth are those that ensure the security of property rights and provide effective enforcement mechanisms. Another perspective, however, posits that institutions are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, who may prioritize short-term gains over long-term growth. This view suggests that institutions designed to maximize rents for powerful groups may not necessarily promote economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency considerations or by the distributional conflicts among powerful groups. This tension raises the fundamental question of whether institutions that facilitate growth are a product of efficiency-driven processes or are shaped by the interests of powerful groups. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth requires examining both the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth and the factors that shape institutional development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) find that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Europeans adopted different colonization strategies based on mortality rates, setting up institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or created extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where mortality rates were low, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth, as the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity, ultimately shaping the economic trajectories of these colonies. The legacy of these institutions persists, influencing current economic outcomes in former colonies.

The creation of financial institutions and new financial instruments played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth in Europe. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) share the view that the development of institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs and fostered secure property rights. These institutions addressed the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War by trading certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights over time. This institutional evolution was crucial for the long-term capital market and economic growth. The Bank of England, established in 1694, marked a significant decline in transaction costs, facilitating market exchanges and economic growth. This financial revolution encompassed both economic rules and the political factors that ensured enforcement and change. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) emphasize the critical role of credible commitment by the regime or sovereign to these rules, which underpinned economic growth and market development. This perspective highlights the complexity of institutional impact, where financial institutions and secure property rights are necessary but not sufficient conditions for economic growth.

Technological and intellectual advancements, not institutional frameworks, are the primary drivers of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological progress and the spread of knowledge are fundamental to sustained economic improvement (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective emphasizes that technological changes are the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. The institutional and ideological adjustments that accompany these technological changes are crucial for the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the source of technological progress and the pace at which it advances differ over centuries and among regions of the world. This variation highlights the importance of contextual factors in shaping the impact of technological advancements on economic growth. The role of the sovereign state in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts is crucial for the sustainability of economic growth. This resolution is necessary to prevent excessive costs and long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. The growth of economic reasonableness, although not as monotonic and irreversible as technological progress, is essential for the long-term sustainability of economic growth. This underscores the necessity of both technological and institutional advancements for sustained economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) explore specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe, offering detailed explanations for historical economic phenomena. Colonial governments, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local incentives, leading to varied economic outcomes. For instance, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). Ogilvie (2007, p.3) highlights the role of institutions in economic history, advocating for their integration into long-term research programs. She posits that understanding the institutional underpinnings of economic phenomena requires a nuanced approach that considers the complex interplay of factors shaping economic outcomes. This nuanced approach is crucial for unpacking the institutional arrangements that drove economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. Austin further elaborates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which is relevant for understanding the specific institutional arrangements that drove economic growth in pre-industrial Europe. This shared finding underscores the importance of considering the specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe.

Similarly, colonial rule in Africa shaped institutional change through the negotiation and imposition of colonial policies (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights the shift in development policy towards 'governance', which brought institutions and organizations to the forefront of policy recommendations. This shift enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists, emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment and development. This suggests that the type of colonial administration played a significant role in shaping the institutional landscape, with British rule altering the perceived benefits of formal education. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) add that historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa has been relatively limited, which has constrained comparative historical work on economic growth and development. This limitation underscores the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the institutional changes driven by colonial policies.

Studies examining how institutional frameworks can perpetuate inequality further illustrate this complexity (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This mechanism is evident in the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The central role of men in this process highlights how institutional frameworks can entrench inequality by limiting access to productive resources. The implications of this entrenchment are significant, as they can hinder broader economic development by restricting the participation of marginalized groups. The persistence of these institutional frameworks, shaped by historical factors, underscores the need to consider the multifaceted role of institutions in both perpetuating inequality and influencing long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonization, where European settlers established distinct institutional frameworks based on mortality rates. Acemoglu et al. show that in regions with high mortality, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, whereas in areas with lower mortality, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had profound implications for economic development, with extractive institutions hindering growth and inclusive institutions fostering it. North builds on this by highlighting that the creation of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, both of which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutional legacies of colonization, therefore, persist in shaping contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on long-run growth.

The persistence of these institutional frameworks is further illustrated by the enduring effects of colonial policies on economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy is particularly salient in Africa, where historical exploitation has left lasting impacts on economic outcomes. Austin challenges the simplistic view of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a more dynamic interplay between institutions and economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interplay indicates that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures, underscoring their role in shaping long-run economic growth. The institutional legacies of historical exploitation, therefore, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic development over the long term.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the distributional conflicts that arise from their implementation. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the political and social dynamics that shape institutional design and implementation. Hopkins, building on this, shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach highlights the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic growth, as it can guide policy interventions aimed at promoting development and reducing poverty (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The shared finding is that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6; Austin 2008, p.2). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are still debated. Hopkins, however, demonstrates that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence of colonial policies and their enduring effects on economic development, as documented by Acemoglu et al., settles this disagreement by showing that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
