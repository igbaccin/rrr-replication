At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: on one hand, scholars argue that institutions are fundamental drivers of economic growth, shaping the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity. On the other hand, rival explanations emphasize other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, as primary determinants of economic outcomes. Institutions matter because they influence the structure of economic interactions, the distribution of resources, and the incentives for investment and innovation. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic development, as it informs strategies for institutional reform and economic policy. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal in shaping economic growth. North (1989, p.9) describes how the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This development is crucial for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. These contrasting institutional frameworks significantly influenced the economic trajectories of these regions. The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is further illustrated by the fact that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges leads to falling costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grows. This pattern underscores the critical role of institutions in fostering economic growth by reducing transaction costs and enhancing market efficiency. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) note that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups may not promote long-run economic growth. This qualification suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can vary based on the interests and power dynamics of the groups involved.

North and Weingast, alongside Sokoloff and Engerman, assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for securing property rights and catalyzing economic growth. They posit that the political commitment to rules and enforcement mechanisms was a critical factor in this institutional development. This political commitment was evident in the functioning of capital markets, where the credibility of the regime's adherence to rules fostered economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The institutional framework in England, which included secure property rights and effective enforcement, created an environment conducive to investment and market development. This was underscored by the development of financial institutions that supported long-term economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This pattern of institutional evolution contrasts with societies characterized by extreme inequality, where institutions often advantaged elites and restricted competition. In such societies, the institutional framework did not foster broad-based economic growth, as evidenced by the persistence of inequality and limited economic development in these regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This highlights that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not uniform and can vary based on the initial conditions and the interests of the dominant groups. The implication is that the initial conditions of a society, including its factor endowments and the distribution of power, play a crucial role in determining the trajectory of institutional development and, consequently, economic growth.

Mokyr and Kuznets both identify technological advancements as a primary driver of long-run economic growth. They both emphasize the role of technological progress in driving economic growth, suggesting that sustained economic development is closely linked to the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective is supported by Kuznets's observation that technological progress requires institutional adjustments to efficiently harness innovations. He notes that the pace and spread of technological changes, along with the institutional and ideological adjustments, varied across centuries and regions. This highlights the interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks, where the latter facilitates the efficient use of the former. Mokyr, meanwhile, identifies coordination failures as a potential obstacle to sustained economic improvement, suggesting that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological advancements and ensure long-term economic growth. He argues that without such institutional progress, economic gains could be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, underscoring the importance of coordinated efforts to sustain economic development. Kuznets elaborates on the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, emphasizing that the modern national state is crucial for peacefully managing such conflicts and ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. This points to a broader implication: the effectiveness of institutions in managing technological change and coordinating economic activities is pivotal for long-term economic growth.

The slave trades in Africa are argued to have had a detrimental impact on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to factors like colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn jointly posit that this detrimental impact is rooted in the development of a culture of mistrust within Africa. The slave trades fostered an environment of deep-seated mistrust, which persists to this day and has significantly impeded economic development. This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's effects on the trustworthiness of others but is also deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalized mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering economic growth and development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The effects of the slave trades extend beyond the historical period and continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Nunn highlights that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, far outlasts the period of official colonial rule, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trades has had a more enduring impact on Africa's economic development. The specific mechanisms through which this mistrust translates into economic underdevelopment need further investigation. The enduring impact of the slave trades on Africa's economic development underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of the historical factors that shape contemporary economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2); (Simson 2019, p.2) focus on specific aspects of African economies such as per capita income levels and post-independence budget policies, providing a nuanced view of economic performance. Their work highlights that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding is significant as it underscores the complexity of African economic histories, which are often oversimplified or overlooked in broader comparative historical studies. Broadberry and Gardner's emphasis on the availability of extensive economic data for both the colonial era and the post-independence period aligns with Simson's detailed analysis of post-independence budget policies in East Africa. Simson (2019, p.2; 2019, p.11) shows that the post-independence budget policies were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly portrayed, with military spending and debt burdens playing significant roles in national expenditures. This pattern of expenditure shifts, tied to regional instability and domestic dissent, suggests that the economic challenges faced by African countries were influenced by broader geopolitical factors. The role of institutions in shaping these economic outcomes is a critical area for further investigation.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that colonial rule in Africa was a complex interplay of negotiation and imposition, leading to institutional changes driven by both African initiatives and exogenous factors. This perspective is shared by Austin (2008, p.2) who shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic is evident in the fiscal policies of colonial governments, which often encouraged indigenous commodity production even in the absence of European settlers. This pattern of institutional adaptation suggests that the economic trajectories of African countries were influenced by a combination of local initiatives and broader colonial policies.
