The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate, and how the rules and norms governing social interactions can either facilitate or impede economic development. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. One stream argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective governance are essential for fostering investment and development. Another stream challenges this view, suggesting that institutions are not the sole determinants of growth and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs and historical paths, also play significant roles. A third stream proposes that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by other variables, such as the presence of European settlers and the strategies they adopted in colonized regions. The interplay between these positions shapes the current understanding of institutional economics and its implications for policy. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal for economic growth, distinguishing them from neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings. Institutions can significantly reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the example of the Bank of England, whose establishment and subsequent financial innovations lowered transaction costs and enhanced property rights security, both of which are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework enabled the development of a long-term capital market. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this by showing how different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional framework fostered growth by creating an environment conducive to long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The endurance of extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast illustrates how institutional design influences economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics behind institutional design, as they can have profound implications for economic outcomes.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for establishing secure property rights and fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated the growth of markets and capital accumulation. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further elaborate that the institutional differences across New World societies were shaped by the initial conditions of inequality. In these societies, the institutional frameworks that evolved favored elites, often restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the broader population. This pattern of institutional evolution highlights how the initial distribution of power and resources can shape long-term economic trajectories. The persistence of inequality in these societies underscores the importance of understanding how initial conditions and institutional design can interact to influence economic outcomes.

Ogilvie contends that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were formed by distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, and this is evident in the development of core economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds. The shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) that distributional conflicts are primary drivers of institutional design is further supported by the role of gender dynamics in shaping economic institutions. In pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women's access to land and economic opportunities was significantly constrained by patriarchal alliances and customary law. This codification of gender hierarchies under colonial rule reinforced economic inequalities, illustrating how distributional conflicts can shape institutional frameworks in ways that limit broader economic participation. The marginalization of women in access to land for cash crops, for example, highlights how institutional design can be influenced by power dynamics and distributional conflicts. This dynamic is further complicated by the role of colonial rule in codifying and reinforcing these inequalities through legal and customary frameworks. This pattern suggests that the initial conditions of inequality and the institutional frameworks that evolve from them can have long-lasting effects on economic trajectories, shaping who benefits from economic growth and who is left behind. This interplay between distributional conflicts and institutional design underscores the complex ways in which power dynamics can influence economic outcomes.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share this stance. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating technological progress. Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments can be seen as mechanisms through which technological progress translates into economic growth. For instance, the rise of the modern national state, with its authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are the primary driver, institutional frameworks are essential for harnessing their full economic potential.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share the finding that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on economic development, challenging views that attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. The slave trades not only decimated populations but also led to the destruction of social capital, which is critical for economic development. The slave trades disrupted the development of institutions that foster trust and cooperation, which are essential for economic growth. This disruption is exemplified by the lack of sustained trade in regions without centralized governments and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom (Nunn 2008, p.19). The absence of these institutions hindered the ability of African societies to engage in productive economic activities, further impeding their development. Nunn and Wantchekon emphasize that the slave trades' impact on trust is both an external environmental issue and an internal norm of mistrust, which has profound implications for economic development. This internal norm of mistrust, stemming from the slave trades, continues to affect economic outcomes by reducing the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative behaviors essential for economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both highlight that colonial rule shaped economic trajectories differently across continents, particularly in Africa. Colonial administrations often tailored their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. Austin (2008, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that in areas where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged the expansion of indigenous commodity production. This institutional adaptation was driven by the need for fiscal security and the desire to foster a material interest in the status quo among colonial subjects. This pattern shows that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions. The long-term effects of these adaptations are complex. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This implies that the institutional reforms implemented during colonial rule, while responsive to local conditions, may not have been sufficient to overcome deeper structural issues that continue to affect African economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita. This perspective is supported by North, who emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth, highlighting that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for reducing transaction costs and fostering growth (North 1989, p.6). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). Austin complements these insights by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that institutions were responsive to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The long-term effects of these adaptations are complex. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that equilibrium institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This suggests that while institutions were responsive to local conditions, they may not have been sufficient to overcome deeper structural issues that continue to affect African economic development.

The institutional framework of former European colonies highlights the complexities of long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This institutional perspective is further supported by North, who argues that the creation of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Moreover, Hopkins (2009, p.8) emphasizes that 'good' institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth are still debated. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This view contrasts with the belief that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development in Africa and other former colonies provides a crucial case for settling this disagreement. The measurement of income per capita and the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities offer empirical comparisons that highlight the institutional determinants of economic growth.
