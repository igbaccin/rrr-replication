The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the persistence of economic disparities across regions and over time. At stake is the fundamental issue of whether and how economic institutions can be designed to promote growth, or if they are merely epiphenomena of deeper economic forces. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms that link institutions to economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of growth, while others propose that institutions are shaped by other factors. A third group suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is mediated by other variables, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. The critical question is whether institutions can be designed to promote growth, or if they are merely epiphenomena of deeper economic forces. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the emergence of financial institutions and instruments in Europe played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, thereby driving long-term economic growth. This institutional development was crucial in enhancing the security of property rights, which in turn fostered a robust capital market and sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.9) posits that the political and economic institutions that evolved in response to fiscal crises, such as the Hundred Years' War, were instrumental in this process (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of these institutions led to a more efficient enforcement of property rights, which is reflected in the falling costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further emphasize that the political commitment to these rules was a critical factor in ensuring their effectiveness (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They also note that the success of these institutions is evident in the performance of capital markets, which suggests that the institutional changes were not merely revolutionary but foundational to economic development. This qualification underscores the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that while financial institutions and secure property rights are essential, they are not the only factors at play.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks, as they constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world. Kuznets (1973, p.1; 1973, p.6) supports this view, arguing that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Technological progress, in particular, has been a key driver of economic growth, necessitating adjustments in institutions and ideology to facilitate its integration into the economy. The growth of useful knowledge, therefore, is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This perspective implies that while institutions may facilitate or hinder the application of new knowledge, the underlying engine of economic growth is the expansion of useful knowledge itself. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further qualifies this by highlighting the importance of institutional progress to complement technological progress, suggesting that without such complementary progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Therefore, while technological advancements are fundamental, institutions play a crucial role in ensuring that these advancements translate into sustained economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the primary driver behind the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies was distributional conflicts rather than efficiency, highlighting the multifaceted nature of institutions. The distributional conflicts were rooted in the power dynamics between elites and commoners, which shaped institutions to preserve the interests of the powerful. In Europe, institutions like serfdom and guilds served to maintain the status quo and limit the upward mobility of lower classes. For example, craft and merchant guilds, which ostensibly aimed to regulate quality and prices, also restricted entry and competition, thus protecting the interests of established members. In New World societies, institutions similarly reflected the inequality of wealth, human capital, and political power. Sokoloff and Engerman describe how extreme inequality in these societies led to institutional arrangements that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. Ogilvie cautions that while institutions are pivotal, they are not the sole determinants of economic outcomes. She advocates for a holistic approach that considers institutions as part of a broader historical context, where cultural beliefs and political dynamics also play significant roles. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of institutions and their interactions with other societal factors.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This critique is crucial because it challenges the notion that institutional arrangements alone can explain long-run economic growth. Austin notes that economists often view the behavior of older industrial economies within the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experiences spanning centuries. The 'new institutionalist' economic historiography, associated with Douglass North, has struggled with quantitative testing and explaining why favorable institutions were adopted in some countries but not others. Austin's critique implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex than simply adopting favorable institutions.

Nunn (2008, p.2) examines the impact of Africa's slave trades on economic development, offering a different perspective on the role of institutions. Nunn argues that the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound adverse effect on Africa's economic development. This perspective highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is deeply rooted in historical events. Nunn's analysis suggests that the legacy of the slave trades created institutional barriers that hindered economic development, underscoring the enduring effects of historical institutional arrangements. Austin qualifies this perspective by noting that colonial administrations sometimes responded to indigenous economic and political actions, altering their institutional prescriptions. This dynamic suggests that while historical institutions shape long-run economic growth, they are not static and can evolve in response to local conditions. Austin's analysis implies that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their adaptability to changing circumstances and local contexts, which is a crucial factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

These scholars emphasize the importance of governance and policy recommendations in shaping long-run economic growth through the provision of incentives for investment. The mechanism through which institutions influence long-run economic growth is by providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development, and is thus a crucial factor in shaping long-run economic growth. This emphasis on the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. Their analysis suggests that a better grasp of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, will help us to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary, or a structural, effect on long-term African welfare development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this perspective by noting that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This dynamic suggests that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their adaptability to changing circumstances and local contexts, which is a crucial factor in shaping long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional legacy of colonialism has significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The nature of these institutions was shaped by the mortality rates of European colonizers, which in turn determined the type of colonial settlement and the institutions that were established. In regions where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. find that the variation in institutional quality across colonies, measured by constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, correlates strongly with current economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional framework, however, does not account for all variations in economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective underscores the importance of considering both institutional changes and the historical context in which they occurred.
