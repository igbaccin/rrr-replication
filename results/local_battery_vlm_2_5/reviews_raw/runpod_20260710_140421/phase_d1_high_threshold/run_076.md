The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the underlying mechanisms and the relative importance of institutions compared to other factors. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors that can influence development. One position holds that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they create a stable environment that encourages investment and innovation. This perspective emphasizes the importance of property rights, rule of law, and effective governance in fostering long-term economic development. Another position argues that institutions are just one piece of the puzzle, and that factors such as geography, culture, and natural resources can also play significant roles in determining economic outcomes. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one of many factors that can influence development. A related point of contention is the extent to which institutions are shaped by historical and cultural factors, and whether they can be easily changed or adapted to promote economic growth. This debate is important because it has implications for how policymakers approach economic development, and whether they focus on institutional reform or other strategies. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with different scholars emphasizing different aspects of this relationship. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) argue that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, depending on mortality rates, which had lasting effects on economic development. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) contends that institutions matter because they shape the transaction costs of economic activity, which in turn affects growth. This theme of institutions shaping economic outcomes through transaction costs and governance structures carries into the next section.

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, depending on mortality rates, which had lasting effects on economic development. The variation in institutional frameworks, from settler colonies with strong property rights and rule of law to extractive states built for swift resource transfer, influenced economic trajectories. The settlers’ strategies were not uniform; they established extractive states in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where the goal was rapid resource extraction for the benefit of the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. elaborate that these institutions were crafted to maximize rents for European colonists. The impact of these institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting the profound influence of colonial policies on long-term economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. Consequently, costs per transaction fell, and the size of the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GNP, driven by increasing specialization and division of labor (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions is evident in the striking indication from capital markets, where the commitment of the regime to these rules was critical for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This underscores the importance of credible commitment in fostering a stable environment for economic activity.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that different colonial powers shaped African education systems in distinctive ways. British rule facilitated the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa through the introduction of new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional framework fostered a demand for missionary education, which in turn drove the expansion of educational infrastructure (Frankema 2012, p.15). The economic implications of this educational development are significant, as Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) demonstrate that growing economic activity in many Anglophone African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and export markets. The timing and scale of this growth varied across countries, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation highlights the critical role that institutional factors played in shaping economic outcomes during the colonial period. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) further underscore this point by examining real wage levels and trends in British African colonies, revealing that economic development was not uniformly stunted by colonial rule. Instead, the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was complex and multifaceted, with some colonies experiencing significant economic progress despite the challenges of colonial rule. These findings suggest that the legacy of colonial institutions in Africa is more nuanced than often portrayed, with British rule in particular contributing to the development of formal education systems that had lasting economic impacts. The role of these institutions in fostering economic growth underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which colonial rule influenced African economic development.

Akyeampong and Fofack note that the contributions of African women to economic development were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and Hopkins adds that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). The role of women in economic development was intricately linked to the institutional changes brought about by colonial rule. Colonial powers often codified customary laws that marginalized women, reinforcing patriarchal structures and limiting women's access to productive resources. Hopkins underscores that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The institutional reforms that could have empowered women often faced obstacles from the same colonial structures aiming to modernize the economy. This paradox highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development, where the benefits of institutional reforms were unevenly distributed. Hopkins notes that the prominence of institutions in development policy enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of institutional changes is key to fostering long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This perspective suggests that the institutional changes brought about by colonialism were not uniformly beneficial or detrimental to economic growth. Instead, the impact of these changes varied significantly depending on local conditions and the specific institutions that were introduced or modified. For instance, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic development was dynamic and context-dependent (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further illustrated by Nunn (2008, p.2), who highlights that the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets in some African regions facilitated trade and economic activity, despite the broader adverse effects of the slave trades (Nunn 2008, p.19). This does not mean that all institutional changes were beneficial. Colonial institutions were not merely imposed but evolved in response to local conditions, which implies that understanding long-run economic growth requires a nuanced view of institutional changes that considers both the benefits and the costs.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both challenge the notion that institutions are the primary cause of economic development, suggesting instead that the way resources are distributed and the conflicts that arise from this distribution are more decisive. One specific mechanism through which distributional conflicts impact economic growth is the influence of elites on government policies. In societies with high levels of inequality, elites often use their power to shape policies that restrict competition and maintain their privileged status, thereby perpetuating inequality. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies discussed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4), where the initial abundance of land and natural resources led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. These inequalities, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved, creating a feedback loop that reinforced existing power structures and hindered economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) supports this perspective, arguing that institutions should be seen as tools for understanding economic history. By focusing on distributional conflicts, these scholars offer a nuanced view of economic development that highlights the complex interplay between power, resources, and institutions.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the distribution of resources, power, and opportunities within societies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive. These institutions, in turn, significantly impacted the economic trajectories of these regions. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of institutions that enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges is crucial for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the subsequent financial revolution in England illustrate how secure property rights and efficient transaction costs foster economic development. This perspective is further supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who highlights how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This adaptability in institutional design reflects the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in economic growth. In some cases, these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that institutions can be both enablers and constraints on economic development, depending on how they are designed and enforced.

The role of institutions in long-run economic growth is also influenced by distributional conflicts within societies. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who demonstrate that the quality of institutions, as measured by constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, and rule of law, significantly affects economic outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape institutional design and enforcement. The interplay between power, resources, and institutions creates a feedback loop that reinforces existing power structures and hinders economic growth. This perspective is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18), who argues that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This shows that societies can be ethnically homogeneous but still dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, regardless of ethnic or cultural factors. The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This shows that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants.
