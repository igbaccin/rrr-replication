The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive economic performance. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position holds that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights and enforceable contracts. This position is supported by scholars who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and the enforcement of property rights were pivotal in fostering economic growth. Another position contends that institutions are not the sole determinants of economic growth; instead, they interact with other factors, such as geography and culture, to shape economic outcomes. This perspective is supported by scholars who argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The stakes are high: if institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, then understanding their evolution and impact can provide valuable insights for policymakers seeking to promote economic development. If other factors play a more significant role, a more nuanced approach to economic policy is required. The debate highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between institutions, geography, and culture in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) share a central claim: institutions are pivotal for economic growth, as they shape the incentives and constraints that govern economic activity (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The specific mechanism through which institutions influence growth is by reducing transaction costs. This dynamic extends to broader economic and political frameworks that support market exchanges, not just financial institutions. As North (1989, p.9) argues, efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are critical for reducing transaction costs and promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this idea, noting that institutions designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups, such as European colonists, may not promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This qualification underscores that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on whether they are inclusive or extractive. Extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional choices on long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast, along with Austin, highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth are as important as the rules governing economic exchange, as well as the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). The institutional changes in England, including the development of secure property rights, were pivotal in driving economic growth. This suggests that while institutions are essential, their impact on economic growth is contingent on the specific historical and political context in which they operate. Austin's analysis of colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, highlights the role of indigenous economic and political action in shaping institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This indicates that the evolution of institutions is influenced by the actions and incentives of various stakeholders, not a unilateral process. North and Weingast's focus on the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules underscores the importance of credible commitment in institutional development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The implication is that for institutions to effectively promote economic growth, they must be robust and adaptable, capable of evolving in response to changing economic and political landscapes.

This perspective is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in New World societies led to institutions that advantaged elites. These institutions, in turn, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The implications of this distributional conflict perspective are significant. For example, the persistence of institutions that favor elites can hinder the emergence of more inclusive and growth-promoting institutions. This highlights that while distributional conflicts are a key factor, the broader economic context also influences the development of institutions. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intricately linked to the historical power dynamics and economic conditions of societies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the impact of British colonial rule on African education and economic performance was significant. This educational development was closely tied to economic performance, as the growth of export production, driven by foreign trade, varied based on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The specific nature of colonial governance under British rule thus played a significant role in shaping educational and economic outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. This highlights how the institutional framework established by colonial powers could either facilitate or hinder long-run economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr and Kuznets both emphasize the role of technological advancements in fostering economic development. Mokyr highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is pivotal for economic growth, suggesting that the accumulation of knowledge and technological innovations are essential for sustained economic improvement. Kuznets expands on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, thereby ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. Kuznets further elaborates that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, underlining the importance of political stability in harnessing technological progress for economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The role of the state in managing conflicts and fostering an environment conducive to technological progress illustrates how political institutions can facilitate economic growth, even if they are not the fundamental cause.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels, which are crucial for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon document that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms, not solely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others. The internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust significantly hinders economic development, as trust is a critical component for various economic activities. Akyeampong and Fofack further illustrate how the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities. This historical context of mistrust and marginalization has profound implications for contemporary economic development in Africa. The persistent culture of mistrust, exacerbated by historical injustices, creates a challenging environment for economic growth, as it undermines the social capital necessary for effective economic interactions and institutions.

Institutional reforms are pivotal for poverty reduction in Africa, as they can catalyse economic development by fostering good governance and providing incentives for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share this perspective. Institutional reforms can help African countries overcome the adverse effects of historical inequalities and underdevelopment. This is achieved through the establishment of effective governance structures that promote economic growth and reduce poverty. The new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes, has drawn attention to the importance of institutions in development, highlighting how 'good' institutions provide essential incentives for investment. This is crucial for development, as it creates a conducive environment for economic activities. The effectiveness of these institutions can be influenced by various factors, including the historical context and the specific needs of each country. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that African poverty can be addressed through targeted reforms that consider the unique historical and geographical conditions of each region, not solely a product of structural impediments. This underscores the importance of tailored institutional reforms that take into account the specific challenges and opportunities present in different African contexts.
