The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue among economists. The institutions-as-a-cause debate hinges on the extent to which institutions drive economic outcomes or vice versa. Economists such as Acemoglu et al. and North argue that institutions are pivotal in determining economic growth, while others, like Austin and Ogilvie, contend that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex and influenced by various factors. The stakes are high: establishing a clear causal link between institutions and economic growth would provide a robust framework for policy interventions aimed at fostering development. The debate continues regarding whether institutions drive economic growth primarily or if other factors, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts, are more influential. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is far from settled, with competing views on the causal mechanisms at play. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by fostering secure property rights. They observe that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, which is critical for economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth by providing a stable environment for market exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which shows that the regime was committed to enforcing the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment to institutional stability was a critical factor in economic growth, as it provided a predictable environment for investment and market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by noting that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the success of these institutions relies on the regime's commitment to these rules, underscoring the significance of credible commitment in maintaining economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are fundamental to long-run economic development. The mechanism through which this knowledge drives growth is by reducing transaction costs and improving the efficiency of production processes. This institutional progress is crucial for coordinating economic activities and preventing coordination failures that could undermine technological advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that the advancement of economic reasonableness, which supports institutional progress, does not follow a consistent or irreversible path like technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements can drive growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on the ability of institutions to adapt and evolve in response to technological changes. The role of institutions in influencing long-run economic growth involves more than just creating a stable investment environment; it also entails promoting the efficient use of technological advancements and coordinating economic activities to avoid coordination failures.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita by shaping the development paths of colonies through extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, intended to maximize rents for European colonists, had enduring effects on economic development. The persistence of these institutions, even after decolonization, has been a significant factor in the economic trajectories of former colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes, as former colonies struggle to overcome the constraints imposed by their colonial past. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic performance.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the posture that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. A key mechanism for this transformation is the development of formal education, which was shaped by colonial institutions. British rule, in particular, fostered a more conducive environment for formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. This institutional shift had a profound impact on the economic trajectory of the region, as formal education became a crucial factor in fostering long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that the growth in export production, closely linked to foreign trade, varied considerably between and within countries. This variation was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This underscores the complex interplay between institutional reforms and economic growth, highlighting the need for nuanced approaches to institutional development in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation through compressing different historical periods and paths, which contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s formulations (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis posits that the economic trajectories of former colonies were fundamentally shaped by their initial conditions, namely the presence or absence of European settlers. Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) challenge this view by demonstrating how the institution of slavery and its legacy influenced economic development in Africa. The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had profound effects on African societies, including the destruction of social and economic institutions, the depletion of human capital, and the disruption of long-term economic development. These historical dynamics were not merely the result of colonial rule but were deeply embedded in the pre-colonial period. The colonial governments' institutional prescriptions responded to indigenous economic and political action, shaping local commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) point out that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, indicating that the economic development of African colonies was influenced by a complex interplay of historical and geographical factors. This nuanced perspective underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic growth in Africa, considering both historical and contemporary factors.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and inequalities. These conflicts are deeply rooted in the historical and social contexts of societies. This perspective aligns with the idea that institutions are not merely efficient solutions but are also shaped by power dynamics and the interests of different social groups. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, the structure of serfdom and guilds can be understood as outcomes of struggles between different classes. The institutional structures in these societies were designed to protect the interests of the elite, ensuring that they maintained their privileged positions. This mechanism of institutional design is crucial for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The broader historical and geographical contexts qualify the downstream implications of these institutional designs. For example, the persistence of inequality in New World societies can be linked to the initial conditions of colonization, where the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor created a context of extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This initial inequality set the stage for the development of institutions that perpetuated these disparities, highlighting the enduring impact of historical factors on institutional development and economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to facilitate secure property rights and efficient market exchanges. Institutions matter in economic growth, with the development of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms driving economic progress. North argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are central to economic development. For example, Acemoglu et al. show that in colonies where Europeans settled and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was significantly higher (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the importance of institutional frameworks that support economic activity and investment.
