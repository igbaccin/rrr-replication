The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. What is at stake is a deeper understanding of why some regions flourish economically while others stagnate. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for investment and economic activity. Another position contends that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as population density, savings, or cultural beliefs, are more critical in determining economic outcomes. This disagreement highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions influence economic development over the long term. Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In settler colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, in colonies with high mortality rates, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional framework shaped by colonial policies created a path dependency that influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies.

The pivotal role of secure property rights in fostering economic growth, as highlighted by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9), underscores the importance of institutional changes in seventeenth-century England. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. North (1989, p.6) elaborates on this by explaining that political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should manifest in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction would fall, and the size of the transactions sector would grow as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This nuanced view underscores the importance of credible commitment in sustaining economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the evidence from capital markets, highlighting the critical role of political factors in underpinning economic growth. This institutional framework, with its emphasis on secure property rights and effective enforcement, laid the groundwork for sustained economic development in England.

The development of formal education systems under British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, as shown by Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), highlights the broader implications of colonial policies on economic growth. The demand for missionary education was driven by the perceived benefits of formal education, which were influenced by the introduction of new social and political institutions under British rule. This demand was shaped by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, highlighting the role of institutional changes in promoting education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The development of education systems under British rule was not uniform across sub-Saharan Africa; it varied based on local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the complex interplay between colonial institutions and local factors in shaping economic outcomes. The impact of these educational developments is evident in the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies, which achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further illustrate that the real wage divergence across British Africa provides insights into the long-term economic development of the region, suggesting that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon but rather a result of specific historical or geographical conditions (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends in British African colonies reveals the dynamic nature of economic development under colonial rule, emphasizing the importance of understanding the determinants of real wage divergence. This dynamic nature of economic development under colonial rule underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Distributional conflicts are a fundamental driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman show (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts among social groups, rather than efficiency considerations, shaped the development of institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions emerge primarily from efficiency-driven processes. Sokoloff and Engerman further elaborate on this by demonstrating how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. These policies, which often favored the elite, created institutional frameworks that hindered economic growth by limiting opportunities for the broader population. This dynamic highlights how the institutional landscape can be shaped by the power struggles and distributional conflicts within a society. However, the persistence of inequality is not solely determined by institutional design. Ogilvie notes that economic historians must consider the enduring influence of distributional conflicts on institutional development, which can either reinforce or challenge existing power structures. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which institutions emerge and evolve, as these contexts can significantly impact long-term economic growth.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize the importance of collaborative research and historical data analysis in measuring economic performance. They underscore the importance of accurate and detailed economic data for understanding long-term economic growth, a premise that can be extended to the study of institutions. Van Zanden highlights the role of institutions in guaranteeing the fair execution of contracts, particularly in the context of apprenticeship contracts in Western Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This perspective aligns with the broader notion that well-functioning institutions can facilitate the efficient transfer of skills and knowledge, which are crucial for economic development. However, van Zanden also acknowledges that these institutions, such as guilds, have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, which can have counterproductive effects on economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.19). This tension between the beneficial and detrimental effects of institutions is a nuanced aspect that economic historians must consider. Bolt and van Zanden emphasize the collaborative nature of research in economic history, noting that Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance has been pivotal in advancing the field (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). They argue that Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist has induced many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. This collaborative approach not only improves the accuracy of economic measurements but also enhances the understanding of the institutional frameworks that underpin economic growth. However, the subjective nature of estimating margins of error in historical data highlights the challenges in achieving precise measurements. This underscores the need for rigorous methodologies and continuous collaboration in economic history research to better understand the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both highlight how historical and contemporary factors shape economic outcomes in Africa, but they do not directly address the fundamental role of institutions in driving long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) observes that colonial governments often promoted indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, indicating that institutional prescriptions could shift in response to local economic and political actions. In such contexts, colonial administrators like Governor-General Clifford opted for higher production equilibria, illustrating how institutions can adapt to local incentives and economic dynamics (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation suggests that institutional reforms in Africa can be effective when they align with local economic interests, fostering growth by creating incentives for investment. Hopkins (2009, p.18) underscores the importance of 'good' institutions in providing these incentives, noting that they are crucial for development as they encourage investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). However, the effectiveness of these institutions is contingent on their ability to uphold the rule of law and develop progressively, as noted by Englebert (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This implies that while institutional reforms can drive economic development, their success is heavily dependent on the quality and adaptability of the institutions themselves, particularly in diverse and fragmented societies.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, reveal that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust in Africa continues to impact trust levels and long-run economic growth. This mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show, has a profound effect on economic development, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is not merely a product of the slave trade's effect on the trustworthiness of others, but rather a deep-seated cultural phenomenon. The persistence of this mistrust is further supported by Nunn, who argues that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is not just a historical artifact but a persistent cultural phenomenon that continues to shape economic outcomes. The slave trade's impact on economic development is not isolated to the direct effects of the trade itself but is intertwined with the broader cultural and institutional legacies it left behind.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with Pamuk, argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 was driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This divergence was not merely a result of later industrial developments but was rooted in early modern structural changes. One key mechanism underlying this divergence was the role of merchant distribution and finance. This productivity was not limited to agricultural investment but extended to the broader economic landscape, including the distribution of goods and services. The implications of this structural change are significant, as it suggests that the economic divergence was already well underway by the early modern period, challenging views that attribute this divergence solely to later industrial developments.

These structural changes were further reinforced by the institutional flexibility in regions like Holland, where new towns could spring up and rural manufacturing could flourish under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This flexibility allowed northwestern Europe to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, contrasting with regions like Tuscany, where institutional rigidity hindered economic growth. This institutional flexibility not only allowed for the rapid response to economic opportunities but also laid the groundwork for the long-term structural changes that would shape economic development in the region. Van Zanden qualifies this by noting that while economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, differences between English and Continental grain prices remained minor until about 1760 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This qualification suggests that while institutional flexibility and merchant distribution were key drivers of economic divergence, the impact of these factors on the standard of living was more nuanced and varied across regions.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Mokyr contends that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, highlighting how technological progress drives economic development. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This implies that while institutions may facilitate or hinder the diffusion of knowledge, the underlying engine of economic growth is the generation and application of new ideas. Mokyr further explains that coordination failures can impede economic improvement, underscoring the need for institutional progress to complement technological advancements. However, Kuznets qualifies this by emphasizing that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on the resolution of growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in managing conflicts and coordinating efforts, the fundamental driver of long-run economic growth remains the advancement of knowledge and technology.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the evolution of property rights and transaction costs. North shows that the development of efficient property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is because such institutions facilitate market exchanges, thereby reducing transaction costs and fostering a growing proportion of exchanges relative to GNP. Acemoglu et al. further elaborate on this by illustrating how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes, with some colonies establishing institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in determining long-term economic performance. For example, the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This historical evidence highlights that institutions designed to support economic activity can significantly influence long-run growth trajectories.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is also evident in the context of colonial policies and their enduring effects. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin's analysis, which suggests that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). These adaptations were crucial in shaping the economic outcomes of colonized regions. For instance, the fiscal security sought by colonial governments often influenced their policies, leading to higher production equilibriums in some cases. This dynamic shows that institutional changes, even within colonial contexts, can have lasting impacts on economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical evidence underscores the critical role of institutions in economic growth, demonstrating that institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder long-term economic performance.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is reinforced by Hopkins, who emphasizes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8) highlights that institutions that address distributional conflicts and provide incentives for investment are key drivers of economic growth. For example, the craft and merchant guilds in Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts and played a crucial role in the economic development of the region.
