The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement over the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing the importance of property rights, rule of law, and effective governance. Others contend that institutions are merely one piece of a larger puzzle, with factors such as geography, culture, and population density playing equally or more significant roles. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. The stakes are high, as the correct identification of the key drivers of economic growth can inform policies aimed at fostering sustained economic development. The central question is how institutions shape long-run economic growth and what mechanisms underlie this process. This question is particularly salient because the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different historical and geographical contexts. For example, some studies have shown that the extractive institutions established by European colonizers in high-mortality regions have persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This suggests that the type of institutions established in a region can have long-lasting effects on its economic trajectory. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they operate in conjunction with other factors remains a contentious issue. The debate highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was driven by the type of institutions Europeans established, which were shaped by mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. In colonies with low European mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, conducive to economic growth, were shaped by the settlers' intent to stay and develop the colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was also influenced by the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, highlighting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was interwoven with other historical and economic factors. The legacy of colonial institutions, therefore, continues to shape the economic outcomes of former colonies today.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) jointly argue that the rise of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. This institutional framework was essential for the emergence of a robust capital market, as it provided the necessary security for long-term investments. The political institutions that emerged from this process were crucial for specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction (North 1989, p.6). However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) qualify this by noting that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets, and that the political factors underpinning economic growth included not just the rules governing economic exchange but also the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and changed (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective highlights how the evolution of institutions in Europe played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, independent of colonial institutions.

This perspective challenges the notion that economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe were solely driven by efficiency considerations. She and Sokoloff and Engerman share the view that institutions are shaped by the underlying distribution of power and wealth in society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie shows that institutions like serfdom, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by the distributional conflicts of the time. This perspective challenges the view that institutions are always shaped by efficiency considerations. One mechanism through which this distributional conflict shaped institutions was through the influence of elites. Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that in New World societies, greater inequality led to institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the institutions that emerged in these societies were shaped by the power dynamics of the time. Ogilvie, however, qualifies this by noting that while efficiency may not have been the primary driver, it still played a role in shaping institutions (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This nuanced view highlights the complexity of institutional development, where both efficiency and distributional conflicts play a role. The implication is that institutions are influenced by the underlying power dynamics of society. This is a significant finding, as it suggests that institutional development is a complex process that is influenced by a variety of factors, including power dynamics.

McCloskey (2015, p.3) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 were ideas and ideologies, which facilitated technological advancements and institutional adjustments. This perspective posits that the ideological shift allowed for a more open exchange of ideas, fostering an environment where experimentation and innovation could thrive. Kuznets elaborates on the necessity of institutional and ideological adjustments for the efficient use of innovations, highlighting that technological progress required not just new ideas but also the socio-political framework to support them. This framework includes the sovereign state, which plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts without weakening the political fabric of society. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adjustments is thus a key mechanism through which ideas drive economic growth. Pamuk (2007, p.23) shows that institutional flexibility, particularly in regions like Holland, was also instrumental in responding rapidly to new economic opportunities. This indicates that while ideas were pivotal, the institutional environment that allowed for their implementation was equally important. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about creating a stable framework and being flexible enough to adapt to new ideas and technological changes. This adaptability is crucial for sustained economic growth, as it allows societies to leverage both new ideas and technological advancements effectively.

This impact was multifaceted, affecting economic structures and social and political dynamics. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, in turn, has had lasting effects on economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for productive economic activity. Nunn shows that the adverse effects of the slave trades on economic development were substantial, persisting long after the end of the slave trades themselves. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trades, including the erosion of trust and social capital, continues to influence economic outcomes in Africa. The implications of these findings are significant for understanding the role of historical factors in shaping contemporary economic development. While institutions are crucial for economic growth, the historical legacies of events like the slave trades can create persistent barriers that institutions alone may not overcome. The enduring impact of the slave trades on trust and social capital highlights the need for a comprehensive approach to economic development that addresses both institutional frameworks and the historical legacies that shape societal attitudes and behaviors. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) report, “Moreover, if we compare the point estimates to estimates using the same specification and sample, but not including the interethnic slave exports control (which are −0.104, −0.126 and −0.107, respectively), we find that the point estimates are barely affected by the inclusion of the control.” Nunn (2008, p.2) reports, “For a period of nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900, the African continent simultaneously experienced four slave trades.”

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical paths of institutional evolution. They jointly show that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, with institutional changes and economic developments occurring through complex, non-linear processes. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the high productivity of the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, as well as by production. This mechanism highlights the role of the service sector in economic growth during the early modern period, paralleling its role in the modern period. The implication is that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers enjoying a larger surplus over basic food needs than unskilled laborers. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production. This pattern suggests that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. This finding qualifies the notion that colonial institutions uniformly stifled economic growth in Africa, suggesting instead that local contexts and adaptive institutional changes played significant roles.

Colonial rule in Africa influenced institutional change through a complex interplay between African initiatives and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) jointly show how African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations that shaped the demand for missionary education, altering the perceived benefits of formal education. This dynamic highlights the role of local agency in shaping institutional outcomes during colonial rule. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) extend this by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies, revealing that African poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This finding underscores the importance of understanding long-term economic development and the distribution of income in assessing future growth possibilities. This interplay between local agency and exogenous factors shaped the institutional landscape of colonial Africa, influencing long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women in Africa played a pivotal role in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). Their contributions were significant despite the structural impediments they faced, notably the marginalization of women in access to land and the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law. This codification often relegated women to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This pattern of economic growth was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, as Broadberry and Gardner show. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The significance of women's contributions was often overlooked in historical accounts, which tended to focus on men's roles in cash crop production. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast illustrates how women's labor and agricultural practices were integral to the region's economic success, challenging views that marginalize their impact.

Van Zanden and others (van Zanden 2009, p.19; van Zanden 1999, p.1; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3) have focused on various aspects of economic performance and living standards in Europe without directly addressing how institutions influence long-run economic growth. The skill premium is determined by the costs of training, the chance that the apprentice will successfully finish their training period, the interest rate linking higher future earnings to the present, and the number of years the skilled worker can earn the higher wage (van Zanden 2009, p.3). The guilds in western Europe, for example, played a role in the intergenerational transfer of skills by guaranteeing the fair execution of the apprentice contract (van Zanden 2009, p.19). These institutions have also been criticized for monopolizing these skills and restricting entry into their trade, which could counterbalance their beneficial effects on skill development (van Zanden 2009, p.19). The focus of economic development shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, with Holland and England emerging as key players after 1700. This shift confirms the view that economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9).

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that the institutional dynamics of budget policies and land systems in Africa have been shaped by complex political and economic factors that often diverge from conventional narratives. Simson examines how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were influenced by both internal and external pressures. These policies were shaped by the need to respond to regional instability and growing domestic dissent. This is evident in the significant shifts in expenditure patterns, such as increased military spending and high debt burdens, which were necessitated by the new political and economic realities of independent states. Peters, on the other hand, highlights the underlying processes of exclusion and class formation within seemingly flexible land systems. These systems, often portrayed as negotiable and adaptable, actually conceal deeper structures of power and inequality. The flexibility of land systems can be a double-edged sword, allowing for both innovation and exploitation. For instance, the perception of 'custom' as static can be strategically used by certain groups to maintain control and obscure their actions from officials. This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of agricultural development, where the push for modernization often clashes with traditional landholding practices. This tension underscores the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes in Africa.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the development of property rights and the enforcement of contracts. Institutions that ensure secure property rights and effective contract enforcement can significantly reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This is particularly evident in the historical context of Europe, where the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This dynamic is not limited to Europe; the institutional frameworks established by European colonizers in various parts of the world also had profound impacts on economic outcomes. For instance, in colonies where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, economic development was more robust (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The impact of institutions on economic growth varies across different contexts. The nature of the institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly, leading to different economic outcomes. In colonies where extractive institutions were established, the primary goal was to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, which hindered long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions operate. The flexibility of land systems, for example, can allow for both innovation and exploitation, depending on the institutional framework in place (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This dynamic is particularly relevant in the context of agricultural development, where the push for modernization often clashes with traditional landholding practices.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the fact that institutions are often shaped by politically powerful groups. These groups may design institutions to maximize their own rents (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is evident in the context of colonialism, where institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. This is particularly evident in Africa, where the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement hinges on the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. The new economic history of Africa shows that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). However, the institutionalist approach emphasizes the political delivery of secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth, highlighting the importance of understanding the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions operate (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, settles this disagreement by showing that the long-run development of the former colonies was central to the reversal in relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
