The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the identification of causal mechanisms that can explain why some nations prosper while others stagnate. One position holds that institutions, by defining property rights and enforcement mechanisms, foster economic growth by incentivizing investment and innovation. This view contends that secure property rights and rule of law are essential for long-term economic development. A competing perspective suggests that institutions merely mirror deeper structural factors, such as geography or culture, with these underlying factors driving economic growth. A third stream of research focuses on the role of trust within societies, arguing that cultural norms and social capital are crucial for economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. The question of whether institutions shape long-term economic growth, or if they are shaped by other structural factors, remains unresolved. This question is fundamental to the broader debate on economic development, as it informs policy recommendations and theoretical frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Their research shows that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, long-run economic growth was robust. In contrast, in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, leading to stagnant growth. This pattern was driven by the extractive nature of institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than fostering long-run economic development. This dynamic illustrates how colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, prioritized short-term extraction over long-term economic development. A key implication of this is that the persistence of these extractive institutions can help explain the divergent economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, consistently align with the economic outcomes observed.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that economic growth in Europe, particularly in seventeenth-century England, was significantly driven by the creation of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments. These institutions reduced transaction costs, fostering a more efficient economic environment. These developments were not merely economic innovations but were deeply intertwined with political and institutional changes that ensured the security of property rights over time. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further elaborate that the political factors underpinning economic growth were crucial, emphasizing the commitment of the regime to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced costs per transaction, which in turn supported economic growth. This suggests that while institutional changes were critical, their impact varied depending on the specific political and economic contexts.

Mokyr argues that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). He argues that this accumulation is the primary driver of economic growth, not just a byproduct of technological advancements. This perspective is echoed by Kuznets, who highlights that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr further elaborates that the growth of useful knowledge requires institutional progress to complement technological advancements, thereby ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets supports this view by noting that modern economic growth is contingent upon the resolution of conflicts induced by technological changes, which in turn requires institutional and ideological adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological progress and institutional frameworks underscores the importance of a supportive institutional environment in harnessing the economic benefits of knowledge accumulation. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in facilitating economic growth driven by knowledge accumulation, their effectiveness can be influenced by various factors, including the political and social contexts in which they operate.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that British colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations influenced by these institutions, which reshaped the educational landscape. For instance, British rule facilitated the development of formal education systems that were more accessible and beneficial to local populations compared to other colonial regimes. This institutional framework influenced educational outcomes and had broader implications for economic development. Simson (2019, p.2) notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa experienced significant shifts in expenditure patterns, though they were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. For example, military spending increased in response to regional instability and domestic dissent, which could have diverted resources away from educational and developmental initiatives (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that while British colonial institutions laid a foundation for educational development, the post-colonial era presented new challenges that could affect the long-term benefits of these institutions.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have caused a culture of mistrust that persists today, which in turn affects long-run economic growth, as discussed by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2). This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others; it is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. This internalized mistrust can impede economic development by eroding social capital and hindering cooperation, which are crucial for economic growth. It is important to note that the slave trade is not the only factor in Africa's underdevelopment. Nunn argues that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, and that this effect is independent of other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. This suggests that while the slave trade played a significant role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, it is part of a complex web of historical factors that have influenced the continent's development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at reducing poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) contends that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation, suggesting that effective institutions can be developed regardless of ethnic diversity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective aligns with Frankema and van Waijenburg's (2012) assertion that African poverty results from historical and geographical conditions that can be addressed through targeted institutional reforms (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). However, Hopkins also highlights that the prominence of institutions in development policy has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists, underscoring the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment and fostering development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that while historical factors have played a role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory, the development of effective institutions can mitigate these factors and promote economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical development of property rights and secure governance. In seventeenth-century England, the evolution of institutions governing public choice fostered secure property rights, which were crucial for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Similarly, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in England led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutional developments show that effective governance and secure property rights are pivotal for long-term economic prosperity. This effect is not limited to European contexts; it also extends to the colonial experiences of former European colonies, where the establishment of extractive institutions significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, highlights their role in maximizing rents rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, whether extractive or growth-oriented, is central to understanding the economic trajectories of different regions.

The importance of institutions in economic development is further underscored by their role in providing incentives for investment and fostering development. The new economic history of Africa illustrates how "good" institutions are crucial for providing these incentives, which are essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective aligns with the findings that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6). The prominence of institutions in development policy has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists, highlighting the significance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence from capital markets in England and France shows that institutions per se, and the institutional changes they entail, were revolutionary in fostering economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence shows that institutions, whether in Europe or its colonies, play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. The internal norms of mistrust developed within Africa due to the slave trade also highlight the enduring impact of institutions on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel of mistrust, which is at least twice as large as the external channel, underscores the profound influence of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
