The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives sustained economic progress over centuries. The debate centres around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or technological advancements, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and ensure secure investment environments, are crucial for economic growth. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that historical and geographical factors are more determinative. This disagreement highlights the need to understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes over the long term. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, as they shape long-run economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Institutions matter for economic growth because they influence the costs of transacting and provide security for property rights. North argues that the development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. This financial revolution reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. The political model outlined by North suggests that the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, highlighting how political institutions can shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. build on this by showing that the institutions established by European colonizers varied widely. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in other cases, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutional differences had significant long-run effects on economic outcomes. This suggests that the type of institutions established during colonial periods can have enduring impacts on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. also emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This qualification highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, where institutions can sometimes hinder growth despite their role in shaping long-run economic trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that the development of these institutions was pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The credibility of commitments to property rights was a key factor in this institutional evolution. The Glorious Revolution of 1688, for instance, marked a significant shift in England's political landscape, establishing a constitutional monarchy that limited the power of the sovereign and enhanced the security of property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This political transformation fostered an environment where economic actors could engage in long-term investments with greater confidence, knowing that their property rights would be protected. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) build on this by noting that differences in factor endowments across New World societies led to varying degrees of inequality, which in turn shaped the institutions that evolved. These societies, characterized by extreme inequality from their outset, saw institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality. This perspective highlights how the initial conditions and institutional frameworks can have enduring effects on economic development, shaping not only the immediate economic outcomes but also the long-term trajectories of growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Nunn (2008, p.2) share a posture that distributional conflicts are a key driver of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and Africa. They argue that institutions are shaped by the struggles over resources and power among different groups, which can lead to the development of institutions that perpetuate inequality and hinder economic growth. The persistence of these institutions, Ogilvie argues, can be attributed to the ability of elites to maintain their power and influence. These slave trades disrupted social and economic structures, leading to the development of institutions that reinforced inequality and hindered economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) provides a complementary perspective by emphasizing the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. By focusing on the importance of "good" institutions, Hopkins underscores that the quality of institutions can significantly influence economic outcomes. These findings are qualified by the recognition that institutions alone do not determine economic development. The interplay between institutions and other factors, such as cultural beliefs and historical legacies, is crucial. This interplay suggests that while institutions are important, they must be understood within the broader context of a society's historical and cultural dynamics.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside Frankema, examine the economic performance and educational development in sub-Saharan Africa under colonial rule, focusing on specific outcomes like per capita income levels and formal education systems (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema 2012, p.15). Their findings reveal that the impact of colonial rule on African economies was not uniformly negative, as often presumed. Specifically, British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This suggests that the educational outcomes under colonial rule were influenced by the specific institutional frameworks implemented by different colonial powers. However, the economic performance of these regions was also tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports, which varied significantly due to factors like local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation highlights that while formal education systems were developing, the economic outcomes were influenced by a broader set of factors beyond institutional frameworks. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions of colonial impact when assessing long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Frankema and van Waijenburg challenge conventional views of African poverty, along with Austin (2008, p.2), (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They argue that the long-run economic growth of African economies cannot be attributed to a single factor like institutions but instead is influenced by colonial policies and labor dynamics. Colonial administrations' policies were often shaped by the economic and political actions of indigenous populations, which in turn influenced the institutional frameworks. For instance, Austin demonstrates that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic shows that the institutional frameworks evolved in response to local conditions and actions. The implications of this perspective are significant, as they suggest that the economic performance of African colonies was not merely a result of exogenous institutional factors but was also shaped by endogenous responses to colonial rule. This nuanced understanding of colonial policies and labor dynamics underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when assessing long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development. This perspective posits that technological progress is the mainspring of economic growth, driving innovation and productivity improvements that underpin sustained economic development. Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Kuznets elaborates that the institutional environment must adapt to accommodate technological changes, ensuring that conflicts arising from these changes are resolved peacefully. This dynamic is crucial for maintaining the political fabric of society and enabling modern economic growth. Mokyr further highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. He observes that the growth of economic reasonableness, which includes the development of institutions that support technological progress, is essential for long-term economic sustainability. Mokyr also acknowledges that this process is not monotonic or irreversible. The growth of useful knowledge, while fundamental, requires a supportive institutional environment to fully realize its potential. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is complex, with each influencing the other in a dynamic process that shapes long-run economic growth. The relationship between technological innovation and institutional frameworks is bidirectional, with technological progress driving the need for institutional change, and institutional adaptation facilitating further technological advancements. This interplay underscores the importance of considering both technological and institutional factors when assessing long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the dynamic interplay between institutional frameworks and economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks, with some colonies adopting institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others implemented extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) builds on this by emphasizing the critical role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, significantly lowered transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights.

The historical evidence shows that the specific institutional arrangements introduced by colonial powers had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism. These changes, which maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, highlight the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) provides a complementary perspective by showing that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, further emphasizing the adaptive nature of institutions and their role in shaping economic outcomes. The interaction between technological progress and institutional adaptation is complex, with each influencing the other in a dynamic process that shapes long-run economic growth. This process underscores the importance of considering both technological and institutional factors when assessing long-run economic growth.
