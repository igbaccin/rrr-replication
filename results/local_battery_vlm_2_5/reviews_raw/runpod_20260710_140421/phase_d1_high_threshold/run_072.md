The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history. The stakes are high, as the answer to this question can fundamentally alter our understanding of economic development and the appropriate policies for fostering it. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one factor among many. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth because they provide the necessary framework for secure property rights, enforceable contracts, and effective governance. This perspective emphasizes the role of institutions in reducing transaction costs and fostering investment. Others contend that institutions alone are insufficient to explain economic growth, pointing to the importance of geographical factors, cultural influences, and other exogenous variables. These scholars suggest that institutions are endogenous and shaped by these other factors. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for policy, as it determines whether institutional reforms should be the primary focus of economic development strategies. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). These institutions reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, fostering a stable environment for economic activity. The development of the Bank of England, for example, dramatically lowered the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security in property rights (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in this regard, as it provided a stable framework for economic development. The impact of these institutions varies.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This growth includes epochal innovations that constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further highlights that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary to sustain economic growth are equally important. Kuznets (1973, p.1) observes that technology and economic growth advance at different paces over centuries and among regions of the world (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This variation suggests that the institutional and ideological adjustments required for economic growth are not uniform across different contexts. Therefore, the effectiveness of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth may depend on the specific technological and intellectual advancements of a given region or time period.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subjected to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional development is central to understanding the variation in economic growth trajectories among former colonies. This focus on short-term extraction meant that even when institutions were in equilibrium, they were not conducive to economic development. The persistence of these institutions post-colonialism further cemented the economic disparities observed today. The role of these institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further supported by the finding that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the industrialization opportunities and the interaction between institutions and the capacity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic highlights how the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to influence economic development in former colonies.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Nunn, show that institutions shaped by greater inequality restricted competition and perpetuated disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). They argue that the initial factor endowments of New World societies, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality, in turn, influenced the development of institutions that favored elites and restricted competition. The resulting institutional frameworks were characterized by policies that advantaged the elite, such as those that protected their property rights and restricted access to education and economic opportunities for the broader population. These policies, which often included subsidies, favorable taxation, and limited competition, reinforced the existing power structures and perpetuated inequality over time. Nunn's examination of Africa's slave trades further illustrates the enduring effects of institutions on economic development. The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had profound and lasting impacts on Africa's economic and social institutions, shaping subsequent development trajectories in ways that are still evident today. For example, the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African societies hindered their ability to engage in sustained trade with Europeans, further limiting their economic potential. This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding how institutions shaped by inequality can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

These legacies are particularly evident in the educational systems and social structures that have been inherited from colonial rule. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that specific historical institutions, established during colonial rule, continue to impact African societies today. The British colonial administration, for instance, fostered a more conducive environment for formal education compared to other colonial powers. This was partly due to the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by cost-benefit analyses made by African political leaders and families. The implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, leading to increased investment in educational infrastructure (Frankema 2012, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This institutional framework fostered educational development and cultivated a culture of trust within African societies. The slave trade, however, introduced a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, affecting intergroup trust and economic development. The enduring influence of these historical institutions underscores the importance of understanding how past policies and practices continue to shape present-day African development, particularly in the realm of education and social trust.

Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, and this is shared by Broadberry and Gardner (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). They challenge the prevailing narrative of Africa's economic history by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding is supported by historical national accounting data, which reveals the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in economic growth patterns across different regions and time periods underscores the complexity of African economic history, which cannot be reduced to a single narrative of persistent poverty. Frankema and van Waijenburg's reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies further illuminates the dynamic nature of African economic development during the colonial period (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This nuanced view contrasts with recent literature emphasizing structural impediments to economic growth. This highlights the importance of considering both internal and external factors when analyzing African economic history.

This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of African economic history, one that acknowledges the diversity of experiences and trajectories across different regions and time periods. This thesis, shared by Austin and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18), contends that the thesis fails to account for the nuanced institutional changes and economic responses that occurred during different phases of colonial rule. For instance, colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional frameworks in response to local economic and political dynamics, as noted by Austin. Specifically, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments frequently encouraged indigenous commodity production, illustrating a more adaptive institutional approach than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis suggests (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability is crucial as it demonstrates that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, which in turn shaped economic outcomes. Hopkins emphasizes the broader role of institutional reforms in addressing poverty. The new economic history of Africa, Hopkins argues, can inform these reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This highlights the potential for institutional changes to drive economic development, even in the face of historical complexities (Hopkins 2009, p.8).
