The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental question: do institutions drive economic growth, or are they merely a byproduct of economic development? The central disagreement revolves around two competing views. One view posits that institutions are pivotal in fostering economic growth by establishing stable property rights and providing incentives for investment. The opposing view argues that institutions are secondary to other factors, such as geographic endowments or colonial policies, in determining long-term economic outcomes. This debate is crucial because it shapes our understanding of how economic development can be achieved and sustained. The conflict between these views underscores the need to examine the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. This requires a nuanced analysis of how different types of institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, affect economic performance over the long run. By unpacking the causal pathways between institutions and economic growth, scholars can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the drivers of economic development and inform policy recommendations aimed at fostering sustainable growth. This debate is essential for comprehending the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Their findings suggest that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high mortality rates, Europeans implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, while in areas with lower mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, in turn, shaped long-run economic outcomes by determining the extent to which resources were invested in productive activities versus being extracted for immediate gain. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering growth and institutions that enforce the rule of law promoting it. This dynamic is further illuminated by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced during colonialism, not geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy continues to influence economic performance, as evidenced by the enduring impact of colonial-era policies on current income per capita. The long-term effects of colonial institutions are thus a critical factor in understanding the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) posits that the development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and new financial instruments played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, fostering secure property rights, and driving long-run economic growth. This perspective is shared by North and Weingast (1989, p.29) who assert that the evolution of political institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, allowing for a growing proportion of GNP to be allocated to the transactions sector as specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The role of institutions in economic growth involves more than the rules governing economic exchange; it also involves the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment underpins the development of markets and economic growth, as evidenced by the success of England's political institutions in fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological progress can independently fuel long-run economic development without necessarily relying on institutional frameworks. The sources of technological progress, the sectors it most affected, and the pace of its advancement varied across centuries and regions, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. These adjustments are not always straightforward. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress can create conflicts within societies, particularly between traditional modes of life and modern technology. These conflicts must be resolved peacefully to sustain economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a significant driver of economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own. The institutional framework must evolve to support and sustain the benefits of technological advancements. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for long-run economic growth.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as critiqued by Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18), fails to capture the nuanced interplay of historical periods and paths that shaped African economic outcomes. These scholars argue that different historical trajectories have led to distinct institutional arrangements, which in turn have influenced economic growth. For instance, African colonies that did not experience European settler competition for labor often saw indigenous commodity production expand, driven by colonial governments' interests and institutional adaptations to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This dynamic highlights how institutional frameworks can evolve in response to local contexts, even under colonial rule. The role of institutions in African economic history is not monolithic; ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, while heterogeneous ones can uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This nuanced view underscores that institutional quality, not ethnic composition, is the critical factor in economic development. Therefore, understanding the specific historical and institutional contexts is essential for designing effective policies to foster economic growth in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa was particularly effective in fostering formal education compared to other colonial powers. This development was driven by the establishment of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which African political leaders and families then evaluated through cost-benefit calculations. The implementation of these institutions was not uniform across all colonial powers; British rule, in particular, created an environment where formal education became a valuable asset for local populations. This educational investment, in turn, laid the groundwork for broader economic development by enhancing human capital and creating a more educated workforce. The impact of British rule on African economies was not solely about education. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) show that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the benefits of British colonial rule extended beyond education to encompass broader economic growth. This suggests that the institutions established during British rule, which included educational systems, economic policies, and infrastructure development, played a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The legacy of the slave trade in Africa, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), includes a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts Africa's underdevelopment. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade's impact on intergroup trust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. They estimate that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the profound and lasting effects of the slave trade on social cohesion. Nunn (2008, p.2) further elaborates that the economic impact of Africa's slave trades was significant, shaping subsequent economic development over nearly 500 years. This historical context underscores the deep-seated nature of mistrust, which has impeded economic growth by fostering an environment where cooperation and trust are diminished. This mistrust, in turn, affects various aspects of economic development, including trade, political institutions, and firm management practices, thereby offering an alternative explanation to the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented in the literature. North and Weingast (1989, p.2; 1989, p.29) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is echoed by North (1989, p.6), who argues that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, and the subsequent falling costs per transaction, are critical for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and provided the necessary incentives for investment, which is essential for development (North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence from capital markets in England provides a striking indication of this institutional impact (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The long-run effects of institutions are further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), who argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of either extractive or inclusive institutions in colonies. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected current income per capita. The persistence of extractive institutions in some colonies, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth, highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who emphasize the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by Europeans, and the central role of institutions in the long-run development of former colonies.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the relative incomes among former European colonies reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, is a central theme in this literature. This thesis is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.15), who argue that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is a key point of disagreement, as it suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be more nuanced than initially thought.

The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the colonial policies that encouraged indigenous commodity production. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth, as it provides a clear example of how colonial policies influenced economic development in former colonies.
