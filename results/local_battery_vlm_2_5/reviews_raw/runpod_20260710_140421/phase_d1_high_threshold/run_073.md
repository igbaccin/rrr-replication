The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the role of institutions in long-run economic growth and those who propose alternative explanations. The first camp of scholars, such as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), argue that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for economic growth. They contend that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced current income per capita. North (1989, p.6; 1989, p.9) aligns with this perspective, asserting that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are critical for economic growth. A different group of scholars, including Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by merging different historical periods and paths. They suggest that the institutionalist approach, while valuable, may not fully explain why certain institutions were adopted in some countries but not in others. This disagreement highlights the complexity of understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of the institutionalist perspective and its rivals. The key question remains: how do institutions shape economic growth over the long run?

Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. They and Nunn and Wantchekon (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) show that European settlers implemented different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates and extractive potential. European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment in colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, whereas in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering long-term growth by maximizing rents for European colonists. The mechanism through which these institutions shaped economic outcomes involved the persistence of colonial-era policies and their interaction with local economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that these institutional frameworks were designed to extract resources efficiently. The implications of these findings are profound, as they suggest that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic trajectories in former colonies, influencing factors such as income per capita and industrialization.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, and new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove long-run economic growth. This was achieved through the creation of a long-term capital market, which was critical for economic growth itself. The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, significantly reduced transaction costs. This was because the Bank of England was a key institution that helped to increase the security of property rights over time, which was crucial for a long-term capital market and economic growth. The decline in transaction costs reflects the increased security of property rights. The emergence of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This is evident from the fact that the success of these institutions is remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This article focuses on the political factors underpinning economic growth and the development of markets, including the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts shape economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World. The institutional structure of a society is profoundly influenced by the distributional conflicts that arise from the prevailing economic and political conditions. For instance, in New World societies, institutions evolved to favour elites, who used their political power to implement policies that restricted competition and maintained their advantage (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This dynamic is evident in the persistence of inequality in these societies, where the institutions that emerged were designed to perpetuate the existing power structures. The persistence of inequality in these societies is a direct consequence of the institutions that were designed to perpetuate the existing power structures (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This suggests that the institutions governing economic exchange and enforcement are shaped by the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts within society. This perspective is supported by Ogilvie’s analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for key economic institutions such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The persistence of these institutions can be traced to the distributional conflicts that arose from the economic and political conditions of the time. This view challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency considerations. The underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts within society play a crucial role in shaping the institutions that govern economic exchange and enforcement.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) complements this view by highlighting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring that modern economic growth is possible. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further elaborates that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts. Mokyr (2005, p.49) adds that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, potentially eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is thus essential for long-term economic growth. The institutional environment must adapt to support the accumulation and application of useful knowledge, ensuring that technological progress translates into sustained economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) challenge the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by examining the African economic history. They argue that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The colonial administrations' influence on commodity production and economic policies played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. Austin shows that colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent to compete for African labour. This policy was driven by the colonial governments' interest in fiscal security and their desire to create a material interest in the status quo among their subjects. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered. This gap in data highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. The reconstruction of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies during their period of colonial rule is essential to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This historical perspective underscores the importance of institutional adaptations in response to economic and political actions, which is crucial for sustained economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) highlight that institutional changes under colonial rule in Africa influenced public goods provisioning and educational development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the way colonial powers introduced new social and political frameworks that altered the benefits of formal education. The demand for missionary education, for example, was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This institutional shift was pivotal in fostering new incentives for investment, which is crucial for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) complement this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the colonial institutional framework supported economic development in some regions. The timing and scale of economic growth varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The variability in economic outcomes across different regions underscores the complex interplay between exogenous factors and African initiatives in shaping long-term economic development. This variability suggests that while colonial institutions set the stage, local adaptations and responses were instrumental in determining the ultimate economic trajectory.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, posit that the African slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust that has persisted over centuries, significantly influencing economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, they argue, is not merely a response to the slave trade's external environment, such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others, but is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalized mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it erodes the social capital necessary for cooperation and collective action. Nunn further underscores the historical depth of the slave trade's impact, noting that Africa experienced four simultaneous slave trades over nearly 500 years, a duration far exceeding the period of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). This prolonged exposure to the slave trade has left an indelible mark on African societies, shaping economic institutions and attitudes in ways that continue to resonate today. Nunn cautions that the impact of the slave trade is not uniform across Africa. Local endowments, such as the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets, played a crucial role in determining a region's ability to support trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the enduring effects of colonial institutions, which persistently influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates, which have had lasting impacts on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions, through their role in specifying and enforcing property rights, are critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The institutional framework established during colonial rule, therefore, continues to shape economic trajectories in post-colonial societies. The legacy of colonial institutions is further evidenced by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Hopkins builds on this by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Hopkins 2009, p.18; North 1989, p.9) highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

The persistence of these institutions is not uniform across Africa. Local endowments, such as the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets, played a crucial role in determining a region's ability to support trade and economic development (Austin 2008, p.15). Austin argues that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability of institutions in response to local conditions is crucial for understanding the varied economic outcomes across different regions. Ogilvie underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that they should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This emphasis on the role of institutions in economic development is further supported by North, who argues that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights (North 1989, p.6). The historical evidence from the Bank of England shows that institutional changes can lead to significant economic growth.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement is the comparative analysis of colonial institutions and their long-run effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical analysis provides a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic trajectories. The measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic growth, as documented by Acemoglu et al. and North, settles the debate on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
