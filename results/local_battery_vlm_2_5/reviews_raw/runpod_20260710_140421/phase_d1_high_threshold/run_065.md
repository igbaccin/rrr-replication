The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. The stakes are high because if institutions are crucial, then understanding their dynamics is key to fostering growth. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the incentives for investment and growth. Others propose that institutions are merely one of many factors, with geography and cultural beliefs being equally or more important. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and the allocation of resources in development efforts. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can guide policies aimed at fostering sustainable development and reducing poverty. Therefore, the question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is pivotal for both theoretical and practical reasons. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. In colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for economic development. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies created barriers to investment and economic growth, while the inclusive institutions in low-mortality colonies fostered long-term economic prosperity. The impact of these institutions on economic growth is further qualified by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who show that the results hold across various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency. This consistency across different institutional metrics underscores the robustness of the findings and highlights the enduring influence of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes.

This development of financial institutions, as highlighted by North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9) and (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), was pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework created a conducive environment for economic exchanges, with the emergence of political institutions that specified and enforced "efficient" property rights (North 1989, p.6). The result was a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP, reflecting the increasing specialization and division of labor that multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This transformation was underpinned by a critical political factor: the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange and their enforcement (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes is qualified by evidence from capital markets, which suggests that economic performance in England and France was nearly at parity before the French Revolution. This finding implies that while institutions were important, they were not the sole determinant of economic growth, as other factors, such as bureaucratic structures, also played a role. The implications of these findings are profound, suggesting that while institutional development is crucial, it is not the only factor driving economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets and Mokyr both underscore the importance of technological progress as the mainspring of economic growth. Kuznets argues that technological progress is not only a driver of economic growth but also necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This is because technological progress and economic growth are intertwined and require a supportive institutional framework to resolve conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, and that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This is because technological progress requires a stable political and social environment to thrive, and without it, the benefits of technological progress can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors. However, Mokyr qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that while technological progress is a necessary condition for economic growth, it is not sufficient on its own. This implies that while technological progress is a crucial driver of economic growth, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustained economic improvement.

The adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development challenge the notion that institutions are the sole fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. This is shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) (Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade created a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust, in turn, has significant economic implications, as it undermines the social capital necessary for economic development. This is not merely a matter of institutions but a deep-rooted cultural shift that has persisted over centuries. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this by showing that trust is crucial for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. The slave trade's impact on trust, therefore, has far-reaching economic consequences. Nunn (2008, p.2) provides a historical context for this, noting that the slave trades in Africa lasted for nearly 500 years, a period significantly longer than the duration of official colonial rule. This long-term impact suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is a persistent factor shaping Africa's economic development. Nunn (2008, p.19) further qualifies this by highlighting that sustained trade in Africa required centralized governments and well-developed markets, institutions that were often disrupted by the slave trade. This disruption not only hindered economic growth but also created a legacy of mistrust that continues to affect economic development. This legacy underscores the complex interplay between historical events, cultural factors, and economic institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both examine specific institutional arrangements and their impacts within pre-industrial Europe, offering nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial governments, in the absence of European settlers competing for African labor, often encouraged indigenous commodity production to align with their fiscal interests. This dynamic illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, challenging simplistic views of institutional change. This perspective underscores that the adoption and evolution of institutions were influenced by the need to manage conflicts and maintain social order, rather than solely by efficiency considerations. This adaptive nature of institutions suggests that historical economic growth was shaped by a complex interplay of institutional responses to local conditions and broader economic forces.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and  Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) show that colonial institutions shaped educational development in sub-Saharan Africa by altering the perceived benefits of formal education. British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, driven by a complex interplay of social and political institutions that influenced both macro-level decisions by African political leaders and micro-level decisions by African families. These institutions altered the cost-benefit calculations of African families, making formal education more appealing. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) suggest that factor endowments, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, also played a role in shaping these institutions. However, the specific focus on how the extremely different environments in which the Europeans established their colonies may have led to societies with very different degrees of inequality, and on how these differences might have persisted over time and affected the course of development through their impact on the institutions that evolved. This dynamic highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional impacts on long-run economic growth, where the interplay of colonial institutions and local conditions shaped educational outcomes and, by extension, economic development.

Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2) explore how institutions shape African economic development without directly addressing the fundamental mechanisms by which they cause long-run growth. Hopkins emphasizes that while ethnic fragmentation does not inherently determine the quality of public goods, it is the nature of institutions that truly matters. This perspective is echoed by Simson, who highlights how post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as often portrayed. This suggests that the institutional frameworks in place significantly influence economic outcomes, even when confronted with challenges such as high debt burdens and military spending. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28; 2012, p.2) argue that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that the historical determinants of economic development are more nuanced than often assumed. This nuance is crucial because it implies that the long-term economic welfare of African nations can be influenced by targeted institutional reforms, which can address specific historical and geographical conditions that have shaped economic development thus far.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that historical economic performance in Africa and Europe reveals nuanced patterns of economic development that challenge simplistic narratives. This data reveals that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) complement this by showing that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800. This early divergence suggests that the institutional frameworks in Europe were already fostering economic growth long before the Industrial Revolution. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) further elaborate that economic growth in African economies was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. This indicates that African economies were integrated into global trade networks, which influenced their economic performance. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) argue that high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This implies that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, highlighting the importance of institutional support for trade and finance. This nuanced understanding of economic history suggests that targeted institutional reforms could address specific historical and geographical conditions that have shaped economic development in Africa and Europe.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by defining the rules and incentives that govern economic interactions. The extractive institutions established by European colonizers in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The institutional frameworks that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, differ from the extractive states set up in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This supports the broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the need for secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms (North 1989, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic development.
