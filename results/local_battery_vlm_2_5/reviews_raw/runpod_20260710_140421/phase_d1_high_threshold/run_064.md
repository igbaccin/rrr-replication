The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, can either propel or hinder economic growth. The live disagreement centers on the nature of this influence: is it direct, or mediated through other factors? One position holds that institutions directly cause economic growth, while another posits that institutions are merely a conduit for other underlying factors. The debate is crucial as it informs policy decisions and theoretical frameworks in economic development. The stakes are high: understanding the causal mechanisms can guide efforts to foster sustainable economic growth in developing economies. A related question is whether the influence of institutions is uniform across different historical and geographical contexts. If institutions indeed play a direct role, then policy interventions aimed at institutional reform could yield significant economic benefits. Building on this historical context, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional transformation was marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. These changes were instrumental in fostering a long-term capital market and stimulating economic growth. North (1989, p.6) further elaborates that as political institutions specified and enforced "efficient" property rights, the costs per transaction decreased, and the transactions sector grew as a proportion of GDP. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges, which were crucial for economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that the impact of these institutional changes can be significant. They note that recent research has challenged traditional interpretations by showing that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was not as poor as previously thought. This implies that while institutional changes in England were significant, they were not the sole determinants of economic growth. Instead, the interplay between institutions and other factors, such as the commitment of the regime to these rules, played a critical role in shaping economic outcomes. This nuanced view underscores the complex relationship between institutions and economic growth, suggesting that a multifaceted approach is necessary to fully understand the dynamics of long-run economic development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2) examine how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality contributed to the persistence of inequality. The institutional structures that emerged in these societies often favored elites, restricting competition and limiting economic mobility. This dynamic is evident in the policies and governance structures that evolved, which tended to entrench the power of the elite class. These institutions, shaped by the initial conditions of inequality, perpetuated a system where the wealthy and powerful maintained their advantage. This pattern is evident in the economic policies and social structures that developed in these societies, which often served to reinforce the existing power dynamics. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this by noting that the presence of a centralized government and well-developed markets can facilitate trade and economic growth. This suggests that while inequality can be a significant barrier to economic development, the presence of certain institutional structures can mitigate some of these negative effects.

Institutional reforms in Africa have been pivotal in shaping governance and economic policies, but their direct impact on long-run economic growth remains under-explored. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both highlight the significance of institutional changes in Africa, arguing that these reforms are crucial for understanding the continent's economic trajectory. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the broader institutional framework (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This suggests that even ethnically homogeneous societies can be dysfunctional if their institutions are weak, while ethnically diverse societies can thrive if they develop robust institutions However, Hopkins qualifies the impact of these institutional reforms by noting that the new economic history of Africa has gained traction because 'good' institutions provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary, their effectiveness in promoting long-run economic growth depends on their ability to foster a conducive environment for investment.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Akyeampong and Fofack, show that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This mistrust is a persistent social norm that continues to influence contemporary African societies. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and fostered a pervasive environment of mistrust, which has enduring effects on interpersonal interactions and societal structures. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that this mistrust is deeply ingrained, with internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internalization of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical component for fostering economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how the codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced patriarchal alliances, marginalizing women and exacerbating social divisions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This legal framework, coupled with the enduring mistrust, creates a complex web of social and economic challenges that hinder development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Austin (2008, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Austin 2008, p.2) assert that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions. These institutions, shaped by power struggles and the interests of dominant groups, were not merely products of efficiency-driven processes. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that institutions should be seen as indispensable instruments in economic history, and their analysis should go beyond the initial excitement of incorporating them into research. Distributional conflicts can be traced through the lens of the role of guilds in pre-industrial Europe, which were about power and control. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) illustrates how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of these institutions. This adaptability suggests that institutions evolve in response to the changing incentives and power dynamics within societies. This evolution of institutions in response to distributional conflicts has profound implications for understanding long-run economic growth, as it underscores the importance of considering power dynamics and the interests of dominant groups in shaping economic outcomes.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, a view shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This perspective is supported by Kuznets' assertion that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. Mokyr explains that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the driving force behind technological progress. This process involves the creation and dissemination of new ideas, innovations, and technologies that enhance productivity and economic efficiency. Kuznets adds that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations, suggesting that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional changes to fully realize its economic benefits. Mokyr elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, highlighting the interconnected nature of technological and institutional developments. Kuznets cautions that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is essential for modern economic growth. This implies that while technological progress is a key driver, the ability of societies to adapt and manage the social and political challenges that arise from technological change is also critical.
