The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the ability to explain why some regions have prospered while others have stagnated over centuries. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions designed to secure property rights and enforce contracts are crucial for economic development, while a rival position suggests that the role of institutions has been overstated, and that other factors, such as the actions of colonial powers or the influence of cultural beliefs, are more determinative. The disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-makers aiming to foster economic growth in developing regions. Institutions matter in economic growth because they shape the incentives for investment and innovation, which are essential for long-run development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with high mortality leading to extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Low mortality rates fostered the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment. This institutional divergence had profound effects on economic development. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic trajectories in former colonies, underscoring the importance of understanding historical institutional frameworks in long-run economic growth.

Institutional reforms are pivotal for African economic development, as they address governance issues and policy recommendations that can eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He posits that the quality of public goods provisioned by African governments is not necessarily tied to ethnic fragmentation. Hopkins's shared finding with Austin (2008, p.2) (Hopkins 2009, p.18) is that the quality of public goods provisioned by African governments is not necessarily tied to ethnic fragmentation. This insight is significant because it challenges the notion that ethnic diversity inherently hinders institutional development. Austin elaborates that colonial governments often encouraged commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives for indigenous economic activity. This mechanism shows that institutional prescriptions can adapt in response to local economic and political dynamics, which can inform effective policy reforms today. However, Austin observes that colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo. This condition suggests that institutional adaptations are possible, often driven by the need for stability and economic growth. This nuance is crucial for understanding the complexities of implementing institutional reforms in contemporary Africa.

North and Weingast, together with North (1989, p.9), demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional development reflected an increased security of property rights, which is critical for long-term capital markets. The political model underlying this transformation involved a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This trade underscores the interconnectedness of political and economic institutions in shaping economic outcomes. North and Weingast further argue that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, highlighting that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical factor in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). North (1989, p.9) notes that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should manifest in the development of economic institutions facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This pattern suggests that the institutional framework in England supported economic growth and adapted to changing economic conditions, ensuring sustained development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) show that historical factors such as inequality and the slave trade significantly influenced the development of institutions in different regions, shaping long-run economic growth. They argue that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power that characterized many New World societies from their inception led to the development of institutions that advantaged elites. This institutional framework restricted competition and hindered economic growth. This pattern of inequality was further exacerbated by the slave trade, which had immediate economic impacts and left lasting institutional legacies. The slave trade disrupted social and economic institutions, leading to a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) note, has significant implications for economic development, affecting trust in others and the rule of law. The institutional framework that emerged from these historical factors, characterized by inequality and mistrust, has been shown to have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms through which these institutional impacts manifest can vary. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade's effect on intergroup trust is not fully explained by the trustworthiness of others, suggesting that internal norms of mistrust play a significant role. This internal mistrust, in turn, can hinder economic development by reducing cooperation and investment in public goods.

Institutional and social factors in Africa significantly shaped economic development, with the role of women and the impact of colonial education systems being crucial. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share that various social and institutional factors influenced African development. The role of women in economic growth was particularly pronounced during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women significantly contributed to economic development, often relegated to marginal lands for food farming (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization was reinforced by colonial rule, which codified customary law to privilege men's access to land for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The impact of colonial education systems varied, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial education system, therefore, had a more profound impact on African development than other colonial powers.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental to long-run growth than institutional frameworks (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr's work highlights the role of technological progress in economic growth, asserting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements would be unsustainable. This perspective is supported by Kuznets, who argues that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Kuznets further notes that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. This implies that technological advancements are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth. Kuznets also recognizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that institutions are essential for sustaining economic growth. This nuanced view acknowledges the importance of institutions in managing the social and political challenges that arise from technological progress, thereby qualifying the role of technology as the sole driver of long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue, with various scholars emphasizing different aspects of institutional frameworks and their impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly influenced their long-term economic trajectories. The presence of extractive institutions, which prioritized resource extraction over local development, led to lower income per capita in the long run. Institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment fostered economic growth. This perspective is supported by North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth. These findings highlight the importance of institutional quality in determining economic outcomes.

The institutional framework's role in economic growth is further underscored by the work of Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who emphasizes the need for 'good' institutions that provide incentives for investment and development. Hopkins shows that effective institutions are crucial for delivering public goods and upholding the rule of law, which are essential for economic development. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) complements this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complexities of institutional evolution and economic growth. Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that institutions evolve in response to local conditions. This dynamic nature of institutions is crucial for understanding their role in long-run economic growth.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) provides a nuanced perspective on the role of institutions by arguing that distributional conflicts often drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie shows that institutions like serfdom, the community, and guilds were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution, which had significant implications for economic growth. This perspective is consistent with Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.6), who argue that equilibrium institutions are often designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) is that institutions are shaped by political and economic forces, and their impact on economic growth depends on how these forces interact. The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their long-term effects on economic development, as well as the empirical comparisons of institutional quality and economic outcomes, settles the disagreement by showing that institutions matter for economic growth.
