The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding the fundamental drivers of economic prosperity and the mechanisms through which societies can achieve sustained economic development. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social and economic interactions, are causal in economic growth. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in driving economic outcomes, arguing that secure property rights, effective governance, and stable legal frameworks are essential for fostering investment, innovation, and economic dynamism. Another position, however, challenges this view, proposing that factors such as geography, culture, and initial endowments play a more significant role in determining economic trajectories. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or merely one of many factors that interact in complex ways. The debate is further complicated by the question of how institutions themselves evolve and change over time, and whether they are shaped by exogenous factors or endogenous processes. The answer to this question has profound implications for policy, as it informs the strategies that governments and international organizations can employ to promote economic development. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The institutions established by European colonizers varied widely depending on the mortality rates they faced. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach prioritized immediate resource extraction over long-term economic development. In colonies where European mortality rates were low, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. The persistence of these colonial institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-oriented, were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional legacy has shaped the economic outcomes of former colonies, with extractive institutions often leading to lower economic growth and development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that the results are consistent across various measures of institutions, reinforcing the idea that the type of institutions established during colonialism has a profound impact on economic development. This institutional framework laid the groundwork for the economic disparities observed today, as former colonies with extractive institutions continue to grapple with the challenges of economic development.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the development of secure property rights in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering economic growth. They identify the establishment of the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments as critical factors in reducing transaction costs, thereby enhancing the security of property rights. As a result, costs per transaction will be falling; but the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges. This institutional framework not only reduced transaction costs but also fostered a long-term capital market, which was essential for sustained economic growth. This highlights the importance of political stability and the rule of law in sustaining economic growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) illustrate how institutional arrangements in New World societies favored elites, shaping development paths. The institutional frameworks in these societies, shaped by initial factor endowments, created significant inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. These inequalities were perpetuated through policies that restricted competition and benefited elites, such as land grants and monopolies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). The persistence of these inequalities had profound implications for long-run economic growth, as they influenced the evolution of institutions relevant to growth, such as property rights and financial sectors. Broadberry and Gardner note that these patterns of growth were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, highlighting the role of political institutions in shaping economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced growth vary across regions and time periods, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional dynamics. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is contingent on historical and contextual factors.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) contend that the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth in sub-Saharan Africa is contingent on the historical context and specific impacts of British rule on educational development. This mechanism highlights how colonial institutions shaped local demand for education, which in turn influenced long-run economic outcomes. However, the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty and the long-term effects of these institutional changes on economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This understanding is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have influenced long-term African welfare development.

The great divergence between Europe and Asia has been attributed to various factors, including economic development and living standards, with institutions often playing a secondary role in these analyses (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This suggests that service sectors, along with agricultural productivity, were crucial for economic growth during the early modern period. The shift in economic development to North Sea bordering countries, such as Holland and England, further illustrates how regional economic dynamics influenced the relative price levels of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries after 1740 (van Zanden 1999, p.9). However, the role of institutions in facilitating this shift remains less clear, as the focus has often been on economic and geographical factors. This obscures the potential for institutional changes to drive long-run economic growth, highlighting a gap in the understanding of the great divergence.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, offering nuanced explanations for historical phenomena. These institutional arrangements are not merely a byproduct of broader economic trends but actively influence economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political dynamics, illustrating how institutions evolved to promote commodity production and fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity of institutions suggests that they are not static but responsive to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, thereby influencing long-run economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborates on this by arguing that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These institutions, while not directly addressing long-run economic growth, reflect the underlying social and political structures that shape economic behavior and outcomes. This complexity underscores the need for a more nuanced approach to studying the role of institutions in long-run economic growth, one that considers the adaptive and responsive nature of institutions within specific historical contexts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade, through its direct impact on African economic development, offers an alternative explanation to those who attribute underdevelopment to institutions or more recent events. The slave trade, which spanned nearly 500 years, had profound implications for Africa's economic trajectory. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by eroding social capital and undermining cooperative behavior. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight that trust is crucial for economic development, international trade, and political institutions. The specific mechanism through which the slave trade's adverse effects manifest is complex. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with Europeans, suggesting that pre-existing institutional strength could mitigate some of the slave trade's negative impacts. This implies that while the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on African development, the presence of robust institutions could have buffered some of its detrimental effects.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. These epochal innovations have a profound impact on economic growth by altering the production landscape and fostering the development of new industries. Mokyr notes that the growth of useful knowledge is not merely a byproduct of economic growth but rather its mainspring, suggesting that the accumulation of knowledge is a necessary condition for sustained economic improvement. This process requires not only technological advancements but also institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize these innovations. Kuznets elaborates on this by highlighting that the pace of technological progress and economic growth varies across centuries and regions, underscoring the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating these advancements. Mokyr cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement could be limited. This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be accompanied by institutional developments to ensure long-term economic growth. Kuznets further emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that political stability and effective governance are essential for harnessing the benefits of technological progress. This interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is key to understanding the mechanisms behind long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that institutions designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are pivotal for economic development. They show that European colonies with extractive institutions experienced lower income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) further elaborates on the significance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms, which are critical for economic growth. The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments reflect increased security in property rights, which in turn facilitates long-term capital markets and economic growth. North's analysis underscores that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic development.

The historical context of institutional evolution provides additional insights into how institutions shape economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are central to understanding the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. They show that extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than long-run growth, were a key factor in this reversal. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, suggesting a more dynamic interaction between institutions and economic development. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15) is that institutional frameworks, whether extractive or growth-oriented, significantly influence long-term economic trajectories. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide further evidence that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Their analysis shows that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, highlighting the importance of credible commitment in economic growth.

The underlying agreement among these scholars is that institutions matter for long-run economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). Where they disagree is over the precise mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19; 2002, p.33) argue that the type of institutions established during colonialism, whether extractive or growth-oriented, is the key determinant. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) counters that the effectiveness of institutions in providing secure property rights and reducing transaction costs is crucial. The historical evidence of colonial institutional frameworks and their impact on income per capita, as measured by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), settles this disagreement. The empirical comparison of different colonial institutions shows that those designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment led to higher income per capita, while extractive institutions resulted in lower economic outcomes. This evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.
