The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, encompassing the rules and norms governing economic and political interactions, are widely acknowledged to influence economic outcomes over extended periods. This debate centers on the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence and the relative importance of different institutional factors. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic performance. Another perspective argues that institutions interact with other factors, such as geography and colonial history, to shape economic growth. The debate hinges on whether institutions operate independently or in conjunction with other variables, and whether their effects are direct or mediated through other mechanisms. This debate is crucial for understanding the enduring disparities in economic development across regions and for designing effective policies aimed at fostering growth. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they work in tandem with other factors, remains a contentious issue. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. The extractive institutions in former colonies limited the development of private property and the rule of law, which are crucial for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that even if institutions are in equilibrium, they may still be extractive and not encourage economic development. This is because politically powerful groups may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development. In the context of the development experience of the former colonies, this implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. The long-run economic growth in former colonies was significantly influenced by the colonial institutions established by European policies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of institutions is crucial for economic growth, focusing on the role of financial institutions and secure property rights. This financial revolution was pivotal in facilitating market exchanges and reducing costs per transaction, which in turn fueled economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions is evident in the robust performance of capital markets, underscoring the importance of credible commitments by the regime to enforce rules and facilitate economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). However, the extent to which these institutions drove growth can be qualified by evidence that France, despite having a different institutional framework, showed nearly equivalent economic performance before the French Revolution.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine how different historical periods and paths influenced economic outcomes. This literature argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the thesis simplifies the complex interplay of factors that shaped economic growth in Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) show that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors. This variation was driven by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, illustrating that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be mediated by these contextual factors. However, Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness in promoting economic growth can depend on how they are adapted to local conditions. The interplay between institutions and local factors highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is argued to be a significant factor in Africa's underdevelopment, rivaling the role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The impact of the slave trade on African development is multifaceted, with both internal and external channels at play. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the internal channel, which involves individuals' internal norms of mistrust, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn further elaborates that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, highlighting that for a period of nearly 500 years, the African continent experienced four simultaneous slave trades, which significantly outlasted the period of official colonial rule (Nunn 2008, p.2). The historical context of Africa's slave trades underscores the deep-seated nature of this mistrust. The Kongo kingdom, for instance, was able to support trade with the Europeans due to its centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks. This suggests that while the slave trade had a profound impact on African development, the presence of strong institutions could have mitigated some of its adverse effects (Nunn 2008, p.19).

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the finding that institutional reforms can provide a pathway out of poverty for Africa's bottom billion (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This is especially true when the reforms are designed to foster investment incentives. These incentives are crucial for development because they encourage private and public sector investment, which in turn can stimulate economic activity and create jobs (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa suggests that the state can play a pivotal role in this process by providing the necessary governance and institutions to support investment. This is particularly relevant for Africa, where historical and contemporary challenges have often hindered economic progress. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that while historical data on African GDP per capita is scarce, real wage levels and trends can offer insights into long-term economic development and the potential for future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the importance of historical context in understanding the current state of African economies and the potential for institutional reforms to drive growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that colonial powers had differential impacts on the development of formal education systems in Africa, with British rule being more conducive to educational development than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa depended on cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. These calculations were influenced by the new institutions introduced during colonial rule, which changed the benefits associated with formal education. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes under colonial rule could reshape educational outcomes, potentially influencing long-term economic development. The specific mechanisms through which these institutional changes translated into economic growth are not fully elucidated. This suggests that while colonial education policies may have had immediate effects on educational attainment, their long-term economic impacts would depend on the broader institutional framework that evolved. Thus, the role of colonial education policies in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a range of institutional factors that require further investigation.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly influenced by their ability to enforce secure property rights and facilitate market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly impacted the economic trajectory of colonies, with some fostering growth through secure property rights and others extracting resources with detrimental long-term effects. This institutional framework, as North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues, is critical for economic growth, as it reduces transaction costs and encourages investment. The development of institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms is essential for fostering a dynamic economy. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) further highlights that the path of institutional evolution is complex and context-dependent, influenced by both exogenous shocks and endogenous responses to local conditions. This complexity is evident in the varied institutional outcomes observed across different colonies, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic growth.

The reversal of fortune experienced by many former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced during colonial rule. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide evidence from capital markets, showing that the institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This highlights the importance of credible commitment in underpinning growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) argues, can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This synthesis shows that institutions matter for long-run economic growth, as they provide the necessary incentives for investment and development. The historical evidence of institutional changes in England and their impact on economic growth, as well as the varied institutional outcomes across former colonies, settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the type and effectiveness of institutions are critical determinants of long-run economic growth.
