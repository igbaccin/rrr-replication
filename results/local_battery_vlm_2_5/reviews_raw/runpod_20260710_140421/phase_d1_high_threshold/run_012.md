The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography or cultural traits, play a more decisive role. Some scholars argue that the nature of institutions, whether they are inclusive or extractive, determines economic outcomes. Others contend that the impact of institutions is secondary to geographic factors or initial endowments, or that institutions are themselves shaped by underlying economic conditions. Additionally, the influence of colonial institutions on long-term economic development remains a contentious issue, with debates on whether these institutions fostered or hindered growth. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex, involving multiple variables and historical contexts. This review explores the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, emphasizing the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on development trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North argues that specific institutional developments were crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9), (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and increased economic efficiency. The success of these institutional developments is evident in the capital markets, where the commitment to rules and their enforcement played a crucial role. However, North clarifies that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as initially thought, as France's economic performance before the French Revolution suggests that similar institutional frameworks could yield comparable results. This indicates that while institutional developments are critical, their impact can vary depending on the specific historical and political context.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Kuznets (1973, p.1) further notes that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge, those that constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods and spread to a substantial part of the world, may be termed epochal innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that the impact of technological progress on economic growth is profound and long-lasting. Kuznets (1973, p.1) also acknowledges that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and on a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such growth-induced conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is a key driver of economic growth, the role of institutions in managing the conflicts arising from such progress cannot be overlooked.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced. When mortality was high, settlers implemented extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction and transfer wealth back to the metropole. This institutional framework, characterized by weak property rights and limited economic opportunities, stunted long-term economic development. Where mortality rates were low, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for the economic trajectories of the colonies. The persistence of these institutions is due to their initial design and to the political and economic interests of the ruling elites. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is a persistent structural feature that continues to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. The institutional framework established during colonial times thus remains a critical determinant of economic growth in the post-colonial era.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the impact of inequality and the slave trade on economic development shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). These scholars demonstrate that societies with greater inequality, such as those in Latin America, developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This institutional bias emerged from the initial factor endowments and environmental conditions that characterized colonial societies, leading to stark differences in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional framework, which advantaged elites, had profound implications for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) also highlights that the impact of the slave trades was not uniform across Africa. Regions with more centralized governments and well-developed trading networks, such as the Kongo kingdom, were better positioned to support trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This suggests that while the slave trades had a broad adverse effect, local institutional variations moderated their impact.

Economic performance in Anglophone African economies was more robust than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2); (Frankema 2012, p.15) establish that many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a finding that runs counter to the narrative of broad underdevelopment. This higher income level was driven in part by the development of formal education systems, which were more robust under British rule. The development of these systems was influenced by the demand for missionary education, which was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This demand was shaped by the implementation of new social and political institutions under colonial rule, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). While these factors contributed to growth, they also created variability in economic performance across regions. This variability underscores the importance of local institutional contexts in shaping economic outcomes, even within the broader framework of colonial rule.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) share the finding that specific institutional reforms can be effective in reducing poverty in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. These institutions are crucial for creating an environment that encourages economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) support this view by highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa, which is essential for assessing the possibilities of future growth. This understanding can help identify the specific historical or geographical conditions that have affected long-term African welfare development. This indicates that institutional reforms tailored to the African context may be more effective in promoting economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) caution that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This highlights the need for more comprehensive data on African economic history to fully understand the impact of institutional reforms on long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the incentives and constraints that affect economic actors. The role of institutions in economic development is highlighted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), who demonstrate that different colonization strategies led to the establishment of diverse institutional frameworks. In colonies with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were imposed, which significantly impacted current income per capita. This underscores the enduring effects of institutional legacies on economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) further elaborates on the importance of institutions by arguing that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. The development of financial instruments and the decline in transaction costs, facilitated by institutions like the Bank of England, illustrate how institutional changes can drive economic growth.

The historical context of colonial rule in Africa provides further insights into the role of institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes often prioritized the extraction of resources rather than long-run economic development. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. The focus on 'good' institutions, which provide incentives for investment, is crucial for development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide additional evidence by highlighting the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which fostered secure property rights and economic growth. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets and the commitment to enforceable rules.

The role of British colonial rule in shaping African institutions is also significant. Frankema (2012, p.15; 2012, p.1) argues that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This suggests that the institutional legacies of colonial rule can have varying impacts on economic development, depending on the specific policies and practices implemented. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6) that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence growth, with some sources emphasizing the role of secure property rights and others highlighting the importance of extractive versus inclusive institutions. The historical evidence of colonial rule in Africa, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), shows that extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers had long-lasting negative effects on economic development.
