The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and secure contracts, are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth. This perspective emphasizes the importance of credible commitments and the rule of law in fostering an environment conducive to investment and innovation. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs and geographic determinants, play a more significant role. There is also a view that the reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors. The stakes are high, as understanding the true drivers of economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies. This debate underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes, as the complexity of these interactions can reveal the underlying mechanisms that drive growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional framework was particularly evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the introduction of new financial instruments, which collectively constituted the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). These financial innovations not only reduced transaction costs but also enhanced the security of property rights over time, a critical factor for both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. Moreover, the political institutions that emerged during this period were instrumental in specifying and enforcing property rights, further reinforcing economic development. The extent to which these institutional changes were revolutionary is debated. The implications of these findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth, even if the specific mechanisms and outcomes vary across different historical contexts.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, and efficiency considerations, were the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. This perspective challenges the conventional view that these institutions were shaped primarily by efficiency concerns. These institutions were the outcome of conflicts over resource distribution. The persistence of these institutions can be understood as a result of elite interests maintaining their control over resources and labor. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This pattern of institutional development highlights how distributional conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that prioritize the interests of the powerful over broader economic efficiency. The implications of these findings are qualified by the observation that the specific mechanisms through which distributional conflicts shape institutions can vary significantly across different historical and geographical contexts. For instance, the role of cultural beliefs in shaping institutions, as discussed by Ogilvie, adds another layer of complexity to the understanding of how distributional conflicts influence institutional development. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a crucial factor, they interact with other social and cultural factors to produce the institutional outcomes we observe.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the impact of colonialism, which introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either extractive or growth-promoting institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional divergence was driven by the differing interests and power dynamics of European colonizers, who established extractive states in regions with high mortality rates to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with the institutions set up in areas where Europeans settled, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where the rule of law and investment-friendly policies were implemented. The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on economic development, with extractive institutions hindering growth and investment. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further illustrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes, highlighting how the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity. The specific mechanisms through which these institutional changes occurred and their long-term effects can vary significantly across different colonial contexts. This variability suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, the interplay between institutional design and local conditions is complex and multifaceted.

The growth of useful knowledge, as highlighted by Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1), is a critical factor in economic growth that institutions must support. Mokyr's emphasis on the role of "useful knowledge" as the mainspring of economic growth is echoed by Kuznets' assertion that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth over long periods. The institutional adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of innovations are crucial for economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the pace of technological progress and the institutional changes required to harness it differ across regions and over time. Mokyr (2005, p.49) extends this idea by noting that without complementary institutional progress, technological advancements risk being undermined by rent-seeking behaviors. This perspective suggests that institutions are pivotal in enabling the efficient use of new technologies and the resolution of conflicts that arise from economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that economic growth and income levels in historical contexts challenge conventional narratives. This is evident in the economic performance of Anglophone African economies, which had per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, and in the great divergence between Europe and Asia, which was already underway before 1800. The key mechanism that explains this pattern is the role of foreign trade and export markets. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) document that the growth of export production in Anglophone African economies was tied to foreign trade, with the timing and scale of growth varying due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This highlights the importance of external markets in driving economic growth in these regions. However, Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) qualify this by noting that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, indicating that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This suggests that while external markets were important, the domestic institutional framework, including the role of merchants and financiers, was also critical in facilitating economic growth. Thus, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not only about managing conflicts and harnessing technology but also about creating an environment that supports trade and the service sector.

Long-term economic changes and structural impacts in Europe between 1500 and 1800 were marked by significant shifts in economic development and urbanization rates, with the North Sea countries emerging as economic leaders (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.23). The focus of economic development shifted to the countries bordering the North Sea, notably Holland and England, which experienced a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs. This shift was accompanied by a divergence in urbanization rates, with northwestern Europe, including Holland, becoming one of the most advanced, urbanized, and commercialized regions. This divergence was not solely due to later developments but was initiated by long-term structural changes set in motion by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Pamuk 2007, p.16). The institutional flexibility in Holland, where new towns could spring up and rural manufacturing could flourish under weak monopolies, played a crucial role in enabling this region to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. In contrast, the political authorities in Tuscany worked to strengthen the powers of existing monopolies, including their own, which hindered similar economic growth. This institutional flexibility in Holland highlights the importance of adaptable institutions in facilitating economic development and urbanization.

Institutional reforms are proposed as a means to eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, but they do not directly engage with the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty, emphasizing that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Together, these studies suggest that institutional reforms in Africa, informed by historical economic development, can drive growth by enhancing investment incentives. The extent to which these reforms can address the structural impediments that have historically affected African poverty remains uncertain.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon's findings indicate that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is deeply ingrained, affecting both internal norms and external environments. The authors demonstrate that this mistrust is rooted in a broader cultural shift that has persisted over centuries. This internalised mistrust, which Nunn and Wantchekon argue accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust, has profound implications for economic development. Austin further elaborates on this historical context, noting that the institutional evolution of African economies has been shaped by a complex interplay of colonial policies and indigenous economic actions. Colonial administrations often responded to local incentives, which in turn influenced the development of economic institutions. This dynamic highlights how the historical legacy of the slave trade, combined with colonial policies, has created a persistent culture of mistrust that continues to impact economic development in Africa.

North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories which emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. This perspective underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic growth.
