The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question that has sparked intense debate among scholars. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop and sustain economic prosperity over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture, in driving economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key determinants, emphasizing their ability to foster investment and economic development. Others contend that institutions, while important, are not the sole drivers and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and cultural beliefs also play significant roles. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutions are shaped by efficiency concerns or by distributional conflicts. This review examines these competing perspectives to shed light on the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The role of institutions in driving economic growth involves both the presence of good institutions and the specific types that foster long-term development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, facilitated a dramatic decline in transaction costs. This financial revolution was pivotal in securing property rights over time, which is critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. This process reflects a broader pattern where the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement mechanisms leads to falling costs per transaction, despite an increasing volume of market exchanges. This institutional evolution involves both economic rules and the political factors that govern how these rules are enforced and changed. Credible commitment by the regime or sovereign to these rules is a critical political factor in underpinning economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) illustrate this with evidence from capital markets, showing that the success of these institutions hinges on the degree to which the regime is committed to the rules governing economic exchange. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, as seen in the development of markets in seventeenth-century England. The implication is that the institutional changes were fundamental to economic performance.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These changes were driven by the differing strategies European colonizers employed in their colonies. In other instances, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks was influenced by the European mortality rates in these regions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic development of these regions. For example, the efficiency of the judiciary and the rule of law, as measured by various indices, show a strong correlation with the historical institutional frameworks set up by the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This institutional legacy continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

Distributional conflicts are a key factor in shaping economic institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) both show. These conflicts are often rooted in the unequal distribution of power and resources, which can lead to the creation of institutions that protect the interests of the elite at the expense of the broader population. This dynamic is evident in pre-industrial Europe, where serfdom and guilds were efficient economic structures and mechanisms for maintaining social and economic control. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) emphasizes that these institutions were shaped by the "shared beliefs" of society, which in turn influenced the economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) further illustrate this point by examining New World societies, where initial inequality in wealth and political power led to the development of institutions that perpetuated these disparities. This institutional framework maintained the status quo and stifled economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader participation and innovation. The persistence of these institutions highlights the enduring impact of distributional conflicts on economic development, as they create barriers that hinder the emergence of more inclusive and growth-oriented policies.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade in Africa had a profound adverse effect on economic development. They posit that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists and has a significant impact on economic outcomes. The slave trade led to a pervasive mistrust among African populations, which hinders economic development by reducing cooperation and social capital. Nunn (2008, p.2; 2008, p.19) further elaborates that the adverse effects of the slave trade have lasting consequences that shape economic development over centuries. This enduring impact is evident in the historical data, which shows that regions heavily affected by the slave trade exhibit lower levels of economic development compared to those less affected. These findings suggest that the legacy of the slave trade continues to influence economic outcomes, offering an alternative explanation for Africa's underdevelopment that competes with institutional theories. The internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the slave trade, create a barrier to the formation of institutions that support economic growth.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation and application of useful knowledge, not institutions (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This knowledge-driven growth requires continual technological innovation and institutional adjustments to harness these innovations effectively. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that technological progress necessitates significant institutional and ideological changes, as traditional societal structures may not align with modern technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advancements can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors, underscoring the importance of institutions in sustaining economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This nuance suggests that while institutions play a crucial role, their development is more complex and less predictable than the advancement of technology. The implication is that the interplay between technological innovation and institutional adaptation is dynamic and multifaceted, with institutions serving as both enablers and constraints on economic growth.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) demonstrate that the impact of colonial rule on African education and poverty varied significantly across different colonial powers. The British colonial administration, in particular, fostered the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa. This development was driven by a complex interplay of factors, including the demand for missionary education, which was influenced by the cost-benefit calculations of African political leaders and families. The benefits of formal education were closely tied to the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived advantages of education (Frankema 2012, p.15). These institutions created new opportunities and incentives for Africans to engage in formal education, thereby contributing to its growth. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates means that the persistence of poverty remains an open question (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This underscores the need for further research to fully understand the long-term economic impacts of colonial education policies and their role in shaping contemporary African economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Simson, challenge conventional narratives about African economic performance by revisiting historical data and policy assessments (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Simson 2019, p.2). They argue that the economic trajectory of Africa has been misrepresented, with many Anglophone African economies achieving levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective is supported by recent advancements in African economic history, which have uncovered extensive economic data for both the colonial era and the post-independence period. Broadberry and Gardner highlight the availability of this data, which has been crucial in demonstrating the region's economic performance during these periods. Simson's analysis of post-independence budget policies in East Africa further complicates this narrative by showing that these policies were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly believed. Simson notes that the "neopatrimonial" tendencies in Africa, which involve treating government resources as private property and using them to buy political loyalty, have been overemphasized in the literature. This is a direct challenge to the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services. Simson also acknowledges that significant shifts in post-colonial expenditure occurred, particularly in military spending, which grew in response to regional instability and domestic dissent. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between economic policies and broader socio-political dynamics in shaping Africa's economic trajectory.

Austin emphasizes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that African economies were well-endowed in 1500 but diverged due to colonial institutions, simplifies the complex historical trajectories of African development (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective is crucial because it challenges the assumption that colonial institutions were uniformly extractive and detrimental to economic growth. Austin highlights that in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could align with their fiscal interests (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This dynamic illustrates that colonial policies adapted to local economic and political conditions. Hopkins cautions that while good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, the relationship between institutions and economic development is complex. Hopkins points out that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation, suggesting that institutional effectiveness is influenced by a variety of factors beyond ethnic diversity. Therefore, while colonial administrations and indigenous commodity production played significant roles in shaping African development paths, the complexity of these interactions underscores the need for nuanced historical analysis.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonial administrations and their impact on African development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the institutions established by European colonizers were critical in determining the economic trajectories of their colonies. The settlers implemented institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, influenced by mortality rates and settlement patterns, persists and significantly affects current economic outcomes. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional evolution reflects increased security of property rights, a dimension crucial for economic development.

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting the complexity of historical periods and paths. He argues that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and that these administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation shows that colonial institutions were responsive to local conditions, influencing long-run economic growth through the incentives they created. Hopkins builds on this by emphasizing that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation. Instead, the effectiveness of institutions is influenced by a variety of factors, including the responsiveness of colonial administrations to local economic conditions (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Austin 2008, p.15) underscores the multifaceted nature of institutional influence on economic development.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency. She contends that these institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, which often prioritized rent extraction over long-term economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) suggests that the design of institutions is heavily influenced by the interests of those in power, which can have lasting effects on economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of institutions is primarily determined by the interests of powerful groups or by the responsiveness of colonial administrations to local conditions. The historical evidence of colonial administrations adapting their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action shows that the impact of institutions is shaped by both the interests of powerful groups and the responsiveness to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This adaptation, in turn, influences long-run economic growth through the incentives it creates.
