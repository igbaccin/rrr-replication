The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. The stakes are high: understanding the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth can inform policy decisions and economic strategies. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One key position is that institutions matter because they shape incentives and constraints that affect economic behavior. This perspective is supported by scholars who argue that the quality of institutions, such as property rights and rule of law, directly influences economic outcomes. Conversely, other scholars contend that institutions are not the sole determinants of economic growth and that factors like geography and initial conditions also play crucial roles. The debate underscores the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other variables to drive long-run growth. This interaction is evident in the varying impacts of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This emphasis on formal institutions as economic drivers is echoed by van Zanden (2009, p.19), who highlights that guilds were crucial for ensuring fair execution of apprentice contracts, thereby influencing economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.19; Pamuk 2007, p.23). In Western Europe, guilds played a pivotal role in the transfer of skills across generations. These institutions addressed the challenges of ensuring that apprentice contracts were executed fairly, which was essential for maintaining the quality and integrity of skilled labor. The guilds' role in economic development has been reassessed by recent authors who studied their functioning in detail. While guilds have been criticized for monopolizing skills and restricting entry into trades, their beneficial effects on intergenerational skill transfer were significant. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) builds on this institutional framework by examining the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800. He argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development, as measured by urbanization, and changes in the standard of living during this period. This finding suggests that institutional factors, such as the presence of guilds, influenced economic outcomes independently of broader economic trends. Pamuk (2007, p.23) further elaborates on this institutional perspective by highlighting the role of institutional flexibility in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. The unusual degree of institutional flexibility in Holland allowed for the rapid development of new towns and the flourishing of rural manufacturing under weak monopolies. This institutional environment contrasts with the stronger monopolies and political authorities in regions like Tuscany, where economic stagnation was more pronounced.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) assert that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting the increased security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was a product of fiscal crises, notably the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. The political institutions that specified and enforced "efficient" property rights facilitated market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This institutional environment fostered increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6) further argue that the development of these economic institutions was crucial for market exchanges, leading to a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP. This institutional framework was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that distributional conflicts better explain economic institutions than efficiency considerations. This perspective is supported by Peters' (Peters 2004, p.5) analysis of land systems in Africa, where the push for agricultural development was driven by perceptions of African landholding practices as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment. Ogilvie and Peters both highlight that these distributional conflicts shaped economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and colonial Africa. Ogilvie's analysis of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild in pre-industrial Europe shows that these institutions were not merely efficient responses to economic conditions but were deeply rooted in power struggles and the distribution of resources. This approach challenges the notion that economic institutions are primarily driven by efficiency, instead emphasizing the role of political and social conflicts. Peters' work on Africa illustrates that the flexibility and contingency of land systems can be seen as a means of exclusion and class formation. This nuanced view suggests that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific outcomes of these conflicts can vary significantly across different contexts. Peters' analysis indicates that the perception of 'custom' as static can advantage groups seeking to keep officials ignorant of their activities. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how power and resource distribution shape economic institutions, and how these institutions, in turn, influence long-run economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust that endures to this day, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, establish that the slave trade's adverse effects on economic development are not merely a product of more recent historical factors. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust manifests in both external and internal channels, with the internal norms of mistrust being at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Nunn shows that this mistrust is deeply ingrained, having persisted for nearly 500 years. Nunn's examination of the Kongo kingdom illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets facilitated trade, suggesting that the absence of such institutions in other regions may have exacerbated the negative effects of the slave trade. This highlights how the slave trade's impact on institutions and governance structures has had lasting implications for economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Simson (2019, p.2) share that economic performance in Africa is shaped by the interplay of colonial legacies and post-independence policies. The colonial period saw varied institutional developments: British rule, for instance, encouraged formal education in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15). This educational foundation was not merely a byproduct of colonial rule but a strategic investment, as it aligned with the broader political and economic interests of the colonial administrations. Colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive capacity of colonial institutions highlights how external forces could shape local economic trajectories. However, the post-independence era brought new challenges, including shifts in budget policies and military spending, which had profound effects on economic development. This nuance is further illuminated by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2), who argue that many Anglophone African economies had per capita incomes above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the economic performance of these regions was more robust than previously thought (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This challenges the notion that colonial rule uniformly stunted economic development. The economic performance of Africa, therefore, is a complex interplay of colonial legacies, post-independence policies, and the adaptive capacity of institutions, all of which have shaped long-run economic growth in varied and sometimes unexpected ways.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the institutional legacies of colonialism played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. The differing colonization strategies employed by European powers resulted in varied institutional frameworks, which in turn influenced economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) highlight that where European settlers faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, which prioritized the extraction of resources over long-term economic development, persisted even after independence and significantly impacted current income per capita. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where extractive institutions stifled economic growth. This institutional design, shaped by the interests of the colonizers, had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. The persistence of these extractive institutions, even after the end of colonial rule, underscores the deep-seated impact of colonial legacies on economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) both emphasize that specific institutional reforms in Africa are crucial for poverty reduction, but do not directly address long-run economic growth. They argue that the focus on institutional reforms should be more nuanced, highlighting the importance of understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes. Hopkins notes that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not solely determined by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. This suggests that institutional quality can vary independently of ethnic diversity, and that effective institutions can be developed even in heterogeneous societies. This is significant because it implies that the design of institutions can be tailored to specific contexts, potentially leading to more effective poverty reduction strategies. Frankema and van Waijenburg point out that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa leaves many questions unanswered about the persistence of poverty. This gap in historical data underscores the need for a deeper understanding of long-term economic development in Africa. The historical perspective provided by Frankema and van Waijenburg suggests that while institutional reforms are necessary, they are not sufficient on their own to ensure long-run economic growth. The complex interplay between institutions, economic outcomes, and historical context must be carefully considered. This complexity is further highlighted by the fact that the effectiveness of institutional reforms can be influenced by a variety of factors, including historical legacies and geographical conditions.

Sokoloff and Engerman, alongside Akyeampong and Fofack, show that economic inequality is deeply intertwined with institutional policies and the role of women in economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). For instance, in the context of colonial rule, the codification of customary law often reinforced patriarchal structures that marginalized women's access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional bias not only limited women's economic contributions but also shaped broader economic policies that favored elite groups. The persistence of these inequalities is evident in the New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth and political power was entrenched from the outset. This pattern is further illustrated by the role of women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, where their economic activities were crucial despite institutional barriers. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding how institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, particularly when considering the long-term impacts of historical inequalities.

The growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of long-run economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). These innovations require institutional adjustments for efficient use, as Kuznets notes that technological progress is not compatible with traditional modes of life and family patterns. This implies that the institutional environment must adapt to accommodate new technologies, suggesting a complex interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) support this view by noting that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, indicating that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by pointing out that growth-induced conflicts must be resolved without excessive costs to maintain the political fabric of society. This underscores the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable. The role of institutions in facilitating economic growth is therefore intertwined with technological advancements, as institutions must adapt to support the efficient use of new technologies.

Collaborative research is essential for refining historical economic data. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative efforts in improving the accuracy of economic performance measurements. The inherent subjectivity in estimating margins of error for historical economic data underscores the need for continuous scholarly engagement and debate. Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when the data was partial, thereby inviting scholarly disagreement and new research, has been instrumental in advancing the field. This collaborative approach has led to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than was available in the 1960s. However, the subjectivity in estimating margins of error suggests that while collaborative research improves accuracy, it does not eliminate the uncertainty inherent in historical economic data. This ongoing debate and the continuous updates to historical economic data highlight the dynamic nature of economic history research.

McCloskey (2015, p.3) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) show that the economic transformation in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by shifts in ideas and ideologies, which fostered an environment conducive to experimentation and open dialogue. This ideological shift allowed for the open exchange of ideas and the acceptance of new techniques and institutions, such as the electric motor and the modern research university. This environment facilitated the uptake of new technologies and institutions, which in turn drove economic growth. The influence of these ideas varied across societies. For instance, the Catholic Church's condemnation of modern ideas in 1864 highlights the resistance to these changes in some quarters, despite the broader societal shift towards accepting new ideas. This resistance shows that the impact of ideas and ideologies on economic growth was contingent on the prevailing social and political contexts, which could either facilitate or hinder the adoption of new ideas.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between political and economic factors, which can either facilitate or hinder the adoption of new ideas and technologies. Institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, such as those established in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, have been pivotal in driving economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Extractive states that prioritize resource transfer to the metropole, as seen in the Congo or the Gold Coast, illustrate how institutions can impede long-term development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic highlights the critical role of institutional frameworks in determining economic outcomes. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments exemplify how institutions can lower transaction costs and enhance economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Austin and North share a view on the importance of secure property rights in fostering long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6). The specific paths of institutional evolution and their impact on economic development remain a subject of debate. The reversal of fortune thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, oversimplifies the causal mechanisms at play (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate is further complicated by the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by European colonizers, which shaped the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence and empirical comparisons between different colonial experiences show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors, are the primary drivers of economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic development.
