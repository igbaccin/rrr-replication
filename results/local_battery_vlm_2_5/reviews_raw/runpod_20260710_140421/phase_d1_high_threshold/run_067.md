Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the precise mechanisms and historical contexts through which they exert this influence remain subjects of intense scholarly debate. The disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or whether they are endogenous, evolving in response to political and economic forces. One position argues that institutions are fundamentally shaped by exogenous factors, such as disease environments and settler mortality rates, which in turn determine the type of institutions established in colonies. This perspective suggests that institutions are largely predetermined by these factors, leading to either extractive or inclusive institutions. A rival position posits that institutions are the result of endogenous political processes, where powerful groups shape institutions to maximize their own rents, often at the expense of long-run economic growth. This view highlights the role of distributional conflicts and the political dynamics that underpin institutional development. The question of whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors or endogenous political processes is crucial for understanding the long-term economic trajectories of nations. The resolution of this debate has significant implications for policy, as it informs whether economic development strategies should focus on addressing exogenous constraints or on fostering inclusive political processes. The role of institutions in economic growth is a critical issue for contemporary economic policy, as well as a matter of historical curiosity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This revolution was pivotal in securing property rights over time, a critical factor for both capital markets and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War catalyzed this institutional change, highlighting the political trade-offs that underpinned economic development. North and Weingast further elucidate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights. They illustrate this through the success of capital markets, demonstrating that institutional changes were fundamental to economic growth. This success was underpinned by the regime's commitment to rules and their enforcement, a critical political factor. The institutional changes in England were less revolutionary than previously thought. This implies that while institutions are crucial, other factors such as bureaucratic structures and political commitment also play significant roles. The interplay between institutional development and political factors remains a complex area of study, with secure property rights and reduced transaction costs being key drivers of long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are fundamental drivers of economic inequality and growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the key to understanding economic inequality lies in the conflicts over resource distribution. Ogilvie maintains that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts. These institutions were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the elite, which in turn influenced economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) extend this argument to the New World societies, where institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies. This elite capture of institutions restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) further suggests that institutions should be integral to economic historians' toolkits. This implies that understanding the origins and evolution of institutions is crucial for comprehending long-run economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) warn that the impact of institutions on economic growth is complex. They propose that the factor endowments of societies, which influenced the initial distribution of resources, played a significant role in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic growth. This suggests that while distributional conflicts are a key driver, the initial resource distribution also shapes institutions and economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) demonstrate that colonialism's institutional changes have had a lasting impact on current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. find that European colonization strategies varied greatly, leading to divergent institutional outcomes. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This pattern was evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where institutions were not conducive to long-term economic growth. Where Europeans could settle and establish rule-of-law institutions, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic development was robust. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by initial colonization strategies, continues to influence economic outcomes today. The specific mechanism by which these institutions affect growth is through their impact on investment and economic development. The institutional framework established during colonial times has lasting effects on a country's economic trajectory, as these institutions shape the incentives for investment and economic activity. Furthermore, Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by the observation that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The implications of these findings are profound, highlighting that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic disparities today.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide a counterpoint to the narrative of persistent African poverty by revealing significant economic activity during the colonial era. They argue that African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that the region's economic performance was not as dire as previously thought. This perspective is supported by the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which has enabled recent work in African economic history to reconstruct real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This emphasis on real wages and per capita income aligns with Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) observation that economic growth in many African countries was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also note that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied significantly between and within countries. This variation underscores the complex interplay between economic factors and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2) jointly consider the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa as a distinct phenomenon, separate from the broader discussion on institutions and long-run economic growth. Frankema's examination of the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa reveals that the demand for missionary education was driven by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. This process was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. Simson, meanwhile, explores how East Africa's post-independence budget policies were shaped by the transition from colonial rule, where defence and foreign policy were imperial matters, to independent states responsible for their own security. This shift led to a significant increase in domestic military spending, particularly in response to regional instability and growing domestic dissent. The post-colonial expenditure shift highlights how budgetary policies, influenced by new political and social institutions, redirected resources away from capital and recurrent spending towards military expenditure. This pattern underscores the complex interplay between educational policies and budgetary decisions in shaping long-run economic growth in post-colonial Africa. Simson's analysis of the specific budgetary policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda shows that the growth in military spending was particularly pronounced during the late 1960s and 1970s, indicating a significant reallocation of resources that could have otherwise been invested in economic development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) underscore the slave trade's direct adverse impact on Africa's economic development. They propose that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. The slave trade's impact on trust is significant, as it affects both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors. This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it hinders the establishment of trust-based economic interactions. Furthermore, Nunn (2008, p.2) highlights the extensive duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years from 1400 to 1900, which is more than six times longer than the period of official colonial rule. Nunn (2008, p.19) adds that the slave trade disrupted the development of local economic and social institutions. This disruption prevented the emergence of centralized governments and well-developed markets that could have supported trade and economic growth. The slave trade's adverse effects on trust and institutional development offer a compelling alternative explanation for Africa's underdevelopment, challenging those who attribute it solely to more recent events or colonialism.

Mokyr argues that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth, a stance shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from the introduction of new technologies and ensuring that economic growth is sustained. Mokyr highlights that economic growth requires institutional progress to complement technological progress. Without this complementarity, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains made from technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Kuznets emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that modern economic growth is possible without long-term weakening of the political fabric of society (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments underscores the importance of a supportive institutional environment in harnessing the benefits of technological advancements for long-term economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) offer a nuanced perspective on African development, challenging the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. They argue that colonial administrations and indigenous commodity production significantly influenced economic trajectories. Austin (2008, p.2) posits that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating incentives that shaped institutional responses. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. Hopkins (2009, p.18) extends this by emphasizing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is crucial for development. These insights suggest that understanding the complex interplay between colonial policies and indigenous economic actions is essential for crafting effective institutional reforms in Africa. The complexity of African development paths, influenced by colonial administrations and indigenous commodity production, underscores the need for nuanced institutional reforms. The role of institutions in providing incentives for investment is crucial for development, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18). This perspective informs the broader understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth, particularly in the context of Africa's historical development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as institutions provide the framework within which economic activities occur. North and Weingast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. This perspective is echoed by Ogilvie, who highlights that institutions are indispensable tools in the economic historian's toolkit (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These shared findings (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Ogilvie 2007, p.3) underscore the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs, which are essential for long-term economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) adds that the institutional evolution of older industrial economies offers valuable insights into the paths of economic growth, emphasizing the need for secure private property rights. This aligns with North's argument that the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is crucial for reducing costs per transaction and increasing the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6).

The historical context of colonialism further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, which enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, were established in regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Extractive institutions, designed to transfer resources to the metropole, were implemented in areas like the Congo and the Gold Coast. This distinction highlights how the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Hopkins (2009, p.18) supports this by noting that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is influenced by the presence of progressive institutions. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.18) demonstrates that the institutional legacy of colonial rule continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is another key area where institutions play a crucial role. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that colonial administrations often encouraged indigenous commodity production to ensure fiscal security and stability. This shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not prioritize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence of institutional changes during colonial rule and their impact on economic development provides a clear measurement of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. The case of African development paths, influenced by colonial administrations and indigenous commodity production, underscores the need for nuanced institutional reforms.
