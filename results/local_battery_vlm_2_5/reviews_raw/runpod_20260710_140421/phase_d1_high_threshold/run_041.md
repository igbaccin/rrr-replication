The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development. The question of whether institutions are the primary driver of economic growth or if they merely complement other factors is a live disagreement in the field. Some scholars argue that institutions are the fundamental determinant of economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective governance are essential for fostering investment and development. Others propose that the influence of institutions is secondary to other factors, such as geography or cultural attributes, which are believed to have a more direct impact on economic outcomes. The stakes are high: understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions and shape economic strategies aimed at promoting sustainable growth and reducing poverty. Scholars continue to explore the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, leaving the debate unresolved.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the establishment of institutions in colonies, which in turn affected current income per capita. These strategies ranged from settler colonies where Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, to extractive states where resources were rapidly transferred to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. Austin acknowledges the complexity of institutional evolution, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He emphasizes the need to consider the broader context of institutional development, including the political and economic actions of indigenous populations. These perspectives underscore the importance of understanding the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional outcomes and their long-term effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) contend that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. They argue that the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments were crucial in increasing the security of property rights, thereby facilitating economic growth. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues. The institutional changes in England were both economic and political, involving the creation of institutions that enforced property rights and facilitated market exchanges. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which provided a striking indication of the security of property rights. Some argue that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost at parity with England's, suggesting that institutions alone were not the sole determinants of economic growth. This debate highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice played a critical role in fostering secure property rights and driving economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated through the type of institutions established by European colonizers, which varied based on mortality rates and settler motivations. In colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences persisted and had lasting effects on economic outcomes, as measured by current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions highlights their role in shaping long-run economic growth. The institutional legacies of colonialism thus continue to influence economic growth in former colonies, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) reveal that historical institutions rooted in inequality have profoundly influenced economic outcomes in Africa. This institutional framework, characterized by extreme wealth, human capital, and political power disparities, persisted over time and affected the course of development. Nunn (2008, p.2) extends this perspective by highlighting that Africa's slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, played a crucial role in shaping subsequent economic development. The long duration of these slave trades, compared to the relatively brief period of official colonial rule, underscores their enduring impact on African economies. Nunn (2008, p.2) further notes that the centralized government, national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks of the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with Europeans. This suggests that the presence of certain institutional structures could mitigate the adverse effects of slave trades. Frankema (2012, p.15) contends that the implementation of new social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education. These institutions influenced the demand for missionary education, which was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families. The interplay between historical institutions, inequality, and economic development in Africa highlights the complex and enduring legacy of colonialism and slavery on the continent's economic trajectory.

Broadberry and Gardner's research, in tandem with Frankema and van Waijenburg's findings, challenges the persistent narrative of Africa's economic history as one of unrelenting poverty (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Their work underscores that many Anglophone African economies had achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, a period before independence. These economies' trajectories were significantly influenced by the market for exports and foreign trade (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The availability of large amounts of economic data from the colonial era and the period since independence underscores the potential for more comprehensive historical analyses (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves critical questions unanswered, necessitating further reconstruction and comparison of real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal mechanisms of economic development. They both point out that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, which overlooks the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the thesis fails to account for the varying institutional prescriptions adopted by colonial administrations in response to indigenous economic and political action. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the predictions of Nunn's hypothesis. This suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic and could adapt to local conditions, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. Hopkins builds on this by noting that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, which are crucial for providing incentives for investment and development. The effectiveness of these institutions depends on the broader historical context, including the legacy of colonial rule and the specific paths of institutional evolution. Austin notes that the 'new institutionalist' economic historiography associated with Douglass North already emphasized the political delivery of secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth. This indicates that while institutions play a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effectiveness is contingent on the broader historical and political context.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. The Black Death led to a labour shortage, which in turn increased wages in the northwest. This wage increase was not uniform across Europe; it was more pronounced in regions with higher levels of institutional flexibility, such as Holland. This flexibility allowed for the rapid response to new economic opportunities, fostering urbanisation and commercialisation. The institutional framework in regions like Tuscany, however, was less adaptable, leading to stagnation. The differing trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe highlight the role of institutional flexibility in shaping long-run economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further emphasise that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, contributing to the uneven spread of the fruits of early modern development. Pamuk (2007, p.16) notes that urbanisation rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, further illustrate the different trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe. This divergence in urbanisation rates underscores the long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which ultimately shaped the economic landscape of Europe.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, shaping technological and intellectual advancements that are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). He suggests that the growth of economic reasonableness was far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements drive growth, the institutional adjustments needed to sustain this growth are more complex and less predictable. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this view by highlighting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by appropriate institutional changes to ensure long-term economic growth. The implication is that the relationship between technology and institutions is dynamic and interdependent. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further emphasizes that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in managing the complexities of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the rules and frameworks that govern economic activity. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonies with extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for colonists, illustrate how institutional design can significantly impact economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share a finding that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic growth. These institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges, which are essential for economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This historical evidence underscores the importance of credible commitment in economic development. Credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) demonstrate that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are closely linked to economic performance. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. This dynamic interplay between institutions and economic conditions is further illustrated by Hopkins (2009, p.8), who argues that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. These findings suggest that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures, shaping long-run economic growth in complex ways.

The historical evidence from the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9) and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2) provides a clear example of how institutional changes can drive economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This institutional framework was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) further illustrates how institutional changes introduced by colonialism can have lasting effects on economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) as well as the historical experience of the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9).
