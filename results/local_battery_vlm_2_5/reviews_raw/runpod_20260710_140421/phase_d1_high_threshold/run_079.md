The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of significant scholarly debate. At stake is the fundamental question of what drives economic development over extended periods. One position contends that institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth, shaping the incentives for investment and the efficiency of markets. Another position argues that geographic factors, such as climate and natural resources, play a more decisive role in determining economic outcomes. A third position suggests that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, emphasizing the importance of historical context. The disagreement centers around whether institutions, geographic factors, or historical context are the key drivers of long-run economic growth. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms underlying economic development and for informing policy decisions aimed at promoting growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus a pivotal area of inquiry, one that intersects with broader questions about the nature of economic development and the factors that sustain it over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established vastly different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans settled in large numbers, institutions were designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment. In regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to transfer resources to the metropole quickly. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, were not conducive to long-run economic growth. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial policies of European colonizers, had a profound impact on the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences are robustly associated with current income per capita, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) emphasize the importance of understanding that these institutions were designed to extract resources for the benefit of the colonizers, not to maximize long-run growth. This underscores the critical role of institutional design in shaping economic development paths.

The creation of financial institutions and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights, driving long-run economic growth in Europe (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional development was intertwined with political factors, particularly the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, not merely a response to economic needs. North and Weingast further emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for this process, as it provided a framework for enforcing rules and ensuring commitment to property rights (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This institutional framework was essential for the development of capital markets and economic growth, as evidenced by the remarkable success of England's financial revolution. This discrepancy underscores the importance of specific political and institutional contexts in determining economic outcomes. The development of secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms was a critical factor in Europe's long-run economic growth, illustrating how institutions can shape economic development paths.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both posit that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. The growth of useful knowledge is seen as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This knowledge growth encompasses the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of technological innovations, not just the innovations themselves. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that economic growth requires resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes, which can only be achieved through effective institutional frameworks. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. The implication of this is that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be supported by institutional changes that can manage the social and political conflicts they generate. The presence of institutions that can adapt to and manage the conflicts arising from technological changes is therefore a necessary condition for sustained economic growth.

The slave trade has left a lasting legacy of mistrust within African societies, which continues to hinder economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, cultivated over centuries of exploitation, has created a deeply ingrained cultural norm that affects intergroup relations and economic interactions. This internal mistrust is at least twice as significant as external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Nunn (2008, p.2) further underscores the historical depth of this issue, noting that the slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, a period far longer than the roughly 75 years of official colonial rule. The prolonged nature of the slave trades suggests that their effects on African societies are profound and multifaceted, extending beyond economic factors to include deep-seated cultural and social changes. The persistence of this mistrust today is a significant barrier to economic development in Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the multifaceted nature of African economic history. Austin (2008, p.2) emphasizes that the thesis overlooks the complex interplay of historical periods and paths, which have shaped economic outcomes in Africa. This perspective challenges the notion that African economic decline can be attributed solely to the legacy of colonial institutions. Colonial administrations, for example, often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, rather than imposing uniform policies (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptability suggests that African economies were more dynamic and responsive than often portrayed. Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that while institutions matter, they are not the sole determinant of economic outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa, which focuses on the role of institutions, has gained traction in development policy. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact is mediated by other factors, such as local economic and political actions. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple factors when examining African economic history.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa through a complex interplay of political and economic factors. This assessment influenced the demand for missionary education, which in turn shaped the development of formal education systems. Frankema (2012, p.1) further illuminates this dynamic by questioning the notion that all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts on African education. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) extend this analysis by reconstructing real wage levels in British African colonies, providing a historical context for understanding the economic impacts of colonial education policies. This nuanced view highlights the multifaceted nature of colonial education's impact on African poverty, underscoring the need for a comprehensive understanding of historical and contemporary factors in economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Broadberry and Gupta, show that historical economic performance in Africa and Europe was marked by significant variations in per capita income levels and trade patterns (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This is demonstrated by the availability of substantial economic data for the colonial era in Africa, which reveals that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous assumptions about Africa's economic performance and highlights the importance of export production and foreign trade in shaping economic growth. Local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions all played pivotal roles in determining the timing and scale of growth in export production. In Europe, the service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This sector's contribution to economic development is evident in the increased living standards of north-western Europeans, who benefited from the falling relative price of manufactured goods. Broadberry and Gupta caution that the benefits of early modern development were unevenly distributed, with skilled labourers experiencing larger surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled labourers. This uneven distribution suggests that while institutional changes and investment in agriculture were key drivers of economic growth in north-western Europe, the benefits of these developments were not uniformly shared across different segments of the population. This uneven distribution of economic benefits underscores the complex interplay between institutions, economic growth, and social inequality.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to enforce property rights and provide a stable framework for economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) demonstrate that the political commitment to secure property rights is a critical factor in economic growth. This is supported by North (1989, p.9), who highlights the significance of the Bank of England and new financial instruments in reducing transaction costs and enhancing economic growth. The creation of these institutions reflects an increased security of property rights, which is essential for long-term capital markets and overall economic development. The development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges is a direct result of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement (North 1989, p.6). This interplay between political and economic institutions underscores the importance of a stable institutional framework in fostering economic growth. The development of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is crucial for long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions provide the necessary incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The historical evidence from colonial settings further illustrates the impact of institutions on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the institutions introduced by colonialism were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations sometimes encouraged indigenous economic and political action, leading to institutional changes that reflected the interests of the colonizers. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely linked to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes, which were often extractive, had a lasting impact on the economic development of these regions. The institutional legacy of colonialism continues to influence the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the enduring effects of institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The historical evidence shows that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism had a profound impact on the economic development of former colonies, shaping their long-run economic trajectories. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and Austin (2008, p.15).
