The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic historiography, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement: do institutions shape economic growth by ensuring secure property rights and providing incentives for investment, or are they merely a byproduct of deeper cultural or historical forces? This debate is not merely academic, as understanding the role of institutions has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Some scholars argue that institutions are crucial for fostering economic growth by providing a stable framework for economic activity, while others contend that institutions are secondary to cultural beliefs or historical paths. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to drive long-term economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth. Institutions can encourage investment by enforcing the rule of law, as seen in the colonies of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework provided the security necessary for long-term capital markets to thrive. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and not geographic factors. They emphasize that institutions shaped by politically powerful groups may not always encourage economic development, as these groups may prioritize maximizing their own rents over long-term growth. This institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies.

North and Weingast, along with Austin, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). The Glorious Revolution of 1688 fundamentally altered the political landscape in England, establishing a constitutional monarchy and a parliamentary system that ensured the rule of law and property rights. This institutional framework provided a stable environment for economic activity, as it guaranteed that property rights would be protected and contracts enforced. The development of capital markets in England, which required secure property rights and reliable institutions for enforcement, was a direct result of these political changes. North and Weingast show that the success of the English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of public debt, was underpinned by the credible commitment of the government to respect property rights and honor financial contracts (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Austin also points out that the institutional changes in England were part of a broader pattern of institutional evolution that shaped economic growth. This perspective underscores that the institutional framework in England was a continuous process of adaptation and evolution. This institutional framework was crucial for fostering economic growth, as it provided a stable environment for economic activity and ensured that property rights would be protected and contracts enforced.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon. They show that by the early twentieth century, many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, which challenges recent literature that emphasizes structural impediments to growth. This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gardner's (2022, p.10) work, which highlights that economic growth in many African countries was tied closely to foreign trade and export markets. This linkage suggests that the economic performance of African economies was significantly influenced by external market dynamics, in addition to internal structural factors. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This variation implies that the institutional framework, including political and economic institutions, played a crucial role in shaping the economic trajectories of African countries. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) also underscore that the availability of economic data for the colonial era and the period since independence has been relatively limited, which has constrained comparative historical work on economic growth and development in Africa. This data limitation highlights the need for further research to fully understand the historical determinants of African economic development.

Distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) jointly establish. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) posits that the persistence of institutions like serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the power struggles between different social groups. This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that these institutions were efficient responses to economic conditions. Instead, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that they were maintained because they served the interests of the powerful elites who controlled the political and economic landscape. This analysis is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who demonstrate that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites through various government policies that restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, contributing to the persistence of inequality over time. This historical perspective on distributional conflicts influencing institutions can be qualified by Ogilvie's (2007, p.12) broader call for a more integrated approach to economic history, one that views institutions as indispensable instruments.

They argue that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, contra the notion that all colonial powers had similarly negative impacts on African education systems. This shift in perception was driven by cost-benefit calculations that favored formal education, leading to increased demand for missionary education. By fostering formal education, the British colonial administration created a foundation for long-term economic growth by enhancing human capital and promoting social mobility. Hopkins (2009, p.18) supports this view by highlighting the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that the effectiveness of institutions depends on their ability to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, regardless of ethnic homogeneity or heterogeneity. This implies that while British colonial rule may have laid the groundwork for educational development, the long-term economic benefits hinged on the quality and stability of the institutions established.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, assert that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and thereby affecting long-run economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This enduring mistrust is a result of the historical trauma and exploitation experienced during the slave trade, which has shaped societal norms and attitudes toward cooperation and trust. Nunn and Wantchekon's analysis reveals that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as significant as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for effective collective action and institutional functioning. Nunn further underscores the historical context, noting that the slave trades in Africa spanned nearly 500 years, far outlasting the period of official colonial rule. This extended period of exploitation and mistrust has left a deep and lasting impact on African societies, shaping their economic and social institutions in ways that continue to hinder development. Nunn's discussion of the Kongo kingdom illustrates how centralized governance and well-developed markets facilitated trade, suggesting that the absence of such institutions in other regions may be linked to the legacy of the slave trade. This historical perspective highlights the enduring effects of the slave trade on African economic development, underscoring the importance of addressing the roots of mistrust to foster long-term growth.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap by 1450, suggesting an alternative path to economic divergence independent of institutional factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This perspective challenges the notion that economic divergence can be solely attributed to institutional factors, highlighting instead the role of demographic shocks and subsequent economic adjustments. Pamuk demonstrates that the Black Death led to significant shifts in labor supply and demand, creating a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe. This suggests that economic divergence was driven by factors beyond institutional changes, including the impact of the Black Death on labor markets and economic structures. Pamuk further illustrates this point by comparing the trajectories of Tuscany and Holland, where Holland's institutional flexibility allowed it to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities (Pamuk 2007, p.23). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) point out that in the developing parts of north-western Europe, institutional change is usually seen as bringing about investment in agriculture, leading in turn to high agricultural labour productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). This indicates that while demographic shocks played a significant role, institutional factors still contributed to economic development, albeit in a more complex interplay with other factors.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) also notes that epochal innovations, or major breakthroughs in human knowledge, are dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. This implies that the development and diffusion of technology and knowledge are crucial for long-term economic growth. The impact of technological progress on economic growth is significant, as it requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This suggests that while technological progress is a primary driver of economic growth, institutional factors still play a role in sustaining and complementing this growth.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, acting as the underlying framework that determines the effectiveness of economic policies and the sustainability of technological advancements. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective aligns with North's assertion that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The interaction between institutions and economic growth is further illustrated by the historical context of the Bank of England's creation, which led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and reflected increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth extends to shaping the political and economic landscape in ways that either support or hinder development. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, which may design institutions to maximize rents rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This aligns with Ogilvie's argument that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, which were shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists often did not prioritize long-run growth, illustrating the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The historical evidence of institutional impact on economic growth is further supported by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
