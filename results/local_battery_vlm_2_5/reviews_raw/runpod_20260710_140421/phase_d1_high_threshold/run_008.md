The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic theory and history. At stake is the very basis for understanding economic development over centuries: are institutions the primary drivers, or are they merely epiphenomena of deeper forces? The debate centers on the relative importance of institutions versus geographic factors or cultural beliefs. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key to long-term economic growth, while others posit that geographic factors or cultural beliefs play a more significant role. Institutions are understood as the "rules of the game" that structure economic interactions and determine the incentives for economic actors. The disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy and economic development. The stakes are high because understanding the role of institutions can inform policies aimed at fostering long-run economic growth. This debate is not just about the past but also about the future: if institutions are the key, then institutional reforms could be the path to economic prosperity. If geographic factors or cultural beliefs are more important, efforts to change institutions might be misguided. The central question is whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth, or if other factors such as geographic endowments or cultural beliefs are more significant. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and instruments played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. North and Weingast further emphasize that the success of these institutions was underpinned by the political commitment to enforce rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment was critical in creating a stable environment that encouraged investment and economic growth. However, North and Weingast acknowledge that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought, as France's economic performance before the French Revolution was almost at parity with England's (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and influenced by other factors. The evolution of political institutions that specify and enforce property rights is a key mechanism through which financial institutions drive economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and foster a secure environment for investment.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both propose that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective suggests that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on this by showing that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr (2005, p.49) adds that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological progress is crucial, it must be complemented by institutional progress to ensure sustainable economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, which suggests that institutional frameworks are necessary to manage the complexities arising from technological advancements (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adjustments underscores the complex nature of long-run economic growth, where both factors are interdependent and essential for sustained development.

This perspective shifts the focus from efficiency to the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions, as argued by Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective emphasizes that institutions often arise from power struggles and the interests of dominant groups, rather than from a rational pursuit of economic efficiency. For example, in pre-industrial Europe, institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by the interests of elites who sought to maintain their control over resources and labor. This dynamic is evident in the New World as well, where institutions in societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies. This institutional design restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, thereby shaping long-run economic development. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions operate can vary significantly. For instance, while Ogilvie highlights the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions, Sokoloff and Engerman emphasize the impact of factor endowments and the resulting inequality. This variation in mechanisms underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic growth, where both distributional conflicts and economic factors play crucial roles.

This historical legacy of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, continues to influence economic outcomes, as shown by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is not merely a response to the untrustworthiness of others but is deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. Nunn supports this view by highlighting how the prolonged period of slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting impact on Africa's economic development. The adverse effects of the slave trade are not limited to the historical period but continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. This is evident in the reduced levels of trust, which are critical for economic development. Furthermore, the impact of the slave trade on trust is not solely due to external factors such as the rule of law but is also driven by internal norms of mistrust. This internal mistrust, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) note, is at least twice as significant as the external factors in shaping trust levels. The specific mechanisms by which this mistrust translates into economic underdevelopment remain complex and multifaceted. Nunn (2008, p.2) acknowledges that the centralized government and well-developed markets of the Kongo kingdom facilitated trade with the Europeans, suggesting that the absence of such institutions in other regions may have exacerbated the negative effects of the slave trade. This underscores the importance of institutional factors in mitigating the long-term economic impacts of historical events.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) contend that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis overlooks the nuanced historical paths and institutional evolutions that have shaped Africa's development. The thesis assumes that Africa's colonial experience was uniformly detrimental, but Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations often adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, when European settlers were absent, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, and fiscal considerations drove institutional changes. This adaptation suggests that colonial institutions were not monolithic but responded to local incentives, a pattern that challenges the notion of a uniform colonial impact. However, Hopkins (2009, p.18) qualifies this by noting that the role of institutions in Africa's development is complex and that the presence of 'good' institutions is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) challenge the persistent poverty narrative by examining historical economic data and trends. They argue that African poverty has not been a persistent phenomenon, and that there has been a great deal of economic data available for the colonial era, as well as for the period since independence. This approach allows for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic development in Africa, including the distribution of income. For instance, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) argue that the growth in export production in many African economies was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. However, the timing and scale of this growth varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions. This suggests that while economic growth in Africa was influenced by external factors such as foreign trade, internal factors such as local endowments and political institutions also played a significant role. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) emphasize the importance of reconstructing and comparing real wage levels and trends for British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development. This underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of African economic history that takes into account both external and internal factors.

Frankema (2012, p.15) (Simson 2019, p.2) illustrate that British colonial rule introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. British colonial administrators promoted missionary education, altering the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families, which in turn reshaped local demand for education. This demand was driven by cost-benefit calculations that considered both macro and micro levels. This suggests that the institutional framework of colonial rule played a pivotal role in shaping educational outcomes, which had long-term implications for African development. The legacy of British rule was not uniformly positive. The post-independence era saw significant shifts in budgetary policies, particularly in military and debt servicing expenditures. Simson (2019, p.11) argues that these shifts, driven by regional instability and domestic dissent, had profound implications for economic development. These budgetary policies, characterized by high military spending and significant debt burdens, often displaced capital and recurrent spending, which could have otherwise supported broader public goods and services. This indicates that while colonial institutions laid the groundwork for educational development, post-independence policies complicated the trajectory of economic growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) explore long-term structural changes in Europe during the Black Death era, but they do not directly attribute these changes to institutional factors. This divergence was not solely due to later developments but was significantly influenced by the institutional flexibility that allowed regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities. The unusual degree of institutional flexibility in Holland enabled new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies. These structural changes were crucial in transforming Holland into one of the most advanced, urbanized, and commercialized regions of the continent. The economic growth in the early modern and modern eras was characterized by structural change, a shift that Kuznets (1966) emphasized as a key indicator of productivity and economic development. This shift was evident in the varying urbanization rates across Europe, with regions like Tuscany stagnating while Holland flourished. The differing trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe in the era of the Black Death highlight the significant role of institutional flexibility in economic development. However, Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) remind us that estimates of national accounts, particularly those from the distant past, are subject to margins of error and are often based on partial data and assumptions. This uncertainty suggests that while structural changes and institutional flexibility played a role, the extent and specificity of their impact on long-run economic growth remain subject to interpretation.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with van Zanden, highlight that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already evident before 1800, with Europe's development shifting towards the North Sea countries by the 17th century (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1). This shift was marked by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, indicating significant economic changes that went beyond mere agricultural productivity. One key mechanism driving this divergence was the role of merchant distribution and finance, which facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector of northwestern Europe. This sector's growth was not merely a result of agricultural investment but also stemmed from institutional changes that spurred investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This pattern suggests that while institutional changes drove economic growth, they also contributed to inequality. Van Zanden further qualifies this pattern by noting that the changes in economic development did not uniformly translate into improvements in the standard of living across Europe. The lack of a clear relationship between economic development and living standards implies that the benefits of economic growth were not universally shared (van Zanden 1999, p.1).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. European colonizers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of their colonies. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans often implemented extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, creating long-term barriers to growth. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where extractive institutions persisted and hindered economic development. Conversely, in areas with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering long-term economic growth. This institutional framework had profound implications for the economic development of former colonies, with extractive institutions inhibiting growth and more inclusive institutions facilitating it. The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes, shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in the historical and political contexts in which they emerge. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by European colonists in their colonies significantly influenced long-term economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions were designed to maximize rents for the colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions, which either facilitated or inhibited economic development based on whether they were inclusive or extractive. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes economic divergence to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the complex historical paths of different regions (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin shows that the institutional evolution in older industrial economies reflects patterns and paths of institutional development, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding that colonial institutions shaped long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2) underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts in which these institutions were established.

The institutional framework set by European colonists had profound implications for the economic development of former colonies. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth by specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement, which reduces transaction costs and facilitates market exchanges (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Ogilvie contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6) highlights the role of political dynamics in institutional development. Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This underscores the practical implications of understanding the role of institutions in economic development.

The persistence of these institutions over time highlights their enduring impact on economic outcomes, shaping the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies. The shared finding that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; North 1989, p.6) and that they significantly influence long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2) underscores the importance of understanding the historical and political contexts in which institutions emerge. The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement between Acemoglu et al. and Austin is the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and its relation to industrialization during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This empirical comparison shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
