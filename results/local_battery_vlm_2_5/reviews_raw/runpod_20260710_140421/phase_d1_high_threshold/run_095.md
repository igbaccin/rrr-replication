The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. The debate centers around the extent to which institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic behavior, drive economic growth over the long term. On one side, scholars argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that protect property rights and enforce contracts, is pivotal for sustained economic development. On the other side, some researchers contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one among several important factors. Institutions are not merely a backdrop to economic activity but are actively shaped by political and economic forces, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks was driven by the mortality rates experienced by European settlers, with high mortality rates leading to the establishment of extractive institutions. These extractive institutions, in turn, had a profound impact on the long-run economic growth of the colonies. In colonies where extractive states were established, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, economic growth was hindered. The persistence of these institutions over time further exacerbated the differences in economic outcomes. Another significant implication is that the initial conditions set by colonial institutions continued to shape economic trajectories long after the colonial period ended. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on the economic development of former colonies.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9)(North and Weingast 1989, p.29) jointly establish that the development of financial institutions and instruments was pivotal for reducing transaction costs and fostering economic growth. The implications of these institutions extend beyond economic performance. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) illustrate that the commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical factor in sustaining economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were established and effectively enforced, providing a stable environment for long-term economic development. The creation of these institutions reduced transaction costs and fostered a regime committed to economic growth, underscoring the profound impact of institutional development on economic trajectories.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The growth of useful knowledge provides a continuous stream of innovations that drive economic growth, while institutions are often seen as secondary factors that facilitate or hinder the effective use of these innovations. This mechanism is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. For instance, the rise of the modern national state was instrumental in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, enabling sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this perspective by noting that institutional progress is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements provide a steady push for economic growth, institutional developments can be more volatile and subject to setbacks. This volatility underscores the need for robust institutional frameworks that can adapt to technological changes and sustain long-term economic growth.

Pamuk (2007, p.23) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) argue that the Black Death triggered structural changes that led to a significant wage gap between northwestern Europe and the rest of the continent by 1450. This divergence was not solely due to later institutional developments, but rather, it was the result of long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death. These changes included shifts in urbanization rates and the emergence of new economic opportunities in regions like Holland, which responded rapidly due to its institutional flexibility (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The service sector, including merchant distribution and finance, played a crucial role in this economic growth, highlighting the importance of the service sector in early modern economic development (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The uneven distribution of these benefits underscores the complexity of economic growth during this period, where both institutional factors and structural changes played significant roles.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the legacy of Africa's slave trades has been a significant barrier to economic development. They assert that the slave trades instilled a culture of mistrust, which has persisted and hindered economic growth. The slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, exposed Africans to extensive violence, which led to the development of internal norms of mistrust. This mistrust has had a profound impact on economic development by undermining social capital and impeding cooperative behavior. As the slave trades were a prolonged and widespread phenomenon, they had a deep and lasting impact on African societies. This prolonged exposure to violence and exploitation created a pervasive culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic outcomes. This mistrust has been shown to be a significant barrier to economic development, as it undermines social capital and impedes cooperative behavior. Nunn (2008, p.19) notes that the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the presence of strong institutions and markets can mitigate the negative effects of a history of violence and exploitation. The widespread nature of the slave trades means that the adverse effects of mistrust are not limited to specific regions or groups. The slave trades affected virtually all of Africa, leaving a legacy of mistrust that continues to impede economic development.

Frankema's work on British rule in sub-Saharan Africa highlights that colonial institutions fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The demand for missionary education in Africa was driven by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, influenced by the introduction of new social and political institutions. This mechanism altered the perceived benefits of formal education, making it more attractive to African political leaders and families. The British colonial administration's emphasis on formal education differed from other colonial powers, which often overlooked or undermined educational development. Sokoloff and Engerman's analysis of New World societies underscores the role of institutions in perpetuating inequality, highlighting how differential paths of development are rooted in institutional frameworks that advantage elites. This institutional advantage, in turn, restricts competition and contributes to the persistence of inequality. Frankema's work qualifies this perspective by showing that British colonial institutions in Africa, despite their exploitative nature, did promote formal education to some extent. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on education and inequality is nuanced and context-dependent, with British rule in sub-Saharan Africa offering a unique case where formal education was fostered more effectively than in other colonial contexts.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlight that women played a pivotal role in African economic development, both before and during the colonial era. Despite being marginalized and relegated to less productive lands, women significantly contributed to food farming and, indirectly, to cash crop production. This contribution was often overshadowed by patriarchal alliances and customary laws codified under colonial rule. Hopkins (2009, p.8) elaborates that the new economic history of Africa emphasizes the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This institutional framework, however, often failed to acknowledge or support women's economic activities. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) note, further entrenched the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to productive resources and reinforcing their marginalization. This institutional bias had profound implications for African economic development, as it restricted the full participation of women in the economy. The new economic history of Africa, while recognizing the role of institutions, must also account for the gendered dynamics of these institutions to fully understand their impact on economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge the persistent poverty narrative by demonstrating that many African economies had achieved income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner note that the availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era and the post-independence period has enabled recent work in African economic history to demonstrate the growth of export production tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. This growth varied significantly between and within countries due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg show that the reconstruction of real wage levels in British African colonies reveals a more complex picture of economic development, with periods of growth and decline that were not solely determined by structural impediments. The timing and scale of economic growth in these colonies were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This nuanced view of African economic history suggests that the region's economic performance was more dynamic than previously thought, with periods of growth and decline influenced by a variety of factors. These findings underscore the importance of considering the specific historical and geographical conditions that shaped African economic development. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) report, “Historical national accounting for Sub-Saharan Africa Despite its central importance in the modern economic history of most regions, there has until recently been relatively little use of national accounting in the economic history of Africa ( Manning 1987 : 51).” Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) report, “It should also aim for a deeper understanding of the determinants of the real wage divergence across British Africa and explore how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa.”

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both offer nuanced explanations for historical economic phenomena in pre-industrial Europe by examining specific institutional arrangements. Institutional arrangements such as colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). She further argues that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and that institutions should be transformed from a fashion accessory into an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.12)(Ogilvie 2007, p.3). These findings suggest that the institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by a complex interplay of distributional conflicts, shared beliefs, and political action.

Institutional arrangements play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Credible commitment underpins growth (North 1989, p.6). This is evident in the historical development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights, thereby fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9). Institutions matter because they provide the framework within which economic activity occurs, influencing the incentives and constraints that affect investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The divergent paths of economic development in these colonies underscore the critical role of institutions in determining long-term economic outcomes.

The institutional frameworks established by European colonists were shaped by a complex interplay of distributional conflicts, shared beliefs, and political action. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions highlights their enduring impact on economic development. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes reflect increased security of the time dimension of property rights, a dimension critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.6). The institutional arrangements in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by a complex interplay of distributional conflicts, shared beliefs, and political action (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the reversal of fortune thesis, which suggests that the relative incomes of former European colonies are due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This thesis emphasizes the influence of population density and prosperity on the policies pursued by the Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The new economic history of Africa shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). The historical evidence of the Bank of England and the English financial revolution, along with the institutional frameworks established by European colonists in their colonies, provides a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.
