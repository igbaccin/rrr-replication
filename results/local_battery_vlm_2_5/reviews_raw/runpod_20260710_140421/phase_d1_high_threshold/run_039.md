The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of the debate lies a disagreement between scholars who attribute long-run economic growth primarily to the presence of secure property rights and effective governance institutions, and those who argue that other factors, such as geography, culture, or technology, play more decisive roles. Some scholars emphasize that institutions designed to extract resources rather than foster growth have persistently hindered economic development in former colonies. Others point to the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, suggesting that institutional frameworks evolve in response to a multitude of influences, including population density, prosperity, and political dynamics. This interplay shapes the long-term economic outcomes of societies. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they operate in conjunction with other factors, remains a contentious issue. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This approach contrasts with colonies where Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) demonstrate that the creation of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and fostering secure property rights. This institutional framework was instrumental in driving long-run economic growth. This can be seen in the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 led to a significant decline in transaction costs. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency. This perspective challenges the idea that institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Ogilvie posits that core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were influenced by the struggles over wealth and power, rather than by the pursuit of economic efficiency. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of the elite, who had the power to shape institutional structures, with the merchant guild being a prime example. The guilds' restrictive practices, such as limiting competition and controlling access to markets, were not aimed at maximizing overall economic efficiency but at securing the advantages of the elite. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this argument to the New World, demonstrating that institutions in societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. These policies, which included land distribution and labor regulations, were designed to maintain the elite's control over resources and markets. Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe and Sokoloff and Engerman's study of New World societies together show that the distributional conflicts shaping institutions can have long-lasting effects on economic development. The specific mechanisms by which these conflicts influence economic outcomes can vary across regions and historical periods.

Institutions must adapt to accommodate new technologies for long-run economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) both argue. Kuznets's analysis of economic growth across centuries and regions highlights that technological progress necessitates institutional adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from the economic changes. This is because modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and family patterns, requiring significant institutional and ideological shifts to integrate new innovations effectively. Mokyr's focus on the growth of useful knowledge complements this view, emphasizing that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustainable economic improvement. Without complementary institutional progress, the benefits of technological advances can be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors and coordination failures. The sovereign state plays a critical role in managing these growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological changes are integrated into the economy without excessive costs or long-term weakening of societal cohesion. This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions is crucial for understanding the complex relationship between economic growth and institutional development. Kuznets's emphasis on the role of the national state in managing growth-induced conflicts suggests that the institutional framework must evolve to support technological progress, ensuring that the benefits of new innovations are distributed efficiently and equitably.

The slave trade's legacy of mistrust in Africa has significantly impaired long-run economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) identify a culture of mistrust as a profound and enduring consequence of the slave trade, which has persisted and continues to impact economic development. This mistrust manifests as a reduction in social capital, which is crucial for economic growth. Trust facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and encourages investment in human and physical capital. The slave trade's disruption of social norms and the ensuing mistrust have eroded these foundations, creating an environment where economic transactions are less efficient and less productive. Nunn (2008, p.2) shows that this mistrust is not merely a transient effect but a deep-seated cultural phenomenon that has shaped Africa's economic trajectory. The slave trade's impact on trust is particularly significant because it affects both internal norms and external environments. This dual impact means that even when external conditions improve, such as through better institutions or increased trade, the internal mistrust can still hinder economic progress. Consequently, addressing the legacy of mistrust requires not only institutional reforms but also cultural shifts that rebuild trust within African societies. The historical impact of the slave trade on trust levels underscores the importance of understanding how past events continue to influence present-day economic outcomes, even when direct causal links are not immediately apparent.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the economic performance of Anglophone African economies in the early twentieth century was more robust than previously thought, with per capita income levels above subsistence. This reassessment is significant because it challenges the prevailing narrative that colonial rule uniformly stunted African economic development. Broadberry and Gardner's findings are supported by Frankema, who highlights that British rule was particularly conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. This educational impact was driven by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education for African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). The demand for missionary education was influenced by cost-benefit calculations at both macro and micro levels, indicating a complex interplay between colonial institutions and local societal dynamics. Broadberry and Gardner further note that economic growth in these regions was tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports. This pattern underscores the role of external economic factors in shaping internal development, with local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions all playing crucial roles (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, the timing and scale of growth varied significantly between and within countries, suggesting that the impact of colonial rule on economic development was not uniform. This variability highlights the need for nuanced analyses that consider the specific historical and institutional contexts of different African economies, as well as the downstream implications of educational development on broader economic outcomes.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge conventional views of African poverty by examining the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics. Their analysis suggests that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. A key mechanism here is the role of labor policies. Colonial administrations that encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis (Austin 2008, p.15). This approach highlights the nuanced interactions between colonial policies and local economic dynamics, which shaped long-run economic growth in Africa. This qualification underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the institutional and policy frameworks that influenced African economic development.
