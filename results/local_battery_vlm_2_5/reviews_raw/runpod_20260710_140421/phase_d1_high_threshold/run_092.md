The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutions drive growth or are merely epiphenomenal. One camp emphasizes the primacy of institutions in determining economic outcomes, arguing that well-designed institutions can foster growth by providing secure property rights, enforcing contracts, and promoting investment. Some scholars focus on the intricate relationship between institutions and other factors, indicating that institutional quality often results from economic development. This debate is crucial for understanding the mechanisms underlying economic growth and for designing effective policies to promote development. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are shaped by other factors such as geography, culture, or economic conditions. The question of whether institutions are the cause or effect of economic growth is central to this debate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights, which in turn drove economic growth. This institutional framework was essential for the remarkable success of England's capital markets. This security was a critical dimension for both long-term capital markets and economic growth itself. The institutional changes in England, including the trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, were a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional evolution was not just about economic rules but also about the political commitment to these rules, which North and Weingast (1989, p.2) describe as a sovereign's commitment to being bound by these rules. The institutional changes in England fostered a stable environment conducive to economic growth, as evidenced by the development of capital markets and the reduction in transaction costs. The effect of these institutions on economic growth varied among countries. The elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not a significant hindrance to economic growth in France, indicating that other factors, such as cultural or technological differences, may also play a role. This highlights the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, where multiple factors contribute to long-term economic development.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that European colonists established different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to either extractive or growth-oriented institutions. This institutional divergence was driven by the colonists' economic incentives and mortality risks. For instance, in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, rather than investing in long-term growth. These extractive institutions persisted and significantly impacted the long-run economic development of these regions. When European settlers faced lower mortality rates, they were more likely to establish institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. This dynamic highlights the role of institutional design in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This institutional divergence alone does not fully account for the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) and North (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) show that the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that distributional conflicts and inequality shaped the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. Ogilvie argues that institutions such as serfdom, community structures, craft guilds, and merchant guilds were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of powerful elites at the expense of broader economic efficiency. Sokoloff and Engerman extend this analysis to New World societies, where initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the development of institutions that maintained and exacerbated these disparities. The resulting institutions restricted competition and limited economic opportunities for the majority, thereby perpetuating inequality. Ogilvie acknowledges that beliefs can be part of the institutional structure of a society. This perspective highlights how the initial distribution of power and resources can have long-lasting effects on institutional development and economic outcomes. Ogilvie also notes that beliefs differ from institutions. This nuance is important because it underscores the complexity of institutional dynamics. Institutional design in pre-industrial Europe and New World societies was driven by the need to manage distributional conflicts and maintain elite control. This historical precedent suggests that contemporary institutional design may similarly be influenced by power dynamics and the distribution of resources. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) reports, “.” Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) report, “These differentials in paths of development have long been of central concern to scholars of Latin America and have recentiy attracted more attention from economic historians and economists more generally (North, 1988; Engerman and Sokoloff, 1997; Coatsworth 1993, 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, 2000; Engerman, Haber and Sokoloff, 2000).”

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective is particularly relevant for understanding the economic performance of African economies, which have often been characterized by diverse institutional frameworks and varying levels of economic development. This is especially true for African economies, where colonial administrations often responded to local economic and political actions rather than imposing uniform institutional prescriptions. Austin (2008, p.2) further notes that colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production, which was in their fiscal interests. This dynamic is evident in the case of Governor-General Clifford, who opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. Broadberry and Gardner build on this by demonstrating that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding underscores the need to reconsider the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as the institutional frameworks in these economies were not uniformly detrimental to economic development. Instead, they were shaped by a complex interplay of local and colonial factors, which influenced economic outcomes in diverse ways. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further qualify this by arguing that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, but rather a result of specific historical and geographical conditions. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income, to assess the possibilities of future growth.

The literature on institutional reforms and economic development in Africa examines how specific changes in governance and policy can alleviate poverty, addressing the fundamental role of institutions in long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). He also notes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. 'Good' institutions, as the literature calls them, matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema (2012, p.15) contends that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers, which in turn influenced the demand for missionary education. This dynamic altered the (perceived) benefits of formal education, which were related to new social and political institutions (Frankema 2012, p.15). Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). Their estimates show that both channels are important, but that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This suggests that the legacy of mistrust, stemming from historical events like the slave trade, continues to influence contemporary economic development in Africa. The persistence of this mistrust highlights the enduring impact of historical institutions on current economic outcomes.

The role of women and budget policies in economic growth are both crucial yet distinct factors that only obliquely engage the question of how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Simson 2019, p.2). This contribution was not merely incidental but deeply rooted in the institutional frameworks that governed land access and customary law. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal structures, limiting women's access to land and economic opportunities. Simson (2019, p.2) shows that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not as clientelist or unproductive as commonly portrayed. Instead, these policies were shaped by the need to manage regional instability and domestic dissent, which often led to increased military spending and debt burdens. These policies were driven by rent-seeking behavior and were also a response to the institutional legacies of colonial rule and the need for state-building in the post-independence era. The institutional frameworks that governed budget policies and military spending were influenced by the broader economic and political contexts, which in turn shaped the long-run economic growth trajectories of these nations. This underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted roles of institutions in economic development, as they interact with both historical legacies and contemporary policy choices.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share a view that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. They argue that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. This perspective suggests that the accumulation of knowledge and technological progress are essential for sustained economic growth. The role of institutions in this context is to facilitate the efficient use of innovations, ensuring that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economy. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that major technological breakthroughs, or epochal innovations, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, indicating their global impact on economic development. Mokyr (2005, p.49) acknowledges the possibility of coordination failures and proposes policies to address them. Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting and sustaining the benefits of technological advancements.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) argue that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, driven by factors such as merchant distribution and finance in northwestern Europe. This divergence was facilitated by institutional changes that promoted investment in agriculture, leading to high agricultural labor productivity. van Zanden (1999, p.9) supports this view, noting that economic development in Europe led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, with England becoming one of the most expensive countries after 1740. However, Pamuk (2007, p.23) qualifies this by highlighting the role of institutional flexibility in enabling regions like Holland to respond rapidly to new economic opportunities, contrasting with the stagnation in regions like Tuscany. This suggests that while merchant distribution and finance were crucial, institutional adaptability was equally important in driving economic growth and divergence.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that the impact of slave trades in Africa on economic development is a rival explanation to institutional factors. The slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, had a profound and lasting adverse effect on Africa's economic trajectory. The slave trades disrupted social and economic structures, leading to a significant departure from the pre-existing political and economic institutions. The Kongo kingdom, for instance, had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, all of which facilitated trade with the Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). The slave trades disrupted these institutions, leading to a decline in economic development. This disruption was not limited to the immediate effects of the slave trades but also included the long-term effects on the political and economic institutions of the affected regions. The reliance on foreign firms and intermediaries, which replaced the decaying state institutions, further exacerbated the economic decline (Peters 2004, p.19). The slave trades had a direct adverse effect on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to institutional factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central theme in economic history and development studies. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game, shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives, constraints, and opportunities for economic actors. North shows that institutions matter in economic growth, and that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic development (North 1989, p.6). North argues that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should result in falling transaction costs and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate GNP (North 1989, p.9). Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established by European colonizers significantly affected current income per capita, and that the extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. also show that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This explains the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. also show that the results with other covariates are also very similar. Austin shows that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin also shows that, where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This is consistent with the finding that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security, and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Hopkins shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins also shows that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. 'Good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Ogilvie shows that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe: serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie also shows that shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This shows that institutions matter in economic growth, and that efficient property rights and their enforcement are critical for economic development.
