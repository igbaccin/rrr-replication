The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At stake is the identification of the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth over extended periods, and the extent to which institutions, as opposed to other factors, are the primary determinants. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography, culture, or political dynamics, play a more significant role. This debate is crucial for policymakers seeking to understand the roots of economic success and failure, and for scholars aiming to develop robust theories of economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are widely recognized as critical to economic performance. The precise nature of this relationship and the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth remain contested. For example, Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established by European colonists significantly impacted the economic trajectories of their colonies. Austin, meanwhile, challenges the notion that institutions are the sole or primary determinant of economic growth, suggesting that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors at work. To understand the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, it is essential to examine the various theoretical and empirical contributions that have sought to elucidate this relationship. The institutions that Europeans established in their colonies were shaped by the mortality rates they faced, leading to a dichotomy between extractive and growth-promoting institutions. European colonizers adopted different strategies based on the mortality rates they encountered, with high mortality rates leading to the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, did not encourage long-run economic growth. In colonies with lower mortality rates, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic development. This divergence in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, with extractive institutions hindering growth and growth-promoting institutions facilitating it. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a range of factors that influence the establishment and effectiveness of institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that institutions matter in economic growth by fostering secure property rights. This enhanced security was critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further emphasize that the political commitment to enforcing these property rights was crucial. This commitment underpinned the success of capital markets and economic growth, as evidenced by the remarkable performance of England's capital markets. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England, particularly those governing public choice and property rights, were pivotal in fostering economic growth. The extent to which these institutions fostered economic growth is debated. This suggests that while secure property rights are necessary for economic growth, they may not be sufficient on their own.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). They argue that these institutions were designed to preserve the status quo and maintain the power of the ruling class, which often led to the creation of institutions that were inefficient but served the interests of the elite. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies, institutions were shaped by the factor endowments and the extreme inequality that characterized these societies from the outset. They argue that the institutions that evolved in these societies were designed to preserve the wealth and power of the elite, and that this had a significant impact on the course of development. This perspective is further supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that the persistence of these institutions over time was due to the power of the elite to maintain their control over the political and economic systems. This suggests that while distributional conflicts were important, they were not the only factor shaping institutions in early modern societies. Distributional conflicts, therefore, were a significant factor in shaping the economic institutions of early modern societies, but they were not the only factor at play.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both argue. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights that the accumulation of knowledge and technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. He notes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, and that this progress is not monotonic or irreversible. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also observes that institutional progress is necessary to complement technological progress, ensuring the sustainability of economic improvement. Kuznets (1973, p.1) expands on this idea, noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently used. He argues that the major breakthroughs in the advance of human knowledge are the dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is not uniform across different contexts. Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This implies that while institutions are necessary for technological progress, the specific type of institution may vary depending on the context.

Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that economic institutions are the primary determinants of long-run economic growth, oversimplifies causation (Austin 2008, p.2). This critique is shared by Hopkins, who contends that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms to eliminate poverty (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Both authors suggest that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, thereby oversimplifying the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. This simplification can obscure the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. For example, Austin highlights that in the absence of European settlers, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which could foster economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism is nuanced by the role of colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and responded to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. Hopkins emphasises that the quality of public goods provided by African governments is determined by the capacity of societies to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This capacity is crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth varies across different contexts.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonizers established different types of institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole in regions with high mortality rates, to more inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law in colonies with lower mortality rates. This institutional divergence was a function of the colonizers' settlement patterns, which in turn were influenced by disease environments. In this way, disease environments indirectly shaped the institutional landscape of colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that these institutional differences persist and have a significant impact on current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the fact that they were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that these institutions are not static, and that African societies have sought fiscal security and responded to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is not uniform, and that the capacity of societies to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions is crucial for providing incentives for investment.

The slave trade's impact on African economic development is rooted in a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts economic development (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The slave trade's legacy of mistrust operates through both external environmental factors and internal norms of mistrust. The internal norms of mistrust, in particular, are at least twice as large as the external environmental factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This internal mistrust is a critical barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for cooperative economic activity. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that sustained trade did not occur until the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, was able to support trade with the Europeans. This suggests that the lack of centralized governance and economic institutions in many African societies, which can be traced back to the disruptions caused by the slave trade, has hindered economic development. The enduring impact of the slave trade on African economic development is a persistent factor that continues to shape contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs. North shows how the development of economic institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This development was crucial for economic growth as it enhanced the security of property rights and fostered a long-term capital market (North 1989, p.9). The emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is reflected in the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). North argues that these institutions are critical for economic growth as they reduce costs per transaction while increasing the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6). Hopkins emphasizes the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Austin adds that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that institutional prescriptions were responsive to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. These findings show that institutions matter in economic growth, and their development is critical for long-term economic success.
