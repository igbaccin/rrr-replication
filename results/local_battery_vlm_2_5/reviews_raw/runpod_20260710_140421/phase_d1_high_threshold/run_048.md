The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive economically while others stagnate, and how political and economic structures influence this trajectory. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic development. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) highlight how European colonists established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those enforcing the rule of law, which significantly impacted current income per capita. Others, such as North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), emphasize the importance of institutions in reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for economic growth. The disagreement lies in whether institutions are the sole determinant or if they interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) focus on the political factors underpinning economic growth, arguing that the commitment to rules and their enforcement is crucial. This sets the stage for exploring how institutions, through their role in securing property rights and reducing transaction costs, can drive long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions was largely determined by the mortality rates experienced by European colonizers. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole, rather than fostering long-term economic development. This pattern is evident in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where institutions were structured to extract resources efficiently but did not encourage investment or adhere to the rule of law. Where Europeans could settle and establish permanent colonies, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and promoted investment. This divergence in institutional design had profound implications for economic growth, with extractive institutions stifling development and institutions that secured property rights and reduced transaction costs facilitating long-term economic prosperity. The persistence of these colonial institutions into the modern era underscores their enduring impact on economic outcomes. This institutional legacy continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies today, with those that inherited extractive institutions often lagging behind in economic development.

Their work underscores that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement was crucial for economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs, fostering a long-term capital market. These institutional advancements were critical not only for economic growth but also for the stability of the capital market, as they provided a secure environment for long-term investments. North and Weingast's analysis reveals that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War prompted a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues, which laid the groundwork for this institutional evolution (North 1989, p.9). This dynamic highlights that institutional changes lowered costs and expanded the scope of economic activity, driving long-run growth. The institutional legacy of secure property rights continues to influence economic trajectories, even as the nature of these institutions evolves over time.

Technological progress and institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) both show. This is because technological changes introduce new challenges and conflicts that must be resolved. Without proper institutions, these conflicts can weaken the political fabric of society and impede economic growth. This means that while technological advancements steadily drive economic growth, institutions may face setbacks and require continuous adaptation. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is thus dynamic and essential for long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) examine how different historical periods and paths influenced economic outcomes. Colonial institutions did not uniformly hinder African economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that in regions where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production. This indicates that colonial institutions adapted in response to local economic and political dynamics, rather than imposing a uniform set of constraints. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They reconstruct real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (ca. 1880-1965), revealing that economic development varied significantly across different regions and time periods. This variation suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was context-dependent. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights the importance of understanding the specific paths and patterns of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience in different regions. This nuanced view of colonial institutions challenges the notion that they uniformly constrained African economic development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust, which persists and impacts contemporary trust levels, offering an alternative explanation for reduced economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust is a significant factor in the reduced trust levels observed in contemporary African societies. This internal mistrust, more than external factors like the rule of law, has a substantial impact on economic development. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the African continent experienced four simultaneous slave trades over nearly 500 years, a period far longer than the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade had a lasting impact on African societies, shaping their economic development in ways that are still felt today. This mistrust can hinder economic growth by reducing cooperation, investment, and overall economic activity. However, the extent to which this mistrust directly translates into economic outcomes is qualified by the complexity of other factors at play, such as the role of colonial institutions and contemporary policies.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions such as serfdom and guilds emerged from the struggle between different social groups over resources and power, not from any inherent efficiency. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that in New World societies, institutions evolved to advantage elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This perspective suggests that the persistence of inequality can be attributed to institutional arrangements that reflect the interests of powerful groups. However, the extent to which these institutions directly translate into long-run economic growth is qualified by the complexity of other factors at play, such as the role of colonial institutions and contemporary policies.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical evolution of property rights and transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both emphasize the critical role of institutions in fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) demonstrate that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, significantly impacting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). North (1989, p.9) similarly argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These findings underscore the importance of secure property rights and efficient institutions in promoting long-term economic development.

Furthermore, the interaction between institutions and economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) both highlight the importance of understanding the historical context and the specific institutional arrangements that shaped economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, suggesting that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than broad economic efficiency (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These perspectives highlight the importance of considering the political and social context in which institutions operate.

The historical evidence shows that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) both demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and property rights was crucial for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, highlighting the importance of credible commitment in economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, emphasizing the role of institutions in long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes and highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and political context in which institutions operate.
