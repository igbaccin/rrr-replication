The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, encompassing the rules, norms, and organizations that govern societal interactions, are widely acknowledged as critical determinants of economic performance. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: while some scholars emphasize the primacy of institutional factors, others contend that geographic or other exogenous variables play a more significant role. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and the allocation of resources aimed at fostering economic development.

The causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth are multifaceted. Acemoglu et al. 2001, 2002) argue that the type of institutions established during colonial periods had lasting effects on economic outcomes. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were implemented, which persistently hindered economic development. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This perspective is supported by North 1989), who highlights that institutions specifying and enforcing property rights are crucial for economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for instance, significantly reduced transaction costs and facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework provided the necessary security for long-term capital markets, underscoring the importance of secure property rights in fostering economic development. Institutions that ensure the rule of law and encourage investment are essential for sustained economic growth, as they create an environment conducive to economic activity and innovation. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth, particularly through the establishment and enforcement of property rights. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, for instance, led to a significant reduction in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This financial revolution was underpinned by increased security in the time dimension of property rights, a critical factor in both long-term capital markets and overall economic growth. The trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues during the Hundred Years' War exemplifies how political institutions can specify and enforce property rights efficiently. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) explore how the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which were critical for economic growth. The regime's commitment to these rules was a crucial political factor in ensuring their effectiveness. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.29) caution that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as initially thought, as France's economic performance before the French Revolution was nearly on par with England's. This suggests that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent. The elaboration of bureaucratic structures, such as those inherited from Louis XIV, did not necessarily hinder economic growth, indicating that the specific nature of institutions matters more than their mere existence.

However, Mokyr and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1) argue that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth. The advancement of technology requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. The major breakthroughs in human knowledge are termed epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, transforming economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.54) highlights that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Economic reasonableness, which facilitates the uptake of new techniques and institutions, is essential for long-term economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.3) argues that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. This perspective suggests that the ideological conditions for the uptake of new techniques and institutions are crucial for economic growth. The growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. The sustainability of economic improvement depends on the interplay between technological progress and institutional progress, as well as the ideological conditions that facilitate the uptake of new techniques and institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions established by European powers were instrumental in shaping long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions were not merely byproducts of geographic conditions or technological factors, but rather were deliberately designed to extract resources and maximize rents for European colonists. European settlement policies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that encouraged investment and economic development or extractive institutions that focused on rapid resource transfer to the metropole. For example, inclusive institutions were set up in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, while extractive states were established in regions such as the Congo and the Gold Coast. These institutional differences had profound and lasting effects on the economic trajectories of the colonies, influencing current income per capita and overall economic growth. The persistence of these institutions and their impact on economic development are not solely determined by their initial design. This insight suggests that the sustainability of economic growth in former colonies depends on the interplay between initial institutional designs and subsequent institutional changes, as well as the political dynamics that shape these changes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks emerging from initial factor endowments and inequality shaped long-term economic outcomes in the New World. The extreme inequality observed in many New World societies from their inception led to institutions that concentrated wealth, human capital, and political power in the hands of a few. This concentration of power enabled elites to implement policies that restricted competition and preserved their advantages. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) highlight the role of institutional changes in driving investment and productivity in agriculture, which supports the idea that the initial inequality and the resulting institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic development. However, Pamuk (2007, p.16) underscores the importance of institutional flexibility, noting that regions with greater institutional adaptability were better positioned to respond to new economic opportunities. This suggests that while initial inequality and institutional design were significant, the ability of institutions to adapt over time also influenced economic outcomes. The persistence of inequality in many New World societies reflects the enduring impact of initial institutional frameworks on long-term economic development.

Similarly, Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28) argue that the economic development of Africa has been shaped by a complex interplay of historical factors and structural impediments, including colonial legacies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa has emphasized the role of institutions in driving development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The institutional framework established during the colonial period has had lasting effects on African economies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) provide evidence that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, suggesting that colonial institutions played a significant role in economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this perspective by noting that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Their work suggests that the economic trajectories of African colonies were influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments and transport costs, which interacted with colonial institutions to shape economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This nuanced view underscores the importance of understanding the multifaceted nature of institutional influences on African economic development.

The slave trades have had a direct adverse effect on African economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the impact of the slave trade is not only a result of external factors but also deeply rooted in the cultural and social fabric of African societies. They assert that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and significantly impedes economic development. This mistrust operates through both external and internal channels, with the internal channel being at least twice as impactful as the external one. This internal mistrust can be attributed to the historical trauma inflicted by the slave trade, which created deep-seated skepticism towards others and undermined social cohesion. Nunn (2008, p.2) supports this view by highlighting the prolonged duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, which had a profound and lasting impact on African societies. The institutional legacies of the slave trades, including the disruption of social networks and the erosion of trust, continue to hinder economic progress. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this perspective by noting that the impact of the slave trades on African economies was not uniform and varied across different regions and historical periods. He argues that the influence of the slave trades must be considered in conjunction with other factors, such as indigenous economic and political actions, which also played a role in shaping African economic trajectories. This nuanced understanding underscores the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate secure property rights and enforce the rule of law, as shown by North (1989, p.6). This institutional framework is crucial for fostering economic development by reducing transaction costs and encouraging investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in securing property rights and promoting economic growth. This perspective is shared by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who highlight that European settlers established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those enforcing the rule of law, which significantly influenced current income per capita. The variation in institutional structures across colonies demonstrates that the type of institutions introduced by colonial powers had lasting effects on economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.8) further supports this view, emphasizing that good institutions are essential for providing incentives for investment, which is vital for development. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Hopkins 2009, p.8; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) underscores the critical role of institutions in economic growth, particularly in the context of colonial legacies.

The nuanced impact of colonial institutions on African economies is further elaborated by Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that the influence of the slave trades must be considered alongside indigenous economic and political actions. This perspective aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional design had profound implications for the economic trajectories of these regions. North (1989, p.9) provides a historical example of how the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth. This historical evidence (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15) shows that the type of institutions established during colonial periods, and their interaction with local economic and political factors, played a central role in shaping long-run economic growth.

The remaining disagreement centers on the uniformity of the slave trade's impact on African economies. Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies the perspective by noting that the impact of the slave trades on African economies was not uniform and varied across different regions and historical periods. This nuanced understanding underscores the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic outcomes in Africa. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional structures across colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and the role of indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15).
