The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. The stakes are high, as the answer determines whether institutions are a mere facilitator or a fundamental driver of economic prosperity. Two key positions exist in this debate: one emphasizes the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes, while the other argues that institutions are shaped by underlying economic and demographic factors. The former position holds that institutions, by providing a stable framework for economic activity, can significantly influence growth trajectories. The latter position suggests that institutions are more of a response to economic conditions rather than a primary cause of economic development. Understanding the relationship between institutions and economic growth is crucial for policy makers aiming to foster sustainable development. The extent to which institutions can be deliberately designed to promote growth, or whether they are merely a byproduct of economic conditions, is a contentious issue. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and new financial instruments played a pivotal role in reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering long-run economic growth. The establishment of institutions like the Bank of England in 1694 exemplifies this trend. The creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security in property rights. This security was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. This development was part of a broader political model where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues, addressing fiscal crises. This institutional framework helped secure property rights, which are fundamental to economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.6) further elaborate that the emergence of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and their effective enforcement are crucial. As these institutions developed, costs per transaction fell, but the aggregate volume of exchanges increased due to specialization and division of labor. The size of the transactions sector as a proportion of GNP grew, indicating a more robust economic framework. However, North (1989, p.6) notes that while transaction costs per exchange decreased, the overall transactions sector expanded, suggesting that the efficiency gains were offset by the increasing complexity of the economy. This dynamic highlights the intricate relationship between institutional development, transaction costs, and economic growth, where institutional changes are both a cause and a consequence of economic evolution.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) contend that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to distinct institutional frameworks in different colonies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. This contrasts with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences, rooted in European mortality rates, persisted over time and had profound economic consequences. The extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not foster long-run economic growth. The political power dynamics and the reluctance to initiate institutional change played a crucial role in shaping these extractive institutions. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions, shaped by European interests, continue to influence economic outcomes in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that different measures of institutions, such as constraints on the executive, law and order traditions, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, yield similar results in economic regressions. This consistency suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial times has a lasting impact on economic development, regardless of the specific institutional metric used.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This perspective posits that technological advancements, rather than institutional frameworks, are the fundamental cause of sustained economic development. These innovations necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to resolve conflicts arising from technological changes, such as those between modern technology and traditional rural lifestyles. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) cautions that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, as rent-seeking behaviors could erode the gains from technological progress (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutional adjustments in complementing technological advancements, ensuring that the benefits of useful knowledge are fully realized and sustained over time.

Ogilvie and other scholars (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. The institutions of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild are not merely efficiency-driven but are shaped by the struggles between different social groups over resource allocation. This institutional structure was not solely a response to economic efficiency but was deeply rooted in the power dynamics of the time. For instance, the craft guilds, which ostensibly aimed to maintain high standards of workmanship, also served to restrict competition and maintain the economic power of established artisans. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are merely tools for maximizing economic efficiency; instead, they are shaped by the underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts within society. The implications of this perspective extend to modern economic structures, where institutions continue to reflect historical power imbalances and distributional conflicts. This institutional framework, rooted in historical distributional conflicts, has significant implications for understanding contemporary economic disparities.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific changes aimed at improving governance and economic conditions, but does not directly address how these reforms relate to long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This approach emphasizes the role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The focus is on structural reforms that can enhance the quality of public goods and uphold the rule of law, even in ethnically heterogeneous societies. This perspective is complemented by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. They suggest that a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa can help assess the possibilities of future growth. This implies that institutional reforms, particularly those aimed at improving governance and economic conditions, can play a crucial role in addressing long-term economic disparities. However, the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered, highlighting the need for more comprehensive data to fully understand the impact of these reforms on long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2008, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal relationship between historical periods and economic outcomes in Africa. They note that this thesis fails to adequately account for the diverse paths of institutional evolution and economic policy. Austin (2008, p.2) identifies that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when settlers were absent, leading to shifts in institutional prescriptions to enhance material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). This mechanism suggests that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic and political actions, which influenced long-term growth patterns. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further highlight that the economic performance of many Anglophone African economies was above subsistence levels by the early twentieth century, indicating a more complex economic landscape than previously characterised (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding underscores the importance of local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions in shaping economic growth (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10).

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is not merely a reaction to the untrustworthiness of others but is also driven by internalised norms of mistrust. Nunn further elaborates that Africa's slave trades spanned nearly 500 years, significantly outlasting the period of official colonial rule, and had a profound adverse effect on economic development. This prolonged exposure to the slave trade disrupted social and economic institutions, creating a lasting impact on Africa's economic trajectory. The disruption was particularly severe in regions with less centralized governance and underdeveloped markets, as these areas were less equipped to withstand the economic shocks caused by the slave trade. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust and institutional disruption continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, highlighting the enduring impact of historical events on long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the enduring impact of historical events, particularly the slave trade, which disrupted social and economic institutions in Africa. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, which maximize rents to European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth, were established in regions with high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional legacy persists, influencing contemporary economic outcomes and highlighting the importance of institutional frameworks in economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the relationship between institutions and economic growth, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective aligns with North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they specify property rights and provide enforcement mechanisms that reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.9). Together, these perspectives illustrate how institutional frameworks, shaped by historical events and evolving over time, play a critical role in determining long-run economic growth.

The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of distributional conflicts, which Ogilvie argues provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe than efficiency alone (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups, rather than promoting economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shared finding highlights the importance of understanding the political and social contexts in which institutions operate, as these contexts shape the design and impact of institutions on economic growth. The historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on African economic institutions, as well as the role of distributional conflicts in shaping European institutions, illustrates the enduring influence of institutions on long-run economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement lies in how to measure the impact of institutions on economic growth, with Austin suggesting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies this relationship (Austin 2008, p.2). However, the historical evidence of the slave trade's impact on African economic institutions, as well as the role of distributional conflicts in shaping European institutions, shows that institutions play a critical role in determining long-run economic growth, regardless of the measurement approach used.
