The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game in society, can either foster or hinder economic growth by influencing incentives, shaping behavior, and determining the distribution of resources. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that cultural factors or geopolitical dynamics play a more significant role. The latter view suggests that institutions are merely a product of deeper cultural or historical forces, rather than independent variables that can be manipulated to achieve economic growth. The former view argues for the primacy of institutions, which can be shaped by policy and political will. This debate has substantial intellectual stakes, as it informs our understanding of how to promote economic development in underdeveloped regions. The resolution of this debate hinges on whether institutions can be designed to encourage growth, or if they are merely reflections of underlying cultural or historical determinants. North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. explain that European colonizers established divergent institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Conversely, in areas with low mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. further argue that these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions in former colonies significantly constrained their economic development trajectories. However, Acemoglu et al. note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century, suggesting that the interaction between institutions and the chance to industrialize played a crucial role in long-run development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This indicates that while extractive institutions impeded growth, the ability to industrialize could mitigate some of these negative effects.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the development of specific institutions, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, were pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694, along with the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby facilitating economic growth. This reduction in transaction costs was a direct result of increased security in the time dimension of property rights, which was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This institutional development was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight the importance of credible commitment in fostering economic growth. They argue that the success of economic institutions in England was underpinned by a strong commitment to property rights and the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This nuance is supported by the observation that France, despite its bureaucratic structure, showed almost parity in economic performance with England before the French Revolution. This indicates that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth can be influenced by other factors, such as the overall economic and political environment.

McCloskey and Mokyr argue that the economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was primarily driven by shifts in ideas and ideologies (McCloskey 2015, p.3; McCloskey 2015, p.4). These authors posit that the Industrial Enlightenment, characterized by a newfound tolerance for experimentation and open discourse, fostered an environment conducive to economic innovation. This ideological shift allowed for the uptake of new techniques and institutions, such as the electric motor and the modern research university. The tolerance for new ideas was crucial, as it enabled "eccentric men and women" to propose innovative concepts without fear of retribution. This openness was not merely a cultural shift but had tangible economic impacts, as seen in the Dutch Republic after 1600 and in England after 1688. The economic changes driven by these ideological shifts were profound, even influencing the stance of religious leaders. By the late nineteenth century, even the popes began to favor "capitalism" over socialism, reflecting the widespread acceptance of new economic ideas (McCloskey 2015, p.4). This ideological transformation highlights that economic growth can be significantly influenced by the prevailing beliefs and rhetorical conditions of a society.

Mokyr argues that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.49; Kuznets 1973, p.1). This perspective highlights that technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are fundamental to sustained economic improvement. Mokyr's emphasis on useful knowledge aligns with Kuznets's observation that technological changes require institutional adjustments to be effectively integrated into the economy. Kuznets notes that epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth, necessitate corresponding institutional and ideological adaptations (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This interaction between technological progress and institutional frameworks is crucial for economic growth. Kuznets further elaborates that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts, such as those arising from technological changes, is essential for maintaining the political fabric of society and ensuring sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6). The role of the sovereign state in resolving these conflicts underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating the benefits of technological progress. Mokyr cautions that while technological progress is relatively irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness is not as straightforward. This suggests that the sustainability of economic improvement depends on continuous institutional progress to complement technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Therefore, the interplay between knowledge growth and institutional frameworks is pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth.

The slave trades have had a profound and enduring impact on Africa's economic development, offering a critical perspective on the region's underdevelopment that extends beyond colonialism or recent events, as Nunn (2008, p.2); (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27) argue. The slave trades' legacy is evident in the deep-seated mistrust that persists within African societies, a cultural residue that significantly impedes economic progress. Nunn and Wantchekon's research highlights that this mistrust, which accounts for at least half of the slave trade's negative effect on trust, is a crucial barrier to economic development. The authors demonstrate that the internal norms of mistrust, shaped by the historical experience of the slave trades, are at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors. This internal mistrust erodes the social cohesion necessary for effective economic cooperation and institutional development. The slave trades' long-lasting effects are further exacerbated by the historical absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African regions. This institutional weakness, compounded by the legacy of mistrust, creates a challenging environment for economic growth. However, Nunn's work (Nunn 2008, p.19) suggests that the presence of centralized governance and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, can facilitate trade and economic development. This historical example underscores the importance of strong institutions in mitigating the adverse effects of the slave trades, highlighting the potential for institutional reforms to overcome the legacies of mistrust and underdevelopment.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that distributional conflicts, rather than institutional efficiency, are key to understanding the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe and New World societies. These institutions, such as serfdom and craft guilds, were shaped by the interests of elites, who used their political power to maintain inequality. The persistence of these institutions can be traced to the initial factor endowments of societies, which influenced the distribution of wealth, human capital, and political power. This dynamic is evident in the New World, where societies with extreme inequality saw the development of institutions that favored elites. The result was a system where government policies restricted competition and perpetuated inequality. Ogilvie points out that the belief that institutions are shaped by efficiency overlooks the role of power dynamics and distributional conflicts. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the enforcement of property rights. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or growth-promoting, directly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These findings underscore the importance of institutions in determining economic outcomes over the long term. North and Weingast argue that credible commitment underpins growth, as evidenced by the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This historical evidence demonstrates that secure property rights, facilitated by effective institutions, are crucial for economic development. The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This development highlights how institutions can reduce transaction costs and promote economic activity. The role of institutions in fostering economic growth is further illustrated by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions significantly influence long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the type of institutions established can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. The precise remaining disagreement is whether the persistence of extractive institutions is due to the initial distribution of resources or the political power of elites. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the political power of elites played a significant role in the establishment of extractive institutions. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence shows that the type of institutions established can have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes, regardless of the initial distribution of resources or the political power of elites.
