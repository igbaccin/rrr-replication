The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in the field of economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some economies thrive while others stagnate, and whether the institutional frameworks can be manipulated for better outcomes. The debate centres around the extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors. One perspective posits that institutions, by providing a stable framework for economic activity, are crucial for sustained growth. Another view suggests that while institutions are important, they are not the sole determinant and that other factors, such as geography or culture, play significant roles. Institutions shape long-run economic growth by establishing the rules that govern economic interactions, influencing the incentives for investment, and determining the distribution of resources. The extent to which institutions can be adapted to foster growth remains a contentious issue, with some arguing for the primacy of institutional change while others see it as just one piece of a complex puzzle. Institutions are not static entities; they evolve in response to economic and political pressures, and their impact on growth can vary significantly over time. This dynamic nature of institutions adds another layer of complexity to the debate, as it implies that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is a complex interplay of various factors. This interplay is evident in the historical context of European colonization, where the type of institutions established in colonies had a lasting impact on their economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and settler motives, and these institutions had lasting impacts on economic development. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutional differences persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional design was a response to the political and economic interests of the colonizing powers, which prioritized short-term extraction over long-term development. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This perspective highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, suggesting that the legacies of colonial rule continue to shape economic development paths in former colonies.

Frankema argues that British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers, contributing to higher per capita income levels by the early twentieth century (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The British colonial administration implemented a range of social and political institutions that altered the perceived benefits of formal education. This shift was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, who saw value in the new educational opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.15). These educational investments were closely tied to economic growth, as the demand for missionary education increased alongside the development of new economic institutions. The British colonial strategy, which emphasized formal education, played a crucial role in shaping the economic landscape of sub-Saharan Africa. This educational legacy contributed to the region's economic performance, as evidenced by the higher per capita income levels observed by the early twentieth century. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied across countries, influenced by local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The British emphasis on formal education set a foundation for economic development that persisted as other factors fluctuated.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the institutional framework in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for economic growth, as it fostered secure property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms. The evolution of political institutions in England, which specified and enforced property rights, significantly reduced transaction costs. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that this institutional framework facilitated market exchanges by lowering costs per transaction, which in turn stimulated economic growth. The institutional changes in England involved economic rules and the political factors that ensured these rules were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This commitment to institutional stability was crucial for fostering economic growth, as it provided a reliable framework for long-term investments and market exchanges.

The interplay between distributional conflicts and institutions suggests that economic growth is influenced by a complex web of factors, not just institutional frameworks alone. Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman shared this finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Distributional conflicts are argued to be the fundamental drivers of long-run economic growth, challenging the notion that institutions are the primary cause. Ogilvie argues that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts. This perspective challenges the conventional view that institutions are primarily driven by efficiency goals. Similarly, Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, thereby restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. This suggests that institutions are shaped by underlying power dynamics and distributional conflicts. These findings underscore the importance of considering the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions and, consequently, economic growth. The relationship between distributional conflicts and institutions is complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie highlights that institutions influence distributional conflicts through the rules and norms they establish. This reciprocal relationship means that while distributional conflicts shape institutions, institutions also reinforce or alter these conflicts over time. This dynamic interplay suggests that institutions and distributional conflicts are interdependent, with each influencing the other in a continuous feedback loop.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the causal pathways of economic development, particularly in the context of African economic history (Austin 2008, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This simplification is evident in the thesis's treatment of colonial institutions, which it often reduces to binary outcomes of extractive or growth-promoting. Austin shows that colonial administrations could adapt their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's preference for a higher production equilibrium contradicted Nunn's hypothesis of a lower equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). Frankema and van Waijenburg further qualify this by noting that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis may overlook the dynamic nature of institutional change and the potential for endogenous growth even under colonial rule. The implications of this are significant, as it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional evolution and its impact on long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Hopkins, examine African economic development through the lens of women's contributions and colonial influences, focusing on these factors as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Hopkins 2009, p.18). African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The marginalization of women's roles in economic development is a significant oversight, as women were integral to agricultural production and market activities. This dynamic underscores the importance of recognizing women's economic agency, which has been historically underappreciated. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, further marginalizing women's access to land and economic opportunities. This legal framework, introduced through a dual legal system, had profound implications for gender dynamics and economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This perspective highlights the need for a more inclusive approach to economic development, one that acknowledges the multifaceted contributions of women and the enduring impacts of colonial legal frameworks.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trades in Africa had a direct adverse effect on long-run economic development. The slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, left a lasting imprint on African societies, fostering a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust, in turn, has hindered economic development by undermining social capital and cooperation. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade's impact on trust is deeply rooted in internal norms of mistrust. This internalization of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in various economic activities, including trade and political institutions. Nunn emphasizes that the duration of the slave trades, far exceeding the period of colonial rule, played a pivotal role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. This historical context is crucial for understanding the enduring effects of the slave trades on African economic development. This points to a broader implication: the legacy of the slave trades is about the economic losses incurred and the institutional weaknesses that have persisted over centuries.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. This progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments to ensure efficient use of innovations. In this context, the growth of useful knowledge is about technological advancements and the institutional frameworks that support and facilitate these advancements. These frameworks are crucial for resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes. For example, Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts. This suggests that while technological progress is essential, the institutional environment must also evolve to sustain economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) qualifies this by noting that the growth of economic reasonableness is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while technological progress can drive economic growth, the sustainability of this growth depends on the ability of institutions to adapt and evolve. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is about providing a stable framework for technological progress and ensuring that this progress is sustained over the long term.

The literature on budget policies and land systems in Africa reveals specific institutional dynamics that, while important, do not directly address the broader question of long-run economic growth. Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) share a focus on the institutional dynamics of post-colonial Africa, with Simson (2019, p.2) examining East Africa's budget policies and Peters investigating land systems. This dynamic reveals a mechanism where institutional design, rooted in patrimonial practices, can hinder the delivery of broad-based public goods and services, thereby impacting economic development. Peters (2004, p.5) qualifies this by noting that the final decade of colonial rule saw a push for agricultural development, suggesting that institutional changes can occur. This implies that while patrimonial institutions may initially impede economic growth, there is potential for institutional evolution that could support long-run economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) share the posture that predicting long-run economic performance requires a nuanced understanding of the skill premium and the economic data that underpin it. They argue that the skill premium is a crucial predictor of long-term economic performance, as it reflects the costs and benefits of investing in human capital. Van Zanden elaborates that the skill premium is determined by factors such as training costs, the probability of successful skill acquisition, the interest rate, and the duration of skilled employment (van Zanden 2009, p.3). Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) highlight the challenges in estimating national accounts, particularly for distant historical periods, and the subjective nature of these estimates. This implies that while the skill premium is a valuable indicator, its measurement is fraught with uncertainties that can obscure its predictive power.

McCloskey (2015, p.3; 2015, p.4) argue that the economic transformation in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was primarily driven by shifts in ideas and ideologies, which fostered an environment conducive to innovation and experimentation. These ideologies facilitated a cultural shift where people were encouraged to experiment and openly discuss their findings, leading to the uptake of new technologies and institutions. This ideological change allowed for the creation of environments where innovative ideas could flourish without fear of persecution, as seen in the Dutch Republic after 1600 and in England after 1688. Such societal tolerance was crucial for the acceptance of new economic practices and technologies, which were driven by a broader cultural acceptance of innovation. This intellectual environment, where radical ideas were not met with violent responses, paved the way for the Industrial Enlightenment. The Catholic Church's eventual acceptance of capitalism over socialism by the late nineteenth century further underscores the influence of ideological shifts on economic practices. The impact of these ideas on economic growth was not immediate; it required time for these new ideologies to permeate society and for economic practices to align with these new beliefs.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to mitigate transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges. North (1989, p.6) argues that as economies grow, the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement will lead to falling costs per transaction. This institutional framework is critical for economic growth, as it fosters an environment where market exchanges can flourish. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) also highlights the transformative potential of institutions, suggesting that they should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian. This shared emphasis on the role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), who show that European colonists established different institutions in their colonies based on mortality rates, which in turn significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, have long-lasting effects on economic outcomes. This continuity of institutional influence is further illustrated by the role of the Bank of England, which North (1989, p.9) describes as a key institution in the English financial revolution, leading to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and supporting economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of colonialism and its lasting effects on the economies of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and show that this reversal was related to industrialization. They provide evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This institutional perspective is further supported by Austin (2008, p.2), who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic interaction between institutions and economic development highlights the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The specific historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates. The creation of these institutions, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), had long-lasting effects on economic outcomes, demonstrating the enduring impact of institutional changes on economic growth. This historical evidence provides a clear measurement of the institutional influence on economic growth, as it shows the direct relationship between the type of institutions established and the economic outcomes in former colonies.
