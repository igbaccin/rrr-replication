The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic literature. The stakes are high: if institutions are key drivers of growth, then understanding their mechanisms can guide policy and development efforts. The live disagreement centers around the nature of institutions and their causal pathways. Some scholars argue that institutions matter because they shape incentives, enforce property rights, and facilitate market exchanges, thus driving economic growth. Others contend that distributional conflicts and political dynamics are more critical in determining institutional outcomes. Additionally, there is a debate over whether institutions are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or endogenous processes like political struggles. The interplay between these factors and their impact on long-run economic growth remains a subject of ongoing scholarly inquiry. Austin and North both acknowledge the importance of institutions in economic growth, with Austin noting that the institutionalist approach emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; North 1989, p.6).

North and Weingast, along with North, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The development of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, for instance, led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which in turn enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution was driven by the need to resolve fiscal crises, such as those stemming from the Hundred Years' War, which necessitated a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework facilitated market exchanges and ensured that transaction costs were falling while the transactions sector was growing as a proportion of GNP. This dynamic was underpinned by increasing specialization and division of labor, which multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). However, North and Weingast's emphasis on the revolutionary nature of these institutional changes is qualified by recent research that suggests France's economic performance before the French Revolution was not as disparate from England's as traditionally thought, implying that the institutional changes in England might not have been as transformative as initially posited.

Building on this, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and McCloskey (2015, p.3) argue that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by distributional conflicts and ideologies, not just efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions were primarily driven by efficiency, highlighting instead the role of power dynamics and ideological shifts. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) illustrates this with the example of craft guilds, which were not merely efficient structures but also tools for maintaining power and control over resources. This ideological shift was about economic efficiency and creating an environment where new ideas could flourish without fear of disapproval. However, while Ogilvie and McCloskey both underscore the importance of ideologies and distributional conflicts, McCloskey (2015, p.4) qualifies this by noting that even religious institutions, which were often seen as barriers to economic change, could eventually adapt to new economic realities. This adaptation suggests that while ideologies and distributional conflicts were crucial, they were not the sole determinants of institutional change. The interplay between these factors and the broader economic landscape created a dynamic environment where institutions could evolve in response to both internal and external pressures.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) establish that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not foster long-run economic growth. Instead, they created persistent barriers to development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) elaborate on this by explaining that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often extractive, shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their own rents rather than promote economic development. This dynamic highlights a key mechanism: the design of institutions by colonizers to serve their interests, rather than those of the colonized, had lasting effects on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by demonstrating that the type of institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced current income per capita. This finding underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) qualify this by noting that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity for industrialization during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies. This suggests that while extractive institutions were detrimental, the potential for industrialization could mitigate some of the negative effects, offering a pathway for economic growth despite the institutional legacies of colonial rule.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The mechanism they identify is that these institutions were designed to advantage elites through various government policies, which in turn restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This institutional bias toward elites was rooted in the initial factor endowments of these societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, which led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This historical context is crucial because it highlights how initial conditions, such as the presence of slave trades, can shape institutional development and economic outcomes in the long run. Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this implication by showing that sustained trade and economic development were contingent on the presence of centralized governments and well-developed markets and trading networks, as seen in the Kongo kingdom. This underscores that the impact of inequality on institutions is mediated by the presence of centralized governance and economic infrastructure.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that institutional change in African colonies was influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, impacting the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This is because the quality of public goods provided by African governments is not significantly affected by ethno-linguistic fragmentation. Thus, African societies can be ethnically heterogeneous and still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that the demand for missionary education depended on a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders on the macro level and African families on the micro level. The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities. This dynamic suggests that the quality of public goods, such as education, was contingent on the interaction between colonial policies and local adaptations. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that the timing and scale of growth in export production, tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, varied owing to a range of factors, including political institutions. This implies that the effectiveness of colonial institutions in promoting economic growth was moderated by the broader economic context and local political dynamics. The presence of well-developed markets and trading networks, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, underscores that the impact of inequality on institutions is mediated by the presence of centralized governance and economic infrastructure.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it hinders the establishment of strong institutions. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has eroded social cohesion, making it difficult to build the necessary social capital for effective institutional development. The internal norms of mistrust, fostered by the slave trade, are at least twice as impactful as external factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin complements this perspective by highlighting that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which could mitigate but not fully overcome the entrenched mistrust (Austin 2008, p.15). The persistence of this mistrust suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape Africa's institutional landscape, influencing the effectiveness of contemporary governance and economic policies.

Institutional legacies from colonial periods, shaped by European mortality rates and settler presence, persistently influence contemporary economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that high mortality rates led Europeans to establish extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27), which continue to affect current income per capita. This perspective is complemented by Austin's analysis, which highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation, however, did not fully mitigate the entrenched mistrust and extractive nature of the institutions imposed. The legacy of these institutions, therefore, continues to shape Africa's institutional landscape, influencing the effectiveness of contemporary governance and economic policies.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by North's emphasis on the creation of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North argues that the development of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling transaction costs and increased economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework is crucial for facilitating market exchanges and promoting economic development. Ogilvie extends this by noting that shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights can block the institutions that might guarantee property rights and contract enforcement (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This interplay between institutions and cultural beliefs highlights the complex nature of institutional development and its impact on economic growth.

The persistence of these institutional legacies is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that this reversal is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The new economic history of Africa, informed by institutional analysis, can guide reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The historical evidence of colonial institutional legacies and the empirical comparison of economic outcomes across former colonies settle the disagreement over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific measurement of income per capita and the case of former colonies show that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a lasting impact on economic development.
