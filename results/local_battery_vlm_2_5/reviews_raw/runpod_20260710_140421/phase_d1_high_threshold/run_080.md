The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic interactions, are central to understanding why some economies prosper while others stagnate. At the heart of this debate lies the disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic growth and those who argue for alternative explanations. The former group posits that institutions determine the incentives for investment, innovation, and productivity, thereby driving long-term economic performance. Rival explanations emphasize factors such as population density, fiscal crises, and the specific historical contexts of colonial policies. The stakes are high: a clear understanding of the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geographic conditions or historical legacies, play a more significant role. This leads to the question of how institutions interact with other variables to shape economic outcomes over the long run. For example, the creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution reflected increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. This example illustrates how the emergence of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement can reduce transaction costs and facilitate market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) establish that colonial institutions shaped economic growth in former colonies. Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, leading to varying institutional setups. In settler colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elaborate that these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. The institutional frameworks established during colonial times persist, influencing the economic trajectories of these regions even today. The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) argue that colonial impacts on African development are more complex than simplistic causal narratives suggest, and that different historical periods and paths influenced economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that European settlers' presence or absence influenced the expansion of indigenous commodity production and the institutional prescriptions of colonial governments. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to have a material interest in the status quo, often leading to higher production equilibriums. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision, which countered Nunn's hypothesis by opting for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). Hopkins emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa reveals that 'good' institutions are crucial for this process (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The implications of these findings are nuanced by Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28), who argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments; rather, a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa is necessary to assess the possibilities of future growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). These insights collectively suggest that the institutional legacies of colonialism in Africa are shaped by specific historical contexts and economic actions.

This complexity is further illustrated by the profound adverse effect of the slave trades in Africa on long-term economic development, as argued by Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn both argue that the slave trade had profound effects on African economic development. The slave trade disrupted social structures and impeded the development of strong institutions, which are crucial for sustained economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hinders cooperation and collective action. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, contra those who might attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events. Nunn also documents that sustained trade did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, located just south of the Zaire river. Because the Kongo kingdom had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, it was able to support trade with the Europeans. Nunn and Wantchekon also show that the internal channel of mistrust is at least twice as large as the external channel, suggesting that the internal norms of mistrust are a significant barrier to economic development. This highlights the enduring impact of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's economic development trajectory.

This historical perspective aligns with Mokyr’s argument that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, a position shared by Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are pivotal for sustained economic improvement. This perspective is echoed by Kuznets, who notes that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Furthermore, Mokyr highlights the role of institutions in complementing technological progress, asserting that without institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This institutional support is crucial for mitigating coordination failures and ensuring that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economy. Kuznets also points out that modern economic growth is contingent on resolving growth-induced conflicts without excessive costs, suggesting that the political fabric of society must remain strong to support long-term economic development. This implies that while technological progress is essential, the institutional environment plays a critical role in facilitating and sustaining economic growth.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman show that distributional conflicts, not efficiency, shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe and the New World societies. Ogilvie contends that these institutions, including serfdom and guilds, were the result of conflicts over resource distribution. This perspective is supported by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that New World societies with greater inequality were characterized by institutions that advantaged elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality. These institutions were shaped by the unequal distribution of wealth and power, which in turn influenced the development of policies that favored the elite. This distributional conflict is evident in the establishment of institutions that secured elite interests, such as property rights and political structures that maintained their dominance. A key implication of this perspective is that the persistence of inequality can be understood as a result of institutional designs that reflect and reinforce the power dynamics of the elite. This view is nuanced by the recognition that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific mechanisms through which these conflicts shape institutions can vary significantly across different contexts. For instance, the specific historical and cultural contexts of Europe and the New World societies influenced how distributional conflicts manifested and the types of institutions that emerged. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with scholars emphasizing different aspects of institutional impact. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European settlers' strategies, significantly influenced economic outcomes in colonies. In regions with high mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in areas with lower mortality, settlers implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence highlights how initial colonial policies laid the groundwork for enduring economic disparities. Austin agrees that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which is shown by the fact that the behavior of older industrial economies is understood in the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin shows that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were responsive to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that local contexts shaped institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This shared finding demonstrates that the specific strategies and responses of colonial powers, as well as the local contexts of colonies, played crucial roles in determining the types of institutions that emerged and their long-term economic impacts.

The enduring influence of institutions on economic growth is further underscored by North's work, which emphasizes that institutions matter in economic growth because they specify "efficient" property rights and provide effective enforcement, which in turn facilitates market exchanges and reduces transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights and contributing to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The development of political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is critical for economic growth. This is shown by the fact that the emergence of these institutions should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges, which in turn reduces costs per transaction, but increases the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies the aggregate volume of exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework supports long-term economic growth by enhancing market efficiency and reducing transaction costs. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. Hopkins shows that the prominence attached to the role of institutions enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Hopkins also shows that good institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is shown by the fact that the scope of development policy widened to incorporate what became known as 'governance', and policy recommendations allowed institutions and organizations to come to the fore (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This shows that institutional reforms are essential for addressing poverty and promoting economic development in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is also shaped by distributional conflicts. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie shows that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property, or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development, or simply may be reluctant to initiate institutional change because they would not be the direct beneficiaries of the resulting economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).
