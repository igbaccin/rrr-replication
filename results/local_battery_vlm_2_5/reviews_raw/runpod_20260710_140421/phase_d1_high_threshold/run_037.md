The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. At stake is the identification of the causal mechanisms that drive economic performance over extended periods. The live disagreement revolves around the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography, culture, and initial conditions. Some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in creating stable property rights, enforcing contracts, and reducing transaction costs. Others contend that institutions are merely one of many factors influencing growth, with geography and initial conditions playing crucial roles. The debate extends to the nature of institutions themselves, with some scholars focusing on their extractive versus inclusive character and others highlighting their efficiency or distributional aspects. The core of the disagreement lies in whether institutions are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth or if they are secondary to other factors. The answer to this question carries profound implications for policy and theory, influencing how economists and policymakers approach economic development. The nature of institutions and their causal mechanisms are central to this debate, as evidenced by the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) and North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This institutional framework was shaped by politically powerful groups who benefited from the existing extractive institutions. These groups had little incentive to initiate institutional change that would not directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This pattern of institutional development was influenced by population density and prosperity, which in turn shaped the policies pursued by the Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional legacy persists, affecting current income per capita in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

North (1989, p.9) argues that specific institutional developments, such as the creation of the Bank of England, were crucial for long-run economic growth, challenging neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The emergence of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement led to a decline in transaction costs, fostering economic growth. This institutional change was not merely about creating new rules but also about establishing mechanisms that ensured these rules were enforced and could be adapted over time. This financial revolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) further underscore the importance of political institutions in underpinning economic growth, noting that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The commitment of the regime to these rules was a critical factor in ensuring their effectiveness. This qualification suggests that while institutional changes were important, their impact on economic growth was not as singularly decisive as some theories might suggest. The implications of this research indicate that while specific institutional developments were pivotal, the broader context and existing economic conditions also played significant roles in shaping long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49)  argue that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks. The mechanism by which this occurs is the development of epochal innovations, which constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, driving economic growth by enhancing productivity and efficiency. The growth of useful knowledge and technological progress are central to economic growth, as they foster innovation and efficiency gains. Mokyr highlights the role of institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, while Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate the efficient use of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further suggests that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring the sustainability of economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that economic reasonableness, which includes institutional progress, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress. This implies that while institutional adjustments are necessary, they are not sufficient on their own to sustain economic growth. The implication is that technological progress, driven by the growth of useful knowledge, is the primary force behind long-run economic growth, with institutions playing a supporting role in facilitating and sustaining this progress.

This perspective shifts the focus from institutions as drivers to the underlying conflicts that shape them, suggesting a more complex relationship between institutions and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both argue that distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions. Ogilvie's work highlights that in pre-industrial Europe, institutions like serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were shaped by the power struggles between different social groups. This distributional conflict perspective posits that institutions are not merely efficiency-driven but are the result of negotiations and power dynamics among various social groups. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this idea to New World societies, showing that initial factor endowments and the resulting inequality led to institutional frameworks that advantaged elites. They argue that these institutional differences, driven by distributional conflicts, significantly influenced the long-term economic trajectories of these societies. However, this perspective is qualified by the recognition that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific outcomes of these conflicts can vary widely depending on the context and the historical period. This variation implies that the relationship between distributional conflicts and institutional development is complex and multifaceted. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) suggests that to fully understand economic growth, we must incorporate institutions as indispensable tools in our research programs and consider the broader social and political contexts in which these institutions operate.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting contemporary trust levels (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it affects both internal norms and external environments. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) find that the internal channel of mistrust, which involves individuals' internal norms, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. This legacy has shaped contemporary economic outcomes by creating a context where trust is diminished, hindering the development of secure private property rights and stable economic institutions. The enduring effects of the slave trade on trust levels in Africa highlight the deep-seated nature of institutional legacies and their role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner highlight that Anglophone African economies exhibited per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging conventional views of the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding is shared by Broadberry and Gupta, who argue that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800 (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The shared posture of these scholars is that historical patterns of growth and income levels in these regions challenge conventional narratives about their economic development. Export production and foreign trade were key drivers of economic growth in African economies, with variations in growth timing and scale tied to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The uneven distribution of the fruits of early modern development is evident in the differing surpluses over basic food needs yielded by skilled and unskilled laborers. This uneven distribution suggests that institutional changes, such as those in agriculture, played a crucial role in driving economic development. However, the role of institutional changes in driving economic growth in Asia is less clear, as Parthasarathi's explanation of high productivity in southern Indian agriculture raises logical difficulties (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.15). The implication is that the institutional context in which economic growth occurs is critical, and that the same institutional changes may not yield the same results in different regions.

The literature on institutional reforms in Africa highlights specific interventions aimed at reducing poverty, but does not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009 (Hopkins 2009, p.18); Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012 (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28)). The role of institutions in this context is crucial, as they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Englebert's findings, as cited by Hopkins, suggest that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not significantly affect the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Therefore, the presence of poor institutions is not necessarily linked to the ethnic composition of a society. This insight is important because it implies that institutional reforms can be effective regardless of a country's ethnic makeup. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered. This gap in historical data means that the long-term effects of institutional reforms on economic growth remain uncertain. The implication is that while institutional reforms can address poverty in the short term, their impact on long-run economic growth is less clear.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by creating an environment that encourages investment and economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, with some enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment while others set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of these colonies, demonstrating that institutions can significantly influence economic outcomes over the long term. North further emphasizes the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are crucial for economic development (North 1989, p.6). These findings highlight that the quality of institutions, rather than just their presence, is a key determinant of long-run economic growth.

The historical context of institutional development provides further insights into their role in economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interactions between institutions and economic development by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve over time and interact with other factors to shape economic outcomes. Hopkins builds on this by showing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This indicates that a deeper understanding of historical institutional development can guide effective policy interventions to promote economic growth. Hopkins also shows that ethnically homogeneous societies can still be dysfunctional, and ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This insight is important because it implies that institutional reforms can be effective regardless of a country's ethnic makeup.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth are also well-documented. North highlights that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This shows that institutions can reduce transaction costs and enhance economic efficiency. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the distributional effects of institutions are a critical factor in their impact on economic growth. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This underscores the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape institutional development and their impact on economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The remaining disagreement centers on whether the impact of institutions on economic growth is primarily driven by their efficiency or by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). The historical evidence of European colonial institutions shows that extractive institutions designed to maximize rents to politically powerful groups had lasting negative effects on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This evidence settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the distributional effects of institutions are a critical factor in their impact on economic growth.
