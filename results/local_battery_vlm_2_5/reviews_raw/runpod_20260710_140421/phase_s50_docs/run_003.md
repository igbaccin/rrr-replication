The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory and history. At stake is the ability to explain divergent economic trajectories among societies over centuries. Institutions are the rules of the game that shape economic interactions and incentivize investment and innovation. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the role of institutions in securing property rights and enforcing contracts, thereby fostering economic growth. Another position argues that institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts and cultural beliefs, which can hinder or promote economic development. A third position contends that the impact of institutions on economic growth is contingent on historical and contextual factors. This debate is crucial for understanding the root causes of economic disparities and for designing effective policies to promote growth. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether they are merely one factor among many, remains unresolved. This question is central to the debate over how best to understand the causes of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001; 2002) share a finding that institutional differences produce economic consequences, highlighting that institutions matter in economic growth. This institutional framework fostered an environment conducive to economic development, as it provided the necessary security for property rights and facilitated market exchanges. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) build on this by noting that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to encourage long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This implies that the institutional framework established during colonialism could have long-lasting effects on economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), underscores the importance of institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These changes, which were influenced by factors such as population density and prosperity, played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. Thus, the institutional legacy of colonialism continues to shape economic growth patterns in former colonies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This evolution was marked by a shift towards institutions that ensured credible commitments and secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast highlight the role of political factors in underpinning economic growth, emphasizing that the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange was critical. This commitment encompassed both the rules themselves and the institutions that enforced and changed these rules. Austin suggests that the institutionalist approach, which has been influential in economic historiography, has limitations but also offers valuable insights into the long-term economic growth of countries. Austin's work points out that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as they might seem. This is because France's economic performance before the French Revolution has been significantly upgraded, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not the sole determinant of economic growth. Austin (2008, p.15) further qualifies this by showing that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This implies that the evolution of institutions is influenced by various factors, including the actions of the colonized populations. The implication is that the institutional framework established during colonialism could have long-lasting effects on economic development.

The economic contributions of women and the educational policies implemented during colonial rule also played significant roles in shaping these long-lasting effects. This shared finding (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15) underscores how the economic activities of women and the educational policies of colonial powers were intrinsically linked. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. The British colonial administration's promotion of formal education had a significant impact on the economic landscape. However, the codification of customary law under colonial rule created patriarchal alliances that further entrenched women's subordinate status, limiting their economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The implications of these gender dynamics and educational policies are profound, as they shaped the economic landscape in ways that persist today.

The institutional reforms in Africa are seen as a means to eliminate poverty, as Hopkins and Simson assert, challenging the broader notion that institutions themselves are the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). To address this, Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is supported by Simson's observation that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8; Simson 2019, p.2). Simson's work on East Africa's budget policies underscores the importance of understanding the mechanisms through which institutions operate. Simson argues that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, contra the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services. This suggests that the role of institutions in economic growth is more nuanced than simply providing incentives for investment. The focus on consumption over public goods, as Simson notes, indicates that institutional reforms must address the underlying political and economic structures that shape budgetary decisions. This highlights the complexity of institutional reforms and their impact on economic growth.

The legacy of these trades is not just economic but also deeply embedded in the political and social fabric of these regions, influencing contemporary development challenges. Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trades fostered a culture of mistrust, which continues to impede economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust operates through two channels: an external environment of weak rule of law and low trustworthiness of others, and an internalized norm of mistrust. Nunn and Wantchekon find that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel. This suggests that the legacy of the slave trades is a deeply ingrained social norm that continues to affect economic interactions. Nunn notes that the duration of the slave trades, spanning nearly 500 years, was far longer than the period of colonial rule. This temporal depth indicates that the slave trades had a more formative influence on African societies than colonialism. The role of centralized governance in facilitating trade, as Nunn notes, highlights the importance of strong institutions in economic development. The historical absence of such institutions in many African regions, exacerbated by the slave trades, underscores the enduring impact of these trades on contemporary economic structures.

Distributional conflicts are a more fundamental driver of economic institutions than efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) show that these conflicts shape economic institutions by influencing the distribution of power and resources among different groups. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power led to institutions that favored elites. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) illustrate that the initial factor endowments in these societies, such as abundant land and natural resources relative to labor, created conditions for extreme inequality. This inequality, in turn, influenced the development of institutions that restricted competition and maintained elite advantages. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that understanding the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions requires a nuanced approach that considers the historical and contextual factors at play. The persistence of inequality in these societies highlights the enduring impact of initial distributional conflicts on economic institutions, which can have long-lasting effects on economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in the historical context of property rights and political structures. North argues that institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing enforcement are critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, which are essential for a growing economy. North's emphasis on the political dimension of property rights aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s argument that the type of institutions established during colonization significantly influenced long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The extractive institutions set up by Europeans in colonies with high mortality rates persisted and had lasting effects on current income per capita. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of initial institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. The development of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as seen in the United States and New Zealand, contrasts with the extractive states in regions like the Congo, illustrating how different colonization strategies shaped economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The historical evolution of institutions is also shaped by distributional conflicts and the interests of powerful groups. Ogilvie emphasizes the need to incorporate institutions into long-term research programs, suggesting that understanding the role of distributional conflicts in shaping institutions requires a nuanced approach that considers historical and contextual factors (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of inequality in societies with extractive institutions underscores the lasting effects of initial distributional conflicts on economic development. The shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North 1989, p.6) is the underlying point of agreement among these scholars. The precise remaining disagreement lies in the mechanisms through which institutions shape economic growth. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement is settled by the empirical comparison of institutions in former European colonies and their impact on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among these colonies shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in long-run development.
