The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development, as it addresses the mechanisms through which societies achieve sustained prosperity. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural beliefs, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for long-term economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated and that factors like population density, prosperity, or cultural beliefs are more influential in determining economic outcomes. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth in both developed and developing countries. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the key to unlocking sustained economic prosperity or if they are merely one piece of a much larger puzzle. Institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. In colonies with high European mortality rates, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas in colonies with low mortality rates, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. This institutional design persisted over time, leading to persistent economic underdevelopment in these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores the importance of historical institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth.

North and Weingast argue that secure property rights fostered by specific institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). They contend that the evolution of these institutions was critical for enhancing the security of property rights over time, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This institutional framework provided a stable environment for long-term capital markets and economic growth. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights. However, North (1989, p.9) cautions that while these institutions were instrumental, the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate would be a growing proportion of GNP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges. This suggests that while secure property rights were necessary for growth, the complexity and scale of economic exchanges also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic growth. The institutional framework that ensured secure property rights was not only about the rules themselves but also about the enforcement mechanisms that backed them, which is why the political factors underpinning these institutions were so critical.

The critical role of political factors in shaping institutions that favored elites is evident in the inequality of New World societies, as argued by Sokoloff and Engerman, alongside Nunn. These institutions, shaped by the initial conditions of extreme inequality, persisted over time and restricted competition, thus maintaining high levels of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the initial factor endowments of New World societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, creating a feedback loop where institutions reinforced existing inequalities. The persistence of inequality in these societies can be traced to the institutional frameworks that restricted competition and favored elites. Nunn's work (Nunn 2008, p.19) complements this by showing how the slave trade, which exacerbated inequality, also had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development. This suggests that the institutional frameworks that emerged in societies with high levels of inequality were shaped by both economic factors and the historical context of slavery and colonialism. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further emphasize the importance of trust in economic development, highlighting that the slave trade's impact on trust has had enduring effects on economic outcomes.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both argue (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective posits that technological advancements and knowledge accumulation are fundamental to sustained economic development, rather than institutions. The mechanism through which this operates is the efficient use of innovations, which requires institutional and ideological adjustments to mitigate conflicts arising from technological changes. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and large family structures, necessitating significant societal adjustments (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process is not merely about adopting new technologies but also involves creating institutions that facilitate their effective use. Mokyr (2005, p.49) elaborates on this by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements are not sustainable and may be eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) qualifies this by emphasizing the crucial role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, suggesting that while technological progress is key, the institutional framework must also evolve to support it (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is essential, the institutional environment must adapt to ensure that technological advancements lead to sustained economic growth.

The complex interactions between economic, political, and social factors shaped institutions in pre-industrial Europe, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial governments often adapted their institutional policies in response to local economic and political conditions, which in turn influenced the production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This highlights that the adoption and efficacy of institutions were contingent on the specific contexts in which they were embedded. This suggests that the institutional landscape was shaped by power dynamics and the need to manage conflicts, rather than purely economic rationales. This dynamic nature of institutions underscores the importance of adaptability in institutional frameworks. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) cautions that institutions must be seen as indispensable instruments in the economic historian's toolkit (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This implies that while institutions are shaped by various factors, they also play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly influenced institutional change, with Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15) arguing that the negotiation and imposition of colonial policies played a pivotal role in shaping African institutions. This dynamic process highlights how colonial rule could both constrain and enable institutional development, depending on local contexts and colonial policies. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2; 2022, p.10) qualify this by showing that economic growth in many Anglophone African economies was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. This qualification underscores that while colonial policies shaped institutions, their impact on economic growth was mediated by broader economic and political factors.

Colonial policies had profound impacts on the institutions of African economies, with extractive institutions being established in colonies where European settlers faced high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The impact of these institutions on economic growth varied. For instance, in Anglophone African economies, economic growth was tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This shows that while colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes, their effects were mediated by broader economic and political factors. This theme of mediation continues into the work of Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin shows that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating that local factors played a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further underscored by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants. North shows that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This security is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself (North 1989, p.6). Ogilvie builds on this by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This suggests that the institutions established during colonial rule reflected both efficiency considerations and the interests of politically powerful groups. Hopkins extends this line of thought by arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18). Hopkins shows that 'good' institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by North (1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was uniform or mediated by broader economic and political factors, as shown by Austin (2008, p.2). The historical evidence of how colonial administrations changed their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action settles this disagreement.
