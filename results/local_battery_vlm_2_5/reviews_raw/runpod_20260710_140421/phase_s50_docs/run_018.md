The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the understanding of why some societies thrive while others stagnate. Two key positions dominate the debate. One emphasizes the foundational role of institutions in fostering economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and market exchanges. The other position challenges this view, arguing that the role of institutions has been overstated and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs and historical contingencies, play a more significant role in economic development. The debate is further complicated by the recognition that institutions themselves are shaped by a complex interplay of historical, political, and social factors. This interplay suggests that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is a dynamic process where institutions both influence and are influenced by economic outcomes. The key question is how and under what conditions institutions exert their influence on long-run economic growth. The debate is particularly salient in the context of former European colonies, where the legacies of colonial institutions continue to shape economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional evolution fostered a long-term capital market and promoted economic growth. This institutional development was crucial for economic growth, as it allowed for a reduction in transaction costs per transaction, despite the growing proportion of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutions in securing property rights was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts played a significant role in shaping economic institutions, especially in pre-industrial Europe, where institutions were influenced by power dynamics and social structures. The role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions highlights that these institutions were influenced by power dynamics and social structures (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are solely driven by efficiency, and instead emphasizes the role of power dynamics in their formation. Ogilvie asserts that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were better explained by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. This argument is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight the role of patriarchal alliances and customary law in codifying the subordinate status of women, which in turn shaped economic institutions. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities. This dynamic is evident in the case of Krobo and Akuapem farmers in the Gold Coast, where men played a central role in the codification of customary law, propelling the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. The role of women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods was significant, highlighting the complexity of distributional conflicts and their impact on economic institutions. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how power dynamics and social structures shape economic institutions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) establish that colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. This influence was mediated by the mortality rates of European settlers. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans established extractive institutions designed to maximize resource extraction. This institutional design was driven by the desire to quickly transfer resources to the metropole, leading to the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment only in colonies with low mortality rates. The persistence of these institutions over time significantly affected current income per capita in former colonies. This effect is evident in the stark differences between colonies like the United States and Australia, where European settlers established inclusive institutions, and those like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive institutions were imposed. The long-term impact of these institutions highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies on economic development. This influence is also evident in the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding the historical context and the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2) examine how inequality influenced economic performance in colonial settings. They argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This dynamic was shaped by the influence of factor endowments, which varied significantly across colonies. For instance, while all New World economies began with an abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, other aspects of their factor endowments led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset. This inequality was deeply entrenched in the institutional frameworks that evolved. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) complement this view by highlighting that the growth and shrinkage of economies in Sub-Saharan Africa were tightly linked to foreign trade and the market for exports. This pattern underscores how local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions shaped economic performance. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries, suggesting that the impact of inequality on economic performance was not uniform. This variability highlights the complex interplay between economic factors, institutions, and historical contexts in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonial rule in Africa significantly shaped institutional change through both negotiated and imposed governance structures. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share a common stance that colonial rule influenced institutional change in Africa through both negotiated and imposed governance structures. This influence was particularly pronounced in the development of formal education systems, which were shaped by the demands of African political leaders and families, whose cost-benefit calculations were altered by new social and political institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of colonial rule on education varied across different colonial powers, with British rule being more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.1). This variation in educational development highlights how colonial institutions not only imposed structures but also negotiated with local contexts, shaping long-run economic growth through the quality and accessibility of education. The new economic history of Africa, by focusing on institutions, has enabled development specialists to understand the role of governance in long-run economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping economic growth is crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

The literature on budget policies and land systems in Africa focuses on specific institutional dynamics without directly addressing long-run economic growth (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). Instead, these policies were influenced by regional instability and growing domestic dissent, which led to increased military spending and debt burdens. This pattern of expenditure shifts, particularly in sectors like military and debt servicing, reveals how institutional frameworks established during colonial rule continued to influence post-independence economic management. These systems, while appearing adaptable, can reinforce existing power structures and limit economic opportunities for certain groups. This interplay between budget policies and land systems illustrates how institutional legacies from colonial rule continue to shape economic outcomes in Africa.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) show that the slave trade instilled a culture of mistrust in Africa, which persists today and is a significant factor in Africa's underdevelopment. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, which spanned nearly 500 years, from 1400 to 1900. During this period, Africa experienced four simultaneous slave trades, a duration far exceeding the approximately 75 years of official colonial rule. The prolonged and pervasive nature of the slave trade left a lasting impact on social institutions, fostering a pervasive mistrust that continues to shape economic outcomes. This mistrust operates through both internal and external channels, with the internal channel, individuals' norms of mistrust, being at least twice as large as the external channel, which includes factors like the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates how this mistrust is a distinct legacy of the slave trade, which disrupted pre-existing economic and social institutions. For instance, local coastal societies were too small and undifferentiated to facilitate foreign trade until the Portuguese encountered the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets. This historical context underscores how the slave trade's legacy of mistrust has persisted, influencing contemporary economic development.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.2); (Austin 2008, p.2) challenge the conventional view of African poverty by emphasizing the role of colonial policies and labor dynamics. They argue that the long-term economic development and welfare in Africa were influenced by colonial policies that aimed for fiscal security and encouraged indigenous economic and political action. This perspective highlights that colonial administrations responded to local incentives and actions. For instance, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local economic conditions suggests that African poverty is a product of complex historical processes. Austin (2008, p.15) further underscores this point by noting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political actions. Thus, the colonial era in Africa was characterized by a more nuanced interplay between colonial policies and local economic dynamics than often portrayed. This nuanced understanding of colonial policies and labor dynamics reveals that African poverty is a result of dynamic interactions that have shaped long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by determining the incentives and constraints that influence economic behavior. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers in different regions significantly impacted long-term economic outcomes. These institutions ranged from those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) underscores this point by highlighting that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. The institutional frameworks established during colonial times, therefore, had enduring effects on the economic trajectories of the affected regions.

The dynamic interplay between colonial policies and local economic actions further illustrates the nuanced role of institutions in economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptation reveals that the colonial era in Africa was characterized by a more complex interplay between colonial policies and local dynamics than often portrayed. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) adds that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups who may prioritize maximizing rents over long-run growth, further emphasizing the importance of understanding the motivations behind institutional design. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.9) shows that institutions evolve in response to economic and political pressures, influencing long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between extractive and inclusive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that extractive institutions, which maximize rents for the colonizers, were more likely to be established in regions where the colonizers faced high mortality rates. These institutions, designed to extract resources, had lasting negative effects on economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) supports this by showing that "good" institutions, which provide incentives for investment, are crucial for development. The historical evidence from colonial Africa and the empirical comparisons across different regions settle the disagreement by showing that the type of institutions established during colonial times had enduring effects on economic outcomes. This historical evidence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6; Austin 2008, p.15; North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Ogilvie 2007, p.3; Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8) shows that the long-run economic growth of former colonies is closely tied to the institutions established during the colonial era.
