The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake intellectually is the extent to which institutional frameworks can either facilitate or hinder economic progress over extended periods. The debate is intense, with scholars arguing over the mechanisms through which institutions exert their influence. One key disagreement centers on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors such as geography or colonial policies. Another point of contention is the nature of these institutions: are they extractive, designed to benefit a select few, or are they inclusive, fostering broad-based economic development? The interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes is complex, with some scholars emphasizing the long-term effects of colonial institutions and others highlighting the adaptive nature of institutions over time. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote sustainable economic growth. The question of whether institutions matter in economic growth is not new. North argues that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing the importance of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. North (1989, p.6) posits that the development of political institutions specifying "efficient" property rights and providing effective enforcement should lead to falling transaction costs, which in turn fosters economic growth. This perspective underscores the foundational role of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic activity. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) further elaborate on this by highlighting the political factors underpinning economic growth, noting that the regime's commitment to rules governing economic exchange is critical. This commitment ensures that property rights are secure, which is essential for fostering long-term economic growth. These arguments together suggest that the institutional framework drives economic development fundamentally.

The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering secure property rights and thereby promoting long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was both an economic and a political event. North further elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying property rights and providing effective enforcement should reduce costs per transaction, with the aggregate volume of exchanges increasing as specialization and division of labor grow. This institutional framework is crucial for fostering long-term economic growth, as it ensures that property rights are secure and transaction costs are minimized. North and Weingast underscore this point by noting that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which reflects the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange. The implications of these institutional changes vary across different contexts. This variability underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth, where the effectiveness of institutions depends on the broader political and economic environment.

Colonial institutions established by European powers significantly influenced the long-run economic growth of their former colonies, a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies varied widely, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These extractive institutions persisted over time and significantly affected current income per capita. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was robust. This variation in institutional outcomes highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial strategies of European colonizers, underscores the long-term effects of colonial governance on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. also show that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This finding is supported by evidence that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial strategies of European colonizers, underscores the long-term effects of colonial governance on economic trajectories. This pattern suggests that the initial conditions set by colonial powers have lasting implications for economic growth, even after independence.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema 2012, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) discuss how colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, which codified customary law and reinforced the subordinate status of women. This institutional framework, shaped by colonial governance, had profound implications for economic activities. For instance, women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor, reinforced by colonial institutions, influenced economic outcomes and the overall development trajectory. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this pattern by highlighting that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This suggests that the type of colonial governance and the specific institutions it introduced played a nuanced role in shaping economic development, with some colonial powers having more positive impacts on certain aspects of economic infrastructure. The varying impacts of different colonial institutions underscore the complex relationship between governance structures and long-run economic growth.

The slave trade has had a lasting impact on African economic development, shaping a culture of mistrust that persists today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is not merely a result of the slave trade's impact on the external environment, such as the rule of law, but also stems from internal norms of mistrust. Nunn further elaborates that this mistrust is a legacy of the prolonged period of slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, significantly outlasting the era of official colonial rule. The adverse effects of the slave trades are evident in the underdevelopment of economic and social institutions, which hindered Africa's ability to support foreign trade. This institutional underdevelopment, compounded by the culture of mistrust, creates a formidable barrier to economic growth. This variation underscores the complex interplay between historical factors and economic outcomes, highlighting that the legacy of the slave trade is just one piece of the puzzle in understanding Africa's economic trajectory.

Technological progress and the accumulation of useful knowledge are the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, a stance shared by Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1); (Mokyr 2005, p.49). These authors highlight that technological advancements necessitate institutional and ideological adjustments to facilitate efficient use and dissemination of innovations. The interplay between technological changes and institutional adaptations is crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) emphasizes that technological progress requires resolving conflicts that arise from the adoption of new technologies, which in turn demands institutional progress (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This perspective is echoed by Mokyr (2005, p.49), who argues that without complementary institutional progress, the sustainability of economic improvements would be limited, potentially leading to rent-seeking behaviors that erode economic gains (Mokyr 2005, p.54). These insights underscore the importance of institutional frameworks that support and facilitate technological advancements, thereby driving long-term economic growth.

This influence is particularly evident in how institutions determine access to resources and opportunities, thereby shaping economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) examine how institutions affect economic development by highlighting the historical evolution of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic growth. This focus on institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth offers valuable insights into long-term economic outcomes. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. Austin (2008, p.15) further elaborates that colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes by responding to local incentives and actions. This dynamic highlights how institutions can adapt to local conditions, shaping economic development in nuanced ways. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) build on this by noting that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional advantage for elites can limit economic opportunities for broader segments of society, thereby affecting long-term economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) demonstrate that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset of New World economies created enduring institutional frameworks that influenced development. This suggests that initial conditions of inequality can lock in institutional structures that perpetuate economic disparities, underscoring the role of institutions in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by establishing the framework within which economic activities occur. Acemoglu and colleagues argue that the type of institutions established in colonies during the European settlement significantly impacted their economic trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The presence of extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than foster long-term growth, highlights how institutional design can lock in economic disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional path dependency is further supported by North's analysis, which underscores the critical role of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms in facilitating economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, illustrates how institutional changes can reduce transaction costs and promote economic activity (North 1989, p.9).

The impact of institutions on economic growth extends from the colonial era to contemporary economic development. Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). He emphasizes that the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have enduring effects on economic performance, shaping the paths of development for former colonies (Austin 2008, p.2). Hopkins reinforces this perspective by highlighting the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The quality of public goods and the rule of law, as influenced by institutional frameworks, are crucial factors in economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18). North and Weingast also demonstrate that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of England's economic development can be attributed to the institutional changes that ensured the commitment to property rights and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts. The underlying agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu and colleagues' work and North's framework show that the type of institutions established and their enforcement mechanisms are critical for long-term economic development (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence growth remain a point of disagreement. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis does not fully capture the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.2). This disagreement can be settled by examining the historical evidence of institutional changes and their effects on economic performance. The empirical comparison of former European colonies shows that those with institutions designed to maximize rents for colonists experienced slower economic growth compared to those with institutions that encouraged investment and economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical evidence underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping long-run economic trajectories.
