The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic development. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policy decisions and economic strategies to foster sustained growth. Scholars agree that institutions matter, but they disagree on the mechanisms and the types of institutions that drive economic growth. Some argue that secure property rights and rule of law are crucial. Others emphasize the role of extractive institutions that hinder growth. Still others point to the importance of political stability and effective governance. These differing views highlight the complexity of the relationship between institutions and economic growth, and the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes over the long term. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions are primarily driven by efficiency concerns or by distributional conflicts, and whether they are shaped by exogenous factors such as geography or by endogenous processes such as political dynamics. Institutions are not neutral; they can either facilitate or hinder economic growth, depending on their design and enforcement. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This reduction reflected increased security of property rights, which are critical for both long-term capital markets and economic growth. They note that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, where the development of markets and the rules governing them were underpinned by political factors that ensured the regime's commitment to these rules. This institutional framework both facilitated market exchanges and ensured that transaction costs were falling, even as the transactions sector's proportion of aggregate GNP grew. This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can be nuanced and context-dependent. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus multifaceted, involving both the creation of secure property rights and the political commitment to enforcing these rights.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, shaped long-run economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions hindered economic growth by discouraging investment and enforcing the rule of law poorly. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, promoting long-run economic growth. This pattern suggests that the type of institutions established during colonial rule had a lasting impact on economic development. However, Acemoglu et al. also note that the equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutional design and economic outcomes, where political and economic interests of the colonizers played a significant role in shaping the institutional landscape.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Nunn and Wantchekon, argue that inequality in New World societies shaped institutions in ways that favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This institutional effect can be traced through the mechanism of elite capture, where those in power use their influence to design institutions that maintain their dominance. Sokoloff and Engerman describe how the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset led to societies where institutions were structured to benefit the elite. This elite capture mechanism is further supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who show how the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, affecting economic development by reducing trust and cooperation. The culture of mistrust has long-lasting effects on social capital, which is crucial for economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings suggest that the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external environmental factors, highlighting the deep-rooted nature of these institutional effects. This perspective is qualified by the recognition that the persistence of inequality is influenced by both institutional design and the broader social and economic context, including the historical legacies of colonial rule and the ongoing dynamics of power and resistance. Sokoloff and Engerman acknowledge that the variation in institutions across societies is deeply rooted in the initial conditions of inequality.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both assert that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth. This perspective emphasizes that technological progress and the accumulation of knowledge are fundamental to long-run economic development. Mokyr highlights that the growth of economic reasonableness, which encompasses institutional progress, is crucial for sustaining economic improvements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) suggests that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvements might be limited and eventually eliminated by rent-seeking behaviors. Kuznets (1973, p.6) complements this view by noting that modern economic growth is possible only if conflicts induced by technological changes are resolved without excessive costs, underscoring the importance of institutional adjustments. This interplay is evident in the historical context of industrialization, where societies had to adapt their institutions to accommodate new technologies. This highlights the necessity of institutional frameworks that can adapt to and support technological advancements, ensuring that economic growth is not hindered by social or political instability.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped the trajectory of African economic development by influencing the quality of public goods provided by African governments. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) shared finding that institutional change in African colonies was influenced by both African initiatives and exogenous factors, impacting the quality of public goods provided by African governments. African political leaders and families made cost-benefit calculations regarding missionary education, highlighting the role of local agency in shaping educational outcomes. This local agency, in turn, influenced the broader institutional landscape, affecting the provision of public goods and services. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) further illustrate this point by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions played a role in economic development (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The timing and scale of growth in export production varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions, indicating that the impact of colonial institutions was not uniform across Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variability underscores the complex interplay between colonial legacies and local conditions in shaping African economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique institutional explanations for historical economic phenomena, arguing that they oversimplify causation and fail to account for distributional conflicts and other factors. They contend that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth, does not adequately explain why some countries adopted institutions favorable for economic growth while others did not. This critique underscores the complexity of historical economic development and the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional change. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this complexity by highlighting how colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic illustrates that institutional changes were influenced by local conditions and actions, not merely top-down impositions. She suggests that these institutions were shaped by underlying social and political conflicts, not by a straightforward pursuit of economic efficiency. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are merely tools for economic growth, instead viewing them as products of complex social and political dynamics. This nuanced view of institutional change highlights the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that shape economic development.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profound, as they create the framework within which economic activities occur. Institutions influence economic growth through the establishment of property rights, enforcement mechanisms, and the overall stability they provide. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) demonstrates that institutions matter in economic growth, emphasizing that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19), who argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutions, which in turn affected the economic outcomes of colonies. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-enhancing, have persistent effects on economic development. Shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, which can influence the adoption of institutions that either promote or hinder economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).

Moreover, the historical context and the specific nature of these institutions play a crucial role in determining their impact on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This highlights the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, demonstrating that institutions evolve in response to economic and political dynamics (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This dynamic nature of institutions is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8), who argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development and their impact on economic growth.

The precise remaining disagreement centres on the extent to which shared beliefs influence the adoption of institutions that promote or hinder economic growth. While Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that "equilibrium institutions" may be extractive, even though such institutions do not encourage economic development. The historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies settles this disagreement. This evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This historical evidence provides a clear measurement of the impact of institutions on economic growth, highlighting the importance of understanding the specific historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development.
