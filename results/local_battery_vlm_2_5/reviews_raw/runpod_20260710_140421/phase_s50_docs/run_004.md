The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, defined as the rules of the game that structure human interaction, can either facilitate or hinder economic growth. The live disagreement centres on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes, particularly the extent to which institutional quality is shaped by exogenous factors such as geography and demography, or by endogenous processes such as political dynamics and historical path dependence. The debate extends to whether institutions are primarily shaped by efficiency considerations or by distributional conflicts. The stakes are high, as understanding these mechanisms can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic development. The disagreement persists around the causal pathways that link institutions to economic growth. For instance, some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by the need for efficient resource allocation, while others contend that distributional conflicts play a more significant role. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the financial revolution in seventeenth-century England was a pivotal moment that reduced transaction costs and strengthened property rights, thereby promoting long-run economic growth. This was achieved through a political trade-off that involved Parliament exchanging certain rights for revenues during the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The political institutions that emerged from this process were critical in specifying and enforcing property rights, which in turn facilitated market exchanges and reduced transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). This institutional framework supported economic growth and underscored the importance of credible commitment in sustaining long-term economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The broader implications of these institutional changes are nuanced. This perspective invites further exploration into the interplay between institutions and other economic factors, such as technological innovation and market dynamics, in shaping long-run economic growth.

Colonial institutions shaped by European policies significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) show. The type of institutions established in colonies was largely determined by European mortality rates, which in turn shaped the colonization strategies. This institutional setup had profound implications for long-run economic development. The extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies hindered economic growth by failing to encourage investment and development, whereas the institutions in low-mortality colonies fostered growth by promoting investment and the rule of law. In this context, the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The implications of these findings are significant, as they highlight the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, suggesting that institutional legacies can persist for centuries and shape economic trajectories in profound ways.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) challenge simplistic views of causation in economic development, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This simplification risks overlooking the nuanced ways in which institutions adapt and evolve over time. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates this complexity by examining the dynamics of colonial administrations, where institutional prescriptions were influenced by local economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive nature of institutions suggests that economic growth is determined by the presence of secure property rights and by the responsiveness of institutions to changing circumstances. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further complicates this picture by arguing that distributional conflicts often drive the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the social and political contexts in which institutions operate, as these contexts can significantly influence institutional outcomes and, by extension, economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) calls for a deeper integration of institutions into economic historical research (Ogilvie 2007, p.3), emphasizing the need to move beyond superficial analyses and delve into the intricate relationships between institutions and economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Austin and Broadberry (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9), demonstrate that Africa and Europe have followed distinct economic trajectories, with Africa's per capita income levels exceeding subsistence in the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner highlight the availability of extensive economic data for the colonial era, and the fact that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied across Africa due to local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) argue that the divergence between Europe and Asia was already underway before 1800, driven by high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe. This productivity was due to production efficiencies and to merchant distribution and finance, indicating the crucial role of the service sector in economic growth. The implication is that the benefits of economic development were unevenly distributed, with skilled labourers experiencing larger surpluses over basic food needs compared to unskilled labourers. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.15) further note that the reduced need for shelter, fuel, and clothing in warm climates may have reinforced this effect, suggesting that geographical factors also played a role in shaping economic outcomes.

The institutions in New World societies with greater inequality restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). This institutional framework, which was skewed in favor of the elite, restricted competition and hindered the development of inclusive institutions that could have fostered broader economic growth. Nunn (2008, p.2) further argues that the adverse effects of inequality were compounded by the impact of slave trades on Africa, which had a lasting influence on economic development. This suggests that the persistence of inequality in the New World was a function of institutional constraints and of the historical legacies of exploitative labor practices. However, the role of institutions in perpetuating inequality is not uniformly detrimental; Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that centralized governments and well-developed markets, as seen in the Kongo kingdom, could facilitate trade and economic activity. This indicates that the impact of institutions on economic development is nuanced, with some institutional structures potentially mitigating the adverse effects of inequality.

The growth of useful knowledge underpins economic development, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) (Mokyr 2005, p.49) both demonstrate. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological and intellectual advancements. This perspective is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who argues that technological progress and the diffusion of innovations are pivotal in economic development. The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is crucial. Institutions must adapt to efficiently utilize these innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the major breakthroughs in human knowledge, termed epochal innovations, have constituted dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This suggests that while institutions may facilitate or hinder the adoption of new technologies, the primary driver of long-term economic growth is the accumulation of useful knowledge. However, Mokyr (2005, p.49) notes that technological progress alone is not sufficient; institutional progress is also necessary to sustain economic improvement. Mokyr (2005, p.49) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them, implying that institutions play a supporting role in ensuring the sustainability of economic growth driven by technological advancements (Mokyr 2005, p.54). Without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply rooted in the historical evolution of property rights and political structures. Institutions, particularly those governing property rights, are crucial for economic growth. North argues that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are vital for economic development (North 1989, p.6). These institutions facilitate market exchanges by reducing transaction costs, thereby fostering a more efficient economy (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast emphasize that the political commitment to these rules is essential for sustaining economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the differences in colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. Acemoglu et al. highlight that in colonies where Europeans settled, institutions were established to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, leading to sustained economic improvement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, in colonies where extractive states were set up, the focus was on rapidly transferring resources to the metropole, which hindered long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin challenges the oversimplification of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it compresses different historical periods and paths, and that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15). These changes shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies, with some developing more favorable institutions for long-term growth. The underlying point of agreement among these scholars is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. North and Weingast show that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that extractive institutions can persist and significantly affect current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence of colonial institutions and their impact on long-term economic development provides a clear measurement of this effect. The comparison of colonies with different institutional frameworks, such as those established by Europeans versus extractive states, offers a detailed empirical basis for understanding the role of institutions in economic growth.
