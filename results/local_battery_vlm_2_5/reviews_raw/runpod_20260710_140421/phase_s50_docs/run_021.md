The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense debate among scholars. At stake is the fundamental question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play more significant roles. One key position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and rule of law, are crucial for fostering economic growth. This perspective is supported by the work of Acemoglu et al. who argue that differences in European colonization strategies led to the establishment of extractive institutions in some colonies, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Another position contends that institutions are not the sole determinants of economic growth but rather interact with other factors, such as geography and culture. This view is exemplified by Austin, who suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate highlights the complexity of economic development and the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of institutions. The live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth versus the extent to which they interact with other factors. The stakes are high because resolving this debate could provide clearer policy directions for fostering economic development.

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)  argue that institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-term economic performance. North (1989, p.9) points out that the development of financial institutions, such as the Bank of England, significantly reduced transaction costs by enhancing the security of property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional development is crucial because it creates an environment where long-term investments are more secure, encouraging economic activity. Extractive institutions in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, which were designed to quickly transfer resources to the metropole, differed from the growth-promoting institutions in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, as different institutional setups can lead to vastly different economic trajectories.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 significantly altered the political landscape, establishing a constitutional monarchy that limited the monarch's power and enhanced the role of Parliament. This shift was pivotal in creating a stable political environment that supported economic growth by ensuring that property rights were secure and that the rules governing economic exchange were consistently enforced. The secure property rights that emerged from these institutional changes were instrumental in attracting investment and fostering economic development. The stability of these institutions resulted from both their design and the credible commitment to uphold them (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that the specific factors shaping institutions in New World societies were shaped by their unique colonial histories, where extreme inequality in wealth and political power led to the entrenchment of institutions that favored elites. This perspective underscores the importance of initial conditions in shaping institutional trajectories, suggesting that the specific historical contexts in which institutions evolve can significantly influence their impact on economic growth. The long-term economic growth of a society is thus deeply intertwined with the evolution of its institutions, which are shaped by both historical contexts and the political dynamics that govern them.

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that gender dynamics and educational policies under colonial rule significantly influenced economic activities, particularly in agriculture. This is echoed by Frankema, who notes that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land, particularly for cash crops (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This was a direct result of patriarchy alliances, which were used to codify the subordinate status of women in the colonial era (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The demand for missionary education was driven by cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families, altering the perceived benefits of formal education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The implementation of a new set of social and political institutions under colonial rule influenced the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities (Frankema 2012, p.1). This suggests that while colonial education policies may have opened new economic avenues, they also entrenched gender inequalities that persisted long after independence.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18) both critique the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for its oversimplification of the causal factors behind economic growth. Austin argues that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, while Hopkins focuses on the new economic history of Africa to inform institutional reforms. Colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often responded to incentives created by indigenous economic and political action by changing their institutional prescriptions. This adaptive behavior led to varied production equilibria, as seen in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations tailored their institutional responses to the specific economic and political contexts they encountered. This adaptability highlights the importance of understanding the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in shaping long-run economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.18) further emphasizes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa, by providing incentives for investment (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This suggests that the dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth is a critical area for further investigation, particularly in understanding how colonial institutions shaped long-run economic trajectories in Africa.

Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the role of distributional conflicts is central to the origins and evolution of economic institutions. Ogilvie argues that the structure of pre-industrial Europe's core economic institutions, serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, can be better understood through the lens of distributional conflicts than through efficiency considerations. This perspective challenges the prevailing narrative that institutions are shaped primarily by efficiency concerns, highlighting instead the role of power struggles and the redistribution of resources. Distributional conflicts, in this view, drive the formation of institutions that serve to entrench the interests of powerful groups. Ogilvie's focus on distributional conflicts underscores how the allocation of power and resources shapes institutions, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Frankema and van Waijenburg suggest that understanding the long-term economic development in colonial Africa requires a deeper exploration of the determinants of real wage divergence. This approach helps clarify the role of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions, as the dynamics of power and resource allocation are crucial in determining economic trajectories. Frankema and van Waijenburg's emphasis on real wage levels and trends in British African colonies highlights the importance of understanding the historical context of economic development. Their work highlights that the persistence of poverty in Africa is influenced by both structural impediments and the dynamics of distributional conflicts. This perspective challenges the notion that African poverty is a persistent historical phenomenon, instead suggesting that it is shaped by the interplay of institutional structures and distributional conflicts.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) both argue that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust and disrupted economic institutions has had a profound impact on Africa's economic development. The slave trade, spanning nearly 500 years, had lasting effects on African societies, shaping their economic trajectories in ways that extend beyond colonialism. This historical trauma fostered a pervasive culture of mistrust, which continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. This mistrust, in turn, impedes economic growth by undermining social cohesion and cooperation, which are crucial for sustainable development. The slave trade's disruptions also affected the development of economic institutions. Nunn (2008, p.2) notes that the Kongo kingdom, with its centralized government and well-developed markets, was able to support trade with Europeans, highlighting the importance of institutional strength in fostering economic activity. The broader impact of the slave trade was to undermine institutional development across Africa. The legacy of the slave trade includes both mistrust and weakened institutions, both of which have hampered Africa's long-term economic growth.

Technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Mokyr and Kuznets both argue that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets observes that technological progress involves both new inventions and the institutional and ideological adjustments that facilitate their efficient use. For example, the shift from rural, agrarian societies to industrialized ones required significant changes in social structures and values. Mokyr builds on this by highlighting the importance of the growth of useful knowledge, which underpins technological progress. He emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological advancements, economic improvements may not be sustainable. This interplay between technology and institutions is evident in the historical development of European scholarly societies, which Mokyr notes played a crucial role in reducing access costs to knowledge. Kuznets also acknowledges that technological progress can induce conflicts within societies, such as those between traditional and modern values. These conflicts must be resolved peacefully for modern economic growth to be possible. The sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, is crucial in managing these conflicts, ensuring that technological progress does not lead to long-term societal weakening. This dynamic interplay between technology and institutions underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where technological advancements are necessary but not sufficient on their own.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical area of study, with scholars emphasizing the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) both highlight that institutions are pivotal in determining economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. For example, in regions where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic growth was more robust. Extractive institutions resulted in economic stagnation. North (1989, p.9) argues that the development of institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement is crucial for economic growth. North (1989, p.9) shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which in turn facilitated economic growth. Both Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and North (1989, p.9) emphasise that the institutional environment shapes long-run economic growth by influencing the costs and benefits of economic activities. This underscores the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for sustainable economic development.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further elaborate on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic development. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that the institutional evolution in older industrial economies cannot be understood without considering the long-term patterns and paths of institutional change. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) contends that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She argues that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and the craft guild were shaped by conflicts over resource distribution. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) shows that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to politically powerful groups. This highlights the complex nature of institutional design and its impact on economic outcomes. Both Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) show that institutions are shaped by political and social dynamics that influence economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) provide further insights into the role of institutions in economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They show that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the 19th century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. He shows that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Both Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) show that institutions are pivotal in shaping economic outcomes by influencing investment and industrialization. This underscores the importance of institutions in providing the necessary framework for sustainable economic development.
