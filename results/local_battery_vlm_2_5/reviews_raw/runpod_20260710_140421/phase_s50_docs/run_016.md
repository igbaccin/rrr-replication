The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic theory and history. The extent to which institutions, defined as the rules of the game in a society, including formal rules, informal norms, and their enforcement characteristics, drive economic performance over the long term is at stake. A key disagreement centers on whether institutions are primarily shaped by economic factors or whether they themselves shape economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the role of institutions in providing secure property rights and enforcing contracts, arguing that these factors are crucial for economic growth. Another position suggests that the efficiency of institutions is secondary to other factors, such as cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts. The debate hinges on whether institutions are endogenous to economic development or if they exert an exogenous influence on growth. The question of how institutions influence economic growth is critical to understanding the historical trajectories of different economies and to informing contemporary policy debates. Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing transaction costs and property rights, as North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue. For instance, the establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in the 17th century dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional change facilitated a long-term capital market and stimulated economic growth. These institutions significantly affected long-run economic outcomes in these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) clarify that institutions are not always designed to maximize long-run growth. Instead, they often reflect the interests of politically powerful groups, who may prioritize maximizing their own rents over fostering economic development. The implication is that the long-term effects of institutions on economic growth can be complex and multifaceted, depending on the specific historical and political contexts in which they are embedded.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They emphasize that the development of institutions that ensure credible commitments and enforce property rights is essential for long-term economic growth. North and Weingast demonstrate this through the example of seventeenth-century England, where the establishment of institutions that limited the monarch's power and ensured property rights led to significant economic growth. The credibility of these institutions, rooted in the commitment to enforce property rights, was a critical factor in this process. North and Weingast show that the political factors underpinning economic growth are the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions governing how these rules are enforced and how they may be changed. This focus on credible commitments and enforceable property rights is a crucial mechanism by which institutions shape long-run economic growth. Ogilvie qualifies this view by arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that institutions like serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by distributional conflicts. This qualification suggests that while credible commitments and enforceable property rights are important, the specific historical and social context in which institutions evolve also plays a significant role in shaping their impact on economic growth. The evolution of institutions is thus a complex process influenced by both political and social factors.

This dynamic is particularly evident in sub-Saharan Africa, where British colonial governance played a pivotal role in shaping formal education, as Frankema contends (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). The demand for missionary education was driven by African political leaders and families making cost-benefit calculations, which were influenced by the new social and political institutions implemented during British rule. This dynamic highlights how colonial governance could alter the perceived benefits of formal education, thereby influencing economic development. The British colonial administration's approach to education and governance fostered a more supportive environment for economic growth compared to other colonial powers. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) support this view, noting that many Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic performance was closely tied to export production, which varied due to factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The nuanced impact of British rule on formal education and economic development in sub-Saharan Africa underscores the importance of considering the specific institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers when assessing long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a multifaceted issue that involves understanding how different types of institutions influence economic outcomes. Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) shows that European colonists established various institutions in their colonies, ranging from growth-enhancing to extractive, depending on factors such as mortality rates and settlement patterns. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected the economic trajectories of these regions. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) describe how settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, leading to higher income per capita. Extractive states in regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast transferred resources rapidly to the metropole, hindering economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive and designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This institutional framework highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on contemporary economic disparities.

The complexity of institutional effects is further underscored by the interplay between economic and political factors. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth. This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights over time, a dimension essential for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) argues that institutions are indispensable instruments in the toolkit of every economic historian, suggesting that a deeper understanding of institutional structures can provide insights into long-term economic development. This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. This adaptability is crucial for understanding how institutions can both constrain and enable economic growth.

The historical evidence shows that the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic development are complex and multifaceted. Austin (2008, p.2) acknowledges that the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes secure private property rights as key to long-term economic growth, has limitations in explaining why some countries adopted growth-enhancing institutions while others did not. This suggests that the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is not straightforward and involves a variety of factors, including political dynamics and indigenous responses to colonial policies. Hopkins (2009, p.8) shows that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This highlights the practical implications of understanding institutional effects, as it can guide policy interventions aimed at improving economic outcomes. The specific historical evidence of institutional change in Africa, as documented by Hopkins (2009, p.8), and the empirical comparison of institutional effects across different colonial settings, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), settles the disagreement by demonstrating that the enduring impact of colonial institutions on contemporary economic disparities is a result of the extractive nature of these institutions and the lack of long-term growth-oriented policies.
