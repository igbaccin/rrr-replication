The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic development or merely one factor among many. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement: while some scholars argue that the quality of institutions is the most significant factor in determining economic growth, others contend that institutions are secondary to other factors, such as geography, culture, or economic policy. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and our understanding of how societies develop over time. The stakes are high, as the efficacy of institutional reforms hinges on whether institutions are indeed the key to sustainable economic growth. The resolution of this debate has the potential to reshape our understanding of economic development and inform policy decisions that could impact billions of lives. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth remains unresolved, with scholars continuing to explore the complex interplay between institutions and other factors. Institutions are a critical factor in shaping long-term economic growth, as they provide the framework within which economic activity occurs and influence the incentives and constraints that affect economic decisions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, 2002) (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions are pivotal determinants of long-run economic growth because they shape transaction costs and property rights. This institutional framework underpinned long-term capital markets and economic growth by reducing transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, did not encourage economic development. This pattern implies that the persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-run economic growth by maintaining high transaction costs and insecure property rights.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; Austin 2008, p.2). This institutional framework allowed England to achieve remarkable success in capital markets, illustrating the direct link between credible commitment and economic prosperity. Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that colonial administrations, which sought fiscal security, often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adjustment shows that while secure property rights are fundamental, the adaptability of institutions to local contexts can also drive economic growth. This adaptability is particularly evident in cases where colonial governments encouraged commodity production, illustrating that institutional changes can be responsive to economic and political incentives. This responsiveness underscores the importance of flexible institutions in promoting economic development, even in the face of colonial constraints.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) find that economic development and living standards in Europe from 1500 to 1800 did not correlate clearly. This lack of correlation is evident in the varying experiences of different regions within Europe. This productivity was not uniformly distributed, however, as skilled labourers benefited more than unskilled labourers. The economic development in this period was characterized by a shift in the focus of economic activity to the countries bordering the North Sea, particularly Holland and England, which saw a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This shift had implications for the distribution of economic benefits, as the abundance of Baltic grain and British export subsidies kept wheat prices in Amsterdam lower than in England and France, highlighting the regional disparities in economic development and living standards. This regional variation underscores the complexity of the relationship between economic development and living standards, showing that institutional changes and economic policies can lead to divergent outcomes across different regions.

The literature on colonial impacts on African economic development explores the historical and structural factors influencing poverty and growth in Africa but does not explicitly focus on institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that poor institutions are crucial in the dismal economic record of Africa, as they fail to provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule to assess the long-term picture of economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This approach underscores the importance of understanding the distribution of income and its relationship to long-term African welfare development.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting trust levels and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This mistrust is rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, which created a pervasive environment of suspicion and distrust among African societies. The mistrust generated by the slave trade manifests in both internal norms of mistrust and external environmental factors such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others. While both channels are significant, the internal norms of mistrust are at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust can impede economic development by hindering cooperation, reducing social capital, and discouraging investment in public goods. The legacy of the slave trade's impact on trust is profound, as it continues to shape contemporary African societies. The internal norms of mistrust, in particular, can create barriers to economic growth by undermining the social foundations necessary for productive economic activity. The historical experience of the slave trade has left a lasting imprint on African societies, influencing their economic trajectories and development paths.

Simson (2019, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) share the finding that African economic policies and class formation have been shaped by budget policies and land systems (Simson 2019, p.2; Peters 2004, p.19). This dynamic was particularly marked in Kenya, where subsidies and interest payments peaked at 30 percent of spending in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This is evident in the colonial period, when agricultural officers expressed dissatisfaction with perceived 'communal' landholding practices, which they saw as inhibiting individual investment in agriculture (Peters 2004, p.5). This pattern of exclusion and class formation has had significant implications for economic development in Africa, as it has hindered the formation of a productive middle class and limited the scope for investment in public goods. However, Simson (2019, p.2) qualifies this implication by noting that the impact of these policies has varied across countries, with Uganda experiencing a smaller debt burden due to limited access to capital markets during Idi Amin's rule and earlier debt relief. This variation highlights the importance of considering the specific historical and political contexts of different African countries when analyzing the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Distributional conflicts are argued to be the driving force behind key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argues that institutions such as serfdom and guilds were shaped by the need to manage power dynamics between different social groups. This perspective challenges the notion that institutions are primarily designed to maximize economic efficiency. The distributional conflicts perspective suggests that institutions are often the outcome of negotiations and power struggles among different groups, each seeking to secure their interests. This dynamic can lead to institutions that may appear inefficient but serve to maintain a delicate balance of power. This highlights that while distributional conflicts are crucial, the specific historical and economic contexts in which they occur also play a significant role in determining the nature of institutions and their impact on economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women made significant contributions to African economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, and Frankema argues that colonial rule influenced the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa. The role of women in African development highlights that gender dynamics and access to resources significantly influenced economic outcomes, challenging the primacy of institutions as the sole determinant (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This dynamic had profound implications for economic development, as it limited women's participation in more lucrative economic activities. By the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that despite the gendered constraints, African economies were capable of achieving significant economic growth. Nonetheless, the persistent gender inequalities highlight the need to consider multiple factors, including gender dynamics, when examining the determinants of long-run economic growth.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) argue that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, which contrasts with the view that institutions are the primary driver. Mokyr posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, while Kuznets highlights that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional adaptations is crucial for sustained economic growth. Kuznets emphasizes that the resolution of growth-induced conflicts is essential for modern economic growth, and that the modern national state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving such conflicts (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This suggests that while technological progress is a driving force, the institutional framework must also evolve to support and sustain this progress. Mokyr further elaborates that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This underscores the importance of institutional adaptations in harnessing the full potential of technological advancements for long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to enforce property rights and facilitate market exchanges. North shows that the development of institutions, such as the Bank of England and new financial instruments, led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provides the security necessary for long-term capital markets to function effectively. Moreover, Acemoglu et al. highlight that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly impacted current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, illustrate how institutional design can either support or hinder economic development. This underscores the importance of institutional adaptations in harnessing the full potential of technological advancements for long-run economic growth. The creation of institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment is crucial for sustaining economic improvement, as shown by the contrast between the United States and the Congo or the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This shared finding shows that institutions matter in economic growth, contra neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as principal determinants (North 1989, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).

The evolution of institutions over time is also critical. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This is supported by the observation that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between institutions and economic development highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and adapt over time. For example, the development of new financial instruments and the Bank of England reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical to both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). This interaction between institutions and economic development is further supported by Acemoglu et al., who show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This interaction between institutions and economic development is further supported by Ogilvie, who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12).
