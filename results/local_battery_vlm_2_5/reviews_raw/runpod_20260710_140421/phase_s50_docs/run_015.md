The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the persistence of economic disparities. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who emphasize the causal role of institutions in economic growth and those who prioritize other factors such as geography, technology, or cultural traits. The former group argues that institutions, by defining the rules and incentives that govern economic activity, are the primary drivers of economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are molded by deeper structural factors, and so are not the sole drivers of economic development. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy, as it informs whether institutional reform should be the primary focus of development efforts. The resolution of this debate hinges on understanding the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes and the conditions under which they do so. This suggests that the quality of public goods, including education, was significantly affected by the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) note that many Anglophone African economies had per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, showing that economic performance in these regions was not uniformly poor. This implies that while colonial institutions played a role in shaping economic outcomes, the impact varied significantly across different regions and was influenced by a range of factors, including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions.

Institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating the adoption of new technologies and managing the social and political challenges that arise from such innovations. The establishment of secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and encourage investment (North 1989, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework provided the foundation for long-term economic growth by ensuring that property rights were enforced and that the political regime was committed to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the impact of colonial institutions on the development of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers established different colonization strategies, leading to the creation of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while extractive institutions in colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This divergence in institutional frameworks had a significant impact on the current income per capita of these colonies, highlighting the long-term effects of institutional design on economic growth. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, often resulted in extractive institutions that maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The interaction between institutions and economic development is further complicated by the historical context and the specific policies pursued by colonial powers. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political pressures, influencing long-run economic growth.
