The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. At stake is the extent to which institutions can be harnessed to promote growth, and the degree to which this relationship is causal. The live disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and the conditions under which they are effective. Some scholars argue that institutions matter by facilitating secure property rights and reducing transaction costs, while others contend that the impact of institutions is contingent on historical and cultural contexts. The debate also extends to the types of institutions that are most conducive to growth and the pathways through which they exert their influence. The central themes of this debate are the causal mechanisms and the conditions under which institutions drive long-run economic growth. Institutions can foster growth by shaping incentives for investment and innovation, but their effectiveness is contingent on broader historical and cultural factors. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is far from settled, with ongoing questions about the specific mechanisms through which institutions operate and the contexts in which they are most effective. The causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth remain a central focus of research. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-term economic performance, with historical evidence showing that effective institutions can lead to significant economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). North (1989, p.9) illustrates this with the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, which dramatically lowered transaction costs and facilitated economic growth by enhancing the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This financial revolution was a response to fiscal crises and reflects how institutional changes can drive economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) further elucidate this mechanism by showing that different colonization strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. These institutions, by securing property rights and promoting investment, laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth. The impact of institutions varies. This qualification underscores the importance of understanding the political dynamics that shape institutions, as these dynamics can determine whether institutions foster or hinder economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, for example, highlights how institutional changes introduced by colonialism influenced long-term economic trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional evolution was marked by a shift in the political landscape that allowed for more secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that a critical political factor in this process was the regime's commitment to enforcing rules that protected property rights. This commitment was essential for establishing a stable and predictable environment for economic activity. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) extend this analysis by noting that the factor endowments of New World societies, particularly the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, created conditions that often led to extreme inequality. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, often disadvantaging the majority of the population. The persistence of inequality and its impact on institutional development highlight the complex interplay between economic factors and political institutions in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both posit that economic growth is fundamentally driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge. This perspective emphasizes the role of technological advancements as the primary engine of economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the introduction of new technologies necessitates significant institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize these innovations. He underscores the importance of formal institutions in reducing access costs to this knowledge, thereby facilitating its dissemination and application. This institutional support is crucial for sustaining economic improvement, as technological progress alone is insufficient without complementary institutional advancements. Mokyr (2005, p.54) further argues that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This perspective underscores the importance of coordination failures and the need for policies to rectify them, ensuring that technological advancements are effectively integrated into the economic fabric. Kuznets (1973, p.6) adds that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the political dimensions of institutional support for economic growth. The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is therefore a critical factor in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) critique the oversimplification of causal mechanisms in institutional theories, arguing that historical paths and periods are compressed. This thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, leading to an oversimplified causal mechanism. This critique is echoed by Ogilvie (2007, p.12), who argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. This perspective suggests that the adoption of institutions favorable for economic growth was influenced by the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This pattern is evident in colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium (Austin 2008, p.15). This pattern qualifies the institutionalist approach, which emphasizes secure private property rights as the key to long-term economic growth. This nuanced understanding is crucial for explaining why institutions that were apparently favorable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the historical context and distributional conflicts in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) share the finding that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping public goods and governance structures in Africa. Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa The development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa depended on the demand for missionary education, which in turn was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which were related to new economic opportunities and social mobility. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions could foster or hinder the development of human capital, a critical factor in long-term economic growth. The role of colonial institutions in shaping governance structures is intricate. Simson (2019, p.2) argues that East Africa's post-independence budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, challenging the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services. This indicates that post-colonial governance structures were influenced by both colonial legacies and post-independence political and economic dynamics.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) share the finding that Africa's economic trajectory has been significantly shaped by historical factors, particularly the slave trades and colonial rule. The economic impact of the slave trades was not confined to the immediate period of enslavement; instead, it had long-lasting effects on the continent's economic institutions and human capital development. For instance, regions affected by the slave trades often experienced a depletion of human capital, as skilled individuals were forcibly removed, leaving behind a less productive workforce. This depletion hindered the development of local economies and constrained their ability to engage in productive activities. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The adverse effects of the slave trades were compounded by the subsequent period of colonial rule, which introduced new institutional frameworks that often reinforced existing economic disparities. The legacy of these historical factors continues to influence Africa's economic performance and development, as evidenced by the persistent challenges in human capital development and institutional quality. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered. To repair these lacunae and check for the persistence of poverty, we reconstruct and compare real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2).

The slave trade's cultural legacy of mistrust has had a profound impact on African economic development, as Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15) argue that the persistent mistrust within African societies can be traced back to the transatlantic slave trade. This mistrust, fostered by the exploitative nature of the slave trade, has created an enduring barrier to economic cooperation and growth. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion, leading to a pervasive lack of trust that continues to hinder economic development. This mistrust manifests in various ways, including reduced levels of intergroup cooperation and diminished trust in institutions. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women, further exacerbating social divisions and mistrust. This historical legacy of mistrust has been perpetuated through institutional frameworks, such as the dual legal system introduced by colonial rule, which has entrenched gender inequalities and limited economic opportunities for women. The persistent mistrust within African societies has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) also emphasize that women's economic contributions during both pre-colonial and colonial periods were significant, suggesting that addressing gender inequalities and fostering greater trust within societies could unlock substantial economic potential.

Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) both share the finding that the economic divergence between Europe and Asia was well underway before 1800, with economic growth in Europe driven by institutional changes that facilitated investment in agriculture and the service sector. The high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production, suggesting that economic development was concentrated in specific regions and sectors. The wages of skilled labourers yielded a larger surplus over basic food needs than the wages of unskilled labourers, indicating that the fruits of early modern development were spread unevenly (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The changes in economic development led to a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs, particularly after 1740, when England became the most expensive country (van Zanden 1999, p.9). This implies that while economic development was occurring, it did not necessarily translate into a uniform improvement in living standards across Europe.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is pivotal, as they directly influence the economic environment and incentives for investment. Institutions such as secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are critical for economic growth. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, which is directly linked to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are essential for long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The historical evidence from the English financial revolution and the establishment of secure property rights underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). These findings are consistent with the broader argument that institutions matter in economic growth, as they provide the framework for efficient economic activity.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the differences in colonization strategies employed by Europeans. Acemoglu et al. show that in colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic development was more robust (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In colonies where extractive states were established, the primary goal was to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, which impeded long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical variation in institutional frameworks highlights the enduring effects of institutional choices on economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly related to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from the colonial period shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic trajectories over the long term.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the context of pre-industrial Europe. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, than efficiency-based theories (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective is supported by the historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe, where institutions were shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups. Acemoglu et al. show that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical evidence from pre-industrial Europe and the colonial period shows that institutions are shaped by the interests of powerful groups and have a significant impact on economic outcomes. The shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth is evident across different historical contexts and periods (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; North 1989, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth are still debated. For instance, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The historical evidence from the colonial period shows that the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional frameworks across different colonies and the resulting differences in economic outcomes. This historical evidence shows that institutions matter in economic growth and that the precise mechanisms by which they influence economic outcomes depend on the specific historical context.
