The question of what role institutions play in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central focus of economic historians and economists alike. At stake is the fundamental understanding of why some countries prosper while others stagnate, and how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic development. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutions on economic outcomes, arguing that institutions that enforce property rights and provide secure contracts are crucial for long-term economic growth. This view is supported by studies that highlight the role of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs. Another position, however, argues that the impact of institutions is more complex and is influenced by a variety of factors, including the historical context, the nature of the colonial experience, and the presence of distributional conflicts. These factors can shape institutions in ways that either promote or hinder economic growth, depending on the specific circumstances. The debate is further complicated by rival explanations that propose alternative causes for economic growth, such as geographic factors and cultural beliefs, which may operate independently of institutional frameworks. This interplay between institutions and other factors underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Institutions are not merely static frameworks but dynamic entities that evolve in response to historical, political, and economic forces, and their impact on growth is contingent on these evolving dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) assert that the institutional evolution governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security in the time dimension of property rights. This financial revolution was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in exchange for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The evolution of political institutions that specified efficient property rights and provided effective enforcement facilitated market exchanges, leading to falling costs per transaction and a growing proportion of the transactions sector in GNP (North 1989, p.6). North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) argue that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets. This success was underpinned by the regime's commitment to the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions enforcing them. The role of institutions in economic growth varies. This nuanced view underscores that while institutions are crucial, their impact on economic growth can vary depending on historical and political contexts.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This effect was mediated by the type of institutions established by European colonizers. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic growth and instead maximized short-term rents for the colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This distinction in institutional frameworks had profound implications for the economic development of these regions. The persistence of these institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, shaped the economic trajectories of the former colonies, leading to significant differences in current income per capita. The long-run impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is further qualified by the observation that these institutions were designed to serve the immediate interests of the colonizers. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between historical context, institutional design, and economic outcomes.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, shaped key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). This perspective challenges the conventional wisdom that institutions emerge primarily to enhance economic efficiency, offering instead a view where institutions are molded by power dynamics and the interests of influential groups. Ogilvie's analysis of serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild illustrates how these institutions were designed to maintain the status quo and protect the interests of the elite. This approach is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, had long-lasting effects on economic development. The impact of these institutions on economic growth was not uniform. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that the differences in institutions across societies were influenced by variations in factor endowments, such as land and natural resources relative to labor, which led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This qualitative variation in factor endowments created distinct institutional environments that influenced the course of development. This dynamic underscores the complex interplay between historical context, institutional design, and economic outcomes, suggesting that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is deeply intertwined with the broader social and political landscape.

Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that the thesis oversimplifies African economic history (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.18). Austin and Hopkins argue that this simplification is problematic because it compresses distinct historical periods and paths into a single narrative. Austin shows that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, creating a complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic actions. Hopkins further elaborates that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Austin 2008, p.2; Hopkins 2009, p.8). These reforms are crucial because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Austin shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional evolution in Africa (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic interaction between colonial policies and local economic actions highlights the complexity of African economic history, which cannot be reduced to a single causal narrative. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the determinants of economic development in Africa (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This understanding is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade is a major factor in explaining Africa's underdevelopment, challenging the view that colonialism is the sole cause. The slave trade's impact is mediated through a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day. This mistrust is not merely a result of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others, but is also deeply ingrained within individuals' internal norms. The internalisation of mistrust is at least twice as significant as the external factors, such as the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internalised mistrust has far-reaching implications for economic development, as it hinders the formation of social capital and cooperative behaviour essential for economic growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of this mistrust suggests that the legacy of the slave trade continues to shape African societies. The extent to which this mistrust can be mitigated through policy interventions is an open question. Nunn (2008, p.19) provides historical context, noting that sustained trade in Africa did not occur until the Portuguese found the Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government and well-developed markets. This highlights that the absence of centralized institutions capable of supporting trade may have exacerbated the adverse effects of the slave trade. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust and the lack of centralized institutions capable of supporting trade are two critical factors that have hindered Africa's economic development.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) highlights that the growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development. This is supported by Kuznets (1973, p.1), who identifies epochal innovations as dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for efficient use of innovations are just as important as the innovations themselves. This implies that the institutional framework must be conducive to the adoption and diffusion of new technologies. These conflicts can weaken the political fabric of society, making it difficult to resolve growth-induced conflicts peacefully. Mokyr (2005, p.49) echoes this concern by noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial for economic growth, the institutional framework plays a vital role in ensuring that these advancements are effectively utilized and sustained over the long term.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that colonies with high mortality rates among European settlers, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, developed extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Colonies with lower mortality rates, like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, saw the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This differential institutional development had lasting impacts on current income per capita, highlighting the enduring influence of colonial-era institutions on economic growth.

The persistence of these institutional effects is further supported by North, who argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North's analysis underscores that the security of property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms are essential for long-term economic development. This perspective is shared by Austin, who notes that the institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations were often responsive to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interaction between institutions and local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The long-term economic performance of a region is thus closely tied to the institutional frameworks that were established during the colonial period and their subsequent evolution.

The institutional frameworks established during colonialism shaped economic growth and influenced the distribution of economic benefits within societies. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This points to the extractive nature of many colonial institutions, which prioritized the interests of the colonizers over those of the indigenous populations. Ogilvie's work on pre-industrial Europe complements this view by arguing that distributional conflicts, not efficiency considerations, better explain the core economic institutions of that period (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) underscores the importance of considering the political and social dynamics that shape institutional development and their impact on economic growth.

The legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence economic development in contemporary times. Hopkins highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This perspective aligns with the broader argument that institutions matter for economic growth, as they provide the necessary incentives for investment and development. The historical evidence from colonialism and the ongoing relevance of institutional frameworks in contemporary economic development underscore the enduring role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The specific historical evidence of differential institutional development in colonies with varying mortality rates among European settlers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) and the measurement of economic performance in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) settle the disagreement about the role of institutions.
