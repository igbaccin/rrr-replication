Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, a subject that has sparked intense debate among scholars. At the heart of this debate lies the question of which institutions matter most and how they influence economic outcomes. One live disagreement centers on the role of extractive versus inclusive institutions. Extractive institutions, designed to benefit a narrow elite, can stifle economic growth by concentrating power and resources, while inclusive institutions, which distribute power and opportunities more widely, can foster growth. Another key disagreement revolves around the persistence of institutions over time. Some scholars argue that once established, institutions can be remarkably durable, shaping economic trajectories for centuries. Others contend that institutions are more malleable, subject to change through political and economic forces. Additionally, there is a contention over the antecedents of institutional development, with some scholars emphasizing the role of colonial legacies and others highlighting the impact of indigenous economic and political action. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of institutions in economic growth can inform policy decisions and development strategies aimed at promoting sustainable economic progress. This review will explore these debates through multiple streams of scholarship. The first stream examines the impact of colonial legacies on institutional development and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This period saw the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which dramatically reduced transaction costs. This institutional change was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War, where certain rights were traded to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). The political institutions that specified and enforced efficient property rights also facilitated market exchanges, leading to a growing proportion of transactions in the aggregate GNP. This pattern of increasing specialization and division of labor multiplied the aggregate volume of exchanges, further enhancing economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The regime's commitment to the rules governing public choice was the critical factor underpinning this economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The credibility of these institutions was essential for economic growth, as they provided a stable framework for economic activities.

The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent after 1450, contributing to the origins of the 'Great Divergence' (Pamuk 2007, p.23; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk (2007, p.23) highlights that the institutional flexibility of regions like Holland enabled a rapid response to new economic opportunities, while regions like Tuscany struggled with stagnation due to strengthened monopolies. This institutional flexibility fostered economic development by allowing new towns to emerge and rural manufacturing to flourish. The service sector's contribution to economic growth during the early modern period was crucial, as it facilitated the distribution and financing of goods, which in turn supported the economic development of the region. Skilled laborers enjoyed a larger surplus over basic food needs compared to unskilled laborers, indicating a stratified economic landscape. The institutional framework in northwest Europe promoted economic growth and exacerbated social inequalities.

Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the institutional framework in pre-industrial societies was shaped more by distributional conflicts than by efficiency considerations. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) highlight that the core economic institutions such as serfdom, the community, and guilds were influenced by the struggle between different social groups to secure their share of resources. In this context, institutions like guilds, which ostensibly aimed to regulate quality and prices, actually served to restrict competition and protect the interests of the elite. This mechanism of institutional design, driven by power dynamics, led to the persistence of inequality and economic stagnation. For example, in New World societies, the extreme inequality in wealth and political power from the outset shaped institutions that advantaged the elite, restricting competition and perpetuating the status quo (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). Ogilvie suggests that this perspective challenges the prevailing view that institutions are shaped by shared beliefs about efficiency and economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The institutional frameworks in pre-industrial Europe actively maintained a stratified social order.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates, with high-mortality areas receiving extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional framework, which prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic development, was maintained by politically powerful groups who benefited from the status quo (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these extractive institutions is a key factor in the economic stagnation of many former colonies. However, Acemoglu et al. note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while extractive institutions initially hindered growth, changes in economic opportunities could alter the trajectory of institutional development and economic growth.

The new economic history of Africa, as Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue, can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The new economic history of Africa, by focusing on the role of institutions, has provided valuable insights into the factors that have hindered economic growth in the region. One specific mechanism through which institutions have shaped economic outcomes is through the provision of public goods and the development of progressive institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that ethnically heterogeneous societies can still uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions, despite the challenges posed by ethnic fragmentation (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This finding underscores the importance of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, even in the face of significant social and political challenges.

The development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, as Frankema (2012, p.15) shows, was influenced by a range of cost-benefit calculations made by African political leaders and families (Frankema 2012, p.15). This highlights the role of institutions in altering the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn affected economic outcomes. The implementation of new social and political institutions changed the incentives for investment in education, which is crucial for development. The impact of colonial institutions on economic growth varies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) demonstrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This suggests that while colonial institutions may have had negative impacts in some areas, they also facilitated economic growth in others. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth in Africa.

The interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for long-run economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49) argue. Kuznets suggests that technological progress, particularly epochal innovations, requires significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. This is because modern technology is incompatible with traditional rural lifestyles and extended family patterns, necessitating substantial social changes. Mokyr emphasizes the role of useful knowledge growth, highlighting that technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic improvement. Institutional progress is essential to complement technological advancements, preventing the erosion of gains through rent-seeking and ensuring the sustainability of economic improvements. Mokyr acknowledges that the growth of economic reasonableness is not as monotonic or irreversible as technological progress. This implies that while technological advancements can drive growth, the institutional framework must evolve to support and sustain these advances. The sovereign state, with its authority and community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that technological progress translates into sustained economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.6).

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Austin, argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and economic development (Austin 2008, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This mistrust, fostered by the historical trauma of the slave trade, has created a pervasive environment of skepticism that hinders cooperative behavior essential for economic growth. The institutional framework in Africa, shaped by this historical context, struggles to foster the trust necessary for effective economic interactions. The slave trade's legacy has affected interpersonal trust and the broader social institutions that underpin economic activity. For instance, the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others, crucial for economic development, are compromised by this deep-seated mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin highlights that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, often encouraged indigenous economic and political action, which could have mitigated some of the negative institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). The enduring impact of the slave trade's mistrust culture means that even when colonial policies aimed to foster economic growth, the underlying social dynamics of mistrust persisted. This persistent mistrust continues to shape African economies today, influencing everything from intergroup relations to the effectiveness of governance and economic policies. The legacy of the slave trade in Africa, therefore, remains a critical factor in understanding the region's economic development trajectory.

These institutions ranged from extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole to settlements with rule-of-law institutions that encouraged investment. The legacy of these institutional frameworks persists, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. The institutional legacy of colonialism is further underscored by the work of North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6), who argues that institutions matter in economic growth. North shows that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights. This security is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are essential for economic development. The development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges is a growing proportion of GDP, as increasing specialization and division of labor multiply the aggregate volume of exchanges. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) supports this view, arguing that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. The prominence attached to the role of institutions enables the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists. Good institutions are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development.

(2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The equilibrium institutions designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth, have had a lasting impact on economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) further highlights the importance of institutions in economic growth, arguing that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Shared beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society, and economic failures can be ascribed to a system of shared beliefs about political legitimacy and individual rights. The institutional legacy of colonialism, therefore, remains a critical factor in understanding the region's economic development trajectory.
