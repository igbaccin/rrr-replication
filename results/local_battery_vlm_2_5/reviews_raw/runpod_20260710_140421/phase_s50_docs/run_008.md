The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic scholarship. At stake is the explanatory power of institutions vis-a-vis other factors, and the mechanisms by which institutions influence economic outcomes. Two positions dominate the debate: one emphasizes institutions as the primary driver of economic growth, while the other highlights rival causes. The debate is nuanced by the recognition that institutions can be both a cause and an effect of economic development. Understanding this relationship is crucial for policy-making and economic theory. A key point of contention is whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth. Some scholars argue that institutions are the result of economic growth, while others contend that they are a prerequisite for sustained economic development. This tension underscores the need to unpack the causal mechanisms linking institutions and economic growth. Institutional determinants of economic growth are pivotal, with institutions shaping long-run economic outcomes through their influence on transaction costs and property rights (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments during the English financial revolution lowered transaction costs, fostering economic development. This dynamic is crucial because it illustrates how institutional changes can directly influence economic performance by altering the cost structure of transactions. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping economic outcomes. The implication is that institutions that prioritize private property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms are more likely to support sustained economic growth. The varying institutional strategies adopted by European colonizers in different regions highlight the significance of historical context in determining institutional outcomes. This variability suggests that the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth can differ widely depending on the historical and political context.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. These institutions, which emerged from the Glorious Revolution, established a framework that ensured political stability and credible commitments to property rights. This transformation involved economic rules and the institutions that enforced and adapted these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) highlight that the success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which flourished under the secure property rights and political stability. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) underscores the importance of integrating institutions into long-term research programs, emphasizing that institutions should be seen as indispensable tools for economic historians. She advocates for a deeper understanding of how institutions shape economic outcomes, suggesting that a more nuanced approach is needed to fully appreciate their role. This shared focus on the institutional framework and its enforcement mechanisms underscores the critical role of political stability in fostering economic growth. However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) points out that the role of shared beliefs in shaping institutions is often overlooked, suggesting that cultural and ideological factors also play a significant part in the evolution of institutions. This nuanced view highlights that while political stability and secure property rights are crucial, the broader cultural context in which institutions operate also influences their effectiveness.

Institutions in unequal societies tend to favor elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). These policies, such as land distribution and taxation, were designed to protect elite interests, thereby hindering economic growth and development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) complement this by showing how mistrust, a byproduct of institutionalized inequality, further impedes economic development. Trust is crucial for economic development, international trade, and the functioning of political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Mistrust, rooted in historical inequalities, can undermine these very foundations. This dynamic is particularly evident in Africa, where the legacy of the slave trade has fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). This cultural mistrust, in turn, affects economic growth by reducing cooperation and social capital, essential components for sustainable economic development.

Technological and knowledge-driven growth is a primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is essential for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This perspective highlights the pivotal role of technological advancements and the dissemination of knowledge in fostering economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) supports this idea by noting that technological progress requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These adjustments are crucial for resolving growth-induced conflicts and ensuring that economic growth is sustainable (Kuznets 1973, p.1). However, Kuznets (1973, p.1) also recognizes that the pace and sectors affected by technological progress vary over time and across regions, indicating that the impact of technological advancements on economic growth is not uniform (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This variability suggests that the effectiveness of technological and knowledge-driven growth depends on the specific institutional and ideological context of a society. Mokyr (2005, p.49) further qualifies this by highlighting the importance of institutional progress in complementing technological progress, ensuring that economic improvements are sustainable and not eroded by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This interplay between technological advancements and institutional progress underscores the complex nature of economic growth, where both factors must work in tandem to achieve sustained development. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intricately linked to their ability to facilitate and support technological and knowledge-driven advancements.

The Black Death's demographic impact altered labor markets and urban dynamics, influencing the economic trajectories of early modern societies. van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.23) jointly show that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwest Europe and the rest of the continent after 1450. This wage gap was driven by the differing responses of regions to the demographic shock of the Black Death, with northwest Europe experiencing institutional flexibility that allowed for rapid economic adaptation. The shift in economic development towards the North Sea countries, notably Holland and England, was marked by a rise in the relative price level of basic foodstuffs (van Zanden 1999, p.9). Pamuk highlights that the institutional flexibility in northwest Europe, particularly in Holland, enabled new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish under weak monopolies (Pamuk 2007, p.23). This institutional flexibility was a critical factor in the economic divergence observed during this period. The differing trajectories of northwestern and southern Europe in terms of urbanization rates further underscore the significance of institutional responses to the Black Death (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This underscores the importance of institutional adaptability in shaping long-run economic growth, as regions with more flexible institutions were better equipped to respond to economic shocks and capitalize on new opportunities.

Regional economic performance over the long term is also shaped by the wage premium for skilled labor, reflecting the importance of human capital. According to van Zanden (2009, p.19) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9), the skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance. The skill premium, determined by the costs of training, the chance of successfully finishing training, the interest rate, and the number of years a skilled worker can earn a higher wage, incentivizes investment in human capital. The skill premium has a direct impact on the wages of skilled labourers, which in turn affects living standards. This suggests that while the skill premium is an important factor, it is not the sole determinant of long-term economic performance and divergence.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that African economic development has been influenced by a complex interplay of historical and social factors, including the roles of women and colonial education policies. These factors, while not directly addressing institutional causes of long-run economic growth, reveal that women played significant economic roles during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Colonial rule, however, introduced a dual legal system that often codified the subordinate status of women through customary law, thereby altering the economic landscape (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification of customary law under colonial rule, which marginalized women's access to land and resources, had profound implications for economic development. However, Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, altering the perceived benefits of education and potentially mitigating some of the negative impacts of colonial policies (Frankema 2012, p.15). This suggests that while colonial policies had mixed effects on African economic development, the role of formal education and the alteration of social institutions played a crucial part in shaping long-term economic outcomes.

Simson (2019, p.2) challenges the prevailing narrative that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were excessively clientelist and unproductive, shared by Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) who argue that this view has obscured the true economic performance of Anglophone African economies (Simson 2019, p.2; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). One specific mechanism through which these policies played out was the shift in expenditure patterns. Post-colonial governments in East Africa saw a significant increase in subsidies and interest payments, which peaked in the early 1990s, displacing both capital and recurrent spending (Simson 2019, p.11). This shift was particularly marked in Kenya and Tanzania, where high debt burdens led to substantial interest payments. Additionally, military spending grew in response to regional instability and domestic dissent, further straining government budgets. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) highlight that these patterns of economic growth and decline were closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, influenced by factors such as local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that while post-colonial budget policies were complex and often driven by immediate pressures, they were not uniformly detrimental to economic development. Instead, they reflected a range of factors that shaped the economic landscape of East Africa.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causes of poverty in Africa by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2)(Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This thesis does not account for the varied institutional evolution and policy experiences of different countries, which are crucial for understanding long-term economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, which could lead to higher production equilibria (Austin 2008, p.15). This suggests that colonial institutions were not uniformly detrimental and could be shaped by local conditions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the lack of pre-1950 GDP per capita estimates for Africa leaves many questions unanswered regarding the persistence of poverty (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Thus, while colonial institutions could adapt to local conditions, the broader structural effects of these institutions on long-term economic development remain complex and require further investigation.

Nunn (2008, p.2) and Peters (2004, p.19) show that the slave trades in Africa had profound effects on economic development and ethnic fragmentation, highlighting how historical events can shape long-term socio-economic outcomes. This prolonged period of exploitation disrupted local economic and social institutions, hindering the development of robust trading networks and centralized governance structures that could have facilitated economic growth. The Kongo kingdom, which had a centralized government, a national currency, and well-developed markets and trading networks, exemplifies how such institutions could support trade and economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Peters points out that the perception of African landholding practices as 'communal' and inhibiting individual investment in agriculture further complicated economic development (Peters 2004, p.5). These land systems, often seen as flexible and contingent, actually concealed processes of exclusion and class formation. The push for agricultural development during the final decade of colonial rule exacerbated these issues, as agricultural officers sought to raise productivity through 'modernized' farming methods that often overlooked local contexts. The legacy of these historical events continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes in Africa, underscoring the enduring impact of institutional disruptions on long-term economic growth.

Institutions play a foundational role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by North's argument that institutions matter in economic growth (North 1989, p.6). This role is most evident in the historical context of colonialism, where European settlers established different institutional frameworks based on local conditions. Acemoglu et al. argue that in colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while in colonies with low mortality rates, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These institutional differences persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutional frameworks on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This historical legacy underscores the importance of institutions in economic development, as seen in the case of Africa, where colonial land systems and agricultural policies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the relative incomes of former European colonies reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. show that the reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies was related to industrialization and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating economic growth and development. The new economic history of Africa, which emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, further supports this view (Hopkins 2009, p.8). The historical evidence shows that institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This evidence is consistent with the shared finding that institutions matter in economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth centers on the relative importance of institutions versus other factors such as geography and culture. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter in economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or whether other factors, such as geography and culture, also play a significant role (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence and empirical comparison that settle this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, which shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This evidence supports the view that institutions are a crucial determinant of economic growth and development.
