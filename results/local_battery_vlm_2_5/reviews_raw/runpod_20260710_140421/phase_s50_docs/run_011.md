The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the underlying mechanisms that drive sustained prosperity. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure interactions, can either foster or hinder economic growth. Institutions matter because they create incentives for investment and innovation, and because they shape the distribution of political and economic power. There is a live disagreement between those who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, are more influential. Some scholars emphasize the impact of extractive institutions, which concentrate power and resources in the hands of a few, while others highlight the importance of inclusive institutions that promote broad-based economic participation. The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies to promote sustainable development. The stakes are high, as the right policies can lift billions out of poverty, while the wrong ones can perpetuate inequality and underdevelopment. This issue is particularly salient in the context of former colonies, where the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape economic outcomes. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, or whether other factors play a more significant role, remains a contentious one. The extent to which colonial institutions are extractive or inclusive, and how they interact with other factors, such as geography and culture, is a key area of inquiry. This debate is ongoing, with scholars continually refining their theories and methodologies to better understand the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29) argue that the evolution of financial institutions and secure property rights are pivotal for long-run economic growth. The creation of the Bank of England in 1694 and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering a more efficient market for exchanges. This financial revolution reflects an increased security of property rights over time, a critical dimension for both capital markets and economic growth. The political model underlying this transformation involved trading certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, a response to the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. This institutional change was not merely an economic adjustment but a political one, as North and Weingast emphasize the role of political institutions in enforcing property rights and ensuring that the regime is committed to these rules. The success of these institutions is evident in the development of capital markets, which shows that the institutional framework was pivotal in fostering economic growth. However, the notion that these institutions were revolutionary is contested. Recent research has shown that France's economic performance before the French Revolution was not as disparate from England's as previously thought, suggesting that the institutional changes in England were not as singularly impactful as once believed. This indicates that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness is context-dependent and can vary across different economic and political landscapes.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that European colonists established different institutional frameworks in their colonies. These frameworks ranged from settler colonies, which developed institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. The institutional framework in a colony was not based on geographic factors but on the mortality rates of European colonists. When mortality rates were high, extractive institutions were more likely to be established. This institutional framework was pivotal in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that these institutions persisted over time, influencing current income per capita. The persistence of these institutions has a lasting impact on economic development. However, this impact is not uniform across all colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial, their effectiveness depends on the broader economic context and opportunities for industrialization.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which contrasts with Acemoglu et al.'s formulations (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). Austin's critique highlights the importance of considering the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs. He argues that colonial policies and institutional prescriptions were not monolithic but varied in response to local conditions and economic incentives. For example, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were absent, and fiscal security motivated changes in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This nuanced view of institutional evolution aligns with Ogilvie's emphasis on the importance of understanding the specific historical and institutional contexts in which economic growth occurs. Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation than efficiency for the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe. She contends that institutions are shaped by the interests and actions of various groups within society, and that these institutions can have significant impacts on economic outcomes (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). However, Sokoloff and Engerman qualify this view by arguing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This qualification suggests that while institutions are crucial for economic growth, their impact can be shaped by the broader social and political context, including the distribution of power and resources within society.

Institutional reforms in Africa have been targeted at reducing poverty, but they do not directly address long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Simson 2019, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) notes that "good" institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This focus on poverty reduction reflects a broader governance approach that emphasizes the provision of public goods and services. For example, Simson (2019, p.2) shows that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, challenging the prevailing narrative of bloated payrolls and rent-seeking public services. Simson (2019, p.2) argues that the perception of a 'neopatrimonial' logic, where government resources are treated as private property, is not necessarily detrimental to economic development. Frankema (2012, p.15) qualifies this pattern by showing that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This suggests that the impact of institutional reforms on economic growth can be influenced by historical legacies. This historical dimension underscores the importance of understanding the broader social and political context in which institutional reforms are implemented.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade's legacy of mistrust is a fundamental impediment to Africa's economic development. The slave trade has left a lasting impact on African societies, creating a pervasive culture of mistrust that hinders economic growth. This mistrust is not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's disruption of social structures; it is a deeply ingrained norm that persists independently of external factors. This historical context underscores the deep-seated nature of the mistrust that Nunn and Wantchekon identify. The slave trade's impact on trust is not solely a result of the external environment, such as the rule of law or the trustworthiness of others. Instead, it is primarily driven by internal norms of mistrust that have been shaped by the historical experience of the slave trade. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external factors, making it a critical barrier to economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The legacy of mistrust is not just a historical artifact but a contemporary challenge that continues to shape economic outcomes in Africa.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) jointly assert. This growth is not merely a byproduct of economic development but a fundamental force that propels it. Technological advancements, driven by the accumulation of knowledge, create new opportunities and efficiencies that drive economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, thereby facilitating its diffusion. Kuznets (1973, p.6) complements this by emphasizing the importance of institutional and ideological adjustments that enable societies to efficiently utilize technological innovations. This interplay between knowledge growth and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustained economic development. However, Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is often irreversible, the growth of economic reasonableness, essentially, the institutional progress that complements technological advancements, can be less monotonic and more susceptible to setbacks. This underscores the need for robust institutions that can support and sustain the benefits of technological progress over the long term. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus not just about creating the right environment for technological advancements but also about ensuring that these advancements are sustainably integrated into the economic fabric.

Institutions matter in economic growth. In fact, they are pivotal to the long-term capital market and economic growth itself (North 1989, p.9). Institutions do so by determining the security of property rights and the efficiency of property rights enforcement, which in turn determine transaction costs and the size of the transactions sector in the aggregate (North 1989, p.6). The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus not just about creating the right environment for technological advancements but also about ensuring that these advancements are sustainably integrated into the economic fabric. This is because institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In the context of the development experience of the former colonies, this implies that equilibrium institutions are likely to have been designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, not to maximize long-run growth. Institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others (Austin 2008, p.2). This is because colonial administrations that sought ﬁscal security, and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo, tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15).

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. European settlers adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In the other extreme, as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among the former colonies was related to industrialization.
