Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, but the precise mechanisms by which they exert this influence remain a subject of intense debate. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental disagreement: some scholars argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, while others contend that geographic factors or other variables are more decisive. The former camp emphasizes the role of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic development, highlighting factors such as secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms. The latter camp argues that geographic endowments, such as climate and natural resources, or other exogenous factors, are the true determinants of economic growth. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is crucial for designing effective policies aimed at fostering sustainable development. The resolution of this debate hinges on whether institutions can indeed be the primary drivers of long-term economic growth, or if other factors must be considered. The answer to this question will determine the future direction of economic policy and development efforts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonial institutions, shaped by European policies, significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. The nature of these institutions was largely determined by European mortality rates and settlement patterns. In regions where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as Africa and parts of Asia, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources back to the metropole. These institutions were not conducive to long-term economic development. Where Europeans could settle and establish colonies, such as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This difference in institutional frameworks had profound effects on the economic trajectories of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that these institutional differences persist and significantly affect current income per capita. The mechanisms through which these institutions operate are complex, but they fundamentally alter the economic incentives and opportunities available to the local populations. For instance, extractive institutions often lead to the exploitation of natural resources without reinvestment in local infrastructure or human capital, stunting economic growth. This pattern is evident in regions where colonial powers prioritized resource extraction over sustainable development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.

North and Weingast share a key finding with North that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29; North 1989, p.9). The creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting, reflecting increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This dimension is critical to both a long-term capital market and to economic growth itself. The original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues was a product of the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War. The emergence of political institutions that specify "efficient" property rights and provide increasingly effective enforcement should show up in terms of the development of economic institutions to facilitate market exchanges. The aggregate volume of exchanges multiplies as increasing specialization and division of labor multiplies. The size of the transactions sector in the aggregate will be a growing proportion of GNP, as the costs per transaction will be falling. The success of these institutions was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows. North and Weingast argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. These institutions were not so revolutionary after all, and the elaborate bureaucratic structure inherited from Louis XIV was not such a hindrance to economic growth.

This interplay highlights the multifaceted nature of colonial influence, where institutions were just one piece of a larger puzzle. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the dynamics of colonial rule, including the negotiation and imposition of institutions, played a crucial role in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. Frankema shows that the British colonial administration created a more conducive environment for formal education than other colonial powers, altering the perceived benefits of education for African societies. Hopkins notes that the institutional landscape in Africa is determined by ethnic fragmentation, the quality of governance, and the rule of law, which can vary independently of ethnic composition. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonial rule is multifaceted, with different colonial powers leaving distinct imprints on African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) (Frankema 2012, p.1) build on this by highlighting that the slave trade, a significant aspect of colonial rule, contributed to a culture of mistrust that persists today. This mistrust, in turn, affects economic development by undermining trust in political and economic institutions. The legacy of colonial rule in Africa involves the institutions imposed and the social and cultural norms that were shaped during this period. The complexity of this legacy means that understanding Africa's economic development requires a nuanced approach that considers both the institutional and cultural dimensions of colonial rule.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Frankema and van Waijenburg, challenge conventional narratives about persistent poverty in Africa by highlighting historical data and trends that reveal a more complex economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). They demonstrate that many African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that poverty was not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This finding is supported by the availability of large amounts of economic data for the colonial era, which shows that economic growth was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports. The timing and scale of growth in export production varied both between and within countries owing to a range of factors, from local endowments to transport costs and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical and geographical conditions that influenced economic development in Africa. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions about the persistence of poverty unanswered (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). Frankema and van Waijenburg address this gap by reconstructing and comparing real wage levels and trends for nine British African colonies over their period of colonial rule, providing a deeper understanding of the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This work is crucial for assessing the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have cast a temporary or structural effect on long-term African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Sokoloff and Engerman argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Austin 2008, p.2; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The unequal distribution of resources and power in these societies led to the creation of institutions that protected the interests of the elite, often at the expense of broader economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power from the outset shaped the institutional frameworks in ways that hindered economic development. This institutional bias towards elites resulted in policies that restricted competition, thereby stifling innovation and economic dynamism. Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by noting that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production when European settlers were not present to compete for African labor. This suggests that the institutional responses to local economic conditions varied, but the underlying inequality persisted, influencing the long-term economic trajectories of these societies. This dynamic underscores how institutional frameworks, shaped by initial inequalities, continued to influence economic outcomes over time, even as local conditions evolved.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates that the major technological breakthroughs that constitute dominant sources of sustained growth over long periods are epochal innovations. These innovations require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. The growth of useful knowledge is a continuous process that involves the accumulation of technological and intellectual advancements, which in turn drive economic growth. This process involves coordination failures and the need for institutional progress to complement technological progress. Mokyr (2005, p.49) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them. Without institutional progress to complement the technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited, and in the end might be frittered away and eliminated by the relentless erosion of rent-seeking. This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in supporting and sustaining the benefits of technological advancements. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth involves setting the rules of the game and adapting to and supporting the continuous process of technological and intellectual advancements.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap in Europe, offering an alternative explanation for the Great Divergence and long-run economic growth distinct from institutional factors (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). One such change was the significant increase in wages in northwest Europe, which was driven by a decrease in the supply of labor after the Black Death. This wage increase was not uniform across Europe; rather, it varied regionally due to differences in institutional flexibility. For instance, the institutional environment in Holland allowed for rapid economic adaptation, enabling new towns to spring up and rural manufacturing to flourish. This institutional flexibility fostered economic growth by facilitating investment and productivity enhancements in agriculture. Broadberry and Gupta note that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, as it did in the modern period. The high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwest Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that while institutional flexibility was important, the service sector's role in economic growth was also significant. The structural changes initiated by the Black Death thus had a complex interplay with institutional factors, highlighting that long-run economic growth was influenced by multiple factors beyond institutions alone.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is well-documented. Institutions create an environment where economic activity can thrive by providing secure property rights and fostering investment. North argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, for example, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced economic growth (North 1989, p.9). These institutional changes were critical in creating an environment conducive to long-term economic development.

The nature of these institutions and their impact on economic growth can vary significantly across different historical contexts. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different types of institutions in colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variability in institutional development highlights that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be influenced by a variety of factors, including the initial conditions and the specific policies implemented by colonial powers. For instance, in colonies where European settlers were present, the institutional frameworks were more likely to support economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In contrast, in regions where European settlers were absent, the institutions were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The divergence in institutional frameworks across colonies underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the relative incomes of former European colonies have reversed due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, provides further insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This perspective is supported by Austin, who notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work highlights that the institutional frameworks established during colonialism had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, influencing their ability to industrialize and achieve long-term economic growth. The structural changes initiated by the Black Death thus had a complex interplay with institutional factors, highlighting that long-run economic growth was influenced by multiple factors beyond institutions alone. This interplay is evident in the high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwest Europe, which arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production (Austin 2008, p.15).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the new economic history of Africa. Hopkins argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This perspective is supported by North and Weingast, who emphasize that institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The new economic history of Africa highlights that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This perspective is supported by North, who argues that institutions are crucial for economic growth, emphasizing that secure property rights and effective enforcement mechanisms reduce transaction costs, thereby facilitating market exchanges (North 1989, p.6). The new economic history of Africa thus provides valuable insights into the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, highlighting the importance of institutional reforms in promoting economic development.
