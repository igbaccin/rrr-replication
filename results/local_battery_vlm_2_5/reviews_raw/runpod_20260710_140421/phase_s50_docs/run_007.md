The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding both historical trajectories and contemporary policy challenges. Institutions, encompassing the rules and norms that govern economic and political interactions, are widely acknowledged as critical factors in economic performance. The mechanisms by which institutions influence growth, and the degree to which they function independently of other factors, are still hotly debated. One key position argues that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, emphasizing their role in creating stable, predictable environments that foster investment and innovation. This view is often supported by studies that highlight the importance of secure property rights and effective governance in promoting economic development. Another perspective suggests that institutions are one of several factors affecting growth, with geographic endowments, cultural factors, and technological advancements also playing significant roles. This perspective underscores the complexity of economic development, suggesting that institutions alone cannot explain the diverse growth experiences of different regions. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary causal factor in economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other variables to shape long-run economic outcomes. The resolution of this debate has profound implications for policy, as it determines whether institutional reforms should be the primary focus of development efforts or if a more holistic approach is necessary. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of new financial instruments and political institutions that specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This institutional evolution facilitated long-term capital markets and economic growth. A critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that the success of the English financial revolution was remarkable, as evidenced by capital markets. This institutional commitment to property rights and efficient enforcement mechanisms was pivotal in fostering economic growth. This institutional framework lowered transaction costs and secured property rights, which are vital for sustained economic development.

However, Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) contend that the primary drivers behind these core economic institutions were distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. The institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild, were shaped by the struggles between different social groups over resources and power, not by efficiency considerations. These institutions were designed to protect the interests of the powerful. The same pattern is seen in the New World societies, where institutions were shaped by the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality led to the development of institutions that advantaged elites and restricted competition, contributing to the persistence of inequality. The institutions of the New World societies were thus shaped by the same distributional conflicts that shaped the institutions of pre-industrial Europe. Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different institutions in colonies based on mortality rates, leading to varying economic outcomes. In places with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where mortality was low, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This pattern of institutional development, shaped by mortality rates, set the stage for long-term economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to these institutional changes, not geographic factors. The extractive institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists hindered long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) also note that the impact of these institutions varies and can be influenced by other factors such as population density and prosperity. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism thus played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies, with significant implications for current economic conditions.

The legacy of colonial institutions thus continues to shape the economic landscape of former colonies, offering insights into institutional reforms that can alleviate poverty, as highlighted by Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) explains that some African societies have been able to uphold the rule of law and develop progressive institutions despite ethnic heterogeneity (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This indicates that institutional quality is shaped by other factors, not predetermined by ethnic diversity. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further support this by noting that African poverty is not a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, suggesting that historical and geographical conditions have had a temporary effect on African welfare development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.2). This shared finding underscores that the economic trajectory of Africa can be influenced by institutional reforms. Hopkins (2009, p.18) also notes that the prominence attached to the role of institutions has enabled the new economic history of Africa to claim the attention of development specialists, highlighting the crucial role of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). Therefore, understanding the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa and exploring how these relate to other parts of colonial Africa can help assess the possibilities of future growth and the extent to which specific historical or geographical conditions have affected long-term African welfare development.

Technological advancements, not institutions, are the primary driver of economic growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight the growth of useful knowledge as a primary driver of economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). They argue that technological progress is crucial for sustained economic development, as it requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. In the context of modern economic growth, Kuznets (1973, p.1) points out that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving conflicts that arise from technological changes (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This resolution is essential for the sustainability of economic improvement, as Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement could be limited and eventually eliminated by rent-seeking behaviors (Mokyr 2005, p.54). The technological progress can be seen as a driver of economic growth in the long run, and the institutional progress is a necessary complement to it. The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. This implies that fostering technological advancements and ensuring that institutions adapt to these changes can lead to long-run economic growth.

This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2), who argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, significantly impacting trust levels and potentially long-run economic growth. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the internal norms of mistrust that developed during this period, not merely a byproduct of the slave trade's impact on the trustworthiness of others. This internal mistrust is at least twice as large as the external mistrust, which is shaped by the rule of law and the trustworthiness of others (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this dynamic by showing that colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. This suggests that the legacy of mistrust is an active force that continues to shape contemporary economic and political institutions. The persistence of this mistrust can hinder economic development by undermining the social capital necessary for effective cooperation and trust-based economic interactions.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the enduring effects of colonial institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from extractive states to those that enforced the rule of law. These institutional differences persist and significantly impact current income per capita. Similarly, Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrates that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, illustrating the dynamic nature of institutional development. This continuity highlights that the legacy of colonial institutions is an active force that continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The persistence of these institutional legacies underscores their critical role in long-run economic growth, as they shape the economic and political landscape of former colonies.
