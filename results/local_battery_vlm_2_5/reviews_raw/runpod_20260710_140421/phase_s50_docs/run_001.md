The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue. At stake is the understanding of how societies develop economically over extended periods. One live disagreement centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. The debate also involves the nature of these institutions: are they extractive, designed to benefit a few at the expense of many, or are they inclusive, fostering broad-based economic development? Additionally, the discussion extends to the factors that influence the establishment and persistence of these institutions, such as colonial policies and local conditions. The causal mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth are also a subject of contention, with some arguing for direct effects and others pointing to indirect pathways through investment and innovation. The interplay between institutions and other economic factors, such as technology and human capital, further complicates the picture. The question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth is also debated. Some scholars argue that institutions are shaped by economic conditions, while others contend that they are independent variables that drive economic outcomes. The nature of institutions, whether they are formal rules or informal norms, adds another layer of complexity to the discussion. The debate over the role of institutions in economic growth is not just academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and development strategies. Understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth can inform efforts to promote sustainable development and reduce poverty. The debate over the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, involving a range of scholarly perspectives and empirical evidence. One stream of scholarship emphasizes the direct causal relationship between institutions and economic growth, arguing that the quality of institutions is a key determinant of long-run economic performance. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Building on this, North and Weingast (1989, p.29); (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They highlight that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments dramatically reduced transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights over time. This institutional development was critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.29) note that the success of these institutions is evident in capital markets, showing that the regime's commitment to these rules was a critical political factor. This institutional framework encompassed economic exchange rules and how these rules were enforced and changed. North (1989, p.6) elaborates that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement should decrease costs per transaction, even as the size of the transactions sector grows. This dynamic underscores the importance of institutions in facilitating market exchanges and supporting economic growth. North (1989, p.9) further points out that the fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, which was a product of the evolving political landscape. This historical context shows that the institutional changes in England were deeply intertwined with broader political and economic developments.

This influence extends beyond Europe, as colonialism's institutional legacy has profoundly shaped the long-term economic trajectories of former colonies, as evidenced by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33). These institutions are the product of colonial strategies that varied according to European mortality rates. Extractive institutions, which maximized rents for European colonists, were established in colonies with high mortality rates. This phenomenon is not limited to a single region or time period. For instance, the extractive institutions established in the Congo and the Gold Coast persistently affected current income per capita. These institutions are shaped by powerful groups who may not prioritize economic development, or who may fear losing political power with institutional change. This dynamic is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, which is tied to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. The resulting economic stagnation in these regions is a direct consequence of the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers. These institutions, designed to maximize short-term benefits for colonizers, hindered long-term economic growth by discouraging investment and innovation. The impact of these institutions on long-run economic growth is further underscored by the persistence of economic disparities between former colonies with different institutional legacies. In the absence of these extractive institutions, the economic trajectories of many former colonies might have been very different.

Distributional conflicts are a crucial factor in shaping economic institutions, challenging efficiency-based explanations for pre-industrial Europe's core economic structures such as serfdom and guilds, as Ogilvie and Sokoloff and Engerman (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2) jointly argue. The persistence of these institutions can be attributed to the power dynamics between different social groups, where elites often impose rules that maintain their dominance. For example, guilds in pre-industrial Europe were efficient arrangements for regulating craftsmanship and instruments for controlling access to skills and markets, thereby preserving the economic status of their members. This perspective highlights that institutions are deeply influenced by the distribution of power and the interests of dominant groups. The implications of this power dynamic extend to the persistence of inequality, as institutions that favor elites can stifle economic growth by limiting opportunities for broader segments of the population. The impact of distributional conflicts on institutions varies across societies. The specific historical and political contexts in which these conflicts unfold shape the institutional outcomes, as shown by the differing trajectories of economic development in the New World societies. The varying factor endowments and initial conditions in these societies led to different degrees of inequality, which in turn influenced the institutions that evolved. For instance, the extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power in many New World societies resulted in institutions that reinforced this inequality, restricting competition and limiting economic opportunities for the majority. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, had lasting effects on economic growth, as it hindered the development of inclusive institutions that could foster widespread prosperity.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) share the view that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth. Economic growth is fundamentally driven by technological advancements, which in turn require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use. Mokyr (2005, p.49) emphasizes the role of institutional progress in complementing technological progress, suggesting that without such progress, economic improvements would be unsustainable. These innovations spread to a substantial part of the world, indicating that technological progress has a global impact. Kuznets (1973, p.1) further argues that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the importance of political stability in sustaining economic growth. This suggests that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by institutional developments that can manage the social and political challenges that arise from economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner show that Anglophone African economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.2). This finding, shared with Broadberry and Gupta, challenges conventional narratives that Africa was economically stagnant (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The mechanism driving this outcome was the expansion of foreign trade and export markets. These markets facilitated economic growth by creating demand for local products and integrating African economies into the global market. Broadberry and Gardner demonstrate that the timing and scale of growth in export production varied widely due to factors such as local resource endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This variation highlights the role of institutional factors in shaping economic performance. While export markets drove growth, the institutional environment determined how effectively economies could capitalize on these opportunities. Institutions that facilitated trade and investment were crucial for sustaining and amplifying the benefits of export-led growth. Broadberry and Gupta caution that the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed. The gains from trade were concentrated among skilled laborers, indicating that the fruits of early modern development were unevenly spread (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that while institutions enabled growth, they also influenced the distribution of its benefits, shaping long-run economic trajectories.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impacts highlights various factors influencing public goods provision but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. This approach emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, suggesting that effective institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) states that "good" institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This highlights that the institutional framework established during colonial rule played a significant role in shaping economic conditions, which in turn influenced long-term development. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) also point out that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves many questions unanswered, indicating that the persistence of poverty in Africa is not solely due to structural impediments. This suggests that while institutions are important, other factors such as historical and geographical conditions also play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Frankema, highlight that women's roles and educational development under British colonial rule were pivotal in shaping African economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.15). This gendered division of labor was actively shaped by colonial institutions that codified and entrenched patriarchal norms. This institutional shift created new opportunities for economic advancement, particularly for those who could access formal education. The benefits of this education were not evenly distributed. The implementation of a dual legal system, comprising European courts and native courts, further entrenched the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to the economic opportunities that education might otherwise have provided (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional framework, while promoting education, thus also perpetuated gender inequalities that had profound implications for long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) share a focus on historical and cultural factors that shaped Africa's economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. The thesis of the 'reversal of fortune' and its critiques focus on how historical factors, such as the slave trade, shaped African economies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) highlight the enduring impact of the slave trade on African economies, arguing that it fostered a culture of mistrust which persists to this day. This mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the trust necessary for economic transactions and the functioning of institutions. Austin (2008, p.15) illustrates this mechanism through colonial governance, showing that when European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. These findings suggest that the historical and cultural factors highlighted by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis and its critiques continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is thus intertwined with the broader historical and cultural context in which they operate.
