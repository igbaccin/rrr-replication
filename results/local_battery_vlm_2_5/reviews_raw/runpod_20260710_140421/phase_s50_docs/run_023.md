The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations. At stake is the identification of the fundamental drivers of economic success and failure over centuries. One live disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which institutions influence growth. One position argues that institutions directly shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives and constraints under which economic actors operate. A rival position posits that institutions are merely one factor among many, with other variables such as geography, culture, and initial conditions playing equally or more significant roles. Another perspective suggests that the impact of institutions is mediated through their interaction with other factors, such as population density and prosperity. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other variables to produce long-run economic outcomes. The role of institutions in economic growth depends on the quality and enforcement of rules, which can promote or impede economic development (Author, Year, p.N). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

The development of financial institutions like the Bank of England and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution was crucial for economic growth, as it facilitated long-term capital markets and reduced the costs per transaction. They argue that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by the development of capital markets. A critical political factor in this process was the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment supported economic growth and market development, including the rules governing economic exchange and the institutions that enforce and modify these rules. This implies that while transaction costs were reduced, the overall volume of exchanges increased, driving economic growth.

The legacy of these institutions varied widely, with some promoting growth and others impeding it. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers established different institutions in their colonies depending on the mortality rates they faced. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, they established extractive institutions designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. Where mortality rates were low, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, persisted long after colonial rule ended. This persistence of institutions is critical because it means that the economic outcomes of former colonies are deeply rooted in their colonial past. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.19) show that these institutions have a profound impact on current income per capita, highlighting the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on economic development. The long-run economic growth of former colonies is thus intricately linked to the institutions they inherited from their colonial past, which in turn were shaped by European policies and the specific contexts of colonization.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9) examine how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality shaped economic outcomes by advantaging elites through various government policies. They argue that the initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power led to the persistence of inequality over time, which in turn affected the course of development through the institutions that evolved. The institutions that developed in these societies were shaped by the interests of the elite, who sought to maintain their privileged position by restricting competition and limiting opportunities for social mobility. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.15) support this view by highlighting how the unequal distribution of resources and power in these societies led to the development of institutions that favored the elite. They note that the high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance, suggesting that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This growth was unevenly distributed, with skilled laborers benefiting more than unskilled laborers. This uneven distribution of economic benefits further reinforced the existing inequalities in these societies. The implications of this institutional framework are profound: the persistence of inequality not only hindered economic growth but also created a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite's control over institutions ensured their continued dominance. The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule thus played a central role in shaping the long-run economic trajectory of these regions, with the elite's control over institutions ensuring their continued dominance and the persistence of inequality.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) show that the impact of colonial rule on African education was not uniformly negative, and that British rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers. The implementation of new social and political institutions by the British colonial administration altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which was driven by demand from African political leaders and families. This demand was shaped by cost-benefit calculations, where the benefits of formal education were related to new opportunities created by colonial rule. The British colonial administration's approach to education, which included the establishment of missionary schools and the promotion of Western education, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The impact of colonial education on African poverty is complex. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments, and that the lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves these questions unanswered. This suggests that the impact of colonial education on African poverty is complex and multifaceted, and that the long-term picture of economic development in Africa is influenced by a range of factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) argue that technological progress, driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge, is the primary driver of long-run economic growth. The accumulation of useful knowledge is a key mechanism through which technological progress unfolds. Mokyr (2005, p.49) highlights the role of formal institutions in reducing access costs to knowledge, which facilitates the dissemination of innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) elaborates on how this process is not linear and requires institutional and ideological adjustments. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adaptation is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.6) further explains that the sovereign state plays a crucial role in resolving growth-induced conflicts, ensuring that economic growth is possible without excessive costs or long-term weakening of the political fabric of society. Mokyr (2005, p.54) qualifies this by noting that while technological progress is more irreversible, economic reasonableness is less so. This suggests that while technological progress is a powerful driver of economic growth, it requires sustained institutional progress to ensure long-term sustainability.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is fundamentally tied to their ability to establish and enforce property rights, which in turn foster economic development. Institutions that ensure secure property rights facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, which are critical for economic growth. This is evident in the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, which significantly lowered the cost of transacting and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework allowed for the development of a long-term capital market, which is essential for sustained economic growth. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England further underscores the importance of secure property rights. The political factors underpinning economic growth include the regime's commitment to these rules, which is crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutional changes were pivotal in creating an environment conducive to economic growth, as seen in the remarkable success of capital markets in England during this period.

The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth is profound, as evidenced by the different colonization strategies adopted by Europeans. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic development was encouraged. In colonies where extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, economic growth was hindered (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This differential institutional framework had lasting effects on the current income per capita of these regions. The legacy of mistrust, exacerbated by colonial administrations that often discouraged indigenous economic production, continues to shape contemporary African development. This mistrust is a product of historical institutional responses that have had deep and lasting effects on long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.15; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27). The enduring legacy of mistrust in Africa highlights the critical role of institutions in economic development, as mistrust undermines the trust necessary for economic exchanges and development.

The historical impact of institutions on economic growth is further illustrated by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal is primarily due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The creation of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita, underscores the enduring impact of historical institutions on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The legacy of mistrust in Africa, caused by the slave trade, persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is a product of historical institutional responses that have had deep and lasting effects on long-run economic growth.

There is a shared finding that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. This is evident in the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, which were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The precise mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth remain a subject of debate. For instance, North and Weingast argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Acemoglu et al. emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The specific historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement involves measuring the impact of different colonization strategies on current income per capita. This measurement type, used in Acemoglu et al.'s work, shows that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates had a significant and lasting effect on economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
