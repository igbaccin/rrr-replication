The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses whether and how political and economic structures influence sustained prosperity. At stake is the understanding of why some nations thrive while others stagnate, and the potential for policy interventions to foster growth. The live disagreement centers around the causal mechanisms by which institutions affect economic outcomes. Some scholars emphasize the direct impact of institutions on economic growth, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance can foster investment and development. Others argue that institutions are shaped by deeper historical and political forces, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex and mediated by various factors. The question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one piece of a larger puzzle remains unresolved. The debate hinges on understanding the interplay between institutions and other determinants of economic performance, such as geography, culture, and historical legacies. The resolution of this question has significant implications for economic policy and development strategies, as it informs how resources should be allocated to promote sustainable growth. North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) show that the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments in seventeenth-century England significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering secure property rights and thereby promoting long-run economic growth. This decline reflected increased security of property rights, a critical dimension for both a long-term capital market and economic growth. The fiscal crises of the Hundred Years' War drove the original trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues, setting the stage for institutional changes that would support economic development. North and Weingast further argue that the political factors underpinning economic growth included the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to or bound by the rules governing economic exchange. This commitment was crucial for the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights. The success of these institutional changes is evident in the evidence from capital markets, which shows a remarkable transformation in economic performance. This institutional framework, however, did not operate in isolation. North and Weingast qualify the revolutionary nature of these changes by noting that France's economic performance before the French Revolution had been significantly upgraded, suggesting that institutions per se were not the sole determinant of economic growth. This qualification underscores the complex interplay between institutions and other economic factors, highlighting that while secure property rights and reduced transaction costs were essential, they were part of a broader set of conditions that influenced long-run economic growth. The development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England in seventeenth-century England fostered secure property rights and reduced transaction costs, setting the stage for economic growth that extended beyond the immediate institutional changes.

The interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks is essential for sustaining long-run economic growth, a shared finding of Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). Kuznets highlights that technological progress necessitates corresponding institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently utilized. This is because modern technology often conflicts with traditional social structures, such as rural lifestyles and extended family patterns. These conflicts can only be resolved peacefully if the state plays a crucial role in mediating them. This resolution is a necessary condition for modern economic growth (Kuznets 1973, p.1). Mokyr elaborates on this by emphasizing the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth, noting that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, the sustainability of economic improvement would be limited. Mokyr also suggests that without institutional progress to complement technological progress, economic improvement would be limited in its sustainability (Mokyr 2005, p.54). This implies that while technological advancements are crucial, they must be accompanied by the right institutional frameworks to ensure long-term economic growth. Institutional adjustments are a necessary precondition for the effective deployment of technological changes. This underscores the dynamic nature of economic growth, where institutions must continually evolve to support new technologies and innovations.

Pamuk argues that the Black Death initiated structural changes leading to a wage gap in Europe, contributing to the Great Divergence (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.23). This structural change was a social transformation that altered the distribution of wealth and power, in addition to being an economic shift. The Black Death led to a significant reduction in the labor force, which in turn increased the bargaining power of surviving workers. This power shift allowed workers to demand higher wages, particularly in northwest Europe. The institutional flexibility in regions like Holland enabled a rapid response to new economic opportunities, fostering urbanization and commercialization (Pamuk 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) highlight that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwest Europe was driven by merchant distribution and finance, as well as production. This suggests that the service sector played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period, similar to its role in the modern period. This uneven distribution of benefits challenges the notion that institutional explanations alone can account for long-run economic growth. The institutional flexibility in northwest Europe, which allowed for the rapid adaptation to new economic opportunities, was a key factor in the region's economic success. This institutional flexibility allowed for the rapid adaptation to new economic opportunities, making it a necessary precondition for the effective deployment of technological changes.

The literature on institutional changes due to colonialism argues that varying colonization strategies led to distinct economic outcomes in former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) propose that the differences in European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality. These institutions, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, focused on rapid resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional design had profound implications for the long-term economic development of these colonies. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected the current income per capita of these regions. The impact of these institutions varied across all colonies. For instance, in colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, the economic outcomes were markedly different. This variation in institutional design and its economic implications highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) examine the economic performance of African economies, particularly the colonial period, but do not explicitly tie these patterns to institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Instead, they focus on the role of historical patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that the "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This thesis suggests that the economic performance of African economies during the colonial period can be understood in the context of broader patterns of institutional evolution and policy experience. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that where European settlers were not present to compete for African labor, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This pattern suggests that the economic performance of African economies during the colonial period was influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including the presence of European settlers and the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. The economic performance of African economies during the colonial period was influenced by a range of other factors, in addition to being determined by institutions.

Institutional reforms in Africa have been primarily directed at poverty reduction (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This focus on poverty reduction is evident in the emphasis on 'good' institutions that provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. The new economic history of Africa has gained attention from development specialists precisely because of this emphasis on institutions. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) further contribute to this understanding by highlighting the importance of exploring the determinants of real wage divergence across British Africa. This exploration is crucial for understanding the long-term picture of economic development, including the distribution of income. The lack of pre-1950 African GDP per capita estimates leaves some questions unanswered, particularly regarding the persistence of poverty. This gap in data underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the historical and geographical conditions that have influenced long-term African welfare development. The implication is that while institutional reforms have been effective in addressing immediate poverty issues, their impact on long-run economic growth remains an area that requires further investigation and data collection.

The impact of inequality and slave trade on institutions highlights how historical factors shaped economic development in different regions (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Nunn 2008, p.2). The slave trade, as Nunn shows, had a profound impact on Africa's economic development. The African continent's prolonged experience with multiple slave trades over nearly 500 years suggests that the legacy of the slave trade was more significant than previously acknowledged (Nunn 2008, p.2). Nunn's (2008, p.2) analysis underscores the role of centralized governance and well-developed markets in facilitating trade, which could have mitigated some of the adverse effects of the slave trade. The lack of these institutions in many African societies during the period of the slave trade exacerbated the negative impacts on economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). Thus, the historical legacy of inequality and the slave trade continues to influence the institutional frameworks that shape long-run economic growth in these regions.

The historical legacy of institutions profoundly influences long-run economic growth. Institutions that enforce the rule of law and encourage investment, as opposed to extractive states, are pivotal. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different colonial institutions based on mortality rates, leading to varied economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence persists, significantly impacting current income per capita. Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that different historical periods and paths must be considered (Austin 2008, p.2). He highlights that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which shaped institutional prescriptions. Thus, the nature of colonial institutions, whether extractive or growth-oriented, continues to shape the economic trajectories of former colonies.

The role of institutions in economic growth is underscored by their impact on property rights and transaction costs. North argues that institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This is evident in the English financial revolution, where the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments led to a decline in transaction costs. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional design, shaped by colonial policies, has lasting effects on economic development.

The new economic history of Africa provides insights into institutional reforms that can eliminate poverty. Hopkins argues that good institutions are crucial for providing incentives for investment, essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). He notes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily correlate with poor institutional quality, suggesting that Africa's economic challenges are linked to institutional deficiencies (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This highlights the importance of institutional quality in economic growth, as seen in the historical experiences of former colonies. North and Weingast demonstrate that credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). They show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.
