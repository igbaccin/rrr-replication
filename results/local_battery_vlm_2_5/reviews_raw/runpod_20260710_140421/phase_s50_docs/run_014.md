The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic theory, with significant implications for understanding the trajectories of nations over centuries. The stakes are high: identifying the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth can inform policy, shape our understanding of historical economic development, and predict future trends. At the heart of this debate are two primary positions: one emphasizes the central role of institutions in fostering economic growth, while the other highlights the importance of cultural beliefs and distributional conflicts in shaping economic outcomes. The interaction between these positions reveals a complex landscape where institutions are dynamic entities that evolve in response to economic, political, and social forces. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is multifaceted, involving the creation of property rights, the enforcement of contracts, and the development of financial systems. This interplay is crucial for understanding how nations transition from stagnation to sustained economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” North (1989, p.9) reports, “The creation of the Bank of England (1694) and the development of new financial instruments led to a dramatic decline in the cost of transacting and have been described as the English financial revolution.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that institutions are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Institutions shape economic growth by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights, which are essential for investment and long-term economic development. The implication is that the type of institutions established in a region directly influences its long-term economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these institutions can be extractive, designed to maximize rents for colonizers rather than promote long-run growth, highlighting the enduring impact of initial institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the economic legacy of colonial institutions persists, shaping the development paths of former colonies.

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They show that the political commitment to enforce rules and the development of institutions that supported these rules were crucial for economic development. The success of England's economic growth can be attributed to the establishment of credible commitments that underpinned economic institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further emphasizes the significance of institutions, arguing that they should be an indispensable instrument in the toolkit of every economic historian (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). The shared finding is that institutions play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes. However, the role of institutions is not limited to economic growth; they also influence the distribution of resources and power within societies. This distributional impact of institutions can be seen in the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild. Distributional conflicts shaped these institutions. The implication is that the design of institutions can have long-lasting effects on economic development and the distribution of resources.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2); (Nunn 2008, p.2) and Nunn (2008, p.2) highlight how specific historical contexts shaped institutional frameworks, influencing long-term economic trajectories. Their research underscores the role of inequality and the slave trade in molding institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) focus on the New World, arguing that societies with greater inequality developed institutions that favored elites, restricting competition and perpetuating inequality through government policies. Nunn (2008, p.2) examines Africa, demonstrating that the continent's slave trades, which spanned nearly 500 years, significantly impacted subsequent economic development. The persistence of inequality in the New World and the adverse effects of the slave trade in Africa illustrate how historical contexts can shape institutions in ways that have lasting economic consequences. Nunn further notes that the absence of centralized governance and well-developed markets hindered trade in some African regions, emphasizing the importance of institutional structures in facilitating economic activity. Nunn's findings imply that the impact of the slave trade on African economic development was not uniform across the continent, highlighting the importance of local institutional contexts in shaping economic outcomes. The implications of this historical context for long-term economic development are profound, as the institutional legacies of inequality and the slave trade continue to influence contemporary economic trajectories.

The growth of useful knowledge is highlighted as a primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements and the accumulation of knowledge are crucial for sustained economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This technological progress, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes, requires institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. These institutional and ideological adjustments are crucial because technological progress alone is insufficient for sustained economic growth; it must be complemented by institutional progress to address coordination failures and prevent the erosion of rent seeking. This variability underscores the importance of local contexts in shaping the interplay between technological progress and institutional development. The sovereign state, with authority based on loyalty and a community of feeling, plays a crucial role in peacefully resolving growth-induced conflicts, thereby facilitating the integration of technological progress into the economy.

The literature on African economic development and colonial impact highlights that institutional change in Africa was influenced by both endogenous and exogenous factors (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). Hopkins (2009, p.18) demonstrates that African institutions are shaped by a complex interplay of internal dynamics and external pressures, such as colonial rule. This interplay is evident in the way colonial administrations introduced new governance structures that coexisted with pre-existing African institutions, creating a hybrid institutional landscape. For example, the colonial impact on Africa's institutional development is reflected in the introduction of legal and administrative systems that often replaced or supplemented traditional African governance structures. This institutional hybridity is a key mechanism through which African institutions evolved, shaped by both endogenous factors, such as pre-colonial governance structures, and exogenous factors, such as colonial policies. Frankema and van Waijenburg further illustrate that the colonial era's real wage levels and trends in British African colonies varied significantly, indicating that economic outcomes were influenced by both local and external factors. This variability underscores the complex nature of institutional change in Africa, where both endogenous and exogenous factors played crucial roles. This suggests that while colonial institutions influenced African economic development, the outcomes were mediated by the ability of African societies to adapt and integrate these institutions into their existing frameworks. The interplay between endogenous and exogenous factors in shaping African institutions highlights the importance of understanding the local context in institutional reforms, as these reforms must account for both internal and external influences to be effective.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the role of women and education in African economic history is pivotal, yet the literature does not directly address how institutions shape long-run economic growth. Women's contributions to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods were significant, as they were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming. (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This marginalization was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which privileged men and limited women's access to land for cash crops. (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional changes, such as the introduction of a dual legal system, can alter economic outcomes by reshaping access to resources. The demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. (Frankema 2012, p.15). The impact of these institutional changes varied across different colonial contexts. For instance, British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. (Frankema 2012, p.1). This suggests that the type of colonial institution matters, as it can either facilitate or hinder economic development through education. The varying impacts of colonial institutions on education and economic development in Africa underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of how different institutional frameworks can influence long-run economic outcomes.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the structure and functioning of societies over extended periods. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of vastly different institutional frameworks in colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had profound and lasting effects on economic development, highlighting the critical role that institutions play in determining economic outcomes. North (1989, p.9; 1989, p.6) argues that the development of economic institutions that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs is essential for economic growth. The creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments were pivotal in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, thereby fostering economic growth. This underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment conducive to economic activity.

The specific nature of institutions matters significantly in shaping economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) demonstrate that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, can persist because they benefit politically powerful groups. This persistence of extractive institutions can hinder long-run economic growth, as these institutions are designed to maximize rents for the ruling elite rather than promote broad-based economic development. North and Weingast (1989, p.29; 1989, p.2) provide evidence from capital markets showing that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) illustrates that the type of institutions established can either facilitate or impede economic development, depending on how they allocate power and resources within society.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and cultural beliefs. Ogilvie (2007, p.12; 2007, p.3) argues that distributional conflicts often drive the formation of core economic institutions. This perspective suggests that the institutions that emerge in a society are shaped by the underlying power dynamics and cultural beliefs, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Hopkins (2009, p.18; 2009, p.8) highlights the importance of 'good' institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development. This shared finding (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.8) underscores the importance of institutions in creating an environment that encourages economic activity and growth.

Institutions shape long-run economic growth by creating the framework within which economic activity occurs. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions matter for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). The precise remaining disagreement is that the type of institutions that matter for economic growth is contested (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). The historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles this disagreement is the difference in the effects of extractive versus inclusive institutions on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
