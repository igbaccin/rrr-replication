The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding the divergent economic trajectories of nations. At the core of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who view institutions as the primary drivers of economic growth and those who emphasize other factors such as geography or culture. Some scholars argue that institutions that enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic growth. Others contend that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that factors such as population density, prosperity, and cultural beliefs play a more significant role. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. For instance, if institutions are the key drivers of economic growth, then policy interventions aimed at strengthening institutions could be highly effective. If other factors are more important, different policy approaches may be necessary. Understanding the role of institutions in economic growth is therefore essential for designing effective development strategies and for addressing the persistent economic disparities observed globally. For example, the divergence in economic performance between former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North and Weingast (1989, p.29) (North 1989, p.9) argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional change was a response to fiscal crises, notably the Hundred Years' War, which led to a trade of certain rights to Parliament in exchange for revenues. This shift reflects a broader pattern where political institutions specify and enforce property rights, thereby facilitating market exchanges and reducing transaction costs (North 1989, p.6). The success of these institutional changes is evident in the development of capital markets, which provide a clear indication of the effectiveness of these reforms (North and Weingast 1989, p.29). North and Weingast's analysis highlights the critical role of credible commitment in economic growth, where the regime's adherence to rules governing economic exchange is essential for fostering a stable and predictable environment for investment (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The broader economic performance of England and France highlights that institutional changes must be understood in the context of other factors, including bureaucratic structures and economic policies.

The growth of useful knowledge is a primary driver of economic growth, and technological and intellectual advancements are more fundamental than institutional frameworks in fostering long-term economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.1; Mokyr 2005, p.49). This suggests that while institutions facilitate the application of new technologies, the underlying engine of growth is the advancement of knowledge itself. The institutional framework serves as a necessary but not sufficient condition for economic growth, as it enables the efficient use of technological innovations. Kuznets (1973, p.1) acknowledges the role of the sovereign state in resolving growth-induced conflicts, highlighting the importance of political stability in sustaining economic growth. This stability is crucial for the long-term application of technological advancements, as it ensures that the benefits of new knowledge are not eroded by social or political unrest. This implies that while technological advancements are a consistent driver of economic growth, the institutional framework that supports them may fluctuate, potentially impacting the sustainability of economic development.

The historical context of colonialism provides a unique lens through which to examine the impact of institutional changes on economic outcomes. The core argument of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27), (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) is that European colonization strategies varied significantly, with some colonies, such as the United States and Australia, receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others, like the Congo and the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks was driven by mortality rates among Europeans, with high mortality leading to the establishment of extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term economic development. This institutional legacy has persisted, significantly impacting current income per capita in these regions. This mechanism highlights how the initial conditions set by colonial powers have long-lasting effects on economic development, as the institutions established during colonization can become entrenched and resistant to change. The path to economic growth is determined by technological advancements and immediate economic policies, as well as the historical legacy of institutional frameworks.

In Africa, the legacy of colonial institutions has left a lasting imprint on the provision of public goods, influencing economic development trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) note that the new economic history of Africa highlights the importance of institutions in providing incentives for investment and development (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This underscores the enduring impact of colonial rule on African economies, as the institutions established during this period have shaped the provision of public goods. The mechanism by which this occurs is through the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often reinforced the marginalization of certain groups, particularly women. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the codification process, which further entrenched gender disparities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional framework affected economic activities and influenced the distribution of income and long-term welfare development in Africa. The persistence of poverty in Africa reflects the complex interplay of historical, geographical, and institutional factors. Hopkins (2009, p.18) emphasizes the importance of "good" institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This institutional framework, shaped by colonial legacies, continues to influence African economic development today.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Nunn (2008, p.2) demonstrate that the slave trade in Africa fostered a culture of mistrust that has persisted over centuries, with significant impacts on contemporary economic development. This mistrust is deeply ingrained and affects both intergroup relations and individual norms, creating a pervasive environment of skepticism. The slave trade's legacy is an internal norm that shapes individual behaviors and attitudes. Nunn (2008, p.19) illustrates that the lack of centralized governance and well-developed markets in many African societies hindered their ability to engage in sustained trade, exacerbating the economic stagnation caused by the slave trade. This mistrust, coupled with the historical absence of robust institutions, has created a challenging environment for economic growth. The enduring effects of the slave trade on trust levels highlight the profound and lasting impact of historical institutions on contemporary economic development.

The institutional approach to long-run economic growth oversimplifies the causal mechanisms at play, as Austin (2008, p.2) and Ogilvie (Austin 2008, p.2; Ogilvie 2007, p.12) argue that the role of distributional conflicts and other factors have been overlooked. This thesis fails to account for the nuanced evolution of institutions, investment, and policy experiences that span centuries. Colonial governments' institutional prescriptions responded to indigenous economic and political actions, which contradicts simplified models of institutional change (Austin 2008, p.15). Serfdom, the community, the craft guild, and the merchant guild were shaped by power dynamics and the distribution of resources, not merely by efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). Ogilvie (2007, p.12) also notes that institutions are crucial and must be integrated thoughtfully into economic history research to avoid oversimplification (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted factors influencing long-run economic growth, including the role of distributional conflicts and the dynamic nature of institutional change.

One of the most compelling arguments regarding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is the idea that institutions are shaped by power dynamics and distributional conflicts, not merely by efficiency considerations. Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutions established by European colonizers were designed to maximize rents for the colonizers (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Ogilvie supports this perspective, contending that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These arguments underscore the importance of considering the political and social contexts in which institutions operate, as well as the interests of the powerful groups that shape them. Moreover, institutions matter because they are crucial to providing incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). This is because institutions that enforce property rights and contract enforcement facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs, thereby promoting economic growth (North 1989, p.6).

The historical evidence from colonial institutions shows that extractive institutions, which were designed to transfer resources from colonies to the metropole, significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights the enduring impact of institutional legacies on economic outcomes. The role of institutions in economic growth extends beyond colonial contexts. North argues that the development of new financial instruments and institutions, such as the Bank of England, led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs and promoted economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional change reflects increased security of property rights, which is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The English financial revolution, for example, was a product of fiscal crises and the trade of certain rights to Parliament in return for revenues (North 1989, p.9). This shows that institutions are shaped by historical contexts and that their impact on economic growth is mediated by these contexts. The new economic history of Africa shows that institutions matter for development because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development (Hopkins 2009, p.8). It also shows that institutions are shaped by historical and political contexts (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The precise remaining disagreement is whether the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This argument is grounded in the historical evidence from colonial institutions, which shows that the impact of institutions on economic growth is mediated by historical and political contexts. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which were shaped by the interests of European colonizers and had enduring impacts on economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27).
