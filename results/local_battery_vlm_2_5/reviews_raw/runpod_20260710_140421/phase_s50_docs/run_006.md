The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic historiography, with significant implications for understanding the divergent economic trajectories of nations over centuries. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in economic development and those who argue that institutions are merely one of many factors, with other variables such as geography, culture, or colonial legacies playing equally or more significant roles. This disagreement is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making, as understanding the drivers of economic growth can inform strategies for development and poverty reduction. The debate centers on whether institutions are the primary cause of economic growth or whether they are secondary to other factors. This question is particularly salient in the context of colonial histories and the persistence of economic disparities between former colonies and their former colonizers. Institutions shape economic outcomes by determining the incentives for investment, the security of property rights, and the efficiency of market exchanges. The degree to which these institutional frameworks are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth is a contentious issue. The efficacy of institutional reforms hinges on whether institutions are seen as a cause or a consequence of economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

North (1989, p.9) and Acemoglu et al. (North 1989, p.9; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27) argue that institutions shape long-run economic outcomes through their influence on transaction costs and property rights. This institutional development facilitated market exchanges and economic growth, illustrating how institutions can lower transaction costs and foster economic development by establishing secure property rights. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) extend this argument by showing how different colonization strategies led to varying institutional outcomes. In colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while in other colonies like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence had lasting effects on economic development, with colonies that received law-enforcing institutions achieving higher income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further qualify this pattern by emphasizing that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This suggests that the initial institutional framework set by colonial powers had enduring effects on economic trajectories, with some colonies benefiting from institutions that encouraged investment and economic development, while others were hindered by extractive institutions that prioritized resource extraction over long-term growth.

The enduring effects of these institutions are further illustrated by the impact of property rights on economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.29) and Ogilvie (2007, p.12) argue. The credible commitment underpins growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.29), as it reduces the risk of expropriation and encourages investment. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) elaborate that the degree to which a regime adheres to the rules governing economic exchange is a critical political factor. Ogilvie (2007, p.12) further emphasizes the importance of shared beliefs in shaping institutions, noting that these beliefs make up part of the institutional structure of a society. The evolution of institutions in England during this period was marked by a shift towards more secure property rights and a reduction in arbitrary state actions, which facilitated economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.3) cautions that institutions should be treated as an indispensable instrument in economic history. The institutional changes in seventeenth-century England were about both economic rules and the political factors underpinning their enforcement and change, which had significant implications for long-term economic development.

The historical legacy of the slave trade in Africa, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27), Austin (2008, p.2), and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), underscores the enduring impact of institutions on economic development. This mistrust is both a cultural norm and an external environmental factor that affects economic outcomes. The slave trade's impact on trust is a persistent factor that continues to influence contemporary economic development. This mistrust is a fundamental factor that shapes economic behavior and institutional outcomes. Austin highlights that the institutional evolution in Africa was influenced by colonial policies that often exacerbated mistrust and reduced economic opportunities (Austin 2008, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate that the institutions that emerged in societies with high inequality, such as those affected by the slave trade, were designed to advantage elites and restrict competition, thereby perpetuating economic disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.4). This institutional design, rooted in historical mistrust, continues to shape economic policies and outcomes in contemporary Africa.

This historical context is crucial for understanding the economic divergence between Europe and Asia, as examined by Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and van Zanden (2009, p.19). These authors jointly establish that the productivity of the traded goods sector in north-western Europe was significantly influenced by merchant distribution and finance, which played a crucial role in economic growth during the early modern period. This mechanism was further reinforced by the service sector, which allowed for the falling relative price of manufactured goods. This pattern of economic growth was unevenly distributed, with skilled labourers benefiting more than unskilled labourers. Van Zanden (2009, p.19) further notes that the guilds in western Europe, which were formal institutions, took care of the problems related to apprenticeship contracts. The beneficial effects of these institutions on the intergenerational transfer of skills were counterbalanced by their attempts to form local cartels. This pattern of economic growth is not straightforward, as the skill premium, which is the remuneration for investing in human capital, is determined by a complex interplay of factors including the costs of training, the chance of successfully finishing the training period, and the interest rate linking future earnings to present costs. Van Zanden (2009, p.3) elaborates on the skill premium by noting that it is determined by the costs of producing skilled workers, which include the unearned income during the training period and other expenses related to the training period. The skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance, as it reflects the investment in human capital and the expected returns from that investment. This interplay of factors highlights the complex nature of economic divergence and the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of women and colonial institutional changes in Africa, as discussed by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Hopkins (2009, p.18), further illustrates the multifaceted nature of economic growth. The marginalization of women during colonial periods, where they were often relegated to less productive lands for food farming, highlights a key institutional factor that shaped long-run economic outcomes. This marginalization was reinforced through the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which systematically disadvantaged women's access to productive resources. The colonial administration introduced a dual legal system where European courts operated alongside native courts, further entrenching patriarchal structures that limited women's economic contributions. This institutional framework affected gender roles and had broader implications for economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.8) elaborates on this by noting that the new economic history of Africa emphasizes the role of institutions in providing incentives for investment, which is crucial for development. The impact of these institutional changes varies across Africa. Englebert concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation does not necessarily determine the quality of public goods provided by African governments, indicating that societies can be ethnically heterogeneous but still capable of upholding the rule of law and developing progressive institutions. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role in shaping economic outcomes, the long-run effects are mediated by other factors, including the ability of societies to adapt and develop resilient institutions.

The development of formal education during British rule, as detailed by Frankema (2012, p.15) and Simson (2019, p.2), highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. This framework was shaped by both macro-level political decisions and micro-level family calculations regarding the benefits of education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The growth in military spending and the shift in expenditure patterns post-independence highlight the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic management (Simson 2019, p.11). This suggests that the institutional framework established during colonial rule had lasting effects on economic policies and outcomes, even as new governments emerged. The British colonial legacy, particularly in education, played a significant role in shaping long-run economic development in sub-Saharan Africa.

This legacy of colonial education sets the stage for understanding the broader drivers of long-term economic growth, as discussed by Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49). Mokyr (2005, p.49) and Kuznets (1973, p.1) both highlight that technological advancements require institutional adaptations to be efficiently utilized. This interplay between technological progress and institutional adjustments is crucial for sustaining economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.1) notes that technological progress necessitates changes in societal structures, such as the transition from rural to urban lifestyles and the adaptation of family patterns. These shifts are essential for integrating new technologies into the economy, which in turn drives long-term growth. Mokyr (2005, p.49) also recognizes that institutional progress is not as linear or irreversible as technological progress, indicating that the sustainability of economic growth depends on continuous institutional adaptation. This implies that while technological innovations are the primary drivers, the institutional framework must evolve to support and integrate these innovations effectively.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the necessary framework for technological advancements to drive sustainable economic improvements. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European settlers established different colonization strategies, setting up institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or created extractive states that hindered growth. This variation in institutional frameworks directly influenced the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies. North (1989, p.9) adds that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments led to a significant decline in transaction costs, which is critical for long-term economic growth.

The historical context of institutional development further illustrates the importance of institutions in economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This perspective aligns with North's (1989, p.6) view that the emergence of political institutions specifying efficient property rights and providing effective enforcement is essential for economic growth. The interaction between institutions and economic opportunities, such as industrialization, plays a central role in the long-run development of former colonies.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly impacted the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis can be over-simplified if it compresses different historical periods and paths. This aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial for the long-run development of these colonies. The institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, has enduring effects on economic growth and development.

Institutional development is not a linear process, and the sustainability of economic growth depends on continuous institutional adaptation. This is evident in the historical evidence showing that institutions must evolve to support and integrate technological innovations effectively. The development of institutions that facilitate market exchanges and reduce transaction costs is a growing proportion of GNP, reflecting the increasing specialization and division of labor. The historical record shows that institutions that provide secure property rights and effective enforcement are crucial for long-term economic growth, and the evidence from former colonies supports this view. Furthermore, the new economic history of Africa shows that 'good' institutions matter because they provide incentives for investment, which is essential for development. Hopkins (2009, p.18) concludes that ethno-linguistic fragmentation has no significant effect on the quality of public goods provided by African governments, highlighting the importance of progressive institutions in economic development.
