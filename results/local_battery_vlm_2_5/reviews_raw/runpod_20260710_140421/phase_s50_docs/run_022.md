The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. At the heart of the debate lies a disagreement over the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth. One camp emphasizes the critical role of institutions in fostering economic development, arguing that institutions shape incentives, enforce property rights, and facilitate market exchanges. Another camp, however, proposes that institutions are merely one piece of a broader puzzle, and that other factors, such as cultural beliefs or geographical determinants, play a more significant role in economic growth. A third camp posits that institutions themselves are shaped by deeper underlying factors, such as population density or colonial policies, which in turn influence economic outcomes. Understanding these differing perspectives is essential for comprehending the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. The central question of whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or merely one of many factors remains a subject of intense scholarly inquiry.

Economic historians have long recognized the importance of institutions in economic growth. North argues that institutions matter because they shape the incentives that drive economic behavior. Institutions, according to North, are the rules of the game in a society, and they determine the costs and benefits of economic activities. When institutions are well-designed, they can reduce transaction costs, enhance the security of property rights, and encourage investment (North 1989, p.9; North 1989, p.6). Acemoglu et al. build on this foundation, arguing that the type of institutions established in colonies significantly influenced their long-run economic development. In colonies where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law, economic growth was robust. In colonies where extractive institutions were imposed, economic growth was limited. This divergence in institutional frameworks has had lasting effects on income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by historical events and colonial policies, underscores their enduring impact on economic outcomes.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.29) argue that the development of financial institutions and the creation of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth by establishing secure property rights. This financial revolution involved new instruments and the political institutions that specified and enforced property rights. The impact of these institutions was profound, as the evidence from capital markets shows. This development was remarkable, as the evidence from capital markets shows (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions was underpinned by the degree to which the regime or sovereign was committed to or bound by these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). These institutions were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth.

The legacy of these institutions extends far beyond their immediate economic impact. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that European settlers established different institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. Extractive institutions were more likely in colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, as they aimed to extract resources rapidly. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists. This pattern is evident in regions like the Congo and the Gold Coast, where European powers established extractive states to transfer resources back to the metropole. The persistence of these institutions significantly affected current income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development. This impact is qualified by the role of population density and prosperity, which influenced the policies Europeans pursued. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) also note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.

Distributional conflicts are pivotal in shaping economic institutions, as Ogilvie (2007, p.12) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) both highlight. These conflicts challenge efficiency-based explanations of economic structures by showing that the core institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by the interests of powerful groups rather than optimal resource allocation. In pre-industrial Europe, the powerful interests of landowners and guilds ensured that institutions like serfdom and guilds persisted despite their inefficiencies. These institutions, in turn, shaped the economic landscape by restricting competition and maintaining inequality. The influence of distributional conflicts is evident in the persistence of institutions that favored elites, such as the guilds in pre-industrial Europe. These guilds, which restricted competition and maintained high prices, were maintained because they benefited powerful groups. This dynamic is further illustrated by the persistence of extreme inequality in New World societies, where institutions advantaged elites through various government policies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.4) show that the initial factor endowments of New World societies, such as the abundance of land and natural resources relative to labor, led to extreme inequality in wealth, human capital, and political power. This inequality, in turn, shaped the institutions that evolved in these societies, perpetuating the advantages of the elite. This institutional framework, shaped by distributional conflicts, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality, highlighting the enduring impact of these conflicts on economic development.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.2) shared the finding that the impact of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa was more positive than other colonial powers, and that the region had higher per capita income than previously thought. These calculations were influenced by the implementation of new social and political institutions, which altered the perceived benefits of formal education. The British colonial administration, with its emphasis on indirect rule and local governance, created an environment where education was seen as a pathway to social mobility and political influence. This institutional framework, shaped by the interplay of colonial policies and local agency, fostered the development of formal education systems in the region. This economic growth was closely tied to foreign trade and the market for exports, which were influenced by a range of factors including local endowments, transport costs, and political institutions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, Frankema (2012, p.1) notes that the origins of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa were complex and multifaceted, involving a range of actors and motivations. The British colonial administration's policies, while generally more conducive to education, did not uniformly benefit all regions or social groups. The legacy of British colonial rule on education in sub-Saharan Africa was thus shaped by a complex interplay of colonial policies, local agency, and economic factors. This interplay highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth in the region.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Hopkins (2009, p.18) jointly argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical paths of economic development. This simplification, according to Austin, limits the ability to test the institutionalist approach quantitatively and explain why some countries adopt institutions favorable for economic growth while others do not (Austin 2008, p.2). Hopkins (2009, p.18) highlights that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms, suggesting that the quality of public goods and the rule of law are not solely determined by ethnolinguistic fragmentation but by the broader institutional framework (Hopkins 2009, p.18). This shared finding underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts and institutional paths that shape economic development. However, the institutional reforms suggested by Hopkins (2009, p.18) are qualified by the need for a nuanced approach that considers the unique historical and political contexts of African countries. This nuance is crucial because it acknowledges that the effectiveness of institutional reforms depends on the specific institutional legacies and economic conditions present in each country. Therefore, a one-size-fits-all approach to institutional reform is unlikely to be successful in promoting long-run economic growth in Africa.

The slave trade has had a profound and lasting impact on African development, shaping a culture of mistrust that persists to this day and affects trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.2). This historical context is crucial because it highlights how the slave trade's legacy of mistrust has had a more enduring impact on African societies than colonial institutions. The mechanisms through which the slave trade has influenced Africa's development are multifaceted. This internal mistrust has deeply ingrained norms that continue to affect economic and social interactions. Nunn further supports this by showing that the slave trade disrupted local economic and social institutions, making it difficult for African societies to support trade and economic development. The implications of this mistrust are significant, as it has hindered the development of economic and social institutions necessary for growth. Nunn qualifies this by noting that while the slave trade had an adverse effect on economic development, the specific impact varied across different regions and time periods. This variation suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is complex and interacts with other historical and contemporary factors. Therefore, understanding the full extent of the slave trade's impact on African development requires a nuanced approach that considers both its direct and indirect effects.

Kuznets (1973, p.1) and Mokyr (2005, p.49) both argue that technological progress is a dominant source of sustained growth, and that institutions must adapt to efficiently use these innovations. Mokyr notes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that technological advancements are crucial for sustained economic development. Kuznets further elaborates that the pace of technological progress and economic growth, as well as the institutional and ideological adjustments necessary for their interplay, have varied across centuries and regions. This variation underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and regional contexts in which technological advancements occur. Kuznets highlights that modern economic growth is possible only if growth-induced conflicts are resolved without excessive costs (Kuznets 1973, p.6). This implies that while technological progress is essential, it must be accompanied by institutional progress to ensure sustainability. Mokyr (2005, p.54) recognizes the possibility of coordination failures and suggests policies to rectify them. This indicates that technological progress alone is not sufficient for economic growth; institutional progress is also necessary to complement technological advancements. The growth of economic reasonableness, as Mokyr puts it, is far less monotonic and irreversible than technological progress, suggesting that the institutional framework must continually adapt to support economic growth. Technological advancements, therefore, are a critical factor in shaping long-run economic growth, but their impact is contingent on the institutional environment in which they occur.

Institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by providing the framework within which technological advancements can be effectively utilized. Acemoglu et al. show that different colonization strategies led to varying institutional setups, with some promoting investment and rule of law while others established extractive states focused on resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This highlights that the impact of technological progress is contingent on the institutional environment. North argues that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England and the development of financial instruments were critical in reducing transaction costs and enhancing the security of property rights, which are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only facilitates market exchanges but also encourages long-term capital investment, which is vital for sustained economic development. The interplay between technological progress and institutional development is evident in the historical context of Europe, where the financial revolution and the establishment of secure property rights were pivotal in fostering economic growth (North 1989, p.9).

The role of institutions in economic growth is further underscored by the observation that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributable to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the policies pursued by European colonists, shaped by population density and prosperity, significantly influenced the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Ogilvie, who emphasizes that distributional conflicts often drive the establishment of core economic institutions, rather than efficiency considerations (Ogilvie 2007, p.12). These institutions, whether extractive or growth-promoting, are shaped by the interests of politically powerful groups, highlighting the importance of institutional design in economic outcomes. The historical evidence shows that the presence of good institutions, which provide incentives for investment, is crucial for economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.8).

The debate on the role of institutions in economic growth revolves around the relative importance of extractive versus growth-promoting institutions. Shared findings show that institutions matter for long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.6; Hopkins 2009, p.8). The disagreement lies in whether the reversal of fortune in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes or other factors. Acemoglu et al. argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This is supported by Austin, who notes that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional quality and its impact on economic outcomes, as shown by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This evidence highlights the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, regardless of geographic or other factors.
