The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a fundamental question in economic development. At stake is understanding the mechanisms that drive sustained economic progress and the conditions under which institutions either facilitate or hinder growth. A key debate centers around the extent to which institutional frameworks, shaped by historical and political factors, determine economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions in driving growth, arguing that well-designed institutions foster economic development by ensuring property rights, enforcing contracts, and promoting investment. This perspective is often aligned with the view that institutions can be exogenous, shaped by historical events and colonial legacies. The opposing position suggests that institutions are endogenous, evolving in response to economic and social changes, and that their impact on growth is contingent on broader contextual factors. This view highlights the importance of local conditions and the adaptive nature of institutions. The disagreement hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or whether they are shaped by broader economic and social dynamics, a question that has profound implications for policy and development strategies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. highlight how European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected income per capita. This pattern is evident in the stark contrast between colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where Europeans set up institutions enforcing the rule of law and encouraging investment, and those like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence, driven by differential mortality rates among Europeans, was pivotal in shaping the long-run economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize the importance of population density and prosperity in shaping European policies, which in turn influenced the institutional frameworks established in these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These policies interacted with the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, playing a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. However, the specific impact of these institutions on economic growth varied depending on the colonial context, as Acemoglu et al. document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that it was related to industrialization. This interplay between institutions and industrialization opportunities underscores the complex role of colonial institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austen's analysis (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20) establishes that shifts in land abundance in Ghana during the colonial period triggered significant institutional changes in factor markets. This institutional revolution was marked by the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony, which, alongside the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, fundamentally altered the economic landscape. These legal actions, though not initially induced by changing factor scarcities, had profound economic implications, particularly in facilitating the transition from coerced to free labour. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on the role of colonial administrations in responding to economic and political actions, highlighting that administrations seeking fiscal security often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic activities. This adaptive behaviour underscores the dynamic nature of institutional change in colonial contexts, where economic and political actions could influence institutional outcomes.

Economic growth in African societies during the colonial period was driven by both men and women, despite the latter being often marginalized in historical accounts. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) both show that women played a significant role in economic growth and development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) reveal that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming. Women's marginalization did not hinder their contributions to economic growth. Bohannan (1959, p.10) also highlights the economic shifts brought about by the introduction of money into subsistence economies, noting that this shift led to both increased trade and a greater number of markets (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This economic dynamism was further reinforced by the introduction of a dual legal system under colonial rule, which included European courts based on common and civil law, as well as native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced patriarchal alliances, which in turn shaped the economic roles of women. Women's economic contributions extended beyond food farming. Women also played a crucial role in the cash crop economy, despite being de-privileged in access to land. This pattern of economic change underscores the complex interplay between gender roles, economic activities, and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth. The introduction of money and the codification of customary law under colonial rule significantly altered the economic landscape, highlighting the role of institutions in driving economic growth.

The complexities of economic growth and technological advance are multifaceted, reflecting a dynamic interplay of factors that shape long-term economic performance. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share a view that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) identifies social capability as a crucial element, encompassing technical competence and institutional frameworks that support large-scale enterprise and financial markets. This capability is not easily quantified, making it challenging to integrate into simple convergence models. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) highlight the role of labour market conditions and regional migration in shaping wage rates, which can serve as proxies for average earnings and labour productivity. The vibrant labour market in early modern China, particularly for short-term or day labour, underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in facilitating economic activity. The long-term labor market and income disparities observed in regions like Wuxi county suggest that while institutions support economic integration, they also contribute to income stratification. This points to a nuanced role of institutions in economic growth, where they both enable and constrain economic outcomes, shaping long-term economic performance in complex ways.
