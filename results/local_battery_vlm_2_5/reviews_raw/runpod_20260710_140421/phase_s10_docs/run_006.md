The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics. At stake is understanding how and why certain societies achieve sustained economic progress while others stagnate. The live disagreement revolves around the significance of institutions versus other factors such as geography or initial conditions. One position emphasizes the primacy of institutions, arguing that the quality and type of institutions established during critical periods, such as colonial rule, have enduring effects on economic outcomes. This perspective highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in fostering or hindering economic growth. Another position suggests that while institutions matter, their impact is contingent on other factors and cannot be isolated as the sole determinant of economic performance. The interplay between institutions and other variables, such as economic opportunities and political dynamics, shapes long-run economic outcomes. The debate underscores the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth, and how these mechanisms interact with other factors. The question of how institutions shape long-run economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of historical and contemporary economic processes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share a posture that technological advancement and social change are intertwined with economic growth. They assert that the interplay between these factors influences productivity and living standards over time. One mechanism through which this interplay occurs is the role of social capability. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) identifies social capability with technical competence and institutional frameworks, including political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions. These institutions facilitate the organization and management of large-scale enterprise and mobilize capital for individual firms. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) substantiate this view by documenting the existence of a vibrant and active labour market in early modern China, where wage rates served as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group. This suggests that institutional frameworks that support labour market integration can drive economic growth by enhancing productivity and living standards. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) qualify this implication by noting that the precise proportion of the labour market remains unclear, indicating that the relationship between technological advancement, social change, and economic growth is complex and multifaceted. This complexity underscores the need for further research into the specific institutional arrangements that facilitate or hinder economic growth.

The introduction of money into subsistence economies can lead to profound changes in economic structures, shifting away from multicentric systems despite potential pain points (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bohannan 1959, p.9). This shift is driven by the integration of local economies into broader, global markets, which can alter the dynamics of trade and exchange. This integration increased trade and brought local economic institutions into contact with the world economy, facilitated by colonial trading companies (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The use of brass rods as currency within the prestige sphere illustrates how money can initially fulfill specific functions, such as payment, but struggle to achieve equivalency in other areas like marriage or food exchange (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This partial integration suggests that the introduction of money can create a dual economy, where traditional exchange systems coexist with modern monetary systems. The complexities of this transition are evident in the challenges of achieving equivalency between traditional and modern economic values, underscoring the need for institutions that can mediate this shift. This duality can lead to economic growth, but it also requires institutions that can manage the tensions between traditional and modern economic practices.

Collaborative research is indispensable for precise measurement of economic performance, as Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) demonstrate. The Maddison Project has set a precedent of collaborative research that has been highly successful in producing estimates of long-term economic development, despite the significant margins of error inherent in historical economic data. This project has been instrumental in advancing the field, as it has encouraged scholars to revisit and refine Maddison's estimates, thereby improving the accuracy of historical economic data. Even with these improvements, the estimates are still subject to a degree of subjectivity, as they are based on assumptions about the links between partial data and economic activities. This subjectivity is particularly pronounced when dealing with data from the more distant past, where the margins of error are larger. The Hitotshibashi team's work on Asian national accounts exemplifies this challenge, as they have experimented with various margins of error to account for the subjectivity in their estimates. This ongoing debate underscores the need for continued collaborative research to improve the accuracy of historical economic data and to better understand the long-term economic development of countries.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Allen (2001, p.8) show that the economic contributions of women and the divergence in real incomes between European cities are critical to understanding long-run economic growth. The de-privileging of women in access to land, reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, was a key factor in the economic development of Africa. This pattern of land access and legal codification is paralleled in Europe, where the maintenance of high real wages in some cities contrasted with the falling incomes in others. Allen (2001, p.23) shows that the gains in British real wages between 1815 and 1850 were cyclical, not a long-term trend. This cyclical pattern suggests that the economic development of European cities was influenced by factors beyond simple population changes, including the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.20) highlight that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system, which reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law, further shaping economic development. This institutional framework affected land access and influenced broader economic activities, such as the codification of customary law under colonial rule. The interplay between institutional changes and economic activities underscores the complex role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. These findings suggest that the economic contributions of women and the divergence in real incomes are interconnected through the institutional frameworks that governed land access and legal systems.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) show that shifts in land abundance in Ghana during the colonial period led to significant institutional changes in factor markets. These changes were marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity drove an institutional revolution, as colonial administrations responded to changing factor scarcities by altering their institutional prescriptions. Austin (2008, p.2) illustrates that colonial governments, seeking fiscal security, adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, which created incentives for institutional change. This dynamic is evident in Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to hypotheses that predicted otherwise (Austin 2008, p.15). The institutional changes facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, which had important economic dimensions, as noted by Austin (2007, p.20). The integration of British rule also had significant market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the colonies that would later form Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional adaptation under colonial rule highlights how historical shifts in land abundance and scarcity can drive economic growth by fostering institutional changes that facilitate market integration and labour transitions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, where they did not settle permanently. These institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment. This mechanism is underscored by the fact that Europeans adopted very different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In colonies where Europeans settled, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies with high mortality rates, Europeans set up extractive states with the intention of transferring resources rapidly to the metropole. This institutional divergence had significant economic implications, as extractive institutions hindered economic development and perpetuated poverty. The extractive institutions established by colonial powers in high-mortality colonies were not conducive to economic growth. This pattern is further illustrated by the fact that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization, and that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies.
