The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question that engages scholars in understanding the dynamics of economic development. This question is intellectually significant because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. The live disagreement centers around the causal influence of institutions on economic outcomes. One position emphasizes that institutions, particularly those that enforce the rule of law and secure property rights, are critical for sustained economic growth. Another perspective posits that institutions play a more complex role, with historical contexts and colonial legacies molding the institutional frameworks of various regions. The interplay between institutional design and economic performance is complex, involving multiple factors such as population density, colonial policies, and local economic conditions. This debate is crucial for understanding why some regions have experienced sustained economic growth while others have lagged behind. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is further complicated by the role of colonial policies, which introduced different institutional frameworks in various regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

This institutional legacy, however, was not uniform across all colonies, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue, leading to diverse economic trajectories. They demonstrate this through the divergent strategies Europeans employed in colonizing different regions. In some cases, Europeans settled in colonies and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. In other instances, they created extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, exemplified by the Congo or the Gold Coast. This variation in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. also stress that these institutions were influenced by the interests of politically powerful groups. In the context of colonialism, this meant that institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional design had lasting effects, as these extractive institutions persisted even after independence, continuing to hinder economic development in many former colonies. Acemoglu et al. note that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced during colonialism. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development trajectories of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth, highlighting how the legacy of colonial rule continues to shape economic outcomes in the present day.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) show that the institutional changes in land markets and labor relations are crucial for economic development. This institutional revolution was marked by the transition from coerced to free labor, which had significant economic implications. Austin (2007, p.20) supports this view by noting that the 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony played a pivotal role in this transition. This ordinance, followed by the 1908 prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti, facilitated the shift from coerced to free labor, which in turn drove economic growth. This institutional change was influenced by local economic and political actions as well as external pressures. Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates on this by stating that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This highlights the importance of local agency in shaping institutional changes. This suggests that while local agency is important, broader administrative and economic factors also play a significant role in shaping institutional changes and economic development. The interplay between local and external factors in shaping institutional changes underscores the complexity of economic development processes.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) both argue that the process of economic growth catch-up is complex and multifaceted, influenced by factors beyond mere convergence. They highlight the critical role of social capability and technological change in shaping this process. Social capability encompasses a country's technical competence, institutional frameworks, and experience in managing large-scale enterprise and financial markets. This capability is crucial for absorbing and implementing new technologies, which in turn drives economic growth. The complexity of this process is further underscored by the fact that social capability is not easily quantifiable or measurable, making it a challenging factor to integrate into simple convergence models.

The dynamic interplay between technological advance and social change is central to understanding economic growth catch-up. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that technological change can alter the productivity rankings of countries, including those at the top. This dynamic is influenced by institutional changes that can either facilitate or hinder growth. For instance, institutional sclerosis or age-related institutional maladies can limit the growth of even the most advanced economies. This perspective is supported by Abramovitz (1986, p.21) who identifies that the organization and management of large-scale enterprise, as well as the capacity to mobilize capital through financial institutions and markets, are key components of social capability. These factors are essential for countries to effectively absorb and implement new technologies, thereby driving economic growth. The implication is that countries with strong institutional frameworks and high social capability are better positioned to achieve sustained economic growth through technological advancement. The qualitative nature of social capability means that its impact on economic growth can vary widely across different contexts.

The real wages in China did not follow the same upward trend observed in Europe. This perspective offers a counterpoint to the broader narrative of Asian economic performance. The data on wage rates in China indicate that they served as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole. This suggests that the economic conditions in China during this period were marked by a lack of significant improvement in living standards, despite the presence of a vibrant and active labour market. The existence of a high degree of integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions near major urban centres further supports the idea that wage rates were a reliable indicator of economic conditions. The rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War in Britain (Allen 2001, p.23) underscores the variability in economic performance across different regions and time periods. This variability highlights the importance of contextual factors in shaping economic outcomes, and suggests that the institutional and economic continuities of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries in China may have limited the potential for significant economic growth during this period.

These profound changes, as established by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10), highlight the intricate relationship between social dynamics and economic institutions in shaping development trajectories. Akyeampong and Fofack detail that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, marginalizing women's access to land and economic opportunities. This process is exemplified by the migration of farmers in search of land for cocoa production, which drove significant economic growth in the Gold Coast (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). However, the specific conditions under which these economic transformations occurred varied, as Bohannan notes that the introduction of money did not uniformly displace traditional exchange systems but rather coexisted with them, fulfilling specific functions such as payment (Bohannan 1959, p.9). Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis of the codification of customary law and Bohannan's examination of the introduction of money into subsistence economies together highlight the complex interplay between social institutions and economic development in Africa during the colonial period. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes, even in the absence of direct causal links to long-run growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between political, social, and economic factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes. The extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers in regions with high mortality rates, such as the Congo and the Gold Coast, were designed to maximize resource extraction and not promote economic development. These institutions persisted and had a lasting impact on income per capita. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This institutional legacy highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic growth, even in the absence of direct causal links to long-run growth.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) shows that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic highlights the role of institutions in adapting to local conditions and the importance of considering the complex interplay between social institutions and economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour. This underscores the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes, even in the absence of direct causal links to long-run growth.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes are further illustrated by the codification of customary law and the introduction of money into subsistence economies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) highlight how the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the marginalization of women in access to land, particularly in the context of cash crops. This institutional change had significant economic implications, as women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The codification of customary law through a dual legal system of European courts and native courts further entrenched patriarchal alliances and the subordinate status of women. This institutional framework shaped economic outcomes by limiting women's access to productive resources and reinforcing gender inequalities. Austin (2007, p.20; 2007, p.4) shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This institutional change was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the loss of slaves and the transition from coerced to free labour. The imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, including the removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within Ghana. This institutional framework shaped economic outcomes by facilitating the integration of factor markets and promoting economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the institutional changes introduced by colonialism and the specific mechanisms through which these institutions shaped economic outcomes. The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement centres on the extent to which these institutions were extractive versus adaptive to local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the codification of customary law and the introduction of money into subsistence economies, which highlights the complex interplay between social institutions and economic development. This interplay underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes, even in the absence of direct causal links to long-run growth.
