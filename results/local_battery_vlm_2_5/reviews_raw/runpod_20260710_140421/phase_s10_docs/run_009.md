The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in the study of economic development. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods and what factors are most influential in this process. One live disagreement in this field centres around whether institutions or geographic factors are the primary drivers of economic growth. Some scholars argue that the quality of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, is crucial for sustained economic development. Others contend that geographic factors, such as climate and natural resources, play a more significant role in determining a region's economic trajectory. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for policy-making and development strategies. The institutions-first camp often points to historical examples where strong institutions have led to economic prosperity, while the geography-first camp highlights cases where natural endowments have driven economic growth. The question of whether institutions or geography are more influential is central to understanding the mechanisms behind economic development and the potential for policy intervention to foster growth. The debate is particularly relevant in the context of former colonies, where the legacy of colonial institutions and the impact of geographic factors are intertwined. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly impacted long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. explain that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework was not conducive to long-term economic development, as it prioritized short-term resource extraction over investment and growth. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering more robust economic growth. The persistence of these institutions over time has had lasting effects on the economic performance of these regions. Acemoglu et al. note that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the industrialization opportunities that these institutions either facilitated or hindered. The interaction between these institutions and the ability to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings highlight the critical role that institutions play in shaping economic outcomes, as the initial institutional frameworks set by colonial powers continue to influence economic performance centuries later.

The dynamic interplay between resources and institutions, as Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) observe, is pivotal in understanding how economic growth responds to changing resource landscapes. Austin (2007, p.20) further elaborates that the transition from land abundance to scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 triggered an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional shift was driven by internal dynamics, such as the economic and political actions of indigenous populations. The institutional changes varied across different contexts. For instance, in areas where European settlers were absent, colonial governments often encouraged indigenous commodity production, which led to different institutional outcomes. This nuanced view of institutional change, considering both local and colonial influences, underscores the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Abramovitz 1986, p.5) emphasize that the catch-up process is multifaceted, influenced by technological and social dynamics that go beyond simple convergence. These factors extend beyond simple economic indicators to include the institutional frameworks that underpin economic activity. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that the concept of social capability, encompassing technical competence and institutional robustness, plays a critical role in a country's ability to achieve sustained economic growth. This capability is crucial for the organization and management of large-scale enterprises and the mobilization of capital. The institutional environment, therefore, acts as a catalyst for economic growth by facilitating the absorption and application of new technologies and managerial practices. This includes the presence of well-functioning financial markets and institutions that can mobilize capital efficiently. The dynamics of institutional change varied across different contexts. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Allen (2001, p.8) argue that real wages in historical contexts can provide valuable insights into economic conditions and growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) explore this by examining the stagnation of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. This period saw no significant improvement in living standards, as wages remained largely unchanged despite economic growth in other regions. Allen (2001, p.8) further elaborates on the complexities of real wage measurements, noting the importance of distinguishing between wholesale and retail prices in historical data. This distinction is crucial for accurately assessing living standards and economic conditions. The use of retail prices, which are more reflective of consumer experiences, can provide a more nuanced understanding of real wages and economic growth. Allen (2001, p.23) also highlights the cyclical nature of real wage trends, noting that significant gains in living standards often occur during specific periods. This cyclical pattern underscores the importance of considering long-term economic cycles when analyzing real wages and their impact on economic growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) further supports this view by emphasizing the need for comprehensive data on labour market conditions to accurately assess real wages. The integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas, as well as the vibrant labour market in early modern China, are critical factors in understanding the stagnation of real wages during this period. The interplay between institutional frameworks and economic conditions is crucial for comprehending long-term economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) and Feinstein and Thomas highlight the importance of precise historical economic data, underscoring the need for rigorous measurement in understanding long-term economic growth. The further back in time one goes, the larger the margins of error become, but there are notable exceptions, such as the quality of data for medieval England compared to other regions (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when the data were incomplete has induced many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. This approach has led to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. This collaborative effort, driven by Maddison's pioneering work, highlights the importance of continuous research and refinement in understanding historical economic performance.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) illustrate how institutional shifts, particularly those affecting gender roles and monetary systems, have driven economic and social changes in African economies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that women's contributions to economic growth were significant, but their access to land was limited due to colonial codification of customary law, which privileged men. This institutional change, which privileged men, led to a de-privileging of women in access to land, particularly for cash crops. This codification was a key avenue for reinforcing the subordinate status of women during the colonial era. Meanwhile, Bohannan (1959, p.10) describes how the introduction of money into a subsistence economy led to profound changes. The Tiv economy, for example, shifted away from a multicentric economy as a result of the introduction of alien institutions and the integration into the world economy. This shift was facilitated by increased trade and the establishment of a new road network. The transition presented challenges, as brass rods, a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere, could not fully replace the functions of money in markets and marriage. This limitation highlights the complexities of integrating new monetary systems into traditional economies. The codification of customary law and the introduction of money both illustrate how institutional changes can reshape economic landscapes, but the specific mechanisms and outcomes vary widely.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly impacted income per capita in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). For instance, the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment. Similarly, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). He shows that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe. This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Austin 2008, p.2) underscores the importance of institutions in long-run economic growth, as they determine the framework within which economic activities take place.

The specific mechanisms through which institutions affect economic growth vary widely. Acemoglu et al. emphasize that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by Austin, who highlights how colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, illustrating how institutional changes can be influenced by local economic conditions and political dynamics. These findings (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.15) indicate that the impact of institutions on economic growth is not uniform and can be shaped by various factors, including local conditions and political influences. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between institutional changes and economic opportunities. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights how institutions can either facilitate or hinder economic growth depending on the broader economic context.

The precise impact of institutions on economic growth is further complicated by the role of gender in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). They document how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and how the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced their subordinate status (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This evidence (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20) shows that the impact of institutions on economic growth includes social and gender dynamics.
