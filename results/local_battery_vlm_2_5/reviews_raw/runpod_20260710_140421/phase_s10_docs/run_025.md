The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development economics. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing economic and political interactions, are widely acknowledged as crucial for economic performance. However, there is a live disagreement between scholars who argue that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth and those who contend that other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Acemoglu et al. posit that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, directly influenced long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Yet, this institutional perspective is challenged by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay of factors influencing economic development (Austin 2008, p.2). This debate highlights the need to understand how institutions interact with other variables to shape economic growth trajectories.

Acemoglu et al. assert that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The authors contend that the type of institutions established in colonies, whether extractive or inclusive, directly influenced long-run economic outcomes. This perspective is grounded in the observation that European colonists adopted different colonization strategies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other instances, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional framework was shaped by the mortality rates of Europeans in these colonies. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which in turn significantly affected income per capita. The relationship between institutions and economic development is further complicated by the influence of politically powerful groups. These groups may resist institutional changes that could threaten their political power or fail to maximize their rents. This dynamic is evident in the development experience of former colonies, where institutions were often designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional perspective highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies, institutional design, and long-term economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The complexities of economic growth, as examined through various lenses such as technological advance, social change, and regional wage dynamics, do not directly address how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Allen (2001, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) both acknowledge that economic growth is influenced by a variety of factors, including technological advances and social changes, but they do not explicitly focus on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Allen (2001, p.8) argues that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This period of economic divergence is closely tied to the institutional changes that occurred during this time, such as the development of new financial institutions and markets capable of mobilizing capital for individual firms on a large scale (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). These institutional changes allowed for the maintenance of high real wages in the economic leaders, despite the general decline in incomes in most European cities. This suggests that the institutional changes that occurred in Europe during this time were not universal and that the maintenance of high real wages in the economic leaders was not simply a result of technological advances or social changes, but also of specific institutional factors. The shared finding across these sources is that economic growth is influenced by a variety of factors, including technological advances, social changes, and institutional factors, but the specific role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains complex and nuanced.

The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in African economies led to significant institutional changes, particularly in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). The expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, particularly where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour (Austin 2008, p.15). This institutional shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa (Austin 2007, p.20). The transition from coerced to free labour had an important economic dimension, as it responded to changing factor scarcities and induced institutional innovation (Austin 2007, p.20). The imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). The institutional changes in African economies, driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, highlight the complex interplay between economic factors and institutional evolution. These changes were not merely responses to external pressures but were also shaped by local economic and political actions, illustrating the nuanced role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) argue that national accounts are essential for understanding global economic performance, but they are subject to margins of error that increase with historical distance. They highlight that while national accounts are often based on partial data and assumptions, collaborative efforts can improve their accuracy. This collaborative approach, as exemplified by the Maddison Project, has significantly advanced the field by encouraging scholars to refine and challenge existing estimates. The Maddison Project's strategy of producing numbers even when data is incomplete has induced many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. This strategy has been highly successful, leading to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than was available when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. The subjective nature of estimating margins of error in underlying data remains a challenge. The Hitotshibashi team's work on Asian national accounts is one of the most detailed studies in this area. The collaborative nature of these efforts underscores the importance of ongoing research and revision in accurately measuring economic performance over time.

This underscores the importance of considering the economic contributions of all segments of society, including women, in understanding the long-run economic growth of African societies. The role of women in economic growth during these periods was shaped by the patriarchal alliances and customary laws that governed land access and resource distribution (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women, was a critical mechanism through which these patriarchal structures were maintained. For example, in the Gold Coast, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cash crop sector. The introduction of a dual legal system, comprising European courts and native courts, exacerbated these gender disparities by formalizing the subordinate status of women within the legal framework (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The impact of these institutions extended beyond land access, affecting women's participation in broader economic activities. However, Bohannan's work highlights that the introduction of money into subsistence economies also brought about significant changes, shifting the economic landscape away from a multicentric system. This transition, while challenging, opened new avenues for economic growth and integration into the global economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The implications of these changes were profound, as they not only altered the economic institutions of African societies but also brought them into contact with the world economy. The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where the region became the world's leading producer of cocoa by 1911, exemplifies the transformative potential of these institutional changes, despite the persistent gender inequalities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the allocation of resources and the incentives for investment and innovation. Acemoglu et al. show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers significantly impacted the economic trajectory of their colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The establishment of extractive institutions in regions with high European mortality rates led to persistent underdevelopment, while regions with more inclusive institutions experienced greater economic growth. This dynamic is evident in the Gold Coast, where the transition to cocoa production was driven by both institutional changes and the economic revolution spurred by women's contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The shift from extractive to more inclusive institutions facilitated the economic revolution in the Gold Coast, making it the world's leading producer of cocoa by 1911 (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Austin's work complements this view by highlighting the importance of indigenous economic and political action in influencing institutional changes (Austin 2008, p.15). Colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to local economic developments, demonstrating that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political pressures. Austin shows that the transition from coerced to free labour had an important economic dimension, facilitating the transition to widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This collaborative effort highlights the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth by providing a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth.
