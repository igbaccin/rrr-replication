The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic history and development economics, with significant implications for understanding the wealth and poverty of nations. The live disagreement hinges on the extent to which institutions, rather than purely geographic or climatic factors, drive economic outcomes over the long term. One position emphasises the primacy of institutional factors, arguing that the quality of institutions, such as property rights and the rule of law, determines a country's economic trajectory. The opposing position, however, contends that the role of institutions is overstated, and that geographic and climatic factors play a more significant role in shaping economic growth. This debate is crucial for policymakers and economists seeking to understand the underlying mechanisms of economic development and to design effective policies for promoting growth. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with various scholars offering nuanced perspectives on how institutions shape economic outcomes. This review synthesizes the literature on how institutions influence long-run economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which institutions operate and the historical contexts in which they emerge. The literature on colonial institutions and economic growth provides a rich context for understanding the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes over the long term. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that the nature of institutions established by European colonizers varied significantly across colonies. In some cases, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, colonizers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In contrast, other colonies, like the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subject to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional structures had profound implications for long-run economic development. The extractive institutions in many colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. This institutional design was influenced by the political power dynamics and the reluctance of colonizers to initiate changes that would not directly benefit them (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is a direct consequence of these institutional changes. Acemoglu et al. document this reversal and show that it was closely related to industrialization processes during the nineteenth century. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The persistence of these institutions beyond the colonial period highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic growth. The institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes in many former colonies, underscoring the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional development.

The institutional revolution in factor markets, particularly in Ghana during 1874-1939, illustrates how colonial legacies can drive significant economic transformations, such as changes in land use rights and hired labour markets (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during this period drove an institutional transformation in factor markets. This transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The 1874 British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony, for instance, played a crucial role in this transition. The ordinance, which prohibited slavery and pawning, had significant economic implications. It shifted the labour market from coerced to free labour, which in turn influenced land use rights and the overall economic landscape. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule further facilitated market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional change was not merely a result of top-down colonial policies but was also shaped by indigenous economic and political actions. Colonial administrations, seeking fiscal security, adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by local economic activities. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to the hypothesis that colonial governments would opt for lower production (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic forces in shaping institutional changes. The integration of local economic activities into colonial frameworks illustrates the nuanced ways in which institutions can evolve, driven by both external and internal factors.

The literature on African economies highlights the complex interplay between women's roles, monetary systems, and economic growth, but it does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Bohannan and Akyeampong and Fofack both discuss how economic institutions are shaped by the introduction of new economic systems and the codification of customary law. Bohannan shows how the introduction of money into a subsistence economy led to profound changes, such as increased trade and the establishment of new markets, which in turn influenced economic institutions. This shift away from a multicentric economy was driven by the integration of local economic activities into colonial frameworks, which altered the traditional roles of women and men in economic production. Akyeampong and Fofack note that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the production of cash crops. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by customary law under colonial rule, which codified the subordinate status of women. The codification of customary law under colonial rule illustrates how institutional changes can be driven by both external and internal factors, as local economic activities shaped colonial policies and vice versa. However, Akyeampong and Fofack qualify this pattern by showing that women's economic contributions were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, despite their marginalization in access to land and resources. This qualification suggests that while institutional changes can shape economic growth, they do not operate in isolation from broader social and economic dynamics. The integration of local economic activities into colonial frameworks illustrates the nuanced ways in which institutions can evolve, driven by both external and internal factors.

Bolt and van Zanden, together with Allen et al. highlight the importance of historical economic data in understanding long-run economic growth (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). The measurement of economic performance through national accounts, as pioneered by Angus Maddison, is fundamental to this endeavour. Maddison's work, which relied on partial data and certain assumptions, laid the groundwork for estimating national accounts in the distant past. However, the margins of error in these estimates are significant, and the further back in time one goes, the larger these margins become (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This is particularly true for regions like Sub-Saharan Africa and pre-Colombian Latin America, where historical data is sparse. Despite these challenges, Maddison's approach has been highly successful in inducing scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This collaborative research has been instrumental in improving historical economic data, as evidenced by the detailed overview of long-term economic growth now available. Allen et al. provide a concrete example of this collaborative effort, demonstrating how real wages can serve as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour in early modern China (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). However, the accuracy of these proxies can be influenced by regional economic conditions and the integration of labour markets. For instance, in commercialized regions near major urban centres, there is evidence of a relatively high degree of integration between urban and rural labour markets, which can affect wage rates and economic performance. This highlights the nuanced ways in which historical economic data can be used to understand long-run economic growth, even in the face of significant margins of error.

Abramovitz argues that the forces of technological advance and social change are critical in shaping long-run economic growth, but the role of institutions in this process is complex and multifaceted (Abramovitz 1986, p.21); Allen (2001, p.8) suggests that economic divergence is influenced by factors such as technological advance and social change, but does not explicitly address institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth (Allen 2001, p.8). The institutional changes that Abramovitz highlights are not merely a symptom of economic growth but can also act as a catalyst. Institutions can influence economic performance by shaping the environment in which technological advances occur and social changes unfold. For instance, institutions that facilitate the diffusion of new technologies can accelerate economic growth by enabling firms to adopt and implement innovations more efficiently. This is particularly relevant in the context of Abramovitz's discussion of social capability, which includes the organization and management of large-scale enterprise and the mobilization of capital (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). However, Allen (2001, p.8) points out that prices, which are influenced by institutional factors, can change frequently and may not be fixed for long periods, indicating that the impact of institutions on economic growth can be dynamic and subject to fluctuations (Allen 2001, p.23). This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, their effects can be nuanced and dependent on various factors, including technological advancements and social changes.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical contexts of European colonization and the establishment of extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or set up extractive states aimed at rapid resource transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are shaped by politically powerful groups and designed to maximize rents rather than long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions significantly affected income per capita in former colonies, illustrating the profound impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. Austin counters that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This highlights the complexity of institutional impacts, where the same institutional framework can yield different outcomes depending on the historical context and local actions. Austin also highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interplay between institutions and local conditions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interplay is crucial for understanding how institutions shape long-run economic growth, as it shows that institutional effects are not static but evolve in response to various factors.

The role of women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa adds another layer to the understanding of institutional impacts. Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution was often shaped by institutional factors such as land access and customary law, which were codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, further illustrates how institutional changes can drive economic growth. Austin shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to significant institutional changes in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional revolution was driven by the need to adapt to changing factor scarcities, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutions and their role in economic growth. The imposition and extension of British rule in Ghana had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade (Austin 2007, p.4). This institutional framework facilitated economic growth by integrating markets and promoting trade, further underscoring the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement lies in the complexity of institutional impacts, where the same institutional framework can yield different outcomes depending on the historical context and local actions (Austin 2008, p.2). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the dynamic interplay between institutions and local conditions, as shown by the adaptation of colonial administrations to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This interplay highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of institutional impacts on economic growth, considering the historical context and local factors.
