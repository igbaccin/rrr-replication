The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central question in economic development studies. What is at stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic progress over extended periods. A key disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth versus other factors such as geography or cultural traits. One position argues that institutions designed to enforce property rights and the rule of law are crucial for sustained economic development. Another position posits that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as the actions of colonial powers and the specific historical contexts of different regions, play a more significant role. Yet another perspective highlights the contributions of marginalized groups, such as women, in economic development, challenging the notion that institutional frameworks alone determine economic outcomes. The interplay between these views underscores the complexity of understanding the causal mechanisms behind long-run economic growth. The significance of institutions in shaping economic growth is particularly evident in the context of colonial legacies and the resulting institutional frameworks in former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different types of institutions in colonies based on mortality rates, with settlements like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were subjected to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks resulted directly from colonial policies, not merely from geographic or climatic factors. The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to persistent economic underdevelopment, as these institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. highlight that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which interacted with the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that equilibrium institutions in colonies were often extractive, designed to maximize rents for the colonizers rather than foster economic development. This institutional design, shaped by politically powerful groups, had long-lasting effects on the economic trajectories of the former colonies. The institutional legacies of colonialism, therefore, continue to influence economic outcomes in many parts of the world today, underscoring the enduring impact of historical institutional changes on long-run economic growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) show how changes in factor endowments can drive institutional adaptation. They find that shifts in land abundance and scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes, particularly the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional revolution was shaped by endogenous factors, such as the actions of colonial governments and local economic and political dynamics. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This finding underscores the importance of local agency and the complex interplay between external and internal factors in shaping institutional outcomes. The extent to which these institutional changes fostered long-run economic growth is a subject of ongoing debate. The role of institutions in economic development is not straightforward, as different institutional arrangements can have varying impacts depending on the specific historical, political, and economic contexts.

This variability in institutional impact is further complicated by the challenges in measuring economic performance over time, as highlighted by Allen et al. (2011, p.22); (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3). This is due to the subjective nature of estimates and the margins of error inherent in partial data and assumptions about economic activities. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2) contend that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, which challenges the view of significant improvement during this period. This finding underscores the complexity of assessing long-run economic growth. They note that Angus Maddison's pioneering work, despite its initial limitations, induced many scholars to engage in quantifying long-term economic development. This collaborative effort has led to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than was previously available. Nonetheless, the subjective estimates and margins of error in historical data remain a challenge for accurately measuring economic performance.

The introduction of money into African subsistence economies, as described by Bohannan (1959, p.10); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15), exemplifies how economic institutions can catalyze significant transformations. Bohannan (1959, p.10) observed that the integration of the Tiv economy into the global market through trade and new road networks altered local economic institutions. This shift involved a fundamental reconfiguration of how value was exchanged and perceived within the society, not merely increased trade. The transition to a monetary economy disrupted traditional systems of exchange, such as those based on prestige and social relationships. The introduction of money as a medium of exchange simplified transactions but also created new economic dynamics. The shift from a multicentric economy, where various forms of exchange coexisted, to a more unified monetary system had profound implications for social and economic structures. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) note that this shift also had gendered implications. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated the cash crop economy. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by colonial legal systems that codified customary laws, further marginalizing women's economic roles. The introduction of money changed economic institutions and exacerbated existing social inequalities, particularly along gender lines. This dynamic underscores how economic institutions can both drive growth and reinforce social hierarchies.

Abramovitz and Allen argue that economic divergence and technological advance play crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth. They show that the dynamics of technological progress and social change can significantly alter the rankings of countries in the productivity scale. This is particularly evident in the historical context of European cities between 1500 and 1750, where real incomes fell in most cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This period saw the gains from 1815 to 1850 as a cycle, not a trend, with important features in British real wage history reflecting population changes in the medieval period and the maintenance of high real wages after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century. The rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War marked decisive breaks with the past, each in different ways. These historical patterns underscore how technological advances and social changes can drive economic growth, even in the absence of direct institutional interventions. The complexity of the catch-up process in economic growth highlights that exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change are crucial. The social capability of a country, encompassing technical competence and institutional frameworks, is essential for absorbing new technologies and achieving sustained economic growth. This capability includes experience with large-scale enterprise organization and financial institutions capable of mobilizing capital for individual firms on a similarly large scale. The implication is that while technological advances and social changes can drive economic growth, the institutional framework that supports these advances is equally important. Allen (2001, p.8) reports, “Grain prices, which play a minor role in this paper, were wholesale, while the bread prices which loom large in the cost of living indices are based on retail information from noninstitutional sources.” Abramovitz (1986, p.21) reports, “They will tend to produce changes in the ranks of countries in the productivity scale and these may include the top rank itself.”

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is multifaceted, with historical evidence showing that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonizers implemented vastly different institutional frameworks depending on the mortality rates they encountered. In colonies with high mortality, extractive institutions were established to maximize resource extraction, which hindered long-run economic growth. Conversely, in areas with low mortality, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established, fostering economic development. This dynamic is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The institutional frameworks that maximized rents for European colonists often did not promote long-run growth, illustrating how the nature of colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes.

The institutional legacies of colonialism are further complicated by the role of local factors and the interaction between colonizers and indigenous populations. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies the complex interplay of historical periods and paths, noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions. This adaptive behavior shows that institutions evolved in response to local conditions and pressures, not static. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) add to this by demonstrating that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced the subordinate status of women, but their economic contributions were nonetheless crucial. This interaction between colonial institutions and local economic activities underscores the importance of considering multiple dimensions of institutional influence.

The measurement of economic performance and the role of institutions in long-run growth are central to understanding these dynamics. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) emphasize the need for collaborative research to continue and improve the work of Angus Maddison in measuring economic performance. The margins of error in estimating national accounts, particularly in the distant past, highlight the challenges in quantifying long-term economic development. Collaborative efforts and the continuous refinement of measurement techniques can provide a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth. Austin (2007, p.20) provides a concrete example of this, showing how the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was driven by the economic pressures of the time, illustrating how localised scarcity can spur institutional innovation.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions play a critical role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by the extractive institutions that hindered growth in high-mortality colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement centres on the adaptive nature of institutions and the role of local factors, as Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the detailed analysis of institutional changes in Ghana, where the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to significant institutional innovations in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20).
