The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms that drive prosperity over extended periods. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement over the efficacy of institutions in promoting growth: one position emphasizes the primacy of institutional quality, arguing that robust institutions are essential for sustained economic development, while another position highlights the complex interplay between institutions and other factors, such as geography and colonial history, suggesting that institutions alone do not determine economic outcomes. Institutions, in this context, refer to the formal and informal rules that govern economic and political interactions, including property rights, legal frameworks, and governance structures. The central question is whether and how these institutions influence economic growth over the long term, and what factors mediate this relationship. This review will explore how institutions shape economic growth, focusing on the causal mechanisms and the interplay with other variables. Institutions' role in economic growth is not merely about providing a stable framework for economic activity, but also about the historical and contextual factors that shape their development and impact. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Colonialism reshaped institutions, which significantly influenced long-run economic growth, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) establish. The colonial powers introduced diverse institutions based on their settlement strategies. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, setting a foundation for economic growth. In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, had profound implications for the economic trajectories of the colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional changes introduced by colonialism. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) clarify that this interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, highlighting the complex interplay between institutional frameworks and economic opportunities.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) examine how institutional changes in Ghana during the colonial period were influenced by shifts in land abundance and scarcity. Specifically, the loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This transition from coerced to free labour had significant economic implications, marking a shift in the institutional landscape of Ghana. Austin (2007, p.20) notes that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region. This institutional change facilitated the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The institutional changes in Ghana during this period were not merely a response to colonial policies but were also driven by local economic and political actions. Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For example, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher rather than lower production equilibrium, contrary to Nunn's hypothesis. This dynamic interplay between local economic conditions and colonial policies shaped the institutional framework in Ghana, ultimately influencing long-run economic growth. The institutional changes in Ghana during the colonial period illustrate how local economic conditions and political actions can drive institutional evolution, even within the constraints of colonial rule.

Akyeampong and Fofack and Bohannan together highlight the significant economic contributions of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods and the profound changes brought about by the introduction of money into subsistence economies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The economic activities of African women were pivotal in these periods, yet their access to land and resources was constrained by patriarchal alliances and customary law. These constraints were exacerbated under colonial rule, where men played a central role in codifying customary law (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This codification reinforced the marginalization of women, relegating them to inferior lands for food farming while men controlled cash crops. A similar dynamic is observed in the Tiv economy, where the introduction of money led to a shift from a multicentric economy to one more integrated with the world economy. The new road network and increased trade facilitated by colonial rule perturbed the internal marketing system, altering economic institutions (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The transition was not seamless. The introduction of brass rods as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere underscores the complexities of this economic shift. Brass rods could not fulfill all functions of money, particularly in areas like marriage and food exchange, highlighting the challenges in achieving equivalency between new economic institutions and traditional social structures (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This interplay between economic changes and social institutions illustrates how the introduction of new economic systems can both drive and complicate growth, even as it integrates local economies into broader networks.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2001, p.8) share a posture that economic growth and income divergence are complex phenomena shaped by multiple factors (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen 2001, p.8). Both authors highlight the role of social capabilities and historical wage patterns in influencing economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that social capability encompasses technical competence and institutional frameworks, which are crucial for mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprises (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This institutional dimension is echoed in Allen (2001, p.8), who discusses how prices and wage patterns reflect broader economic changes, including population dynamics and technological advances (Allen 2001, p.23). These factors collectively influence the long-run economic growth trajectory of nations. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further illustrate this complexity by showing that real wages in China remained stagnant from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, despite significant economic activity (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This stagnation suggests that institutional factors, such as labour market integration and social capabilities, play a critical role in shaping economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms by which these institutions influence growth remain nuanced and context-dependent.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) demonstrate that the measurement of economic performance requires collaborative research, as historical economic data is often subject to margins of error and assumptions. This is because economic data from the past, especially the distant past, are often based on partial data and certain assumptions about the links between these data and the economic activities they represent. The further one goes back in time, the larger the margins of error will probably be, but there may be important exceptions to this rule. Collaborative research not only helps in improving the accuracy of economic measurements but also encourages scholarly debates and updates to historical economic data. This ongoing debate and updates are crucial for presenting a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). However, the subjective nature of these estimates suggests that while collaborative research improves accuracy, it does not eliminate the inherent uncertainties in historical economic data.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as seen in the varied colonial experiences across different regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the institutional frameworks established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. In regions where Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States and Australia, economic growth was robust. In areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, where extractive states were set up to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, economic stagnation prevailed. These findings underscore the importance of institutional quality in fostering long-term economic development. The nature of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, directly impacts the economic outcomes of a region. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) further elaborates on how colonial policies and institutional changes influenced economic development. For instance, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This dynamic interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions highlights the multifaceted role of institutions in economic growth. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), further illustrates how institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had lasting effects on economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This interaction shows that institutions are not static but evolve in response to economic and political pressures, shaping the economic landscape over time. The central point of agreement across these studies is that institutions are crucial for economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates led to significant differences in income per capita. Austin (2008, p.15) highlights that colonial administrations adapted their institutional policies in response to local economic conditions. The key disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth. Acemoglu et al.'s focus on extractive institutions contrasts with Austin's emphasis on the adaptive nature of colonial policies. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the detailed documentation of institutional changes in response to local economic conditions, as seen in the case of the Gold Coast. This evidence shows that while extractive institutions may have been prevalent in some colonies, the adaptive nature of colonial policies also played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes.
