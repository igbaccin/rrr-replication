The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the fundamental question of whether economic growth is primarily driven by institutional factors or whether institutions are merely one among many factors. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions determine economic outcomes or if they are shaped by other factors such as geography, culture, or economic conditions. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce the rule of law and encourage investment are crucial for sustained economic growth, while others contend that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is more complex and influenced by a multitude of factors. The debate is nuanced by the historical context, with scholars examining different periods and regions to understand the causal mechanisms at play. A key point of contention is whether the institutional framework established during colonial periods has had a lasting impact on economic development in former colonies. This debate underscores the importance of understanding the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development and their impact on economic growth. Institutions are not static; they evolve over time and are influenced by a variety of factors, including political power dynamics and economic conditions.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that the institutions established during colonial rule significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, is a central theme in this debate. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of institutions that either enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or created extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole. This distinction is crucial because it highlights how institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic growth. For instance, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, which laid the foundation for long-term economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. who emphasize that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal of relative incomes among former colonies is thus attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and not to geographic factors. This institutional perspective is further supported by Austin, who argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This approach highlights the need to consider the historical context and the specific institutional frameworks established during colonial rule to understand their impact on economic development. Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, depending on the mortality rates they faced. These institutions ranged from inclusive, which encouraged investment and enforced the rule of law, to extractive, which were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks was a direct result of the varying colonization strategies employed by Europeans. For example, in colonies like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established inclusive institutions. In places like the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, particularly the extractive ones, significantly affected the income per capita of these colonies. This institutional legacy has had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on contemporary economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies underscores the critical role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping long-run economic growth.

These shifts in factor scarcity, as Austin (2007, p.20) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlight, can lead to significant institutional changes that drive economic growth. This shift led to the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal in this transformation (Austin 2007, p.20). These institutional changes facilitated the integration of markets, which was further enhanced by the removal of tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the Gold Coast Colony (Austin 2007, p.4). Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change. The implication is that the institutional changes observed in Ghana during the colonial period are likely to have had significant long-term effects on economic growth. This suggests that the institutional changes in Ghana may have been more effective in promoting economic growth if they were accompanied by a strengthening of these institutions (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). This interplay between institutional changes and social capability underscores the complex nature of economic growth and the need for a holistic approach to understanding its drivers.

The literature on the economic impact of institutional changes in Africa, as noted by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10), suggests a nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) illustrate that colonial rule introduced a dual legal system which codified customary law and reinforced patriarchal alliances that marginalized women's access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This institutional change had profound implications for economic development, as it redirected agricultural activities and limited the economic contributions of women. Bohannan (1959, p.10) shows that the introduction of money and new road networks led to increased trade and markets, integrating local economies into the world economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). However, this integration was not seamless; the shift away from a multicentric economy presented challenges, such as the inability of new currency to fulfill all functions of money within traditional exchange systems (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, they also create complexities that must be navigated, such as the disruption of traditional economic institutions and the need for new frameworks to support growth.

Allen (2001, p.8) focuses on the period from 1500 to 1750, identifying this as a critical era where real incomes in most European cities declined while economic leaders managed to maintain their income levels (Allen 2001, p.8). This divergence is crucial for understanding the long-run economic growth trajectories of different regions. The economic leaders, which included cities like London, were able to maintain high real wages, which in turn supported their economic development. This pattern is further substantiated by Austin (2007, p.20), who highlights that institutional changes, particularly those related to trade and markets, played a significant role in shaping economic growth. The maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders was a complex process. This suggests that while institutions are crucial, they interact with other economic variables, such as population changes and long-term supply arrangements, to shape long-run economic growth (Allen 2001, p.23).

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causal relationships (Austin 2008, p.2; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This thesis, which posits that institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth, is challenged by Austin's assertion that it compresses different historical periods and paths. Austin and Allen et al. suggest that this simplification fails to account for the complexity of institutional evolution and its interaction with other economic variables. Specifically, Austin notes that colonial governments often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that local dynamics significantly influence institutional outcomes (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation shows that institutions evolve in response to various pressures, including local economic conditions and political actions. Allen et al. further illustrate this point by highlighting the stagnation of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, which contradicts the view of significant improvement during this period (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This stagnation suggests that institutional changes alone do not guarantee economic growth and that other factors, such as labour market conditions and regional migration, play crucial roles. The precise mechanisms by which these factors interact with institutions to shape economic growth remain elusive. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with other economic variables to drive long-run economic growth.

These institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, significantly affected income per capita in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This implies that institutional changes alone do not guarantee economic growth, but rather, they interact with other economic variables to drive long-run development. Austin's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al.'s findings, showing that colonial institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This suggests that the precise mechanisms by which institutions interact with other economic variables to shape economic growth are complex and multifaceted.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further complicated by the interaction between institutions and local economic conditions. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions are shaped, at least in part, by politically powerful groups that may obtain fewer rents with institutions of private property or fear losing their political power if there is institutional development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This indicates that the evolution of institutions is influenced by the political and economic dynamics of the time. Austin's work on Ghana during 1874-1939 shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to changing factor scarcities, highlighting the importance of local economic conditions in shaping institutions. The interaction between institutions and local economic conditions is further evidenced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced the subordinate status of women in access to land (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This suggests that institutions evolve in response to changing economic and political dynamics.
