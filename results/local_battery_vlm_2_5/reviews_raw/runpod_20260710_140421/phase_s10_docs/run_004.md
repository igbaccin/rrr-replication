The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development and historical studies. At stake is the understanding of how different institutional frameworks can either foster or hinder economic progress over extended periods. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutional factors in determining economic outcomes and those who argue for the importance of other factors, such as geographic or climatic conditions. Some scholars contend that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the economic trajectories of former colonies, while others suggest that the reversal of fortune in economic development is more complex and cannot be solely attributed to institutional factors. This debate underscores the need to carefully examine the mechanisms through which institutions operate and their interactions with other variables. The reversal of fortune in economic development is a central theme, as it highlights how institutional changes introduced by colonialism have shaped the long-run economic growth of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The European colonizers established different institutions depending on the mortality rates they faced, which in turn shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where mortality rates were low, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound long-run effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the institutions established were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists. This institutional design, shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, had lasting impacts on the economic performance of the colonies. Acemoglu et al. also note that the influence of population density and prosperity on European policies played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was pivotal in determining the economic trajectories of these regions. This institutional legacy continues to influence the economic performance of former colonies today, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) examine how shifts in land abundance led to institutional changes in Ghana, illustrating how local responses to external policies can shape economic trajectories. This institutional transformation was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. Austin (2007, p.20) highlights that the transition from coerced to free labour had an important economic dimension, driven by the loss of slaves and the subsequent debt incurred by Akyem chiefs, which made them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This institutional shift was influenced by the economic and political actions of indigenous populations as well as legal changes. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that colonial governments, seeking fiscal security, adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This adaptive behaviour contradicts the hypothesis that colonial administrations uniformly imposed institutional changes without considering local dynamics. These findings suggest that the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay between external policies and local responses.

Specific historical and societal factors played a crucial role in shaping economic development in Africa, particularly the contributions of women and the introduction of money into subsistence economies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) show that women in Africa significantly contributed to economic growth both before and during colonial periods, often working on marginal lands and playing a central role in food farming. While these societal factors were pivotal in the economic development of Africa, the introduction of money into subsistence economies also had profound effects. Bohannan (1959, p.10) argues that the introduction of money led to increased trade and a greater number of markets, integrating African economies into the global market system. This integration presented significant pain points as the transition from a multicentric economy to a monetized one was challenging (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This integration process was further complicated by the colonial administration's dual legal system, which introduced European courts based on common and civil law alongside native courts (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The dual legal system and the introduction of money into subsistence economies thus shaped economic development in Africa through complex interactions with societal factors, particularly the role of women.

The stagnation of real wages in China from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, as Allen et al. (2011, p.22) and Austin (Allen et al. 2011, p.24) show, challenges the notion of significant economic improvement during this period. This stagnation was deeply intertwined with institutional constraints as well as economic factors. The vibrant and active labour market in early modern China, where wage rates served as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour, suggests that institutional factors played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) demonstrate that the existence of a dynamic labour market, particularly for short-term or day labour, indicates a high degree of integration between urban and rural areas, which could have facilitated economic growth. The stagnation of real wages implies that these institutional factors were insufficient to drive significant economic improvement. The institutional framework, which included the dual legal system and the codification of customary law, limited the economic opportunities and contributions of certain groups, such as women. The economic integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas did not translate into widespread economic improvement, highlighting the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. This suggests that while institutional factors are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own to drive significant improvement.

The dynamics of technological advance play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process is influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen 2001, p.8). This technological advance dynamic involves institutional changes that facilitate or hinder this growth as well as economic growth. The institutional framework, including the organization and management of large-scale enterprise and financial institutions capable of mobilizing capital, is crucial for economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) emphasizes that these institutions are essential for mobilizing resources and managing large-scale enterprise, which in turn drives technological advance (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The institutional framework can also act as a barrier to growth. For instance, the maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders, while beneficial in the short term, can lead to institutional sclerosis over time, limiting the growth of these economies. The implications of this are significant, as it highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions and technological advances interact to shape long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is complex, with different scholars emphasizing various factors. The extractive nature of institutions established by European colonizers significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes in their colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonizers set up extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, focusing on rapid resource transfer to the metropole and not encouraging investment and growth. This institutional framework persisted, affecting income per capita in these regions. Austin (2008, p.2) reinforces this by highlighting that economic historians see the behavior of older industrial economies in the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience dating back to the original industrialization of Europe. This institutional legacy, shaped by colonial policies, continues to impact economic growth trajectories today.

The persistence of these institutions is further explored by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth. This perspective is complemented by Austin (2008, p.15), who notes that colonial administrations adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating a dynamic interaction between local and colonial institutions. This dynamic highlights that while extractive institutions were prevalent, there were instances where local economic actions influenced institutional changes. The role of institutions in economic growth evolves in response to both external and internal pressures and is not static.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This institutional framework shaped the economic trajectories of these regions, leading to the observed reversal in relative incomes. Austin (2007, p.4) supports this by showing that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the colonies. This institutional integration facilitated economic growth by creating a more cohesive market environment. The historical evidence from these studies shows that institutional changes, driven by both colonial policies and local economic actions, are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illustrated by the experiences of African women during pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development, highlighting their role in food farming and cash crop production. This contribution challenges views that marginalize their economic role. Austin (2007, p.20) complements this by showing that the shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor. This institutional change facilitated economic growth by creating new opportunities for resource allocation and labor utilization. The specific historical evidence from these studies shows that institutional changes, driven by both local economic actions and colonial policies, are crucial in shaping long-run economic growth. The measurement of economic contributions of African women and the institutional revolution in factor markets provides a clear empirical basis for understanding the role of institutions in economic growth.

Underlying the scholarship is a shared recognition that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth, as shown by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27). There remains a disagreement over the specific mechanisms by which these institutions influence growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions are designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not promote long-run growth. The specific historical evidence of institutional changes in factor markets in Ghana, as documented by Austin (2007, p.20), settles this disagreement by showing that local economic actions can influence institutional changes, leading to economic growth. This empirical comparison illustrates the dynamic nature of institutions and their impact on long-run economic growth.
