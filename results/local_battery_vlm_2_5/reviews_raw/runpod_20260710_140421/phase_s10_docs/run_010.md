The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental drivers of prosperity across different societies. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure social interactions, are widely recognized as key determinants of economic performance. A lively debate exists regarding the mechanisms by which institutions influence growth. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutional quality on economic outcomes, arguing that institutions that protect property rights and enforce contracts are essential for fostering investment and innovation. Another position highlights the historical and contextual factors that shape institutional development, suggesting that the impact of institutions on growth is mediated by broader social, political, and economic dynamics. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between institutions and economic development, as it informs policy recommendations and theoretical frameworks in economics. Institutions are dynamic entities that both influence and are influenced by the economic landscape over time. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in explaining long-run economic growth. They show that colonial powers established different institutions depending on settlement patterns, which in turn shaped long-run economic outcomes. In colonies where Europeans settled in large numbers, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions were established that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole. This pattern of institutional development, driven by European mortality rates, had lasting effects on income per capita. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, are strongly correlated with economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, linking this to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This reversal underscores the profound and enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) examine how shifts in land abundance and scarcity led to institutional changes in Ghana during a specific historical period, demonstrating how localised changes in resource availability can drive institutional evolution. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. The thesis has been tested quantitatively, and the theory has been found insufficient for explaining why institutions that were apparently favourable for economic growth were adopted in some countries but not in others. For example, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments, and colonial administrations sought fiscal security, encouraging their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo. Austin (2008, p.2) shows that colonial administrations responded to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, opting for a higher production equilibrium. Austin (2007, p.20) further elaborates on this by describing the institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The British emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony in 1874 and the prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were important legal actions that had significant economic dimensions, driving chiefs into debt and making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. The transition from coerced to free labour had an important economic dimension. This institutional revolution in factor markets was driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which in turn was influenced by the imposition and extension of British rule. The imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects. Tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within 'Ghana' were removed. This institutional change had profound implications for economic development, as it facilitated the emergence of new markets and economic opportunities, driving economic growth.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) together establish that real income trends varied significantly across different regions and periods, with economic leaders maintaining their income levels while others experienced declines. The maintenance of high real wages in economic leaders, such as Britain, can be attributed to the development of institutions that facilitated economic growth. The economic development that gathered strength in the seventeenth century was supported by institutions that ensured a high real wage (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This institutional framework likely included factors such as property rights, contract enforcement, and market integration, which fostered an environment conducive to economic growth. These institutions enabled the maintenance of high real wages by ensuring that economic actors could reap the benefits of their investments and innovations. The stagnation of real wages in regions like China, where the institutional framework did not promote economic growth to the same extent, underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The vibrant and active labour market in early modern China, particularly for short-term or day labour, suggests that wage rates could serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole. The integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions near major urban centres further underscores the importance of institutional factors in determining economic outcomes. The high degree of integration of labour markets in these regions indicates that institutions played a crucial role in facilitating economic growth by ensuring that labour markets functioned efficiently.

The complexity of economic catch-up processes is evident in the multifaceted interactions of exogenous factors and social change, as Abramovitz (1986, p.21; 1986, p.5) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) show. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further elaborate on this by highlighting the role of institutions in mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprise, which are crucial for economic growth. This institutional framework is particularly important in the context of technological advance, as it facilitates the absorption and application of new technologies. The role of institutions in economic catch-up is complex. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) suggest that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting growth can be influenced by the broader social and political context, which can either enhance or hinder their impact. For instance, institutions that are well-aligned with the broader social and political context can foster a conducive environment for technological advance and economic growth. Institutions that do not align with the broader context can create barriers to growth, limiting the ability of countries to catch up with more advanced economies. Therefore, the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on their alignment with the broader social and political context.

This contingency is particularly evident in the profound economic impacts of social and institutional changes in Africa, as Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue. The literature in this cluster examines specific economic impacts of social and institutional changes in Africa, focusing on gender roles and the introduction of money into subsistence economies. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) (Bohannan 1959, p.10) document how these changes have led to significant shifts in economic activities and structures. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, which in turn affected women's access to land and economic opportunities. This marginalization was further exacerbated by the introduction of cash crops, which were predominantly controlled by men (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The shift from a multicentric economy to a more integrated system, facilitated by the introduction of money and new road networks, also had significant economic implications. Bohannan (1959, p.10) notes that these changes led to increased trade and a greater number of markets, but also introduced alien institutions that perturbed the internal marketing system. The economic institutions of the Tiv people, for example, were brought into contact with the world economy, leading to profound changes in their economic landscape. The transition presented its own set of challenges. The introduction of money as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere highlighted the complexities of economic integration. Brass rods, for instance, fulfilled only one function of money, payment, and could not be equated with rights in women or food, illustrating the limitations of monetary integration in traditional economies (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The introduction of money into traditional economies, while fostering economic growth, also brought about significant social and institutional changes that had lasting effects on economic structures.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the impact of European colonization strategies. These strategies varied significantly, with some colonies receiving institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. For instance, in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, European settlers established such institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to quickly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks highlights how different colonial policies directly influenced economic outcomes. The enduring effects of these policies are clear in the long-term economic performance of these regions, where initial institutional choices persistently shaped growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Colonial administrations played a crucial role in shaping economic institutions, often in response to local economic and political dynamics. For example, in regions where European settlers were not present, colonial governments sometimes encouraged indigenous commodity production (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive approach to institutional design shows how colonial powers responded to local conditions, influencing long-term economic development. The interaction between colonial policies and local economic conditions was pivotal in determining the trajectory of economic growth in these regions. This interaction underscores the significance of institutional frameworks in economic development, as these frameworks were not static but evolved in response to local factors (Austin 2008, p.15).

The codification of customary law under colonial rule further illustrates the institutional changes that shaped economic growth. This process often reinforced patriarchal structures, affecting access to land and economic opportunities. For instance, in the Gold Coast, women were relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This gendered division of labor and land access had profound implications for economic development, highlighting how institutional changes during colonial rule could either facilitate or hinder growth. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often marginalized women, shows how institutional frameworks can have lasting effects on economic structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

The shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor, driven by changing economic conditions (Austin 2007, p.4). The introduction of British rule in Ghana had significant market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade. This institutional change facilitated economic growth by creating a more integrated market environment. These changes show how shifts in institutional frameworks can drive economic development by adapting to new economic realities (Austin 2007, p.4).

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal was not merely a result of geographic factors but was deeply influenced by the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century was crucial in shaping the long-run development of these colonies. This interaction underscores the importance of institutions in economic growth, as they determined the ability of colonies to industrialize and develop economically (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the enduring effects of colonial policies. The "reversal of fortune" thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). The persistence of extractive institutions in some colonies and the establishment of growth-enhancing institutions in others show how initial institutional choices have long-term consequences. The specific historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies settles the disagreement by demonstrating that institutional changes, not just geographic factors, were the primary drivers of economic growth. This evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and economic opportunities is crucial in determining long-run economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
