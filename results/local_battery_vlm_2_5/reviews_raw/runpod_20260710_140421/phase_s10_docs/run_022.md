The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms behind sustained prosperity. Institutions, encompassing formal rules and informal norms, are crucial in structuring economic interactions and incentives. A key disagreement revolves around the primacy of institutions versus other factors, such as geography or culture, in determining economic outcomes. One position emphasizes the direct impact of institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, on economic growth. Another position argues that institutions are not the sole determinant and that other factors, such as the actions of colonial powers and the historical context, significantly influence economic development. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of growth or if they operate in conjunction with other historical and contextual factors. This literature review will explore these positions by examining how institutions have shaped economic growth in various historical and geographical contexts. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) examine how shifts in factor markets, particularly land abundance and scarcity, influenced institutional dynamics and economic outcomes in Ghana during 1874-1939. They argue that the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional revolution was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, as local chiefs and colonial administrations responded to new incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For example, the loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa (Austin 2007, p.20). Furthermore, colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to these incentives (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how institutional change can be endogenous to economic conditions, as local actors adapt to new scarcity and abundance patterns. The institutional prescriptions of colonial administrations varied. Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium, which Austin (2008, p.15) notes differs from Nunn's hypothesis. The institutional revolution in factor markets thus had complex and varied effects, shaping long-run economic growth in Ghana through the interplay of local and colonial actors.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) assert that colonialism led to the introduction of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates, which significantly impacted long-run economic growth. They argue that Europeans established different colonization strategies depending on mortality rates, creating institutions that either enforced the rule of law or set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This institutional divergence was driven by the desire of European colonists to maximize their rents, rather than fostering long-run growth. The persistence of these institutions shaped the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions hindering development. This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies and link it to the interaction between institutions and industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) qualify this by noting that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, may persist because politically powerful groups benefit from them. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism thus had profound and lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies, shaping their long-run growth trajectories through the establishment of institutions that either promoted or hindered economic development.

The divergence in real incomes in Europe was driven by a complex interplay of factors, including changes in grain prices and the cost of living. Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that grain prices, which were a significant component of the cost of living indices, were influenced by wholesale markets, while other prices were determined by bulk buying arrangements (Allen 2001, p.8). This suggests that the economic conditions of the time were characterized by frequent price changes, which could have disrupted the stability of real incomes. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further support this by noting that the vibrant labour market in early modern China indicates that wage rates could serve as a proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labour (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This implies that structural factors limited wage growth in China. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) also note that the integration of urban and rural labour markets in commercialized regions near major urban centres was relatively high, suggesting that the stagnation in wages was not due to a lack of market integration (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). However, Allen (2001, p.8) qualifies this by noting that the gains in real wages from 1815 to 1850 were a minor cycle rather than a long-term trend, indicating that the economic conditions of the time were characterized by short-term fluctuations rather than sustained growth (Allen 2001, p.23). This suggests that structural factors limited wage growth in China. These findings suggest that a complex interplay of economic and structural factors influenced the divergence in real incomes and wage stagnation.

The literature on African women's economic contributions and the introduction of money in subsistence economies highlights significant economic changes but does not directly address how institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack and Bohannan both underscore the pivotal role of economic changes in shaping long-run growth. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This is evidenced by the migration of farmers in search of land for cocoa, which propelled the economic revolution that made the Gold Coast the world's leading producer of cocoa in 1911. The de-privileging of women in access to land and the codification of customary law under colonial rule further reinforced the economic contributions of women. The increased trade and greater number of markets, facilitated by peace and the new road network, perturbed the internal marketing system and put the economic institutions of the Tiv in touch with the world economy. However, the transition to a monetary economy was not without its challenges, as brass rods fulfilled only one function of money: payment. The equivalence between brass rods and rights in women, or between brass rods and food, could not exist. This highlights the complexities of economic transitions and the role of institutions in shaping long-run growth. The introduction of money and the codification of customary law both illustrate how institutional changes can drive economic growth, but also how these changes can be fraught with challenges. The long-run economic growth in these regions was shaped by a complex interplay of economic, structural, and institutional factors.

In this context, Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) highlight the importance of collaborative research in accurately measuring economic performance and historical economic development. The authors argue that the estimation of national accounts, particularly for distant historical periods, is subject to significant margins of error. These errors arise from partial data and assumptions about economic activities, which can vary widely depending on the historical context and available information. For example, data for medieval England might be more reliable than data for nineteenth-century Sub-Saharan Africa or pre-Columbian Latin America. This variability underscores the need for collaborative efforts to refine and improve these estimates. Collaborative research not only helps in identifying and mitigating these margins of error but also encourages scholarly debate and the generation of new data. For instance, the Maddison Project has been instrumental in fostering such collaborative efforts, leading to more detailed and accurate overviews of long-term economic growth. The project's strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist has induced many scholars to work on these themes and to try to quantify long-term economic development. This collaborative approach has been highly successful, enabling a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than was possible when Maddison started his project in the 1960s. The subjective nature of these estimates means that ongoing debate and improved data are essential for continued progress. This highlights the importance of collaborative research in shaping our understanding of long-term economic growth, as it allows for the continuous refinement of economic performance measurements.

Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21), a view shared by Allen et al. (Abramovitz 1986, p.5), who highlight that the 'catch-up process' is not merely a matter of technological diffusion but also involves institutional and social adaptations. This perspective challenges simple convergence hypotheses, which posit that poorer countries will automatically catch up to richer ones over time. Abramovitz emphasizes that technological advance and social change are deeply intertwined with the institutional dynamics of a country. The social capability of a country, encompassing technical competence and robust institutions, plays a crucial role in this process. Allen et al. further elaborate that the institutional framework of a country, including its political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, significantly influences its ability to absorb and implement new technologies. This institutional framework is not static but evolves over time, shaped by historical experiences and contemporary challenges. The complexity of measuring social capability means that the exact mechanisms by which institutions influence technological advance and social change remain somewhat opaque. This uncertainty underscores the need for more nuanced approaches to understanding economic growth, as simple convergence models fail to capture the intricate interplay between institutions, technology, and social dynamics.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the adoption and implementation of technologies. The nature of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly affects a country's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies led to the establishment of different institutional frameworks in colonies, with some adopting inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others established extractive states designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This institutional divergence had lasting effects on income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These findings underscore the critical role of institutions in determining economic outcomes.

The historical context of institutional development is crucial for understanding long-run economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective is supported by Austin (2007, p.20), who shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes were driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, further illustrating how institutions evolve in response to historical experiences and contemporary challenges. The interaction between institutions and economic development is complex, with institutions both shaping and being shaped by economic conditions.

Institutional frameworks are not static but evolve over time, shaped by historical experiences and contemporary challenges. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the results of institutional quality on economic outcomes are consistent across various measures, including constraints on the executive, law and order tradition, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). This consistency underscores the robustness of the relationship between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) adds that colonial administrations often changed their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional evolution (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic is further evidenced by the role of women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, illustrating how institutional changes can have differential impacts on various segments of society (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This historical evidence shows that institutions are shaped by and shape the distribution of economic opportunities and outcomes.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism have long-lasting effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize the rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is supported by the work of Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who show that the de-privileging of women in access to land was reinforced by the central role of men in the codification of customary law under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This historical evidence highlights the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on long-run economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further illustrated by the work of Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3), who argue that collaborative research is essential to continue and improve Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance in the world economy (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2). This collaborative approach is necessary to produce numbers even if a solid basis for them does not always exist, expecting that scholars might disagree and carry out new work to show that they were wrong. This approach has been highly successful, but not always understood and appreciated by their colleagues. The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which institutions are shaped by historical experiences versus contemporary challenges. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, which was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).
