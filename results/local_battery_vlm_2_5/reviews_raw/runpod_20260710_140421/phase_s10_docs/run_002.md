The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the explanation of why some regions have prospered while others have lagged. The live disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for economic development. Others contend that the impact of institutions is often overstated, and that historical and contextual factors also significantly influence economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. assert that European colonists established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from those that enforced the rule of law to extractive states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin, however, suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This tension highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions interact with other factors to shape long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. They posit that European colonizers established different institutional frameworks depending on mortality rates and the feasibility of settlement. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, they set up extractive states aimed at rapidly transferring resources to the metropole. This institutional design maximized rents for European colonists rather than promoting long-run economic growth. In contrast, where Europeans could settle and establish institutions that enforced the rule of law, as in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was encouraged. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further elucidate that these equilibrium institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their rents, often at the expense of long-term economic development. The long-run implications of these institutional differences are significant. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was closely tied to industrialization opportunities during the nineteenth century. However, these opportunities were not uniformly distributed due to the institutional frameworks established during colonialism. The interaction between institutions and the ability to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development trajectories of these colonies. This institutional legacy continues to influence economic outcomes in these regions today.

These institutional changes, driven by shifts in land abundance and the need for cocoa production, laid the groundwork for the development of modern economic institutions in Ghana.

Abramovitz and Allen both acknowledge the role of exogenous factors in economic growth, but they focus on different mechanisms by which institutions influence long-run economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by both exogenous factors and the dynamics of technological advance and social change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). He does not directly address institutions as a fundamental cause of long-run economic growth. Allen (2009) examines the role of institutions in facilitating economic growth, focusing on the importance of property rights and the rule of law. He argues that the development of strong property rights and a stable legal framework can enhance economic growth by providing incentives for investment and innovation. This perspective is consistent with Abramovitz's view that technological advancements and social change are crucial for economic growth, as both emphasize the importance of exogenous factors in shaping economic outcomes. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) discusses a country's social capability with technical competence, for which years of education may be a rough proxy, and with its political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions, which he characterizes in more qualitative ways. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, one that takes into account the interplay between institutions and other factors such as technological advancements and social change. This interplay is crucial for understanding the complexities of economic growth and the role of institutions in this process.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) examine the real income divergence and wage stagnation in China without directly linking these phenomena to institutional factors. They argue that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, a period marked by significant economic and social changes. These practices likely buffered the impact of price changes on real wages, contributing to the observed stagnation. The existence of a vibrant and active labour market in early modern China further supports the idea that wage rates can serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This labour market integration suggests that real wages were reflective of broader economic conditions, including the marginal productivity of labour. However, the precise proportion of day labourers and the extent of regional migration remain elusive, indicating that while wage stagnation is evident, the underlying factors are complex and multifaceted. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) highlight that the long-term labour market was extremely thin, suggesting that the stagnation was not merely a result of short-term market conditions but rather a deeper structural issue. This structural issue likely involved broader economic and social factors that influenced the marginal productivity of labour and the overall economic conditions in China. The implications of this wage stagnation are significant, as they suggest that the economic growth during this period was not accompanied by improvements in living standards for a large portion of the population. The literature on African women's economic contributions and the impact of money introduction does not directly address how institutions fundamentally cause long-run economic growth. Instead, it focuses on specific historical and social factors that influenced economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Akyeampong and Fofack highlight how African women's economic roles were systematically marginalized under colonial rule through changes in customary law. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures that restricted women's access to land and economic opportunities, particularly in the context of cash crops. This marginalization was not just a byproduct of colonial rule but a deliberate outcome of institutional changes that reshaped economic activities. The shift from a multicentric economy to a more centralized one, driven by the introduction of money, had significant implications for economic development. The economic institutions of the Tiv, for example, were not seamlessly integrated into the global economy. Instead, the transition was marked by tensions and disruptions, highlighting the complexities of institutional change and its impact on economic growth. This suggests that while institutions play a crucial role in shaping economic growth, the process is fraught with challenges and requires a nuanced understanding of how different factors interact.
