The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the explanatory power of institutional frameworks in understanding the divergent economic trajectories of nations. The debate centres on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more significant role. One position argues that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are crucial for sustained economic development. Another position contends that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as colonial policies or local economic conditions, are more influential. These institutional changes, however, varied significantly across different colonies, leading to diverse economic trajectories. (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Their work highlights how European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks in colonies based on mortality rates. In places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where mortality was low, settlers implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive states were established to transfer resources to the metropole rapidly. (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). These extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists, often hindered long-run economic growth. (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies well beyond the colonial era. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to these institutional changes. (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional perspective underscores the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development, suggesting that the initial conditions set by colonial powers can have lasting effects on a nation's economic growth.

Austin shows that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is dynamic and context-specific. Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) argue that shifts in factor endowments, such as land abundance or scarcity, can drive institutional changes. In Ghana, the transition from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional innovations in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions. The abolition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 further facilitated this transition, as it encouraged the development of free labour markets. Austin (2007, p.20) notes that the British emancipation ordinance of 1874 in the Gold Coast Colony played a crucial role in this process, as it drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This institutional revolution in factor markets had profound economic implications, as it allowed for more efficient allocation of resources and stimulated economic growth. Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by showing that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action. For instance, Governor-General Clifford opted for a higher production equilibrium, contrary to expectations. This adaptive behaviour of colonial administrations highlights the importance of local context and agency in shaping institutional outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15); (Bohannan 1959, p.10) examine how social and cultural changes in Africa have influenced economic outcomes. Economic growth in Africa was driven by women's significant contributions during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was particularly evident in the agricultural sector, where women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced this de-privileging of women, as men played a central role in this process. This dynamic underscores the intricate relationship between gender roles, land access, and economic development. The introduction of money into subsistence economies also catalyzed profound economic shifts. Bohannan (1959, p.9) illustrates this through the Tiv economy, where brass rods served as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere. This economic integration with the world economy, facilitated by colonial rule, led to increased trade and a greater number of markets. The transition presented challenges, as the introduction of alien institutions perturbed the internal marketing system. This transition highlights the complex interplay between economic institutions and social structures in shaping long-run economic growth.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3) and Feinstein and Thomas (2014) (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2) highlight that economic measurement requires careful methodological improvements. The further back in time one goes, the larger the margins of error in economic estimates. Estimates of national accounts for past economies are often based on partial data and assumptions about the links between these data and the economic activities they represent. These margins of error are both objective and subjective, based on estimates of the possible margins of error of the underlying data. Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not always exist was highly successful in inducing scholars to work on these themes. This strategy, though not always understood and appreciated by his colleagues, has led to a more detailed overview of long-term economic growth than when he started his project in the 1960s. The Hitotshibashi team's work on Asian national accounts is a detailed study that experiments with various margins of error. The subjectivity in these estimates highlights the need for continued updates and collaborative research. This is particularly important for understanding the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, as accurate economic measurement is crucial for assessing the impact of institutional changes over time.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2001, p.8) examine various aspects of economic growth and technological change, without directly addressing the role of institutions in long-run economic growth. Both authors underscore the complexity of economic growth, highlighting that it is influenced by a myriad of factors, including technological advances and social changes. This complexity is reflected in the varied experiences of countries in the productivity scale, where institutional changes can limit growth in wealthier nations. Allen (2001, p.8) similarly notes the multifaceted nature of economic growth, observing that the gains in British real wages from 1815 to 1850 were part of a cycle, with decisive breaks in living standards occurring at different historical periods. This cyclical nature of economic growth, influenced by factors such as population changes and technological advances, suggests that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth by facilitating or hindering the absorption of technological advances and social changes. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) further illustrate the importance of understanding the broader economic context, including labour market conditions and regional migration, in assessing the impact of institutions on economic growth. The existence of a vibrant labour market in early modern China, where wage rates served as a proxy for average earnings, highlights the significance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. The precise impact of institutions on economic growth remains elusive, as noted by Abramovitz (1986, p.5). This underscores the need for continued research to better understand the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth.

Institutions play a central role in shaping long-run economic growth by influencing the direction of economic development and the distribution of resources. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that European colonization strategies varied significantly, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions in colonies. Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, whereas extractive institutions in places like the Congo or the Gold Coast were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). Austin (2008, p.2) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths, highlighting the complexity of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15).

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The paper shows that equilibrium institutions may be extractive, designed to maximize rents to European colonists rather than to maximize long-run growth, which is crucial for understanding the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) contribute to this discussion by emphasizing the significant role of African women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20).
