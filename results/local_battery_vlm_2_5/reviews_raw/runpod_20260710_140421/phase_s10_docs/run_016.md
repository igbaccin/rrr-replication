The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a contentious issue in economics. The stakes are high because the question of whether institutions drive economic growth has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies. Two main positions exist in the literature: one that institutions are the primary determinant of long-run growth, and another that argues institutions are merely a secondary factor. The first position is often associated with the work of Acemoglu et al., who argue that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing incentives for investment and productivity growth. The second position is often associated with the work of Austin, who suggests that institutions are influenced by broader economic and political factors, and that the relationship between institutions and growth is more complex than a simple causal link. The disagreement between these positions centres around the extent to which institutions can be seen as an independent variable that drives economic growth, or whether they are better understood as part of a broader system of economic and political factors. Austin's work underscores the need to consider the historical and contextual factors that shape institutions, and the potential for institutions to be influenced by broader economic and political forces. The debate highlights the importance of understanding the complex interplay between institutions and other factors in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Abramovitz's (1986, p.21) and Bolt and van Zanden (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3)'s work jointly highlights the complexities of economic growth and technological advance, emphasizing the role of social capability and institutional change. These scholars describe a process where technological progress and institutional dynamics interact in a way that shapes economic performance over the long run. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) identifies social capability as a crucial element in this process, encompassing technical competence and the effectiveness of political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions. This capability is essential for mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprise, though it is difficult to quantify. Abramovitz acknowledges that measuring and assessing social capability remains challenging. This complexity is further illustrated by Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.2), who note the difficulties in estimating national accounts for historical periods, highlighting the subjective nature of these estimates. This subjectivity extends to the assessment of institutional change and its impact on economic growth, suggesting that while institutions play a significant role, their effects are not straightforward to measure or predict. This nuanced understanding of economic growth and technological advance underscores the need for a comprehensive approach that considers both the quantitative and qualitative aspects of institutional change.

Building on this, Austin shows that the institutional revolution in factor markets was pivotal for understanding economic development in Africa (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional revolution was marked by the emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the emancipation ordinance in the Gold Coast Colony and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2007, p.4). The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule, such as the Gold Coast and later Ghana, further facilitated market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region (Austin 2007, p.4). Indigenous economic and political actions shaped the institutional changes in factor markets, in addition to responses to external pressures. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of local agency in shaping institutional change, even within the context of colonial rule.

This long-term process of real income divergence between China and Europe, as found by Allen et al. (2011, p.22); (Allen 2001, p.8), underscores the enduring impact of institutional and economic factors on economic growth. They show that real wages in China stagnated during this period, while Europe experienced significant growth. The integration of labour markets and the role of day labour in commercialized regions drove this divergence, in addition to technological advance and institutional change. This integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions near major urban centres further substantiates the view that wage rates reflected the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole. Allen (2001, p.8) highlights that prices changed frequently, and it is a mistake to say they were fixed for long periods. This frequent change in prices indicates a dynamic market environment where wages and prices were responsive to economic conditions. However, Allen (2001, p.23) also notes that the gains from 1815 to 1850 were a cycle and not a trend, suggesting that the real wage history was characterized by long swings reflecting population changes and decisive breaks with the past. This qualification underscores the complex and non-linear nature of real income trends, where short-term cycles and long-term trends coexist. The stagnation of real wages in China during this period highlights the importance of understanding the institutional and economic factors that drove this divergence.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight the significant contributions of women to economic growth during pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa, underscoring the profound changes brought by the introduction of money into subsistence economies. Women's roles in economic development were central, particularly in agricultural sectors. This institutional change had far-reaching effects, altering the dynamics of land ownership and economic participation. The introduction of money and new trade networks transformed traditional economic systems, integrating them into the global economy. This shift was driven by the establishment of new road networks and increased trade, which perturbed the internal marketing system and brought African economies into contact with the world economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The transition presented challenges, as the introduction of money did not integrate seamlessly with existing social institutions, particularly those related to marriage and exchange (Bohannan 1959, p.9). The economic revolution in the Gold Coast, where women played a pivotal role in cocoa production, illustrates how gender dynamics influenced economic growth during this period. This highlights the complex interplay between institutional changes, gender roles, and economic development in Africa.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) attribute the divergence in economic outcomes between former European colonies to the institutional legacies of colonialism, in addition to geographical factors. The European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persistently affected income per capita. These extractive institutions were designed to maximize the rents for European colonists, not to promote long-run economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who argue that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the 19th century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies. The establishment of extractive institutions by European colonists in high-mortality colonies led to a lack of economic development. This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19), who find that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, are closely related to income per capita. The implication of these findings is that the institutional legacies of colonialism continue to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is profoundly evident in the historical context of European colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European settlers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment to extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design had lasting effects on economic development. For instance, colonies with inclusive institutions, such as the United States and Australia, experienced significant economic growth, while those with extractive institutions, like the Congo and the Gold Coast, faced stagnation. This dynamic is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The persistence of these institutional legacies continues to shape economic outcomes in former colonies today, highlighting the enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth. This is further supported by the fact that various measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive, law and order, property rights, rule of law, and judicial efficiency, are closely related to income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19).

The institutional legacies of colonialism are not merely economic; they also have deep social and political dimensions. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective is crucial because it underscores the complexity of institutional change and its multifaceted impact on economic development. For example, in regions where European settlers were not present to compete for African labour, the expansion of indigenous commodity production was often in the interests of colonial governments. This dynamic highlights how colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, further complicating the relationship between institutions and economic growth. This is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack, who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, illustrating how institutional changes affected different segments of society. This dynamic is further supported by Austin (2007, p.20; 2007, p.4), who argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change had profound economic implications, as it facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, further highlighting the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth.

The enduring impact of historical institutions on long-run economic growth is a shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The precise mechanisms by which these institutions operate, however, remain a subject of debate. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) agree on the complexity of institutional change and its multifaceted impact on economic development. They disagree on the role of indigenous economic and political action in shaping institutional prescriptions. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) argues that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to local economic and political actions, while Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) argue that the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, illustrating how institutional changes affected different segments of society. This disagreement can be settled by examining the specific historical evidence of indigenous economic and political action in shaping institutional prescriptions.
