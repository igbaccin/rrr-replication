The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a subject of intense academic debate. At stake is the understanding of why some regions have experienced sustained economic development while others have stagnated. A central disagreement revolves around the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions, particularly those that enforce property rights and the rule of law, are critical for long-term economic growth. Others contend that the impact of institutions is more complex and that historical and geographical factors also play significant roles. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they operate in conjunction with other factors. The extent to which institutions can be shaped by exogenous factors, such as colonial policies, further complicates the understanding of their role in economic development. This interplay between institutions and other variables is crucial for comprehending the diverse economic trajectories observed across different regions. The question of whether institutions are endogenous or exogenous to economic growth remains unresolved, with implications for policy and theoretical frameworks. A closer examination of the empirical evidence and theoretical underpinnings is necessary to elucidate the mechanisms through which institutions influence long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. These institutions shaped by colonial powers varied widely, ranging from extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, such as in the Congo or the Gold Coast, to more inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, as seen in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. This variation in institutional design had profound implications for economic development. This institutional legacy persisted, shaping the economic trajectories of these regions for centuries. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) further emphasize that these equilibrium institutions were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to promote long-run growth. The impact of these institutions is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where those with more extractive institutions lagged behind those with more inclusive ones. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document this reversal and link it to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. The relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) point out that the establishment of extractive institutions depended on the specific strategies adopted by colonial powers. This suggests that the institutional legacy of colonialism could have been different if colonial powers had chosen different strategies, highlighting the role of institutional design in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin's work (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20) re-evaluates the economic transitions in Africa by focusing on specific institutional changes and their impacts. For instance, Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. This argument is crucial because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic factors over time. Austin (2007, p.20) further elaborates on this by examining the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This institutional change was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the loss of slaves and the subsequent economic pressures on chiefs. This pattern suggests that institutional changes in Africa were often responses to local economic conditions and pressures, rather than being dictated by exogenous factors. However, Austin (2008, p.2) notes that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional change in Africa, where local economic conditions, political pressures, and colonial policies all played interdependent roles. The integration of these factors into the analysis of economic transitions in Africa provides a more comprehensive understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth in the region.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) argue that the role of African women in economic development and the impact of money on economic transitions are central to understanding the economic changes in African economies. The introduction of money into African societies led to significant shifts in economic activities and social structures. This integration faced challenges. The introduction of alien institutions, such as colonial legal systems, disrupted traditional economic practices and altered social dynamics. For instance, the codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced patriarchal structures, marginalizing women's access to land and resources (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This legal framework not only limited women's economic contributions but also entrenched gender inequalities that persisted long after the colonial period. The economic transformations brought about by the introduction of money and the codification of customary law highlight the complex interplay between economic changes, social structures, and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa.

The interplay between technological advancements, social changes, and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influencing productivity and living standards across various regions and periods (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This perspective is supported by Allen et al. (2011, p.22), who show that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries, reflecting the broader social and economic conditions of the time. Allen (2001, p.8) further illustrates that prices for goods like grain, meat, and linen cloth were subject to frequent changes, indicating the dynamic nature of economic conditions and their impact on living standards. These findings suggest that technological and social changes can both drive and constrain economic growth, depending on the institutional context. The institutional framework, including the organization and management of large-scale enterprise, plays a crucial role in determining how technological advancements translate into economic growth.

Institutional frameworks, shaped by historical contexts, are pivotal in determining how technological advancements and social changes translate into economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) show that European colonization strategies varied widely, with some colonies adopting institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, while others established extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional design significantly affected income per capita in these colonies. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) builds on this by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin's work highlights that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political action, indicating a dynamic interplay between local conditions and institutional evolution.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is further illuminated by the impact of colonial rule on factor markets. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6) argue that equilibrium institutions in former colonies were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. This institutional design was influenced by population density and prosperity, which in turn shaped the policies pursued by Europeans. Austin (2007, p.20; 2007, p.4) provides a complementary perspective, showing that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, underscoring the adaptive nature of institutions in response to economic pressures.

The historical evidence shows that the institutional framework, including the organization and management of large-scale enterprise, plays a crucial role in determining how technological advancements translate into economic growth. The precise remaining disagreement involves the extent to which colonial institutions were extractive versus growth-oriented. This is settled by the empirical comparison of income per capita in colonies with different institutional designs (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The measurement of income per capita in these colonies provides a clear indication of the impact of institutional frameworks on long-run economic growth.
