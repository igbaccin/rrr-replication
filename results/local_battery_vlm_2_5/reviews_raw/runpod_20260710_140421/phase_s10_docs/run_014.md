The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. Institutions, broadly defined as the rules of the game in a society, including both formal rules (constitutions, laws, regulations) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, and self-imposed codes of conduct), are widely acknowledged as critical factors influencing economic performance. The disagreement centres on whether the causal mechanism of institutions is driven by endogenous or exogenous factors. One position asserts that institutions are endogenous to economic conditions, shaped by local needs and historical contingencies. The rival position argues that institutions are exogenous, imposed by external forces such as colonial powers. The stakes are high, as understanding the causal role of institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth. The debate on the role of institutions in shaping economic growth is complex and multifaceted, with scholars offering diverse perspectives on the mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism fundamentally shaped the long-term economic trajectories of former European colonies. In cases where European settlers established colonies, they implemented institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth. In regions with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to transfer resources to the metropole quickly, impairing long-run economic development. This pattern of institutional variation is further supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), who emphasize that the policies pursued by Europeans were influenced by population density and prosperity, leading to divergent economic outcomes. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is thus intrinsically linked to the institutional frameworks imposed during colonial rule. This dynamic underscores the critical role of institutional changes in shaping economic trajectories, even as it challenges the notion that institutions alone determine long-run economic growth.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2007, p.20; 2008, p.2) show that shifts in land abundance and scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 influenced market dynamics. During this period, Ghana experienced a significant institutional revolution in factor markets, driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity. This shift led to the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908, although not direct responses to changing factor scarcities, played a crucial role in this transition. These legal actions facilitated the shift from coerced to free labour, which had important economic implications. The loss of slaves drove Akyem chiefs into debt, making them more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa. This dynamic highlights how institutional changes in Ghana responded to economic pressures and localised scarcity, as well as being imposed by colonial powers. This institutional revolution was also driven by the integration of markets within the Gold Coast Colony, which removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade. This integration fostered economic growth by enhancing market efficiency and facilitating the exchange of goods and services. The integration of markets within the Gold Coast Colony was a key factor in the institutional revolution. Institutional changes in Ghana were driven by colonial policies, among other factors. Colonial administrations sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo, which led to changes in institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. For example, Governor-General Clifford chose a higher production equilibrium. This indicates that colonial administrations were responsive to local economic conditions and adapted their policies accordingly.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argue that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This divergence was caused by shifts in economic conditions and population changes, not directly by institutional changes. Allen (2001, p.8) shows that the maintenance of high real wages in certain regions was a decisive break with the past, reflecting economic development and population changes (Allen 2001, p.23). Allen et al. (2011, p.22) substantiate this view by documenting a vibrant and active labor market in early modern China, where wage rates served as a reasonable proxy for average earnings and marginal productivity of labor (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This suggests that real wages were influenced by broader economic factors, as well as institutional changes. Regional migration and the integration of labor markets between urban and rural areas also played a significant role in shaping real wages. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that in commercialized regions near major urban centers, there was a high degree of integration of labor markets, which could have influenced wage patterns and economic growth (Allen et al. 2011, p.24). This indicates that while institutions may not have been the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, they interacted with economic conditions and labor market dynamics to shape real incomes.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) both acknowledge the intricacies of the catch-up process in economic growth, where exogenous factors and technological advancements play a significant role. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights the influence of social capability, including technical competence and institutional frameworks, in shaping economic growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) (Abramovitz 1986, p.5) further elaborate on this by noting that in regions near major urban centers, labor market integration significantly influenced wage patterns and economic growth. This interplay between technological advancements and institutional frameworks suggests that while institutions may not be the sole driver of long-run economic growth, they are integral to the process. The extent to which these institutional factors can be measured or quantified remains a challenge, as Abramovitz (1986, p.5) notes. This highlights the need for further research into the specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth, particularly in the context of technological advancements and social change.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight that women’s roles and the introduction of money into subsistence economies were pivotal in shaping economic development in Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack describe how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This dynamic was reinforced by customary law under colonial rule, which codified and entrenched patriarchal structures. The shift from subsistence to cash economies, driven by factors such as the codification of customary law, influenced economic growth in regions like the Gold Coast. This transition was marked by significant changes in economic institutions, as evidenced by Bohannan's account of the Tiv people, where the introduction of money led to a shift away from a multicentric economy towards integration with the global market. Despite the profound changes and economic growth, this shift also brought pain points, such as the inability to equate brass rods with rights in women or food. The introduction of money thus had complex implications for economic institutions and growth, highlighting the need for further research into the specific mechanisms by which these changes influenced long-run economic development.

The impact of institutions on long-run economic growth is a central theme in the literature, with scholars emphasizing the role of both historical and contemporary institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the economic trajectories of colonies, shaping institutions that either encouraged investment and economic development or were extractive and hindered growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who further assert that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is primarily due to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The institutional legacies of colonial rule, therefore, are crucial in understanding the long-term economic development of these regions.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further elucidated by the work of Austin (2008, p.2), who contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's argument aligns with Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33), who emphasize the importance of institutional changes during colonial periods in shaping long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Austin (2008, p.2) also highlights that colonial administrations often adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, showing that institutional changes involved complex interactions between colonizers and the colonized (Austin 2008, p.15). This interaction underscores the dynamic nature of institutional evolution and its impact on economic growth.

The specific mechanisms by which institutions influence economic growth are also explored by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This contribution challenges views that marginalize the role of women in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) also discuss how customary law, codified under colonial rule, reinforced the subordinate status of women, highlighting the institutional barriers that affected economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional change was driven by economic factors and had significant implications for economic growth.
