The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how societies evolve economically over extended periods. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms governing behaviour, can either facilitate or hinder economic development. One live disagreement centres on the extent to which colonial institutions have shaped long-run economic growth. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive, have had a lasting negative impact on economic growth in former colonies. Others contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated and that other factors, such as indigenous economic and political actions, have played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are just one of many factors. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

These institutional changes, however, were not uniform across colonies and varied based on the settler mortality rates and the resulting institutional frameworks (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. highlight that European colonizers established different types of institutions depending on mortality rates and settlement patterns. This institutional framework, characterized by the absence of rule of law and property rights, hindered long-term economic development. In colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, fostering economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions, shaped by politically powerful groups, were often designed to maximize rents for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The persistence of these institutions has had lasting effects on the economic trajectories of former colonies, with extractive institutions continuing to impede development in many regions. Acemoglu et al. note that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This suggests that while colonial institutions initially shaped economic outcomes, subsequent industrialization opportunities could mitigate or exacerbate these effects.

Austin (2008, p.2)  argues that the historical institutional changes in Ghana during 1874-1939, driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity, led to significant transformations in factor markets (Austin 2007, p.20; Austin 2008, p.2). This period saw a marked shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which in turn drove an institutional revolution in factor markets. Austin (2007, p.20) notes that the shift from coerced to free labour, facilitated by legal actions such as the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, had profound economic dimensions. This transition was a response to changing factor scarcities, which encouraged the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule further enhanced market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what would become Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes were influenced by indigenous economic and political actions, which colonial administrations responded to by adjusting their institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptive response highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change, where local conditions and actions significantly shaped the institutional landscape. Austin (2008, p.2) also points out that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which compresses different historical periods and paths, oversimplifies causal relationships. This qualification suggests that while institutional changes in Ghana were driven by local factors, the broader historical context and the complexity of institutional evolution must be considered to fully understand long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Bohannan, focus on the economic and social changes in African economies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). The role of women in economic growth is a key focus, with Akyeampong and Fofack highlighting that women's contributions were crucial during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, and their access to land for cash crops was limited by the codification of customary law under colonial rule. This legal framework reinforced patriarchal alliances, further marginalizing women's economic roles. Bohannan, on the other hand, examines the introduction of money into subsistence economies, noting that this shift led to profound changes in economic institutions. Peace and improved road networks facilitated increased trade and the establishment of more markets, integrating local economies with the global market. This transition was not seamless; the introduction of alien institutions perturbed the internal marketing system. The equivalence of brass rods as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere further illustrates the complexities of this economic shift. The introduction of money coexisted with existing economic institutions, highlighting the adaptive nature of economic systems. The economic changes driven by these factors were interconnected, reflecting the broader institutional landscape that shaped long-run economic growth in Africa.

This approach contrasts with the focus on real income and wage stagnation, which often overlooks the broader institutional context that underpins long-run economic growth (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). Allen (2001, p.8) demonstrates that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This period saw significant fluctuations in prices, with grain prices playing a minor role while bread prices, based on retail information from noninstitutional sources, loomed large in the cost of living indices. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) substantiate this view by showing that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. The authors argue that wage rates serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole. This suggests that the stagnation in real wages reflected broader economic conditions. The existence of a vibrant and active labour market in early modern China further supports the idea that wage rates were indicative of overall economic performance. This qualification highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors influencing real income and wage stagnation, even in the absence of direct institutional causation.

Collaborative research is indispensable for refining the measurement of economic performance. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) emphasize the need for collective efforts to improve the accuracy of historical economic data, underscoring the importance of ongoing scholarly engagement. The collaborative approach allows for the rigorous assessment of margins of error and the refinement of assumptions underlying economic estimates. This collaborative approach is essential for producing reliable estimates of economic performance, especially for periods with sparse data. The collaborative approach has been effective in refining Maddison's work, which has been instrumental in quantifying long-term economic development (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2).

This complexity is further compounded by the inherent uncertainties in historical data, which make it challenging to pinpoint the exact factors driving economic catch-up (Abramovitz 1986, p.21; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). They highlight that technological change and social capability play crucial roles in shaping long-run economic growth. Social capability encompasses technical competence and the effectiveness of political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions. These institutions facilitate the organization and management of large-scale enterprises and the mobilization of capital, which are essential for sustained economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that the dynamics of technological advance and social change are pivotal in this process, often leading to shifts in the productivity rankings of countries. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) emphasize that the catch-up process involves the absorption of social capability, which includes the development of institutions that support economic activity. The measurement of social capability remains challenging, as it involves qualitative assessments of institutional effectiveness. This complexity suggests that while technological change is necessary for economic catch-up, it is not sufficient on its own. The role of institutions in fostering social capability is therefore critical for long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the historical impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate how European colonizers established different institutional frameworks in their colonies, ranging from inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law to extractive states designed for rapid resource transfer. These institutional differences significantly influenced income per capita, highlighting the enduring effects of colonial policies on long-run economic outcomes. This perspective is reinforced by Austin (2008, p.15), who shows that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, indicating that the interaction between local conditions and colonial policies shaped institutional outcomes. The persistence of these institutions over time underscores their critical role in economic development, as they continued to influence economic trajectories long after the colonial period ended. The historical evidence from these studies demonstrates that institutional legacies are pivotal in understanding long-run economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, can persist because they maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This view is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15), who highlight how colonial rule reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary laws that marginalized women, affecting economic development. The codification of customary law under colonial rule, which often disadvantaged women, illustrates how institutional changes can have far-reaching economic consequences. These studies collectively show that the design and persistence of institutions are critical factors in economic growth, as they determine the distribution of economic benefits and the incentives for long-term development.
