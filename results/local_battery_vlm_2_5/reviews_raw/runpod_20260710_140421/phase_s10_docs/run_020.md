The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. Institutions, defined as the rules and norms that structure economic interactions, are seen as fundamental to economic performance. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes and the mechanisms through which they operate. One position argues that the type of institutions established during colonial periods significantly influenced the long-run economic trajectories of former colonies. This perspective emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic development, suggesting that extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonial powers, hindered economic growth. Another position contends that the reversal of fortune in economic development is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This view underscores the importance of institutional evolution and adaptation in response to changing economic conditions. The debate highlights the complexity of institutional effects on economic growth, with some scholars arguing that institutional changes are driven by endogenous factors, such as local economic and political dynamics. The central question is whether institutions are the primary drivers of long-run economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, play a more significant role. The interplay between institutions and other factors remains a critical area of inquiry, with implications for understanding the roots of economic development and the potential for policy interventions to promote growth. The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is therefore a multifaceted issue, involving both the nature of institutions and the contexts in which they operate. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies. Their analysis highlights that European colonizers established different types of institutions based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. In colonies with high European mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were implemented to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run economic development. In colonies with low mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were established. This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who sought to maximize their own benefits, often at the expense of long-run growth. This institutional legacy has persisted, influencing the economic trajectories of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) further document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, showing that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. The institutional changes introduced by colonialism thus had profound and enduring effects on the economic growth of former colonies.

Austin (2007, p.20) and Austin (2008, p.2) share a finding that the institutional revolution in factor markets in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional transformation in Ghana was marked by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity. This shift was driven by a combination of factors, including the abolition of slavery and the increasing demand for cocoa production, which led to a greater willingness among chiefs to sell land to strangers, (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change had significant economic implications, as it facilitated the transition from coerced to free labour, a shift that was crucial for economic development (Austin 2007, p.20). Austin (2008, p.2) further elaborates that colonial administrations adapted their policies in response to indigenous economic and political actions, fostering an environment where local economic interests aligned with colonial goals (Austin 2008, p.15). This alignment was pivotal in shaping the economic landscape of Ghana, as it encouraged the development of markets and the integration of local economies into broader trade networks. The colonial administration's role in integrating markets through the removal of tariffs and administrative barriers further underscored this economic integration (Austin 2007, p.4). However, while these institutional changes were significant, they did not directly address the fundamental causes of long-run economic growth. This is because the institutional changes, while important, were part of a broader set of factors that influenced economic development, including the political and economic contexts in which they occurred.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight that African economies have historically relied on significant contributions from women and the introduction of money, which have both shaped economic growth in distinct ways. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) show that women's roles in agriculture were pivotal, particularly in food farming and the cocoa revolution in the Gold Coast (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). The integration of local economies into broader trade networks, facilitated by the introduction of money, was a critical factor in economic development. This integration presented challenges. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often marginalized women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional change, while part of a broader economic shift, did not directly address the fundamental causes of long-run economic growth. The economic impact of women and money in African economies underscores the complex interplay between institutional changes and economic development, revealing that while these factors contributed to growth, they also introduced new challenges that influenced the economic trajectory of African societies.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argue that historical trends in real incomes across different regions highlight how economic leaders maintained their income levels while others experienced declines. This divergence in real incomes was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders. This context is adjacent to our main topic as it does not directly address the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Allen (2001, p.8) shows that grain prices, which play a minor role in this paper, were wholesale, while the bread prices which loom large in the cost of living indices are based on retail information from noninstitutional sources. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) substantiate this view by arguing that wage rates may serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole. This suggests that the economic conditions and wage trends in different regions were influenced by various factors, including institutional arrangements and market dynamics. Allen (2001, p.8) further notes that the important features in British real wage history were the long swings reflecting population changes in the medieval period, the maintenance of a high real wage after economic development gathered strength in the seventeenth century, and the rise in living standards between 1870 and the First World War. These periods were decisive breaks with the past, highlighting the role of economic development and institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) qualify this by showing that, for the early twentieth century, large-scale household surveys reveal that between 30 and 50 per cent of rural households in the 1930s Wuxi county in the Yangzi Delta region hired day labourers during peak season. This indicates that the integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas was relatively high, suggesting that economic conditions and wage trends were influenced by a complex interplay of institutional and market factors. The analysis of real income and wage trends in historical contexts provides valuable insights into the economic conditions and wage trends in different regions, highlighting the role of institutions and market dynamics in shaping long-run economic growth.

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) argue that the process of economic growth and convergence is far from straightforward. The catch-up process is complex and influenced by a multitude of factors, including technological advance and social change. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) explains that the convergence hypothesis is challenged by the dynamic interplay of exogenous factors and the internal dynamics of technological advancement and social transformation. This complexity is evident in the varying ranks of countries in the productivity scale, which are influenced by institutional changes that can either accelerate or hinder growth. One specific mechanism driving this complexity is the role of social capability, which encompasses technical competence and the effectiveness of political, commercial, industrial, and financial institutions. This capability is crucial for mobilizing capital and managing large-scale enterprises, factors that are essential for economic growth. The catch-up process is thus influenced by the institutional and social contexts of different countries. The institutional changes that Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights can limit the growth of even the most advanced economies, as seen in the metaphorical "institutional arthritis" that affects wealthy nations. This institutional dynamic is further complicated by the fact that social capability is difficult to measure and quantify, making it a challenging variable to integrate into economic models. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) provide empirical support for this complexity by examining real income and wage trends in different regions, demonstrating how market dynamics and institutional factors interact to shape long-run economic growth. The interplay of these factors suggests that economic growth is influenced by a complex web of institutional, social, and technological elements. This complexity in the catch-up process underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of economic growth that goes beyond simple convergence hypotheses.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical divergence of income levels among former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. show that European settlers established different institutions based on mortality rates, leading to extractive institutions in high-mortality colonies, which significantly affected income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This perspective is corroborated by Acemoglu et al., who emphasize that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). These institutional frameworks, whether extractive or growth-oriented, were designed to maximize rents for European colonists and not necessarily to promote long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

Austin challenges the notion that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.2). This view is supported by Akyeampong and Fofack, who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). This highlights the complexity of institutional impacts, as the contributions of women were often marginalized by patriarchal alliances and customary law codified under colonial rule (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). The shift from land abundance to localized scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labor (Austin 2007, p.20). This institutional change was driven by the loss of slaves, which made chiefs more willing to sell land to strangers to farm cocoa (Austin 2007, p.20). Allen supports this view, showing that the divergence in real incomes observed in the mid-nineteenth century was produced between 1500 and 1750 as incomes fell in most European cities but were maintained in the economic leaders (Allen 2001, p.23).

While there is agreement that institutions play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth, the specific mechanisms and historical contexts vary. Acemoglu et al. argue that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, rather than promote long-run growth, were a key factor in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). In contrast, Austin shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security encouraged indigenous economic and political action, leading to changes in institutional prescriptions (Austin 2008, p.15). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century, which played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This interaction highlights the importance of institutional changes in shaping long-run economic growth, as well as the need for a nuanced understanding of the complex web of institutional, social, and technological elements that influence economic growth.
