The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a critical question in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of why some regions thrive economically over the long term while others stagnate or decline, and how this process is influenced by the underlying institutional frameworks. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the primacy of institutions in driving economic growth and those who argue for other factors, such as geographic or climatic conditions. This debate is particularly salient in the context of colonial histories, where the impact of institutional legacies on economic development is evident. One position argues that extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonial powers at the expense of local populations, have had persistent negative effects on economic growth. One perspective suggests that the reversal of fortune in economic development is better understood through the lens of institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and geographic factors. This debate highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape long-run economic growth. A key question is whether the institutional frameworks established during colonial periods have enduring effects on economic development, or if other factors, such as local economic and political actions, play a more significant role. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is driven by the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) illustrate that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, where they did not settle in large numbers. This institutional framework, shaped by the political and economic interests of the colonizers, persists and significantly affects income per capita. The persistence of these institutions, even after colonial rule, is a crucial factor in understanding the long-run economic outcomes of these regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) further support this by showing that different measures of institutions, including constraints on the executive and property rights, yield similar results in their regressions, underscoring the robustness of the institutional argument. The long-run impact of these institutions varies across colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) document the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies, highlighting that this reversal was related to industrialization and the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This interaction played a central role in the long-run development of the former colonies, suggesting that the institutional framework set by colonialism could either facilitate or hinder industrialization, depending on the specific context and policies pursued.

The institutional revolution in factor markets in African economies, particularly in Ghana during 1874-1939, marked by widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, did not directly address long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). These changes were responses to external pressures and were also shaped by indigenous economic and political actions, which influenced colonial administrations to adapt their institutional frameworks. Colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel a material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights how local economic conditions and political actions could influence colonial policies, leading to institutional changes that facilitated market integration and economic activity. The institutional changes did not necessarily translate into long-run economic growth, as other factors such as industrialization and technological advancements played crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes. The institutional revolution in factor markets, while important, was just one piece of a complex puzzle that determined long-run economic growth in African economies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) argue that the economic contributions of African women were pivotal to economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show that women were integral to farming and economic activity, despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming. This was partly due to the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which reinforced patriarchal structures and limited women's access to land for cash crops. The introduction of alien institutions, such as a new road network and European courts, further perturbed the internal marketing system and integrated local economies with the world economy. This integration was not without its challenges, as the shift from a multicentric economy to a monetized system required significant adaptation. Bohannan (1959, p.9) illustrates this by showing how brass rods, a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere, could not fulfill all functions of money outside this sphere. This highlights the complex economic changes that occurred as African societies adapted to new economic institutions, and the role of women in these changes. Economic growth in African societies was driven by multiple factors, including the contributions of women and the integration of local economies with the world economy.

Allen et al. establish that real wages in China stagnated from the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This stagnation is notable because it occurred despite a vibrant labour market. The active labour market in early modern China, particularly for short-term or day labour, suggests that wage rates could serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group as well as the marginal productivity of labour in the economy as a whole (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This finding is supported by evidence of a relatively high degree of integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions near major urban centres (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). The stagnation of real wages, despite a vibrant labour market, highlights the need for institutional changes that can foster economic growth. This is because the lack of significant institutional changes during this period meant that China did not experience the same level of economic growth as other regions.

Economic divergence and technological advance are key drivers of long-run economic growth, shaping the trajectory of economies over centuries. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2001, p.8) share a finding that the dynamics of economic divergence and technological advancement are complex and multifaceted, influencing long-run economic growth through various mechanisms. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) highlights that institutional change can limit the growth of Western countries, suggesting that the institutional framework plays a crucial role in determining economic outcomes. Allen (2001, p.8) supports this by illustrating how prices, which are influenced by institutional factors, can affect real wages and living standards. This interaction between institutional frameworks and economic indicators underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.5) qualifies this by noting that the concept of social capability, which includes institutional factors, is complex and difficult to measure. This complexity means that while institutions are important, their impact on economic growth is not straightforward. The nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth suggests that institutional changes must be carefully managed to foster long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical context of colonialism, where European settlers established diverse institutional frameworks. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2001, p.19) demonstrate that the type of institutions introduced by colonial powers significantly influenced economic outcomes. In regions where European settlers established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, economic growth was robust. In areas like the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions designed to transfer resources to the metropole hindered long-run economic development. The persistence of these institutions, shaped by the initial conditions and strategies of colonial powers, highlights the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories. This is consistent with Austin's (2008) (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2008, p.15) argument that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. The interaction between institutional frameworks and economic indicators underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth. The nuances of institutional change are further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.6), who argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This institutional legacy persists, affecting long-run economic development.

The complexity of institutional impact is further complicated by the role of local actors and their responses to colonial institutions. African women, for instance, significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) show that women were integral to economic activities, despite being relegated to marginal lands for food farming. This highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional influence, where local adaptations and resistances shape economic outcomes. Austin (2007, p.20; 2007, p.4) provides a complementary perspective, arguing that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This institutional change, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, illustrates how institutional frameworks evolve in response to economic pressures. The nuanced relationship between institutions and economic growth suggests that institutional changes must be carefully managed to foster long-run economic growth.

The central point of agreement is that the nature of institutions established during colonialism significantly influenced long-run economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which local adaptations and resistances can mitigate the negative effects of extractive institutions. Austin (2008, p.15) shows that colonial administrations that sought fiscal security and encouraged their subjects to feel some material interest in the status quo tended to change their institutional prescriptions in response to the incentives created by indigenous economic and political action. This is settled by the empirical comparison of colonial fiscal policies and their adaptation to local economic conditions, which shows that local adaptations can partially offset the negative impacts of extractive institutions.
