The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of why some regions prosper while others stagnate, and how historical institutional frameworks have influenced contemporary economic trajectories. The debate revolves around the extent to which institutions, shaped by colonial policies and local conditions, have either fostered or hindered economic development. One position argues that extractive institutions, designed to benefit colonial powers, have persistently impeded growth in former colonies. Some scholars suggest that the shift in relative incomes among former colonies resulted from institutional changes brought about by colonialism, with geographic factors playing a lesser role. A third perspective highlights the economic contributions of marginalised groups, such as women, during pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that overlook their role in economic development. The interplay between these positions underscores the complex relationship between institutions and long-run economic growth, with implications for policy and historical interpretation. The causal mechanisms linking institutions to economic outcomes remain a subject of intense scholarly inquiry. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). They show that Europeans adopted different colonization strategies in various regions, leading to the establishment of either inclusive institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment or extractive states designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. This variation in institutional frameworks was largely determined by European mortality rates in these regions. High mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted and significantly affected income per capita in the long run. For instance, regions like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand saw the establishment of inclusive institutions that fostered economic growth, whereas regions like the Congo or the Gold Coast were subject to extractive institutions that hindered development. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were designed to maximize the rents to European colonists, with long-run growth being a secondary consideration (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy has had profound implications for the economic trajectories of former colonies, as evidenced by the reversal in relative incomes among these regions. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in shaping the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies, continues to influence economic outcomes in these regions today, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic growth.

Austin's examination of Ghana's institutional changes during 1874-1939 demonstrates that shifts in land abundance and scarcity drove significant transformations in factor markets, particularly the emergence of markets for land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). These changes were not merely responses to external pressures but were also shaped by internal dynamics, such as the loss of slaves driving chiefs into debt and encouraging land sales to strangers for cocoa farming. This internal economic and political action influenced colonial administrations to adjust their institutional prescriptions, creating a more responsive and adaptive institutional environment (Austin 2008, p.15). The institutional revolution in Ghana highlights how localised scarcity can drive institutional innovation, even in the context of colonial rule. The extent to which these institutional changes were induced by changing factor scarcities remains a subject of debate. Some scholars argue that the transition from coerced to free labour, while important, was not solely an example of induced institutional innovation (Austin 2007, p.20). This nuance underscores the complex interplay between external colonial policies and internal economic dynamics in shaping institutional changes and their impact on long-run economic growth. The role of institutional change in economic development is thus multifaceted, involving both exogenous and endogenous factors that interact in intricate ways.

The introduction of money into an African subsistence economy led to profound changes and a shift away from a multicentric economy, highlighting the transformative impact of economic transitions on traditional systems (Bohannan 1959, p.10; Bohannan 1959, p.9). This transition was not seamless; it was a complex process involving multiple factors, such as the introduction of brass rods as a form of currency. Brass rods fulfilled only one function of money: payment. This limitation underscores the complexity of economic transitions, as the introduction of new economic institutions does not automatically lead to a seamless integration into the global economy. The equivalence between brass rods and rights in women, as well as between brass rods and food, could not exist within the Tiv's traditional economic system. This indicates that the introduction of money into a subsistence economy can lead to a shift away from traditional economic institutions, but it also highlights the challenges and limitations of this transition. The economic contributions of African women during these periods were often overlooked in historical analyses. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) document how women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, a pattern that was reinforced by colonial rule. This marginalization was exacerbated by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which further disadvantaged women's access to land, particularly in the context of cash crops. The central role of men in this process underscores how patriarchal alliances could shape institutional frameworks to reinforce women's subordinate status, limiting their economic contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional shift under colonial rule highlights how changes in land access rights can significantly alter economic dynamics. For example, the economic revolution in the Gold Coast, driven by cocoa production, illustrates that women's agricultural labor was crucial despite these institutional barriers.

The divergence in real wages between regions like China and Europe during the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries highlights significant differences in economic development trajectories (Allen et al. 2011, p.22; Allen 2001, p.8). This disparity in wage trends is attributed to distinct institutional frameworks that shaped economic opportunities and productivity. This institutional framework fostered conditions that supported economic growth, such as vibrant and active labour markets. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) substantiates this view with evidence of a high degree of integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in China, suggesting that wage rates could serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings of a particular socio-economic group and the marginal productivity of labour. Allen (2001, p.8) notes that prices changed frequently, indicating that economic conditions were dynamic and responsive to market forces. This dynamic pricing mechanism influenced real wages, which were maintained at a high level in European cities. The implication is that institutional factors, such as labour market integration and pricing mechanisms, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth. This institutional framework underscores the importance of labour market conditions in determining economic outcomes, which can be seen in the stark differences between China and Europe.

Abramovitz argues that the catch-up process in economic growth is complex and influenced by various factors beyond simple convergence hypotheses, highlighting the role of social capability and technological change (Abramovitz 1986, p.21). This view is supported by his assertion that social capability encompasses technical competence and the organization and management of large-scale enterprise, as well as financial institutions capable of mobilizing capital for individual firms (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The implication is that institutional factors, such as the capability to organize and manage large-scale enterprises and the presence of robust financial institutions, are crucial for economic growth. The complexity of social capability suggests that its impact on economic growth may vary depending on how these capabilities are institutionalized and implemented. This multifaceted process underscores the importance of institutional factors in shaping long-run economic growth.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by the interplay between colonial policies and the establishment of extractive institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) argue that European colonists adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions, such as those in the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. Extractive institutions, on the other hand, were designed to transfer resources rapidly to the metropole, as seen in colonies like the Congo and the Gold Coast (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) extend this argument by showing that the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced by colonialism, with geographic factors playing a lesser role. This institutional framework, shaped by politically powerful groups, often prioritized short-term gains over long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that the behaviour of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional factors in economic growth, as different historical contexts and paths lead to varied institutional outcomes.

The institutional framework also played a crucial role in the economic development of African colonies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They show that women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming, while men dominated cash crop production. This gendered division of labor was reinforced by the codification of customary law under colonial rule, which further marginalized women's economic contributions (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15). Austin (2007, p.20) provides a complementary perspective by arguing that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This revolution was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the loss of slaves and the transition from coerced to free labour (Austin 2007, p.4). These institutional changes highlight the dynamic nature of economic growth, shaped by both colonial policies and local responses to changing factor scarcities.
