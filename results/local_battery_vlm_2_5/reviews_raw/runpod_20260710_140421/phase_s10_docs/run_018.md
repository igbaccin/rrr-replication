The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, as it addresses the fundamental mechanisms behind sustained prosperity. The debate centers around whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or initial endowments, play a more significant role. One side argues that the establishment of inclusive institutions, which protect property rights and enforce the rule of law, is crucial for fostering economic development. Some scholars argue that geographic factors or initial conditions are more influential than institutions. The key question is whether institutions are merely a reflection of deeper structural factors or if they actively shape economic trajectories. This disagreement highlights the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic outcomes is a central theme in the literature on long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. argue that European colonization strategies significantly influenced the institutional frameworks established in colonies. In some cases, Europeans settled and created institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. In other instances, extractive states were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This variation in institutional frameworks had lasting effects on income per capita, highlighting the enduring impact of colonial-era institutions on economic development. Austin also engages with the institutional legacies of colonialism, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex historical paths of institutional evolution. Austin emphasizes that the behavior of older industrial economies today can be understood in the context of institutional evolution, investment, and policy experience going back to the original industrialization of Europe (Austin 2008, p.2). Austin's work underscores the importance of considering the historical context and the multifaceted nature of institutional change in understanding long-run economic growth.

Colonialism introduced institutional changes that significantly influenced long-run economic growth in former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. suggest that European colonizers created extractive institutions in regions with high mortality rates, aiming to maximize resource extraction. These institutions, characterized by weak property rights and limited investment incentives, persisted long after colonial rule ended, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. The extractive nature of these institutions is a direct result of the colonizers' priorities, which were focused on maximizing rents rather than fostering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional legacy is evident in the reversal of relative incomes among former colonies, where those with extractive institutions lagged behind those with more inclusive institutions. The interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). Acemoglu et al. also stress that extractive institutions were actively designed to serve the interests of the colonizers, not merely a result of geographic factors. This institutional design had profound implications for the economic growth of former colonies, as it limited the ability of these societies to develop and invest in productive activities. The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended highlights the enduring impact of colonialism on economic development.

Austin and other scholars focus on specific historical contexts in Africa, examining the role of institutional changes in factor markets rather than directly addressing long-run economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). They argue that shifts in factor markets can drive institutional innovation. This mechanism is evident in the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. The emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour was a direct response to changing economic conditions. Colonial administrations played a crucial role in this process, often altering their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This dynamic highlights the importance of local economic conditions and political actions in shaping institutional changes. For instance, Governor-General Clifford's decision to opt for a higher production equilibrium contradicts Nunn's hypothesis, illustrating how colonial administrations adapted to local economic incentives. Austin also observes that the transition from coerced to free labour, though significant, was not always a direct response to changing factor scarcities (Austin 2007, p.20). This nuanced perspective underscores that while institutional changes can be driven by economic factors, they are also influenced by broader political and social contexts. The integration of these colonies under British rule, which removed tariffs and administrative barriers to trade, further highlights the market-integrating effects of colonial administration (Austin 2007, p.4).

Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen (2009) both highlight the complexity of economic catch-up processes, noting that these processes are influenced by exogenous factors and social change, not solely by technological advancements. Allen (2009) further elaborates on this complexity by emphasizing the role of institutions in shaping economic growth. He argues that institutions, by influencing the allocation of resources and the adoption of technology, play a crucial role in determining a country's economic performance. This institutional influence is particularly evident in the context of factor accumulation and technological progress, where effective institutions can facilitate the efficient use of resources and the diffusion of new technologies. One specific mechanism through which institutions shape economic growth is by enhancing the efficiency of resource allocation. Institutions that promote property rights, contract enforcement, and market competition create an environment where resources are allocated more efficiently, leading to higher productivity and economic growth. For instance, countries with strong property rights and effective contract enforcement are better able to attract investment and foster innovation, which are key drivers of long-run economic growth (Abramovitz 1986, p.5). The impact of institutions on economic growth varies across countries. Allen (2009) qualifies this by noting that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on the specific historical and cultural context of each country. This contextual dependency means that the same institutional framework may have different outcomes in different countries, underscoring the importance of understanding the local factors that influence institutional effectiveness.

Allen (2001, p.8) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) both examine historical trends in real incomes across different regions, highlighting how economic leaders maintained their income levels while others experienced declines (Allen 2001, p.8; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This long-term perspective underscores how the maintenance of high real wages in leading economies, such as Britain, was crucial for their sustained economic growth. The gains in real wages from 1815 to 1850 were part of a cycle, not a trend, and the significant periods of real wage maintenance were decisive breaks with the past (Allen 2001, p.23). This stagnation underscores the importance of understanding the local factors that influence economic growth, as the vibrant and active labour market in early modern China did not translate into significant wage increases. Despite the integration of labour markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions, agricultural day labourers remained at the lower end of the income ladder (Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This historical context suggests that the institutional frameworks in these regions may have hindered the translation of economic activity into widespread wage improvements. The historical trends in real incomes highlight the complex interplay between economic activity and wage trends, suggesting that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth is contingent on various historical and cultural factors.

Women played a pivotal role in economic growth and development in Africa during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, a finding shared by Akyeampong and Fofack and Bohannan (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). Women were integral to subsistence economies, contributing significantly to agricultural production and trade. This marginalization, however, did not diminish their economic contributions. This underscores the economic agency of women despite institutional barriers. The introduction of money into subsistence economies, as documented by Bohannan, led to profound changes and a shift away from a multicentric economy (Bohannan 1959, p.10). This transition, while disruptive, also created new economic opportunities. The internal marketing system was perturbed by the introduction of alien institutions, but it also integrated African economies into the global market (Bohannan 1959, p.10). The transition was not smooth. Bohannan highlights that the equivalence between brass rods and other forms of exchange, such as rights in women or food, could not exist within the new economic framework (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This suggests that while economic transitions brought about by institutional changes could spur growth, they also created complexities that affected gender roles and economic dynamics.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a complex interplay of historical forces and economic dynamics. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.6) argue that the institutional frameworks established by European colonizers significantly influenced the economic trajectories of their colonies. These frameworks ranged from inclusive institutions that promoted investment and growth to extractive ones that prioritized resource extraction for the metropole. This variation in institutional design directly impacted long-run economic outcomes, with inclusive institutions fostering higher levels of economic development. Similarly, Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrates that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change and its impact on economic growth. Colonial policies that encouraged indigenous commodity production and fiscal security were pivotal in shaping economic outcomes, illustrating how institutional adaptations can drive long-run growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) underscore the critical role of women in economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging the marginalization of their contributions. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often relegated women to marginal lands, limiting their economic participation. This institutional change had profound effects on gender roles and economic dynamics, as women were systematically excluded from access to land for cash crops. Austin (2007, p.20; 2007, p.4) further elucidates how institutional changes in factor markets, such as the emergence of land use rights and hired labor markets, were driven by shifts in land abundance and scarcity. The transition from coerced to free labor, facilitated by legal actions like the 1874 British emancipation ordinance, had significant economic implications, highlighting how institutional reforms can reshape economic landscapes.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.19) emphasize that equilibrium institutions, even if extractive, are designed to maximize rents for politically powerful groups. This perspective is crucial for understanding the persistence of extractive institutions in many former colonies, where the primary goal was to extract resources. Austin (2008, p.2; 2008, p.15) contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic factors over time. The interplay between European settlers' presence, indigenous economic actions, and colonial policies highlights the multifaceted nature of institutional change and its impact on economic growth.

The underlying point of agreement among these sources is that institutions play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) show that the type of institutions established by European colonizers directly influenced economic outcomes. The remaining disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms and historical contexts that drive institutional change. Austin (2008, p.15) demonstrates that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the dynamic nature of institutional change. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of institutional adaptations in response to indigenous economic actions, as documented by Austin. This evidence shows that institutional changes driven by local economic dynamics were crucial in shaping long-run economic growth.
