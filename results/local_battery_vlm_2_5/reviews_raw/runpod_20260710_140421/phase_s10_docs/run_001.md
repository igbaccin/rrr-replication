The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development, with scholars debating the extent to which institutional frameworks drive or hinder sustained economic progress. The disagreement revolves around whether institutions are the primary determinant of economic growth or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural traits, play a more significant role. One stream of thought posits that institutions designed to enforce property rights and encourage investment are crucial for economic development. Another stream suggests that the impact of institutions is overstated, and that other factors, such as the actions of colonial powers or the historical context of economic development, are more influential. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if they are merely one piece of a complex puzzle. The influence of institutions on long-run economic growth is often discussed in the context of colonial legacies and their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that European settlers adopted different colonization strategies, leading to the establishment of either inclusive or extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, significantly affected the economic trajectories of the colonies. The extractive institutions imposed by Europeans in colonies with high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, while inclusive institutions in places like the United States, Australia, and New Zealand enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Austin questions whether the 'reversal of fortune' thesis fully captures the intricacies of historical economic development. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causal relationships by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.2). This suggests that the impact of institutions on economic growth may be more nuanced and context-dependent than previously thought.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism are key to understanding long-run economic growth, especially as they explain the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that European colonists established vastly different institutions depending on mortality rates, leading to either inclusive or extractive institutions. When mortality rates were high, Europeans set up extractive states to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole, whereas low mortality rates encouraged settlement and the establishment of institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound effects on long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. further emphasize that these institutions were shaped by politically powerful groups who were reluctant to initiate institutional change that did not directly benefit them. This institutional legacy persisted, significantly affecting income per capita in former colonies. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is directly linked to the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century. This institutional framework, influenced by population density and prosperity, played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6).

Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.2; Austin 2007, p.20). The institutional revolution was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions. The British emancipation ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 were pivotal in transitioning from coerced to free labour. The integration of contiguous colonies under British rule further enhanced market integration by removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within what would become Ghana (Austin 2007, p.4). Austin (2008, p.2) highlights that colonial administrations were responsive to indigenous economic and political actions, adjusting their institutional prescriptions to encourage production and ensure fiscal security (Austin 2008, p.15). This responsiveness shows that colonial institutions evolved in response to local economic dynamics, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic conditions.

Bolt and van Zanden, together with Allen et al., assert that the measurement of economic performance is a collaborative endeavor that relies on refining historical economic data (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.3; Allen et al. 2011, p.22). This variability is due to the partial data and assumptions underlying the estimates. Maddison's strategy of producing numbers even when a solid basis did not exist encouraged scholars to engage with his work and refine it, leading to a much more detailed overview of long-term economic growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) note that the existence of a vibrant labor market in early modern China allowed wage rates to serve as a proxy for the average earnings of specific socio-economic groups. The integration of labor markets between urban and rural areas in commercialized regions indicates a high degree of economic activity and market participation. Allen et al. (2011, p.24) advise that while wage rates can indicate economic conditions, they must be interpreted carefully, as they reflect only one aspect of economic performance. Economic data, whether historical or contemporary, must be treated with nuance to avoid oversimplification, as it forms the foundation for understanding long-run economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Bohannan (1959, p.10) highlight the significant contributions of women and the impact of monetary systems on African economic changes. The contributions of African women to economic growth have been substantial, particularly in agricultural sectors, where they were instrumental in food farming and cash crop production. This role was reinforced by patriarchal alliances and customary law under colonial rule, which codified the subordinate status of women in access to land. The introduction of money into African subsistence economies has led to profound changes, shifting away from a multicentric economy and integrating local economic institutions with the world economy. This shift was facilitated by the introduction of alien institutions, such as European courts and native courts, which altered the economic landscape. The transition was not smooth, as the introduction of brass rods as a general-purpose currency within the prestige sphere demonstrated. Brass rods fulfilled only one function of money: payment, and could not equate to rights in women or food. This illustrates the complex interplay between social structures and economic systems, where the introduction of new monetary systems coexisted with existing economic institutions. This coexistence underscores the intricate nature of economic changes driven by social and monetary factors, where the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth remains multifaceted and dynamic.

Technological advance is a pivotal factor in economic divergence, as Allen (2001, p.8) and Abramovitz (1986, p.21) (Allen 2001, p.8; Abramovitz 1986, p.21) both highlight. Allen (2001, p.8) identifies the period from 1500 to 1750 as crucial for the divergence in real incomes, noting that while incomes fell in most European cities, economic leaders maintained theirs. This period saw significant technological changes that likely contributed to the maintenance of high real wages in leading economies. He posits that technological progress and social capability are key drivers of economic growth. This social capability includes technical competence and the organization and management of large-scale enterprise, which are essential for mobilizing capital and fostering economic development. The implication is that technological advancements, coupled with robust social capabilities, can drive long-run economic growth. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that measuring social capability is challenging, suggesting that while technological advance is crucial, its effects are intricately tied to broader social and institutional factors. This qualifies the straightforward narrative of technological progress, indicating that while it is a key driver, its impact is moderated by the institutional and social context in which it occurs. Therefore, the role of technological advance in shaping long-run economic growth is significant but multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of its interplay with social and institutional factors.

Institutions, particularly those shaped by colonial legacies, play a pivotal role in determining long-run economic growth. This is evident in the historical context of European colonization, where different strategies led to varied institutional outcomes. In colonies where European settlers established themselves, institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment were implemented, fostering long-term economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). In regions where extractive states were established, the primary goal was to transfer resources back to the metropole, which hindered economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). The persistence of these institutions, shaped by initial colonial strategies, continues to influence income per capita today (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27). This dynamic underscores the enduring impact of institutional frameworks on economic trajectories.

The role of institutions in economic growth is further illuminated by the complex interplay between technological advance and social factors. Technological progress, while crucial, is not a standalone driver of growth. Instead, its effects are moderated by the broader institutional and social context in which it occurs. This interaction highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, where technological advancements must align with supportive institutions to drive sustained growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.19). The historical evidence shows that institutions designed to maximize rents for European colonists, have had lasting effects on the economic development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33).

The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule have had profound and lasting effects on economic development in Africa. The codification of customary law under colonial rule often reinforced the subordinate status of women, limiting their access to land and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.20). This institutional bias against women has had enduring economic consequences, as women's contributions to economic growth were significantly marginalized. The historical evidence shows that women played a crucial role in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the need to recognize their contributions and the institutional barriers they faced (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15).

Colonial institutions, shaped by the interplay between European strategies and local conditions, have left a lasting imprint on economic development trajectories. The institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, whether extractive or growth-promoting, continue to influence economic outcomes today. The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is closely tied to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). This underscores the critical role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of their complex interplay with social and technological factors.
