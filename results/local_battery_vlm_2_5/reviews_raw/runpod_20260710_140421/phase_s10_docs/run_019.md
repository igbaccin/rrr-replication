The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been a central theme in economic history and development economics, with scholars debating the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between those who emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions and those who argue for a more nuanced understanding of institutional change over time. The former position posits that institutions established during colonial periods have lasting effects on economic development, while the latter contends that institutional evolution is a complex process influenced by various historical and contextual factors. This debate is crucial for understanding the pathways to economic growth and development, as it informs policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic progress. The interplay between institutions and economic growth is evident in the historical experiences of former colonies, where the legacies of colonial rule continue to shape economic outcomes. This critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutions evolve and interact with economic growth over time.
