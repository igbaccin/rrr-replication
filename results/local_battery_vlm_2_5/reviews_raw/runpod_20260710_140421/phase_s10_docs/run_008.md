The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a pivotal question in economic development. Institutions, understood as the rules of the game that structure economic interactions, can either facilitate or hinder growth. One live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that the type of institutions established during colonialism, particularly those that were extractive, have had lasting effects on economic development. Others emphasize the importance of local factors and the historical context in which institutions operate, suggesting that the impact of institutions on growth is not uniform across different settings. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography, culture, or local economic conditions, play a more significant role. This question is intellectually significant because it informs policies aimed at fostering economic development in regions that have historically lagged behind. The nature of institutions established during colonialism, particularly their extractive or inclusive nature, has been a focal point in understanding long-run economic growth. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) report, “In one extreme, as in the case of the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, they went and settled in the colonies and set up institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment.” Austin (2008, p.2) reports, “Despite this decline, most economists have little difﬁculty in seeing the behaviour of the older industrial economies today in the context of patterns and paths of institutional evolution, investment and policy experience going back at least to the original industrialisation of Europe, its overseas 'off-shoots', and Japan.”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2001, p.27) argue that colonialism introduced institutional changes that fundamentally shaped long-run economic growth in former colonies. These changes varied based on European settlement patterns and mortality rates. In colonies where Europeans faced high mortality rates, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, extractive institutions were established to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole. These institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth. In contrast, in colonies with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This divergence in institutional frameworks had profound implications for economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to these institutional differences. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6) note that the interaction between these institutions and the opportunity to industrialize during the nineteenth century played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies. This interaction highlights the complex interplay between institutions and economic opportunities in shaping long-run economic growth.

Austin (2008, p.2) and Austin (2008, p.2; 2007, p.20) share a finding that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional shift was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, particularly the increasing scarcity of land. The Gold Coast ordinance of 1874 and the subsequent prohibition of slavery and pawning in Ashanti in 1908 played crucial roles in this transition. These legal actions, while not directly induced by institutional innovation, responded to the changing factor scarcities and facilitated the shift from coerced to free labour. This transition had significant economic implications, as it allowed for more efficient use of labour and land resources. Austin (2007, p.4) notes that the imposition and extension of British rule had important market-integrating effects, removing tariffs and administrative barriers to trade within the region. This integration further supported the development of factor markets and the emergence of new institutional arrangements. However, Austin (2008, p.15) qualifies this by noting that colonial administrations often adjusted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial policies and local economic dynamics. This interplay suggests that the institutional revolution in factor markets was not solely driven by colonial policies but was also shaped by local responses to economic changes.

Institutional frameworks, therefore, evolve in response to these drivers. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) and Allen et al. (2011, p.22) share the view that these factors influence long-term economic outcomes through a complex interplay of technological progress and social dynamics. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) notes that a country's social capability, which encompasses technical competence and institutional frameworks, plays a pivotal role in economic growth. This capability is not merely about educational attainment but also includes the organization and management of large-scale enterprises and the mobilization of capital. Abramovitz (1986, p.21) further elaborates on the importance of financial institutions and markets capable of supporting large-scale enterprises, highlighting that these elements are crucial for economic development. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) provide empirical evidence from China, showing that wage rates can serve as a reasonable proxy for the average earnings and marginal productivity of labor. This finding underscores the importance of labor market conditions and regional migration in economic growth. Allen et al. (2011, p.22) also reveal that in early modern China, there was a vibrant and active labor market, particularly for short-term or day labor. This market integration suggests that wage rates reflect the broader economic conditions and productivity levels. This qualification highlights the nuanced role of labor market dynamics in economic growth, showing that while short-term labor markets can drive productivity, long-term employment stability is also crucial for sustained economic development. The interplay between technological advancement, social changes, and institutional frameworks thus shapes long-term economic growth by influencing productivity and living standards.

The introduction of money and the role of women in economic activity in Africa highlight a distinct facet of economic changes that do not directly address institutions as the fundamental cause of long-run economic growth, but rather focus on specific economic changes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.15; Bohannan 1959, p.10). This underscores the significance of women's economic contributions, which were often overlooked in broader narratives of economic development. The new road network and increased trade facilitated by peace and infrastructure development were pivotal in this transition (Bohannan 1959, p.10). However, the introduction of money also brought challenges, such as the inability of brass rods to fulfill all functions of money within the prestige sphere (Bohannan 1959, p.9). This qualification highlights that while the introduction of money drove economic changes, it also presented complexities that required adaptation. This suggests that economic changes due to the introduction of money and women's role in Africa were significant drivers of economic growth, but they also required specific adaptations and faced unique challenges.

Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in refining historical economic data. The Maddison Project, which aims to measure economic performance across the world economy, exemplifies this collaborative approach. By producing estimates even when data is scarce, Maddison encouraged continuous scholarly engagement and debate. This strategy has led to more detailed and accurate long-term economic growth overviews than initially available. The subjective nature of these estimates raises questions about their objectivity. As the Maddison Project continues to evolve, the collaborative nature of its work remains crucial for advancing our understanding of economic performance.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth has been extensively studied, with scholars emphasizing the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes. Acemoglu and colleagues (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6) show that Europeans established different institutions in their colonies, ranging from inclusive to extractive, based on mortality rates and settlement patterns. These institutional differences significantly influenced income per capita in the long run. The extractive institutions, designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than promote long-run growth, persisted and shaped the economic trajectories of these colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin (2008, p.15) (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2) builds on this by highlighting that colonial administrations adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions, demonstrating the dynamic nature of institutional change. This interaction between colonial policies and local responses underscores the complex interplay between institutions and economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic growth is further illuminated by the role of women in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15; 2014, p.20) argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during these periods, often marginalized in traditional narratives. The codification of customary law under colonial rule reinforced the subordinate status of women, affecting their access to land and economic opportunities. This institutional framework, which marginalized women, had profound effects on the economic development of African societies. Austin (2007, p.4; 2007, p.20) similarly shows how the transition from coerced to free labour in Ghana during the colonial period had significant economic dimensions, highlighting the institutional changes that occurred in response to shifting factor scarcities. The emergence of markets in land use rights and hired labour marked an institutional revolution that reshaped the economic landscape of the region.

The role of institutions in economic growth is also evident in the collaborative efforts to measure and understand long-term economic performance. Bolt and van Zanden (2014, p.3; 2014, p.2) emphasize the importance of collaborative research in continuing and improving Angus Maddison's work on measuring economic performance. The Maddison Project's collaborative nature has advanced our understanding of economic performance by producing detailed overviews of long-term economic growth. The subjective nature of these estimates raises questions about their objectivity. Feinstein and Thomas proposed methods to estimate margins of error in historical economic data, highlighting the challenges in achieving precise measurements. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.19) show that the results of institutional studies remain robust across various measures of institutions, providing a consistent framework for understanding the impact of institutions on economic growth. The institutional framework, shaped by colonial policies and local responses, continues to influence economic outcomes in the long run. The precise remaining disagreement between Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27; 2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.15) (Bolt and van Zanden 2014, p.2) on the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is settled by the specific historical evidence of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies. This reversal, documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.6), shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a central role in the long-run development of these colonies.
