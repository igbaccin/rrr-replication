The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic historiography. The debate centres on whether colonial institutions, characterised by their extractive nature, have persistently hindered economic development or whether other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural legacies, play a more significant role. Acemoglu and his colleagues argue that colonial powers imposed extractive institutions in areas where they faced high mortality rates, leading to persistent underdevelopment. This perspective is challenged by those who suggest that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that the reversal of fortune in African economic growth is due to a complex interplay of factors over extended periods. Another strand of scholarship highlights the enduring effects of the slave trade, which fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to impede economic progress. The interplay between these positions reveals the complexity of African economic trajectories and the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and institutional factors at play. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. Their analysis reveals that extractive institutions, which were established by European colonizers to exploit local resources, have persisted long after independence. These institutions, characterized by forced labor and resource extraction, were particularly profitable in densely populated regions, where Europeans could exploit abundant labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional framework significantly impeded the development of human capital, as masses typically did not obtain education under such extractive systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era has had profound implications for economic development in Africa. While this institutional legacy has been detrimental to African economies, it underscores the importance of institutional reform in shaping future economic trajectories. Understanding this historical context is crucial for designing policies that can address the structural challenges inherited from colonial rule.

Austin (2008, p.1) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16) This compression obscures the nuanced interplay between historical contexts and economic outcomes, suggesting that the path dependence of institutional change is more complex than previously acknowledged. Austin (2008, p.1) draws on African and comparative economic historiography to illustrate that growth theories must be examined against long-term history. This approach reveals relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence, identifying a single, overarching causal mechanism. Austin (2007, p.16) provides a concrete example of this complexity in Ghana during 1874-1939, where the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by economic pressures and institutional adaptations. The institutional changes in Ghana were not uniform or straightforward. For instance, the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs implemented a ban on new farms in 1939 due to land scarcity, which was later abandoned in 1946. This policy shift highlights the dynamic nature of institutional change and the importance of local context in shaping economic trajectories. These findings imply that institutional change in Africa is a complex interplay of historical, economic, and institutional factors. This nuanced understanding of institutional change is crucial for designing effective policies that can address the structural challenges inherited from colonial rule.

The slave trade had a profound negative impact on African economic development, offering an alternative explanation to colonial institutions as the primary driver of Africa's economic trajectory (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and impedes economic growth. This mistrust acts as a barrier to cooperation and collective action, which are essential for economic development. The internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is particularly detrimental, being at least twice as impactful as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust hinders the development of social capital, which is crucial for economic growth. The adverse effects of the slave trade extend beyond social capital. The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, continues to shape African economic trajectories in profound ways, underscoring the need for policies that address both the historical and contemporary challenges stemming from this dark chapter of history.

British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). The British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, leading to higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational investment facilitated the development of a skilled workforce, which in turn fostered economic growth. The difference in educational trajectories between British and French colonies highlights the role of colonial institutions in shaping long-term economic development. British rule promoted education, yet the legacy of colonial education systems varied across Africa. Differences in income inequality, such as those between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans in Kenya, underscore the complexity of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The varying degrees of income inequality suggest that the impact of colonial education systems on economic growth was mediated by other factors, including the distribution of wealth and power. This implies that while education is a crucial factor, its effects on economic growth are contingent on broader socio-economic conditions.

Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11) argue that the economic history of Africa offers valuable insights into the institutional reforms that can alleviate poverty among the continent's most impoverished populations. This perspective is grounded in the idea that understanding the historical context of African economic development can inform contemporary policy decisions. One key mechanism through which colonial institutions influenced economic growth is the shift from mercantilist to modern forms of imperialism. Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that by the late nineteenth century, medical advances facilitated European settlement, altering colonial policies toward Africa. This shift allowed for the establishment of both productive and extractive institutions, depending on the specific historical and geographical context. The establishment of these institutions had profound effects on the economic trajectories of African nations. Hopkins (2009, p.13) acknowledges that the time colonial rulers had to implement these changes varied, and in many cases, they did not have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions. This temporal constraint is crucial because it highlights the complexity of institutional change and the enduring impact of historical legacies on contemporary economic conditions. For instance, the persistence of unproductive institutions can create a path-dependent trajectory that limits economic growth. The deterministic view of path dependence risks ignoring the dynamic influences that shape economic development over time. The nuanced understanding of colonial institutions' role in shaping African economic growth trajectories underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach to institutional reform.

Bryceson (2002, p.11) and Peters (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Peters 2004, p.19) both highlight that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to profound social changes and depeasantization, as well as the emergence of new economic dynamics and social formations. These shifts are economic and social, involving changes in land tenure, labor relations, and household structures. Bryceson (2002, p.5) illustrates this with the example of Ethiopian households becoming net buyers of grain, indicating a shift from subsistence agriculture to market dependence. This transformation is driven by factors such as declining yields and recurrent food shortages, which push rural populations towards nonagricultural activities for survival. Peters (2004, p.14) further elaborates on this by noting that the diversification of livelihoods, while often seen as a survival strategy, also reflects broader structural changes that fragment traditional social structures. These changes encompass economic shifts and social and political dimensions, such as democratization and good governance, which are crucial for sustainable development. Peters (2004, p.19) cautions that these transformations are not always positive, as they can also lead to the exclusion of certain groups and the formation of new class structures. The role of colonial institutions in shaping these trajectories is complex, as they interact with contemporary economic and social changes in ways that are not always straightforward.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing enduring structures that persist long after independence. These institutions, often extractive in nature, were designed to maximize resource extraction for the benefit of colonial powers. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas, leading to their establishment in regions with high population density (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This profit-driven approach resulted in the creation of systems that exploited local labor and resources, such as forced labor systems and slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations, as seen in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical continuity underscores the long-term impact of colonial institutions on economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth is further complicated by their interaction with contemporary social and political changes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes Africa's economic decline to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the causal relationships involved (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions interact with other historical factors to influence economic outcomes. For instance, the slave trade depleted Africa's human capital and fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, has significant implications for economic development, as it affects social cohesion, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is multifaceted, encompassing economic factors and social and political dimensions.

The interaction between colonial institutions and contemporary changes in Africa is complex and multifaceted. Colonial institutions have left a lasting imprint on African societies, shaping their economic, social, and political landscapes in profound ways. The persistence of extractive institutions, such as those established during the colonial era, has hindered economic growth and development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The impact of colonial institutions varies across Africa. Different regions have experienced varying degrees of institutional persistence and economic change, reflecting the diverse historical experiences of African countries. For example, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America was conducive to the establishment of forced labor systems, while the relatively high population density in Africa created a profit opportunity for slave traders in supplying labor to American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This shows the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories. The historical evidence of the persistence of extractive institutions into the independence era in Latin America, as well as the contemporary impact of mistrust on economic development, settles the disagreement about the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories.
