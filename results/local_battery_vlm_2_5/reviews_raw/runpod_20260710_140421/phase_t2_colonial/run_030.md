The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. The issue is the degree to which colonial legacies, particularly the institutions established by colonial powers, still affect current economic outcomes. The live disagreement centers around whether colonial institutions are primarily extractive and thus detrimental to long-term economic development, or whether their impact is more nuanced and context-dependent. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive in nature, have left a lasting negative imprint on African economies. Others contend that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is more complex, involving multiple historical periods and paths that cannot be simplified into a single causal narrative. This review will explore these competing perspectives, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which colonial institutions have shaped African economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have been crucial in shaping the economic trajectories of African countries. The profitability of extractive institutions, which were more likely to be implemented in areas with high population density, significantly influenced the economic policies of European colonizers. For example, the presence of abundant labor in Meso-America led to forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa facilitated the slave trade (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional framework persisted post-colonialism, as extractive state institutions remained intact after independence, such as the monopolies and regulations created by Spain in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these extractive institutions has had lasting effects on human capital development, with low levels of education being a primary mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights the enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth, as the extractive policies implemented during colonial rule continued to shape economic policies long after independence.

Austin (2008, p.1) critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This critique is supported by Austin (2007, p.16), who shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, a trend that was not captured by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. The institutional changes in Ghana illustrate how localised scarcity can drive economic growth by fostering market development. This mechanism highlights the importance of examining long-term historical contexts to understand the complex interplay between institutions and economic growth. Austin (2007, p.16) also observes that policy responses to land scarcity were not always effective or sustainable. The ban on creating new farms in Ashanti, for instance, was excessive and largely ineffective, leading to its abandonment in 1946 (Austin 2007, p.16). This qualification underscores the need for nuanced policy interventions that consider the specific historical and contextual factors at play. The institutional changes in Ghana during this period also illustrate how infrastructure development, such as the expansion of motorable roads and the mechanisation of goods transport, can complement institutional reforms to drive economic growth (Austin 2007, p.8).

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Akyeampong and Fofack, highlight the underappreciated economic contributions of African economies during the colonial period, particularly the role of African women (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Broadberry and Gardner estimate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges the conventional view of African economies as stagnant or declining during colonial rule. The role of African women in economic growth and development is also significant, as they contributed to production and reproduction during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and African chiefs systematically disadvantaged women, but even so, women remained central to economic activities. This suggests that the economic performance of African economies during the colonial period was more nuanced and dynamic than often portrayed, with significant contributions from both men and women. The institutional changes that systematically disadvantaged women could be seen as a downside of colonial rule, but it also underscores the resilience and adaptability of African economies and societies. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) extend this idea by emphasizing the varying degrees of income inequality and different economic dynamics that shaped long-term growth trajectories in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) point out that the East-West gap in economic development is due to commercialization and varying levels of income inequality among different groups, such as European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. This nuanced view of economic development suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions is more complex than simply extractive or productive; it includes the interplay of economic dynamics and social structures that have persisted over time. Social changes and economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa have significantly reshaped rural livelihoods and economic dynamics. Peters (2004, p.19) and Bryceson (2002, p.11) share a finding that depeasantization processes are central to these transformations, as rural populations increasingly engage in nonagricultural activities. This shift is driven by the commodification of land and labor relations, which fragment traditional family and kin-based groups, as well as by the growing importance of casual wage labor markets. Economic restructuring has led to a decline in agricultural production for many households, as they diversify their income sources to cope with impoverishment and food insecurity. Peters (2004, p.14) observes that this diversification is a structural transformation that produces conflict between generations and genders. This transformation has implications for the broader economic landscape, as households become more reliant on market transactions and less dependent on state institutions. Bryceson (2002, p.5) qualifies this by highlighting that economic restructuring has also led to increased food aid dependence and recurring food shortages, particularly in semi-arid climates with declining yields. This underscores the complex interplay between economic shifts and social dynamics in shaping African economic growth trajectories.
