The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating whether colonial institutions were extractive and thus detrimental to economic development, or whether other factors such as geography, culture, or postcolonial policies played a more significant role. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of whether colonial institutions were a primary driver of Africa's economic underdevelopment or if they were merely one of many factors. Some scholars argue that extractive colonial institutions, characterized by exploitative policies and lack of investment in local economies, have had a lasting negative impact on African economic growth. Others contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, suggesting that precolonial factors, such as state development and trade institutions, were more influential in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. This debate is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonialism and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic development in Africa. The persistence of these institutions and their impact on current economic conditions underscores the need to examine the historical roots of Africa's economic challenges. The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a complex issue that requires a nuanced understanding of both the historical context and the enduring effects of these institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. They demonstrate that extractive institutions, which prioritize the interests of the colonizers over the local population, were more likely to be established in areas where they were economically profitable. This is exemplified by the establishment of forced labor systems in Meso-America and slave trading in Africa, both of which were driven by the availability of labor and the potential for profit (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions long after colonial rule ended has had a profound impact on the economic development of African countries. This institutional persistence is evident in the continuation of extractive policies, such as monopolies and regulations, which hampered economic growth and development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) note that low levels of human capital, a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization, also played a significant role. This suggests that while colonial institutions were a key factor, they were not the sole determinant of economic growth trajectories in Africa.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). He contends that the causal relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al.'s formulations recognise. Austin highlights that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.8). Land scarcity in Ghana was evident by the end of the 1930s in the older cocoa-growing areas. This scarcity was so pronounced that the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs banned the creation of new farms in 1939, despite their dependence on cocoa income (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional change in Ghana suggests that localised scarcity and the resulting institutional adaptations played a significant role in shaping economic trajectories. The policy was abandoned in 1946, indicating that implementation of such policies was influenced by various factors. This nuanced view of institutional change in response to localised scarcity contrasts with the broader, more uniform causal mechanisms proposed by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis. Austin's analysis underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence. This detailed examination of institutional changes in response to localised scarcity in Ghana highlights the complexity of economic growth trajectories in Africa.

The slave trade had a profound impact on African economic development, creating lasting institutional and cultural legacies that continue to affect growth trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon identify a culture of mistrust as a significant legacy of the slave trade, which persists today and contributes to reduced levels of trust within African societies. This legacy is a major factor in the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. Nunn and Wantchekon's findings align with broader evidence on the importance of trust for economic development. Nunn also notes that the slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, challenging the notion that Africa's underdevelopment can be attributed solely to colonialism or more recent events. This perspective highlights the deep historical roots of Africa's economic challenges, tracing them back to the precolonial period and the impact of the slave trades. The adverse effects of the slave trades are evident in the selection processes during early trade between Africans and Europeans, which favoured societies with sufficiently developed institutions to facilitate commerce. This institutional development was crucial for economic success, as evidenced by the role of precolonial state institutions in providing public goods such as education, health, and infrastructure. The legacy of the slave trade includes cultural shifts and institutional underdevelopment, which continues to shape African economic trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11) argue that the economic history of Africa offers valuable insights into institutional reforms, although they do not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories. A key mechanism that Hopkins (2009, p.12) identifies is the distinction between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. During the mercantilist phase, productive institutions were scarce even in Europe, making them unavailable for export to Africa. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had made European settlement more feasible. This shift allowed for a greater influence of colonial policy on African institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.12) suggests that colonial rulers did not have sufficient time to dismantle the unproductive institutions already in place, which implies that the legacy of these institutions persisted into the post-colonial era. This perspective is supported by Hopkins (2019, p.11), who highlights the challenges in using instrumental variables and the pitfalls of path dependence. This complexity is further underscored by the historical creation of poverty, which was a result of European capitalism drawing other societies into networks of dependence and exploiting them through unequal exchange. This historical context is crucial for understanding the enduring effects of colonial institutions on African economic growth.

Frankema and other scholars found that British colonial rule was more favorable to the development of education systems in Africa than other colonial powers, with British policies promoting missionary education and indirect rule (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). British colonial policies, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations, fostered a more extensive educational infrastructure. This approach stood in stark contrast to French policies, which restricted missionary activities, leading to significantly lower primary-school enrolment rates in French West Africa compared to British West Africa by 1938 (Frankema 2012, p.4). The divergence in educational development trajectories was further exacerbated by the differing governmental expenses per student, with French colonies spending considerably more but achieving less educational output. This pattern suggests that the type of colonial rule significantly influenced the educational landscape, with British policies creating more favorable conditions for educational development. The extent to which these educational developments translated into long-term economic growth remains a subject of debate. Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is complex, with varying degrees of income inequality and differing economic dynamics playing crucial roles (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This complexity underscores the need for future research to delve deeper into the long-term growth trajectories and the specific mechanisms through which colonial educational policies influenced economic outcomes.

Ideas and ideologies were pivotal in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, as McCloskey and Bryant both argue (Bryant 2006, p.17; McCloskey 2015, p.9). McCloskey's account of France and Spain illustrates how the persistence of conservative ideologies, such as a rigid form of Catholicism and aristocratic values, impeded economic growth by prioritizing cultural and political stability over economic development. In Spain, for example, these conservative ideologies throttled economic growth, despite the examples set by the Dutch and British. This was a pattern that persisted until very recently, even as France and Spain eventually began to adopt a new rhetoric that fostered economic growth. Bryant further elaborates on this idea by highlighting the role of patrimonial systems of domination in the East, which were characterized by hierarchical social structures that limited economic progress. These systems were often opposed to the values of wealthy merchants, making it difficult for a predominantly mercantile order of society to arise. This ideological and institutional framework helps explain why economic growth was stifled in these regions. Bryant's discussion of Goody's work qualifies this pattern by noting that while Goody acknowledges the existence of patrimonial systems, he argues that these systems are causally inconsequential. This suggests that the impact of ideologies on economic growth may be more complex than initially thought, with various factors contributing to the economic trajectories of different regions. The interplay of ideas, ideologies, and institutions in shaping economic growth trajectories in Europe underscores the importance of considering similar factors in the African context, where colonial institutions and ideologies may have had profound effects on economic development.

Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions established during colonial rule persisting long after independence. The economic profitability of alternative policies, such as extractive institutions, was a key factor in their implementation. High population density, for example, made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical evidence underscores the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development, as the control structures set up during colonialism often remained in place even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The persistence of extractive institutions is further highlighted by the long-term effects of the slave trade on African economies. The slave trade depleted human capital and fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which continues to affect economic development today (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust has been identified as a significant barrier to economic growth, as it hinders the development of social capital and cooperative behavior essential for economic progress. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are evident in the underdevelopment of African economies, which can be traced back to the colonial era and the institutions established during that period.

While the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is well-documented, the causal relationships involved are more complex than initially thought. The reversal of fortune thesis, which attributes Africa's underdevelopment to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the historical context by compressing different periods and paths into a single narrative. A more nuanced understanding of African economic growth trajectories requires examining the long-term history and recognizing the differentiated causal relationships at play (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This approach reveals that the situations are similar due to path dependence as well as recurring relationships, emphasizing the need for a more comprehensive analysis of African economic history.

The new economic history of Africa provides valuable insights into the role of colonial institutions in shaping economic growth trajectories. This history can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.13). By understanding the historical context and the specific institutions established during colonial rule, policymakers can develop targeted interventions to address the persistent effects of extractive institutions. The historical evidence shows that colonialism entrenched extractive regimes, which have had long-lasting effects on African economies. The persistence of these institutions into the independence era highlights the need for institutional reforms that address the root causes of economic underdevelopment in Africa.
