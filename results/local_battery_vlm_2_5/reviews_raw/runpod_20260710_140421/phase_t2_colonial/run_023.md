The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the extent to which colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic development. Two prominent positions exist in this debate. The first argues that colonial institutions, particularly extractive ones, have had a lasting negative impact on economic growth in Africa. The second position challenges this view, asserting that the impact of colonial institutions has been oversimplified and that other factors, such as path dependence and long-term historical contexts, play significant roles. The live disagreement centers on whether colonial institutions are the primary determinant of Africa's economic trajectory or if other historical and contextual factors are equally, if not more, significant. This extractive nature, however, was not uniform across all colonial powers and territories. Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism were a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Extractive institutions, such as forced labor systems and monopolies, were often established in areas with high population density or abundant resources, as these institutions were more profitable for European colonizers. For example, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated the establishment of forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa created opportunities for slave traders to supply labor to American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This extractive approach was not limited to labor; other types of extractive institutions were also more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. The persistence of these extractive institutions long after colonial rule ended had profound effects on economic development. Acemoglu et al. note that the control structures set up in nonsettler colonies during the colonial era persisted, significantly affecting current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers, which remained intact for much of the nineteenth century in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical legacy of extractive institutions has had lasting implications for economic growth, as these institutions delayed industrialization by limiting education and human capital development in affected regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Austin argues that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This transformation was driven by the increasing demand for cocoa, which necessitated more intensive land use and labour practices. These institutional changes were part of a broader trend of mechanisation and infrastructure development in Ghana. Austin (2008, p.1) highlights that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This nuanced view of institutional change in African economies underscores the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts and the differentiated causal relationships that shaped economic growth trajectories.

British rule, which was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15), promoted education as a means to support their indirect rule system. The British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to aid in administration and governance, leading to higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational focus under British rule had a significant impact on the economic trajectories of these regions, as formal education facilitated the development of human capital. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) support this view, highlighting that Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that the educational policies implemented under British rule contributed to economic growth by fostering a skilled workforce. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also note that improving long-run performance in Africa will require a reduction in the rate and frequency of economic shrinking, a challenge that persists despite the educational advancements (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17).

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) jointly establish that the broader economic history of Africa reveals that the continent's development has been influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including the evolving nature of colonial institutions and external trade dynamics. Hopkins highlights that the nature of colonialism in Africa evolved significantly over time, transitioning from mercantilist to more modern forms of imperialism by the late nineteenth century (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This shift was marked by medical advances that facilitated European settlement, altering colonial policies towards Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg demonstrate that African poverty is a result of varying degrees of income inequality and different types of economic dynamics (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This perspective challenges the notion that African economic development has been uniformly hampered by colonial institutions, suggesting instead that the impact of colonialism has been more nuanced and varied. The specific mechanisms through which these factors influenced economic growth trajectories remain complex. For instance, the impact of colonialism on African economies has been relatively small due to the short period of effective European occupation, contrasting with nearly five centuries of precolonial slave trading (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This nuanced view of colonialism's impact on African economic development underscores the need for further research into the specific mechanisms through which different factors have influenced Africa's economic growth trajectories.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that institutions governing public choice and secure property rights were crucial for fostering economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The development of the English financial revolution, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework was pivotal in facilitating long-term economic growth. North (1989, p.3) highlights that the complexity of institutions evolves to handle increasing interdependence, suggesting that institutional development is not automatic but contingent on addressing challenges like opportunism and cheating. This implies that the effectiveness of institutions in promoting economic growth depends on their ability to adapt to and mitigate these issues. However, the specific mechanisms through which these institutions influenced economic growth in Europe are not directly translatable to Africa, where colonial institutions often failed to provide secure property rights and foster economic growth.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). The slave trade's legacy of mistrust is rooted in the exploitative and violent nature of the transatlantic slave trade, which created an environment where individuals were incentivized to betray and exploit each other. This mistrust, in turn, undermines the development of social capital, which is crucial for economic growth. Trust facilitates cooperation, reduces transaction costs, and fosters a stable environment conducive to economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). However, the historical evidence suggests that the slave trade also affected economic outcomes through a more direct channel, namely the selection of societies that were able to engage in trade with Europeans. During the early phases of the transatlantic slave trade, only societies with sufficiently developed institutions could facilitate trade with the Europeans. This selection process meant that societies with better institutions were more likely to be involved in the slave trade, which had adverse effects on their economic development (Nunn 2008, p.19). This selection effect highlights the complex interplay between institutions, the slave trade, and economic development in Africa.

Colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with their impact persisting long after the colonial era ended. They facilitated the establishment of extractive institutions, which were particularly profitable in densely populated areas. This is evident in the forced labor systems in Meso-America and the slave trade in Africa, where high population density created profit opportunities for Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is well-documented, with examples such as the monopolies and regulations created by Spain in Latin America remaining intact for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional continuity highlights the enduring influence of colonial policies on economic development.
