Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping the economic trajectories of African countries, a question that has sparked significant debate among scholars. The central intellectual stakes revolve around understanding the mechanisms through which colonial legacies have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. One key position argues that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive in nature, have had a lasting negative impact on economic growth. This perspective posits that these institutions were designed to benefit colonial powers at the expense of local populations, leading to persistent underdevelopment. Another position suggests that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced and context-dependent, with different historical periods and paths influencing current economic conditions. This debate highlights the importance of examining the specific institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers and their enduring effects on African economies. The question of whether colonial institutions have uniformly stunted growth or if their impact varies based on historical and contextual factors remains a contentious issue. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for policating effective policies aimed at fostering economic development in Africa. Acemoglu et al. and Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The profitability of extractive institutions, which were often implemented in areas with high population density, played a crucial role in this dynamic. Acemoglu et al. report that "when extractive institutions were more profitable, Europeans were more likely to opt for them" (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This suggests that the economic incentives faced by colonial powers heavily influenced the type of institutions they established. For example, in regions with abundant labor, such as Meso-America or densely populated areas in Africa, extractive institutions were particularly profitable for European colonists. Acemoglu et al. further note that "in societies with extractive institutions the masses typically did not or could not obtain education" (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of education, a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization, highlights the long-term impact of colonial institutions on human capital development. This persistence underscores the enduring legacy of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) jointly establish that the economic history of Africa, including the impact of colonial institutions, must be understood within a broader historical context. This evolution is crucial for understanding the trajectory of African economic growth, as it highlights that the impact of colonial institutions was not uniform across time. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) support this by noting that African poverty results from complex interactions between historical and structural factors. They argue that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development has been overstated. This nuanced view is important because it suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, other factors such as precolonial conditions and post-colonial policies also significantly influenced economic growth trajectories. For example, the varying degrees of income inequality between different groups in Kenya highlight the need to consider multiple factors when examining African economic development. This points to the importance of a holistic approach that considers both historical and contemporary factors.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16; 2008, p.1); (Austin 2007, p.16) examine historical economic changes and institutional shifts in Africa, focusing on specific periods and regions without directly linking these to colonial institutions or African economic growth trajectories. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional shift was driven by population growth and the increasing demand for cocoa, which created new economic opportunities and necessitated changes in land use and labour practices. The development of infrastructure, such as roads and motorable paths, further facilitated this institutional revolution by improving transportation networks and reducing the cost of transporting goods. This infrastructural development was a critical enabler of economic growth, as it allowed for the efficient movement of goods and people, thereby fostering trade and commerce (Austin 2007, p.8). This institutional revolution faced challenges. The ban on creating new farms in 1939, for example, highlights the complexities and tensions that arose as a result of these institutional shifts. The ban was a response to land scarcity and the need to manage resources sustainably, but it also had significant economic implications, including the potential to disrupt cocoa production and income. This example underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the economic and institutional changes that occurred in Africa during this period, as well as the complex interplay between economic growth, institutional shifts, and resource management. The institutional revolution in factor markets and the development of infrastructure were key drivers of economic growth in Ghana during this period, but they also highlight the challenges and complexities of managing resources and institutions in the face of rapid economic change.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Pamuk (2007, p.16) both emphasise the critical role of historical institutional development in shaping long-term economic trajectories. This cluster focuses on inequality and economic divergence in historical contexts, specifically addressing how institutional development and structural changes influenced economic outcomes in different regions. (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Pamuk 2007, p.16). The central mechanism identified by Sokoloff and Engerman is the impact of institutions that advantage elites and restrict economic opportunities for the broader population. They argue that this institutional structure maintains elite status and limits economic growth by curbing competition and innovation. This finding is echoed by Pamuk's analysis of Europe, where structural changes initiated by the Black Death led to significant economic divergence between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk highlights how urbanisation rates, a key indicator of economic development, differed markedly between these regions. This divergence suggests that institutional and structural changes can have profound and lasting effects on economic growth. Pamuk also notes that favourable institutions may have existed prior to the Black Death, indicating that pre-existing conditions can influence the trajectory of economic change. This qualification underscores the complexity of institutional impacts on economic growth, suggesting that while institutions play a crucial role, their effects are mediated by pre-existing conditions and historical events. Understanding these dynamics is essential for comprehending the diverse economic trajectories observed in different regions. The interplay between institutional development, structural changes, and historical events shapes economic outcomes in complex and multifaceted ways.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) show that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England facilitated secure property rights and financial innovations, which were pivotal for long-term economic success. The establishment of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, reflecting an enhanced security of property rights over time. This institutional framework was critical for both the development of a long-term capital market and sustained economic growth. North (1989, p.3) underscores the importance of institutions in managing complex interdependencies, suggesting that the progress of societies to modern economic status is contingent on institutional development. This highlights that while secure property rights and financial innovations are essential for economic growth, their development is contingent on broader historical and political factors. These factors can either facilitate or hinder the establishment of institutions that support economic growth, illustrating the complex interplay between institutions and economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade had a direct adverse effect on African economic development. This effect stems from the loss of human capital, the erosion of social capital, and the creation of a culture of mistrust. This mistrust, cultivated during the era of slave trades, persists today and continues to impede economic development in Africa. The slave trade disrupted social cohesion and weakened institutions, creating a legacy of mistrust that affects contemporary economic interactions. This mistrust manifests in reduced levels of cooperation and social capital, which are crucial for economic development. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.28) shows that the slave trade led to the selection of societies with underdeveloped institutions and less ability to facilitate trade. This institutional underdevelopment, in turn, hindered economic growth by limiting the ability of African societies to engage in productive economic activities. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) further assert that the internal channel of mistrust, which affects intra-African relationships, is at least twice as large as the external channel. This internal mistrust is particularly detrimental as it hinders intra-African trade and cooperation, which are essential for regional economic integration and growth.

Colonial institutions, particularly extractive ones, played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. Extractive institutions were often established in colonies where high mortality rates for Europeans made settlement less feasible, leading to institutions designed to extract resources rather than foster development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional framework persisted long after colonial rule ended, carrying over into the independence era (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). As Acemoglu et al. show, the presence of abundant labor in Africa created a profit opportunity for slave traders (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35), and extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional legacy hindered economic development by limiting opportunities for education and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these extractive institutions, which often involved monopolies and regulations, continued to stifle economic growth well into the post-colonial period (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Sokoloff and Engerman argue that where there was extreme inequality, institutions advantaged elites, limiting the access of much of the population to economic opportunities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). This institutional framework, which favored elites and restricted competition, contributed to the persistence of inequality and hindered economic growth (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12).

This internal mistrust is particularly detrimental as it hinders intra-African trade and cooperation, which are essential for regional economic integration and growth (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). The internal channel of mistrust, which affects intra-African relationships, is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, as documented by Nunn and Wantchekon, underscores the enduring impact of the slave trade on African economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
