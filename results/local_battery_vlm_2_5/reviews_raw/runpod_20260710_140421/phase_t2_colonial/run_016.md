The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how historical institutions influence long-term economic outcomes. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive, have persisted and shaped current economic conditions. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions, especially extractive ones, have had a lasting negative impact on African economies by stifling growth and development. These scholars highlight the persistence of extractive institutions, which were established during colonial rule and continued to hinder economic progress even after independence. Other scholars, however, contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated and that other factors, such as geographic conditions or cultural attributes, play a more significant role in determining economic outcomes. This debate underscores the need to understand the mechanisms through which historical institutions influence contemporary economic performance. The persistence of extractive institutions, as noted by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), has been a significant factor in the economic stagnation of many African countries. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) report, “The economic profitability of alternative policies.”

They argue that the profitability of extractive institutions, which were often established in areas with high population density, significantly influenced economic outcomes. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) explain that high population density in Africa made extractive institutions, such as slave trading, more profitable for European colonists. This economic profitability of extractive institutions is a key mechanism through which colonial institutions shaped economic growth trajectories in Africa. This persistence of extractive institutions has had long-lasting effects on economic development, as they hindered the accumulation of human capital and delayed industrialization. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) suggest that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which was a primary mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization. This highlights the critical role of institutional changes introduced by colonialism in shaping African economic growth trajectories.

The role of institutions in fostering economic development is evident in the secure property rights and effective financial institutions that North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.9) argue are fundamental to economic growth. The financial revolution in seventeenth-century England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional change was pivotal in enabling long-term capital markets and sustained economic growth (North 1989, p.9). North and Weingast (1989, p.30) further emphasize that these institutions resulted from specific historical and political contexts. The English financial revolution, for instance, was a response to fiscal crises and the need for secure property rights, illustrating how institutions can evolve to address economic challenges. North (1989, p.3) cautions that the development of complex institutions requires addressing issues like opportunism and cheating, which can hinder institutional progress. This underscores the importance of understanding the conditions under which institutions can effectively support economic growth, a theme that resonates with the broader discussion on the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic trajectories.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This critique is significant because it highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic growth trajectories in Africa, which cannot be reduced to a single, overarching narrative. Austin's work emphasizes the importance of long-term historical analysis in revealing the complex relationships that drive economic growth. Austin (2007, p.16) provides a concrete example of this complexity by examining the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This institutional change was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The specifics of this transformation were influenced by local conditions and historical context, such as the ban on new farms in Ashanti and the subsequent abandonment of this policy (Austin 2007, p.16). Austin (2008, p.1) underscores that the relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated than previously recognized, suggesting that institutional changes in African economies are shaped by specific historical and local factors. This nuanced view of institutional change in Africa highlights the importance of considering the diverse paths that different regions and countries have taken, and the specific conditions that have driven these paths.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) finds that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This educational development was driven by the British approach to indirect rule, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their administrative efforts. This approach fostered a more robust educational infrastructure compared to other colonial powers. For instance, the British promoted missionary schools, leading to higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). The French restricted missionary activities, particularly non-Catholic missions, which limited educational development in their colonies (Frankema 2012, p.4). The divergence in educational trajectories between British and French colonies highlights the significant role that colonial policies played in shaping African economies. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) suggest that improving long-run economic performance in Africa will require addressing the frequency of economic shrinking, a phenomenon closely linked to institutional frameworks.

The literature on African economic history and poverty highlights diverse factors influencing Africa's development trajectory, including ecological conditions and commercialization dynamics (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Hopkins (2009, p.12) points out that the nature of colonialism evolved significantly over time, with early mercantilist phases giving way to more modern forms of imperialism by the late nineteenth century. This shift was accompanied by medical advances that facilitated European settlement, altering colonial policies towards Africa. This evolution in colonial strategies underscores the dynamic nature of institutional frameworks and their impact on economic development. This perspective suggests that the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic growth are more nuanced than previously thought, with precolonial factors playing a significant role. This interplay highlights the need for future research to focus on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories, assuming a uniform impact of colonial institutions.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28) both argue that the slave trade's adverse effect on African economic development is a competing explanation for Africa's underdevelopment. This effect operates through a mechanism of mistrust. The slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, a legacy that persists to this day. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic development, as it impedes cooperation and reduces the efficacy of social and economic institutions. The slave trade also selected for societies that were able to facilitate trade with Europeans, which may have led to the development of certain institutions that were advantageous for the slave trade but not for long-term economic development. This selection effect may have contributed to the underdevelopment of African economies. The implications of this selection effect are qualified by the fact that the slave trade did not uniformly affect all African societies. For example, some societies were able to maintain their institutional integrity and economic development despite the slave trade. This heterogeneity in the impact of the slave trade on African economic development suggests that the relationship between the slave trade and economic development is complex and multifaceted. The slave trade's impact on African economic development is not uniform across all societies, and its effects are mediated by a variety of factors, including precolonial institutions and the specific historical context of each society.

Colonial institutions have significantly influenced African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions often hindering development. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions were established in areas where European settlers faced high mortality rates (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional framework persisted long after the colonial period, maintaining the extractive nature of governance. The persistence of extractive institutions is evident in the longevity of monopolies and regulations imposed by colonial powers, which remained intact even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The economic consequences of these institutions are profound, as they delayed industrialization by limiting human capital development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The profitability of extractive institutions in densely populated areas further solidified their establishment, as seen in the forced labor systems in Meso-America and the slave trade in Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These institutions created a cycle where low levels of human capital reinforced the extractive nature of governance, thereby stunting economic growth.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic development is further complicated by the legacy of the slave trade. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies, which persists today and significantly affects economic outcomes (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is a critical factor in understanding the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust and, consequently, on economic development. The slave trade's disruptive effects on African societies are multifaceted, encompassing economic, social, and cultural dimensions. The cultural legacy of mistrust, rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, continues to shape contemporary economic interactions and development trajectories (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). The historical dependence on fishing and proximity to the coast are also relevant, as these factors are potentially correlated with the distance from the coast (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3).

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth is further illuminated by the broader context of institutional development and economic history. North and Weingast emphasize that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.5). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in economic development, highlighting how secure property rights and effective governance can drive growth. The financial revolution in England played a critical role in its long-run success, demonstrating how institutions can facilitate economic growth and political freedom (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This historical perspective provides a comparative framework for understanding the impact of colonial institutions in Africa, where the lack of secure property rights and effective governance has hindered economic development.

The precise historical evidence that settles the disagreement over the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is the differential impact of extractive institutions on human capital development. The profitability of extractive institutions in densely populated areas, as seen in the forced labor systems and the slave trade, created a cycle where low levels of human capital reinforced the extractive nature of governance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these institutions long after the colonial period, maintaining the extractive nature of governance, is evident in the longevity of monopolies and regulations imposed by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical evidence shows that the extractive institutions established during the colonial period have had a lasting impact on African economic development, highlighting the need for institutional reforms to foster growth.
