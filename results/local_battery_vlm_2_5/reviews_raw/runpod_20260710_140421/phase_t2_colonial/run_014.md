The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic historiography. At stake is the understanding of the long-term impact of colonial rule on economic development, particularly how institutions established during colonialism have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. One live disagreement concerns the persistence of extractive institutions and their impact on economic growth. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions, particularly those that were extractive, have had a lasting negative effect on economic development. Others posit that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced, with different historical periods and paths influencing economic growth in complex ways.

The persistence of extractive institutions is a key theme in the literature. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that the extractive institutions set up by colonial powers persisted long after independence, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies. These institutions, designed to extract resources and foster economic growth, have had a lasting impact on economic development. For example, in Latin America, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for much of the nineteenth century, hindering economic growth. This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48), who note that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas, leading to their establishment and persistence. The profitability of these institutions meant that they were likely to be adopted and maintained, even if they hindered long-term economic growth. This argument underscores the importance of institutional persistence in understanding the long-term impact of colonialism on economic development. The persistence of extractive institutions has had a profound impact on economic growth, shaping the economic trajectories of former colonies in lasting ways.

The profitability of extractive institutions in densely populated areas led Europeans to implement these institutions, which in turn shaped long-term economic outcomes. For instance, high population density in Africa made it profitable for Europeans to establish slave trade systems, which extracted labor for American plantations (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This extractive approach extended to other resources, creating a system where Europeans could maximize profits by exploiting local populations and resources. The persistence of these extractive institutions beyond the colonial era further solidified their impact on economic development. Furthermore, the correlation between extractive institutions and low levels of human capital highlights another mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization. The persistence of these institutions varies across former colonies. The differences in institutional persistence across regions suggest that other factors, such as post-colonial policies and political stability, also play a role in shaping economic growth trajectories.

British colonial education systems played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, particularly through their impact on formal education development. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) share a finding that British rule fostered a more conducive environment for formal education in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers. This divergence is evident in the higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938. The British approach, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, contrasts with the French policy of restricting missionary activities, particularly non-Catholic missions. This policy difference resulted in significantly higher government expenses per student in French colonies, yet lower enrolment rates. The French policy of shutting down missionary schools after World War I further exacerbated this educational disparity, highlighting the colonial education systems' role in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema 2012, p.4).

The implications of these educational policies extend beyond mere enrolment numbers. The British colonial education system's emphasis on formal education laid the groundwork for the development of human capital, which is crucial for economic growth. This educational foundation, in turn, facilitated the development of skilled labor and administrative capabilities necessary for economic modernization. The persistence of these educational advantages varies across former British colonies. This suggests that while British colonial education systems provided a significant boost to formal education, the long-term economic benefits were contingent on post-colonial policies and socio-economic factors. The recorded differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth in Kenya underscore the need for a nuanced understanding of the path-dependence nature of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28).

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation through compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin argues that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al. recognise. For instance, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.8). This shift underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, thereby revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such. The causal relationships involved are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al.'s formulations. Austin (2007, p.16) demonstrates that the introduction of motorable roads and lorries significantly impacted the transport of goods. This mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana, except from farm to local market, indicates that the colonial infrastructure played a crucial role in shaping economic dynamics. The implications of these findings suggest that the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is complex and multifaceted, necessitating a nuanced understanding of the path-dependence nature of African economic development. This highlights the need for a more granular analysis of the specific mechanisms through which colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) share a posture that African economic history and institutional reforms do not directly explain African economic growth trajectories. They jointly establish that the historical context of colonialism in Africa varied significantly over time, with different phases of imperialism imposing distinct institutional frameworks. The colonial period is not a monolithic entity; rather, it encompasses phases where "good" institutions were scarce and others where both productive and extractive institutions coexisted (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). This temporal variability in institutional quality is crucial for understanding the path-dependence of African economic development. Hopkins (2019, p.11) cautions against over-reliance on path-dependence as a singular explanatory factor. This determinism risks oversimplifying the complex interplay of historical influences, potentially obscuring the nuanced dynamics that shaped African economies (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This nuanced view highlights the need for a more granular analysis of how specific colonial institutions influenced economic outcomes, particularly in light of the diverse historical contexts within Africa.

This nuanced view is further supported by Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods. This perspective challenges the marginalization of women's roles in historical analyses. The authors emphasize that patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and African chiefs systematically codified gender discrimination, which undermined women's economic contributions. This systemic marginalization was pervasive across Africa, including segregationist and apartheid South Africa. Broadberry and Gardner show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. By the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence. This economic performance highlights the potential for African economies to sustain growth, even under colonial institutions that systematically marginalized women. Broadberry and Gardner point out that improving long-run performance in Africa will require reducing the rate and frequency of economic shrinking. This nuanced view underscores the need to address the institutional factors that contribute to economic decline, including those that perpetuate gender discrimination.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) show that this mistrust is a significant factor in Africa's economic underdevelopment. The slave trade disrupted African societies, leading to a breakdown in social institutions and a persistent lack of trust. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on the mechanisms that allowed the slave trade to flourish, noting that early trade between Africans and Europeans was primarily in commodities other than slaves. This indicates that societies with developed institutions were better equipped to facilitate trade, suggesting that the slave trade was a consequence of pre-existing institutional weaknesses. The breakdown of trust, in turn, has hindered the formation of strong social and economic institutions, which are crucial for sustainable growth. This legacy of mistrust continues to affect African economies, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment that is deeply rooted in historical institutions.

The legacy of colonial institutions in Africa has significantly shaped the continent's economic growth trajectories. Colonial powers established extractive institutions in regions where high mortality rates made long-term settlement less viable. These institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in their enduring impact on economic outcomes, as shown by the continuity of extractive policies even after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Extractive institutions were more profitable in densely populated areas, where there was a ready supply of labor to be exploited, such as in Meso-America and Africa (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The economic profitability of alternative policies often hinged on the availability of labor and the potential for resource extraction (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35).

The persistence of extractive institutions has had profound effects on human capital development. In societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of investment in human capital has been a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions have hindered economic growth. The historical evidence supports the idea that the control structures set up during the colonial era persisted long after independence, forming the basis of current-day institutions in many African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional continuity has perpetuated a cycle of underdevelopment, as the extractive policies and practices established during colonial rule continue to shape economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions led to a decline in economic development in Africa, has been both supported and nuanced by recent scholarship. Some scholars argue that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective highlights the need for a more differentiated understanding of the causal relationships involved in economic development (Austin 2008, p.1). For example, the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This perspective challenges the notion that colonial institutions uniformly led to economic stagnation. The persistence of extractive institutions and their impact on human capital development show that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. The historical evidence of persistent extractive policies and practices, along with the differentiated causal relationships involved in economic development, underscores the enduring legacy of colonial institutions in Africa.
