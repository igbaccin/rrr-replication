The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories remains a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the explanation of Africa's relative economic stagnation compared to other regions, and the extent to which colonial legacies continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. One key disagreement centers around the persistence and impact of extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers. Some scholars argue that these institutions have had a lasting negative effect on economic development by creating barriers to investment, innovation, and human capital accumulation. Others posit that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other factors, such as geography or cultural differences, may play a more significant role. Additionally, there is debate over whether the effects of colonial institutions are direct or mediated through other variables, such as trust or social capital. This discussion is crucial for understanding the deep-rooted challenges facing African economies and for informing policy interventions aimed at fostering growth. High population density facilitated the exploitation of labor, making extractive institutions profitable for Europeans. For example, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America led to forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa drove slave trading. In Africa, extractive institutions were also profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas, where there was more to extract. These institutional changes, which were more profitable for Europeans, had long-lasting effects on economic development. In addition, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) find that differences in European mortality rates led to the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. These extractive institutions set up by the colonialists persisted long after the colonial regime ended. The persistence of these institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of prominent extractive policies, such as the monopolies and regulations created by Spain in Latin America, which remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century. This persistence highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on economic development.

Institutions play a crucial role in fostering economic development, as shown by North and Weingast's (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.9) analysis of seventeenth-century England. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in England was pivotal for securing property rights and driving economic growth, challenging views that prioritize population and savings. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) highlight that the financial revolution in England was instrumental in its long-term success, suggesting that even without this institutional evolution, economic development would have been slower and less decisive. This institutional framework was essential for a long-term capital market and overall economic growth. The financial revolution in England illustrates how institutions can enhance economic efficiency by reducing transaction costs and securing property rights over time. North (1989, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic, suggesting that the progress from primitive to modern societies is not unilinear. This implies that the role of institutions in economic growth is contingent on the specific historical and political context, and that the path to economic development is not predetermined.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by highlighting its oversimplification and methodological issues, arguing for a more nuanced examination of growth theories (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). The thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, and fails to account for the complex interplay of factors that influence economic growth. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) examines the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, noting that this transition led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to new economic realities, illustrating how localised scarcity can catalyse institutional innovation. Austin (2008, p.1) further underscores the importance of long-term historical analysis in understanding growth trajectories, revealing that causal relationships are more differentiated than previously recognised. The complexity of these relationships suggests that policy implications drawn from simplistic models might be misleading. This example highlights the challenges of implementing policies based on oversimplified economic theories, as local conditions and historical contexts play crucial roles in shaping economic outcomes.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms provides valuable insights into the historical context of Africa's development but does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins (2009, p.12) emphasizes that colonialism in Africa evolved significantly over time. Initially, colonial policies were largely extractive and lacked productive institutions, even in Europe. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutional options were available, and medical advances enabled European settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The temporal evolution of colonial institutions indicates that the impact of colonial rule on African economic growth varied depending on the historical period and specific contexts. This emphasis on the temporal evolution of colonial institutions and the need for further research on precolonial production underscores the complexity of African economic history and the importance of understanding the historical context of institutional reforms.

Colonial rule, particularly British colonial rule, has played a significant role in shaping African educational development, which in turn has influenced economic growth trajectories. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argues that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. The British welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, which promoted the establishment of educational institutions. This approach differed markedly from that of the French, who restricted missionary activities and consequently had lower primary-school enrolment rates. The British colonial administration's emphasis on education laid a foundation for human capital development, which is crucial for economic growth. The impact of colonial education policies on economic growth differed across Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) note that the effects of colonial institutions have been persistent until today. The Kenyan case, for instance, illustrates how varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans shaped long-run trajectories of wage income growth. This highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions, education, and economic outcomes in Africa. The structural impediments created by colonial rule have left a lasting impact on African poverty, which has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. This underscores the need for further research into the long-term growth trajectories of African economies.

The slave trade in Africa is argued to have had a direct adverse effect on economic development, offering an alternative explanation to colonial institutions as the primary cause of African underdevelopment (Nunn 2008, p.28; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). The internal channel of mistrust, which operates within African societies, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which involves interactions with non-African entities (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal dynamic of mistrust has profound implications for economic development, as it undermines the social cohesion and collaborative frameworks necessary for economic growth. This institutional legacy, however, was severely disrupted by the slave trade, leading to a cycle of underdevelopment that persists to this day. This perspective underscores the need to consider the long-term effects of historical events, such as the slave trade, on contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

Colonial institutions have had a profound impact on African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions being a key factor in shaping these outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates has persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions were more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This perspective is supported by Nunn and Wantchekon, who argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, in turn, has had a negative impact on economic development, as trust is crucial for economic growth and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers, which remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional legacy has had long-term effects on contemporary economic outcomes in Africa, underscoring the need to consider the historical context of colonial rule when examining economic growth trajectories.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is further complicated by the differentiation of causal relationships over time. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This view is supported by Hopkins, who points out that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some of the constraints on European settlement, making disease no longer the important influence on colonial policy towards Africa that it had been earlier (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories.
