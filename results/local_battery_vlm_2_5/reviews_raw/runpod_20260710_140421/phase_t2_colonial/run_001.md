Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, but the precise nature of this influence remains a subject of intense debate. At stake is the understanding of how historical institutional legacies continue to affect contemporary economic development, and whether these legacies can be attributed directly to colonial policies or if other factors, such as pre-colonial conditions or post-colonial state failures, are also at play. One position in the debate asserts that colonial institutions established extractive systems that persist to this day, hindering economic growth. Another position argues that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other historical and contemporary factors are more determinative. A third position contends that the causal relationships are more complex and differentiated than previously recognized, involving multiple historical periods and paths. This debate is crucial for understanding the deep-seated challenges Africa faces in achieving sustained economic growth. A key question is whether the institutional frameworks imposed during colonial rule have left an indelible mark on Africa's economic development, or if other historical and contemporary factors have played a more significant role. Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing extractive institutional frameworks that persist to this day. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial powers introduced institutions that facilitated the extraction of resources, with high population density making extractive institutions more profitable. This institutional framework was particularly conducive to the establishment of forced labor systems and other extractive practices. The persistence of these institutions is evident in the enduring structures and practices that were inherited by post-colonial African states. The extractive institutions, such as monopolies and regulations, remained intact long after independence, creating barriers to economic growth. These extractive institutions obstructed economic development and constrained the accumulation of human capital. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) explain that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization. The persistence of these institutions into the independence era highlights their profound impact on Africa's economic trajectory.

Austin (2007, p.16) and Austin (2008, p.1) argue that colonial institutions in Africa were not the sole determinants of economic growth trajectories. They propose that shifts from land abundance to scarcity drove an institutional revolution in factor markets. This shift fundamentally altered economic growth trajectories by introducing widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. In Ghana, for instance, the increasing scarcity of land led to significant institutional changes, such as the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs banning the creation of new farms in 1939 (Austin 2007, p.16). This policy, though eventually abandoned, reflected a response to localised scarcity. This perspective underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history. Austin (2008, p.1) contends that the causal relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated than previously recognized, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of historical contexts. Austin (2008, p.1) suggests that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This perspective challenges the notion that colonial institutions alone determine these outcomes, highlighting the need to consider other factors, such as shifts in land scarcity and the resulting institutional changes. These shifts in factor markets, driven by scarcity, played a crucial role in shaping economic growth trajectories in Africa.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that British colonial rule fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial regimes in sub-Saharan Africa, suggesting variations in colonial impacts. British rule, in particular, was marked by a policy of indirect rule that encouraged the activities of Christian missionaries, including Protestant and Catholic missions. This policy supported the development of educational institutions, as missionaries were welcomed to establish schools and provide education, which was not the case under French rule. For instance, Frankema (2012, p.15) shows that British West Africa had higher primary-school enrolment rates compared to French West Africa, with rates of 7.1 percent versus 3.6 percent in 1938. Furthermore, the average government expense per student was considerably higher in the French colonies, indicating a lower priority for education. This divergence in educational development trajectories was influenced by the differing colonial policies toward missionaries and education. The British approach, which promoted missionary schools, likely contributed to higher levels of educational attainment in their colonies. The downstream implications of this educational development are intricate. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) suggest that the long-term growth trajectories in Africa were influenced by varying degrees of income inequality, especially between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. This indicates that while British colonial rule may have facilitated formal education, the benefits of this education were not evenly distributed, leading to persistent income inequalities that continue to shape African economic development.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) share a posture that the economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms. Hopkins (2009, p.12) observes that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly over time, with early mercantilist forms of imperialism lacking productive institutions altogether. This temporal variation suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth was not uniform across different periods. Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had reduced constraints on European settlement. This shift in the nature of colonial institutions could have influenced the economic trajectories of African colonies differently. Hopkins (2019, p.11) further elaborates on the complexities of African economic history, emphasizing that significant progress has been made in understanding the continent's economic past. Hopkins (2019, p.11) argues that these methodological challenges can lead to a deterministic view that overlooks intervening influences. This methodological caution highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped African economic growth trajectories, considering the diverse and evolving nature of these institutions over time.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories has been extensively debated, with scholars highlighting the enduring impact of extractive institutions imposed by European colonizers. Acemoglu et al. show that the profitability of extractive institutions was a key factor in their establishment, with high population density making these institutions more profitable for Europeans (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This profitability led to the implementation of forced labor systems and slave trades, which had lasting effects on African economies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Austin argues that the causal relationships involved in African economic growth are more differentiated than previously recognized, emphasizing the need to examine growth theories against long-term history (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective underscores the importance of considering the diverse and evolving nature of colonial institutions over time, as well as the specific historical contexts in which they operated. This is consistent with Nunn and Wantchekon's argument that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The persistence of extractive institutions beyond the colonial era is another crucial aspect of their impact on African economic growth. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the control structures set up in nonsettler colonies during the colonial era persisted long after independence, forming the basis of current-day institutions in many African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Nunn highlights the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, showing that societies with institutions sufficient to facilitate trade with Europeans were able to participate in the early commodity trade (Nunn 2008, p.19). This historical evidence illustrates the long-term consequences of colonial institutions, which continue to influence African economic trajectories today. The precise measurement of these effects is documented by Nunn and Wantchekon's empirical analysis, which shows that both internal and external channels of mistrust are important, but that the internal channel is at least twice as large as the external channel (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This empirical comparison settles the disagreement between those who attribute Africa's underdevelopment solely to other factors such as colonialism or more recent events.
