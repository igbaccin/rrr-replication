Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, a topic that has sparked substantial debate among scholars. The core disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which colonial institutions have influenced economic development. One position argues that colonial institutions established extractive systems that persist to this day, hindering growth. Another position suggests that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced, with different historical periods and paths leading to varied outcomes. Some scholars suggest that the slave trade has been a primary driver of Africa's economic stagnation, with colonial institutions playing a significant role. Understanding the role of colonial institutions in African economic growth is critical for comprehending the continent's development challenges and for informing policy interventions aimed at fostering economic growth. Acemoglu et al. and the shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8) demonstrate that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. High population density facilitated the establishment of extractive institutions by colonial powers, which were economically profitable for Europeans; these institutions were then maintained long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) illustrate that the presence of abundant labor in densely settled areas created profit opportunities for Europeans, leading to the establishment of extractive institutions such as forced labor and slave trading. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate through historical evidence that these institutions were more profitable in areas with high population density and persisted long after colonial rule ended. The persistence of these extractive institutions is a critical mechanism through which colonialism has shaped African economic growth trajectories. The extractive institutions established during colonialism, such as monopolies and regulations, remained intact even after independence, as Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) highlight, and significantly affected current income per capita. One downstream implication of this persistence is the delayed industrialization in African economies, as Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) suggest. The extractive institutions set up by colonial powers often prevented the masses from obtaining education, which is a primary mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization. This underscores the profound and enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth, shaping trajectories that continue to influence contemporary development challenges.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) both highlight the complexity of factors influencing African economic growth, arguing that growth trajectories are shaped by multiple, interacting historical forces. One specific mechanism through which these forces operate is the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which can drive institutional changes in factor markets. For instance, Austin (2007, p.8) describes how the expansion of motorable roads and the mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana during the early 20th century transformed local economies. This infrastructural development facilitated the growth of markets in land use rights and hired labour, illustrating how shifts in resource availability can catalyse institutional changes. Austin (2008, p.2) notes that these causal relationships are more differentiated than often recognized, indicating that the impact of such institutional changes may vary significantly across different regions and historical periods. This variation highlights the need for nuanced, context-specific analyses of African economic growth trajectories, as the effects of institutional changes depend on a multitude of local factors.

The literature on African economic history and institutional reforms (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) offers a rich historical context for understanding Africa's development, even as it does not directly link colonial institutions to economic growth trajectories. Hopkins (2009, p.12) points out that the notion of colonialism as a monolithic entity obscures the significant differences between early mercantilist imperialism and later modern forms of colonialism. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Olsson (cited in (Hopkins 2009, p.12)) both argue that the early phase of colonialism lacked productive institutions, making the choice between productive and extractive institutions unavailable. By the late nineteenth century, however, both options were present. This distinction is crucial because it highlights that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform across different periods. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Nunn (cited in (Hopkins 2009, p.13)) both acknowledge that colonialism often resulted in non-productive institutions but differ on whether these institutions were entrenched or merely left unchallenged. The implication is that the timing and nature of colonial intervention significantly influenced the institutional landscape, which in turn shaped economic trajectories. Hopkins (2019, p.11) advises caution against over-reliance on path dependence and large leaps of faith across centuries. This warning underscores the complexity of linking historical events to current economic conditions, suggesting that a nuanced understanding of African economic growth must consider the diverse and evolving nature of colonial institutions and their impacts.

British colonial policies, particularly those of the British Empire, fostered the development of formal education systems in sub-Saharan Africa (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This was achieved through a combination of indirect rule and the active encouragement of missionary activities. British indirect rule allowed local institutions to manage day-to-day administration, while missionaries established schools and hospitals. Frankema (2012, p.4) describes how this approach differed from French colonial policies, which restricted missionary activities and focused more on direct state control. The divergence in educational development between British and French colonies is evident in the primary-school enrolment rates, which were notably higher in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938. The British approach promoted education and created a framework for public goods provision, which laid the groundwork for future economic development. The impact of colonial education policies on economic growth was complex. For instance, the persistence of income inequality in post-colonial Africa, as evidenced in the Kenyan case, suggests that educational development did not uniformly translate into economic growth (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions, education, and economic outcomes, indicating that while British colonial policies may have laid a foundation for education, other factors such as income inequality and post-colonial policies played crucial roles in shaping long-term economic trajectories.

Akyeampong and Fofack, together with Peters (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Peters 2004, p.19; Bryceson 2002, p.11), highlight the multifaceted roles of women in economic development and the broader social transformations in Africa. They show that women were central to economic production and reproduction, both before and during colonial rule. This was particularly evident in the way women managed households and engaged in trade and commerce. Women's economic roles were often embedded within kinship structures, which were significantly altered by colonial institutions that codified and systematized patriarchy. This codification often led to the marginalization of women's economic contributions, as colonial policies reinforced existing patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and African chiefs and elders. This codification of patriarchy was pervasive, even in segregationist and apartheid South Africa, as noted by Belinda Bozzoli's "patchwork-quilt of patriarchies" (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). The economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa, as documented by Bryceson, further exacerbated these social changes. The shift from family labor to local casual wage labor markets, as recorded in Sokoto State, illustrates how economic restructuring altered traditional labor dynamics (Bryceson 2002, p.11). This depeasantization process, where nonagricultural activities become crucial for rural poverty alleviation, is a significant downstream implication of these social and economic transformations. The role of women in these economic changes is often overlooked, yet it is crucial for understanding the broader dynamics of African economic growth.

The slave trade had a profound impact on African development, leading to adverse effects on economic growth that persist independently of colonial institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which has enduring effects on economic growth. This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical experiences of communities that were subjected to the slave trade, and it has persisted over centuries, shaping contemporary social and economic dynamics. One specific mechanism through which this mistrust manifests is the reduction in the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative economic activities, which are crucial for development. Nunn's work highlights that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, independent of other factors such as colonialism. This effect is particularly pronounced in regions that were heavily involved in the slave trade, where the legacy of mistrust continues to hinder economic progress. The historical evidence indicates that societies with well-developed institutions were better able to facilitate trade with Europeans, suggesting that the impact of the slave trade was not uniform across Africa. The enduring effects of the slave trade on economic development are further qualified by the role of precolonial institutions, which influenced the ability of societies to recover from the adverse impacts of the slave trade. The historical context of the slave trade, including the selection of societies based on their institutional development, underscores the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is marked by the persistence of extractive institutions established during the colonial era. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, which were more profitable in densely settled areas, were established by Europeans to maximize their economic gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional legacy persists, as Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the control structures set up during colonialism in nonsettler colonies remained intact long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The enduring nature of these extractive institutions highlights their significant impact on contemporary economic outcomes.

The reversal of fortune experienced by African economies is deeply rooted in the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers. Austin argues that the causal relationships between colonial institutions and economic growth are more differentiated than previously recognized, emphasizing the importance of examining these relationships within the context of long-term history (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism highlights the evolving nature of colonial institutions and their impact on economic development (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The persistence of these institutions, as well as their differentiated effects, underscores the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary economic outcomes in Africa.

The legacy of the slave trade further complicates the role of colonial institutions in African economic growth. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for a significant portion of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact, coupled with the extractive institutions established during colonialism, creates a multifaceted barrier to economic development. The enduring effects of both the slave trade and colonial institutions highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of Africa's economic trajectory, one that acknowledges the complex interplay between historical events and contemporary outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that colonial institutions, particularly extractive ones, have had a profound and lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Hopkins 2009, p.12). The precise remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which the slave trade's cultural legacy, such as mistrust, compounds the effects of these extractive institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon show that mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This historical evidence underscores the multifaceted nature of Africa's economic challenges, where the legacy of the slave trade and colonial institutions must be addressed together to foster sustainable economic growth.
