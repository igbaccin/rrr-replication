The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue with significant implications for understanding the continent's economic development. The central debate revolves around whether colonial institutions, particularly extractive ones, were the primary drivers of Africa's economic stagnation or if other factors, such as geographic endowments or cultural legacies, played a more significant role. One position argues that colonial institutions, characterized by their extractive nature, were instrumental in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. This perspective posits that the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers created barriers to economic development by limiting investment in human capital and infrastructure. Another position suggests that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other factors, such as the slave trade and cultural legacies, have had a more profound and lasting effect on Africa's economic development. This debate is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial rule and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic growth in Africa. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) contend that European mortality rates influenced the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita. This argument underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical factors that have shaped Africa's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. argue that colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, driven by economic profitability, has had a lasting impact on economic outcomes. For instance, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa created opportunities for slave traders. This institutional persistence has significantly affected current income per capita, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on economic growth in Africa. The relationship between institutions and human capital is intricate. Acemoglu et al. suggest that low levels of human capital may have been a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization, indicating that the legacy of colonial institutions extends beyond immediate economic outcomes to include long-term developmental challenges (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Bryceson (2002, p.11) demonstrate that colonial rule in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly British rule, significantly influenced educational development and social changes. Colonial powers leveraged existing indigenous state structures to facilitate administration, taxation, and investment, which in turn supported the development of public goods like education (Frankema 2012, p.15). The British colonial approach, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations, fostered a more conducive environment for formal education compared to other colonial powers. This is evident in the higher primary-school enrollment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa (Frankema 2012, p.4). The British promotion of missionary schools contrasts with the French approach, which restricted missionary activities and led to lower educational development. The distinction in educational trajectories highlights the role of colonial policies in shaping human capital, which is a critical factor in long-term economic growth. The social changes from economic restructuring, such as depeasantization and the shift towards non-agricultural activities, create a complex picture of rural poverty alleviation (Bryceson 2002, p.11). As rural populations expand and land availability contracts, non-agricultural activities become increasingly important, indicating a broader impact of colonial institutions on social and economic structures. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role in educational development, the broader social changes they initiated have had enduring effects on African economic trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) challenge conventional views of African economic history by highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This perspective is particularly important because it underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic growth trajectories in Africa, one that acknowledges the complexity of historical causation. Austin (2008, p.1) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This view has been further elaborated for Africa by Nunn, with reference to slave trading. Drawing on African and comparative economic historiography, Austin endorses the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, thereby revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such. Austin also argues that the causal relationships involved are more nuanced than Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations acknowledge. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is more complex and varied than often portrayed, with different regions experiencing different economic outcomes due to a multitude of factors. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that most work on institutions and economic performance examines incentives for investment and innovation, which focuses on the link to growing. By contrast, the link between institutions and shrinking has received much less attention. This highlights the need for further research into the specific mechanisms by which colonial institutions influenced economic decline in certain regions, which in turn affects the trajectory of post-colonial economic growth.

The adverse effect of Africa's slave trades offers a rival explanation for African underdevelopment, challenging those who attribute it solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn argue that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust has significant economic implications, as it hinders the development of social capital and cooperative behaviors essential for economic growth. The historical dependence on fishing and the distance from the coast are potentially correlated with the slave trade's impact, highlighting the multifaceted nature of its effects (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This institutional degradation further complicates economic development, as weak institutions struggle to support economic activities and growth. The importance of trust in economic development is well-documented, with studies showing its significance for various economic outcomes, including international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The specific mechanisms by which the slave trade undermined these institutions and the extent to which these effects persist today are areas of ongoing research.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) share a focus on how different historical factors have influenced African economic development, offering a broader context for understanding Africa's economic history. Hopkins argues that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly over time. The early phase of colonialism, characterized by mercantilist imperialism, did not involve productive institutions, as these were not yet established even in Europe. By the late 19th century, however, both productive and extractive institutions were present. Medical advances had reduced the constraints on European settlement, allowing colonial powers to implement more structured policies. This temporal evolution in institutional frameworks highlights the need for nuanced historical analysis when examining the impact of colonialism on Africa's economic development. They note that the Kenyan case illustrates how different types of economic dynamics, such as commercialization, and varying degrees of income inequality have shaped long-term growth trajectories. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and economic contexts in which colonial institutions operated. The shared finding of a dynamic relationship between historical factors and economic development suggests that a more granular examination of colonial policies and their temporal variations can provide deeper insights into Africa's economic growth trajectories.

(2001, p.8) show that the persistence of extractive institutions, introduced by colonial powers, significantly impacted current income per capita. These institutions, characterized by monopolies and regulations, remained intact long after independence, as seen in Latin America, where Spanish colonial policies persisted well into the nineteenth century. This perspective underscores the importance of considering the specific historical and economic contexts in which colonial institutions operated.

The temporal variations in colonial policies further illustrate the dynamic relationship between historical factors and economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) points out that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period overlooks the significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some constraints on European settlement. This period saw the establishment of extractive institutions in densely populated areas, where the presence of abundant labor made such systems profitable (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these institutions into the independence era highlights their enduring impact on economic growth trajectories.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is further complicated by the cultural and social legacies they left behind. This cultural legacy, coupled with the economic institutions introduced by colonial powers, has had a profound impact on Africa's economic development. This evidence suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform and varies across different regions and historical periods.

The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on Africa's economic development, as shown by the persistence of extractive institutions and their effects on current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutions have shaped economic growth trajectories, with some scholars arguing for a more nuanced examination of growth theories against long-term history (Austin 2008, p.1). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the temporal variations in colonial policies, as seen in the establishment of extractive institutions in densely populated areas and their persistence into the independence era. These variations highlight the complex and dynamic nature of the relationship between colonial institutions and economic development in Africa.
