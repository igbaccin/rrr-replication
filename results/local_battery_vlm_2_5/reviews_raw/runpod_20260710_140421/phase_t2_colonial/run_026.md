The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. Understanding the impact of colonial institutions is crucial for identifying the root causes of Africa's economic underdevelopment and for informing policies aimed at fostering sustainable growth. The key debate revolves around the persistence and impact of colonial-era institutions, with some scholars arguing that these institutions have had a lasting negative effect on economic development, while others contend that the impact has been more nuanced and complex. The discussion hinges on whether the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers continue to hinder economic growth or if other factors, such as cultural changes or more recent historical events, play a more significant role. This disagreement underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have influenced Africa's economic trajectory. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. They show that the high mortality rates of European settlers in certain colonies led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted long after independence. These extractive institutions, which include forced labor systems and monopolies, were more profitable for European colonizers in densely populated areas where there was more to extract. The presence of abundant labor in these regions made extractive institutions particularly lucrative. For example, the high population density in Africa created a profit opportunity for slave traders in supplying labor to American plantations. In Meso-America, the availability of Amerindian labor facilitated the establishment of forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional legacy has had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) suggest that the persistence of these extractive institutions has delayed industrialization and hindered economic growth in Africa. This is because extractive institutions typically limit access to education and other opportunities for the masses, thereby stifling human capital development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The correlation between extractive institutions and low levels of human capital highlights a primary mechanism through which colonial institutions have affected economic development. This mechanism, where extractive institutions delay industrialization by limiting human capital, underscores the enduring impact of colonial legacies on Africa's economic trajectory.

The role of specific historical events and institutional changes in shaping economic development in Africa is complex and multifaceted, as evidenced by Austin's examination of Ghana's economic history from 1874 to 1939 (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonial institutions are the primary determinant of current economic disparities, oversimplifies the causal relationships involved. Instead, Austin suggests that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated. This is because historical events and institutional changes can have varied and nuanced effects on economic development, depending on the specific context and the interplay of multiple factors. For example, during the period 1874-1939 in Ghana, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. Austin (2007, p.16) shows that this institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, rather than being imposed by colonial powers. This highlights the importance of considering the endogenous factors that drive institutional change, as well as the exogenous factors imposed by colonial institutions. Austin (2008, p.1) argues that the causal relationships involved in economic development are more differentiated than recognised in Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations. Austin (2008, p.1) further underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, thereby revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such. This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic development is not straightforward, but rather depends on a complex interplay of factors, including historical events, institutional changes, and the specific context in which they occur.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) show that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, which has had a lasting impact on economic growth trajectories. British support for missionary schools, as well as their indirect rule system, enabled a more extensive educational infrastructure to be established. This infrastructure, in turn, facilitated the diffusion of skills and knowledge that are critical for economic development. For example, British West Africa had primary school enrolment rates twice as high as French West Africa by 1938, despite the French colonies spending more on education per student (Frankema 2012, p.4). This disparity in educational development highlights how colonial policies shaped the economic trajectories of African countries. The mere presence of education systems does not ensure economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) argue that the Kenyan case illustrates how differences in long-term growth trajectories can also be attributed to varying degrees of income inequality. This suggests that the impact of colonial education systems on economic growth is mediated by other socio-economic factors, such as income distribution and commercialization. The relationship between colonial education systems and African poverty is not straightforward, but rather depends on a complex interplay of factors, including historical events, institutional changes, and the specific context in which they occur.

Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, identify a significant cultural legacy of the slave trade that has persisted in Africa, which is a culture of mistrust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This enduring mistrust has profound implications for economic development. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust has created a barrier to cooperation and collective action, which are essential for economic growth. This mistrust manifests in various economic and social spheres, including reduced trust in institutions and other individuals, which in turn hinders economic development. Nunn also emphasizes that the historical selection process during the slave trades favored societies with more developed institutions, which had the capacity to facilitate trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). This institutional development, however, did not translate into sustained economic growth due to the enduring legacy of mistrust. The mistrust fostered by the slave trade continues to shape economic interactions and development trajectories in Africa.

The economic restructuring of sub-Saharan Africa has not only transformed its economic landscape but also induced deep-rooted social changes and depeasantization (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Hopkins 2009, p.12). These changes encompass broader social transformations that significantly impact rural poverty alleviation. Bryceson (2002, p.11) notes that as rural populations expand and land availability contracts, nonagricultural activities become increasingly important for rural poverty alleviation. This depeasantization process is driven by the shift from family labor to local casual wage labor markets, as seen in Sokoto State. Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights that while colonial rule often entrenched extractive regimes, the timing and nature of colonialism varied significantly. This variability meant that colonial rulers did not always have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, which could have otherwise facilitated more productive economic structures. The depeasantization process varies across Africa. For instance, in Ethiopia and Zimbabwe, recurring food shortages and declining yields have led to increased food purchase and aid dependence. This pattern underscores the complex interplay between economic restructuring and social changes, where the legacy of colonial institutions continues to shape contemporary economic trajectories.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue. Colonialism in Africa often led to the establishment of extractive institutions, which persisted long after independence. This persistence is evident in the enduring extractive policies in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The economic profitability of alternative policies also influenced the type of institutions Europeans introduced. High population density in Africa made extractive institutions more profitable for Europeans, as the presence of abundant labor facilitated forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This dynamic underscores how colonial institutions, shaped by economic incentives, have left lasting imprints on African economies.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which posits that colonialism led to a decline in African economic development, is supported by evidence showing that extractive institutions delayed industrialization. This delay was partly due to low levels of human capital, as extractive institutions typically prevented the masses from obtaining education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). However, the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This simplification risks missing the nuances of Africa's economic history, where the causal relationships are more differentiated than recognized in some formulations (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). The new economic history of Africa reveals that colonialism did not always have enough time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, which could have otherwise facilitated more productive economic structures. This is evident in the persistence of extractive state institutions into the independence era (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The historical evidence shows that the adverse effect of Africa's slave trades on economic development is also significant (Nunn 2008, p.28). The slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust has had profound implications for economic development, as it affects various aspects of society, including international trade and political institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The underlying point of agreement is that colonial institutions have had a lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The precise remaining disagreement is whether these institutions were always extractive or sometimes productive, depending on the context and economic incentives. For instance, in densely settled and prosperous areas, extractive institutions were more profitable for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the persistence of extractive policies in Latin America, which shows that these institutions were entrenched and had long-lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).
