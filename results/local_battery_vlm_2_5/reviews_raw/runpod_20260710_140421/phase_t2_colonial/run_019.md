The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a critical question for understanding the continent's economic development. At stake is the identification of causal mechanisms that can explain the persistent economic disparities between Africa and other regions. A live disagreement exists between scholars who emphasize the enduring impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic trajectory and those who argue that other factors, such as cultural attitudes or geographic endowments, play a more significant role. This disagreement is crucial because it informs policy decisions aimed at fostering economic growth and development in Africa. The persistence of extractive institutions, which were established during the colonial era and continue to hinder economic development, is a central point of contention. The debate centers on whether these institutions are the primary cause of Africa's economic stagnation or if other factors, such as cultural attitudes or geographic endowments, are more influential. The question of whether colonial institutions are the root cause of Africa's economic challenges or if other factors are more significant is central to this debate. This literature review will explore the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories, focusing on the causal mechanisms through which these institutions operate and their enduring impact on economic development. Colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories through the establishment of extractive institutions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. argue that when European colonizers were able to extract more resources, they were more likely to implement extractive institutions. This was especially true in areas with high population density, where forced labor systems were profitable (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). These institutions were designed to maximize resource extraction for the benefit of the colonizers, often at the expense of the local population. The persistence of these extractive institutions long after colonial rule ended has been a significant barrier to economic development. The extractive policies implemented by colonial powers, such as monopolies and regulations, remained intact in many former colonies well into the 19th century. This institutional persistence has had a profound impact on economic development, as these extractive institutions have hindered the accumulation of human capital. Acemoglu et al. suggest that the low levels of human capital in societies with extractive institutions have been a primary mechanism through which these institutions have delayed industrialization and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) (Nunn 2008, p.28) propose that the slave trade has played a significant role in shaping African economic development trajectories, suggesting that the adverse effects of the slave trade are a key factor in understanding Africa's underdevelopment. This mistrust has led to a reduction in social capital, which is crucial for economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) further elucidate this by noting that trust is vital for economic development, international trade, political institutions, and firm management practices. They suggest that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the profound impact of internal social dynamics on economic outcomes. This internal mistrust has hindered the accumulation of social capital, which is essential for economic growth. However, Nunn (2008, p.19) qualifies this by noting that the slave trade did not uniformly affect all societies; only those with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans. This selective impact suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is intertwined with pre-existing institutional structures, further complicating the narrative of African economic development. The enduring effects of the slave trade, particularly the culture of mistrust, continue to influence economic trajectories in Africa, underscoring the need for a nuanced understanding of historical factors in shaping contemporary economic outcomes.

Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This is evident in the divergent educational trajectories of French and British West Africa, where British colonies saw higher primary-school enrolment rates and lower government expenses per student. The British approach to colonial education was characterized by a willingness to collaborate with missionaries, which facilitated the expansion of educational institutions. This collaborative approach underpinned a more robust educational infrastructure, which in turn supported economic development by fostering a skilled workforce and promoting social mobility. Colonial education's influence on African poverty is not uniform. The implications of this unequal distribution are significant: while formal education may have driven economic growth in some regions, it also exacerbated income disparities, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. This suggests that the legacy of colonial education in Africa is complex, with both positive and negative effects on economic development.

The role of African women in economic growth has been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, with deep-rooted social changes accompanying economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4). Bryceson (2002, p.11) illustrates this through the shift from family labor to casual wage labor markets, which has become increasingly important for rural poverty alleviation as land availability contracts. This economic restructuring has significant implications for gender roles, as patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and local chiefs systematically codified gender discrimination across African societies. This persistent gender discrimination has had lasting effects on economic growth, as fewer studies have analyzed the origins and dynamics of gender discrimination in the context of economic growth. This underscores the need for further research to fully understand the multifaceted nature of economic restructuring and its impact on gender dynamics in sub-Saharan Africa.

This institutional transformation in Ghana was marked by a shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which led to the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was further reinforced by the mechanisation of goods transport, with the length of motorable roads tripling from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles in 1930-32 (Austin 2007, p.8). North and Weingast, along with North, argue that secure property rights are essential for economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). Specifically, the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments significantly reduced transaction costs, thereby increasing the security of property rights over time (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework was pivotal in facilitating long-term capital markets and economic growth. However, North also acknowledges that the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic and can be influenced by various factors, including opportunism and cheating (North 1989, p.3). This underscores the importance of understanding the specific mechanisms through which institutions shape economic outcomes, a theme that resonates with the broader discussion on the role of colonial institutions in African economic growth trajectories.

Pamuk (2007, p.16) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) agree that historical events and institutional differences can shape long-term economic disparities. Pamuk shows that the Black Death initiated structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence is not solely attributable to later developments. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that where there was extreme inequality, institutions often advantaged elites and limited broader economic opportunities. This institutional structure helped maintain elite status over time but stifled growth. The Black Death's impact on Europe was multifaceted, influencing not only demographic shifts but also economic institutions and labor markets. Pamuk notes that the Black Death triggered a decline in interest rates, which likely interacted with existing institutions to shape economic development. The persistence of these institutions and their effects on growth patterns in Europe suggest a parallel mechanism to those observed in colonial Africa, where institutions imposed during colonial rule have similarly shaped economic trajectories. The enduring influence of these historical events and institutions underscores the importance of understanding the long-term effects of institutional legacies.

Hopkins, along with other scholars, have discussed economic history and institutional reforms in Africa but do not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). They do, however, highlight the evolution of colonial institutions over time, noting that the nature of colonial rule varied significantly between the mercantilist period and the modern era. Hopkins notes that, during the early phase of colonialism, productive institutions were scarce, even in Europe. This scarcity meant that there was little option for productive institutions overseas. By the late nineteenth century, however, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had mitigated some of the constraints on European settlement. Hopkins further argues that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions that were already in place. This temporal dynamic suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth may have differed across periods, with earlier phases characterized by a lack of institutional development and later phases by entrenched extractive regimes. Hopkins (2019, p.11) qualifies this view by noting that the use of instrumental variables in economic history is fraught with difficulties. Reliance on the catch-all notion of path dependence to join the distant past to the present compounds the problem. In compressing the past, large leaps of faith across the centuries produce a species of determinism that dispenses with the intervening influences that affect outcomes. This critique underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions interacted with local contexts and evolved over time. The complexity of these interactions highlights the importance of considering multiple factors, including the temporal dynamics of colonial rule and the specific institutional legacies left behind.

The legacy of these institutions is evident in the persistent low levels of human capital and delayed industrialization in many African countries. This institutional persistence highlights the deep-rooted impact of colonial rule on economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping economic trajectories is further underscored by the work of Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4), who show that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa. This mistrust, a direct outcome of colonial policies, continues to affect economic development by hindering social cohesion and cooperation. The slave trade's impact on trust levels demonstrates how colonial institutions extracted resources and eroded social capital, creating long-lasting economic impediments. The enduring effects of these institutions are also evident in the persistence of extractive policies, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8). The full panoply of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers remained intact for decades after independence, underscoring the institutional inertia that has hindered economic growth.

Historians and economists alike have argued that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the causal relationships involved in economic development. This critique is supported by Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13), who argues that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the significant disparities between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism.
