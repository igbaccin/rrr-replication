Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories. The debate centers around the extent to which these institutions have been extractive versus productive, and how they have influenced long-term economic development. A key disagreement involves whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive, as argued by Acemoglu et al., or if the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is more nuanced, as suggested by Austin. Another point of contention is whether the slave trade, as highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon, has had a lasting impact on African economic development through its influence on social trust. These debates are crucial for understanding the historical roots of contemporary economic disparities and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic growth in Africa. The persistence of colonial institutions and their impact on economic outcomes remains a critical area of study, with implications for both historical analysis and contemporary policy-making. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. Specifically, high population density in Africa made extractive institutions profitable for European colonizers, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading. This dynamic was not unique to Africa; in Meso-America, for instance, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor facilitated similar extractive institutions. The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence has had profound implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) provide an example of this persistence in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact long after independence. These extractive institutions hindered industrialization by limiting access to education and human capital development, further entrenching economic disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) elaborate on this mechanism, suggesting that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, thereby delaying industrialization. This institutional legacy continues to influence contemporary economic outcomes, underscoring the enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) extend this analysis by examining historical institutional changes in Africa, emphasizing the significance of long-term history. They jointly assert that the causal relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated than previously recognized, highlighting the need for a nuanced examination of historical periods and paths. This approach reveals that institutional changes in Africa are deeply rooted in specific historical contexts and are not merely outcomes of colonial institutions. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This revolution was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, illustrating how localised scarcity can drive significant institutional changes. This nuanced understanding of institutional changes in Africa underscores the complexity of economic growth trajectories and the need for a more comprehensive analysis that considers both colonial and local factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that secure property rights and financial innovations were pivotal in driving long-term economic success in Europe. This institutional framework fostered a robust capital market, which in turn stimulated economic growth. The financial revolution in England is thus seen as a critical factor in the country's economic trajectory, illustrating how institutions can facilitate growth by providing a stable environment for long-term investments. However, the automatic development of complex institutions to manage increasing interdependence is not guaranteed, as opportunism and cheating can impede progress (North 1989, p.3). This highlights the need for effective enforcement mechanisms to ensure that institutions function as intended. This institutional perspective underscores the importance of secure property rights and financial innovations in shaping economic growth trajectories, a lesson that can be applied to understanding the role of colonial institutions in Africa.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Bryant, build on this perspective by examining how inequality and institutional frameworks influence economic development. They share a finding that institutions can be shaped by societal inequalities to advantage elites and restrict economic opportunities for the broader population (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.17). This pattern of institutional development can, in turn, affect economic growth by maintaining elite status over time (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). Bryant qualifies this by noting that the specific institutional arrangements can vary significantly across different societies. This variation suggests that the impact of inequality on economic growth is contingent on the specific historical and political contexts in which institutions evolve. This highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional frameworks emerge and function within different societal structures.

Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2019, p.11) argue that the economic history of Africa is crucial for understanding contemporary poverty, yet it does not directly address the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories. The literature examines how historical factors, including colonialism, have influenced present-day conditions. For example, Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, noting that in the 1500s, colonial powers lacked the institutional capacity to implement productive institutions overseas (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical context is crucial for understanding the institutional legacies that persist in Africa today. However, Hopkins (2019, p.11) cautions that the use of instrumental variables and the notion of path dependence can oversimplify historical influences, potentially leading to deterministic views that overlook intervening factors (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This complexity suggests that while colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping economic trajectories, their impact is mediated by a range of historical and contextual factors. This nuanced understanding is essential for designing effective institutional reforms that can address contemporary economic challenges in Africa.

The slave trade in Africa had a detrimental impact on economic development, independent of colonial institutions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This mistrust has a profound impact on economic development, as it hinders cooperation and reduces the likelihood of successful economic ventures. The historical impact of the slave trade is further supported by Nunn's discussion of the early trade between Africans and Europeans, which was initially focused on commodities other than slaves (Nunn 2008, p.19). Only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate this trade, suggesting that the slave trade's impact on economic development was mediated through its effect on institutional development. This perspective is echoed by Gennaioli and Rainer, who find that countries with centralized precolonial state institutions today provide more public goods, such as education, health, and infrastructure (Nunn 2008, p.28). The legacy of the slave trade, therefore, extends beyond its immediate economic impact, shaping the institutional landscape of Africa in ways that continue to influence contemporary economic trajectories.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions often hindering development. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the economic profitability of extractive institutions was a key factor in their establishment, particularly in areas with high population density (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This aligns with the findings of Hopkins, who notes that the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism is crucial in understanding the impact of colonial institutions on African development (Hopkins 2009, p.12). The persistence of these extractive institutions beyond the colonial era is evident in the continued extraction of resources and labor, which has stifled economic growth. Acemoglu et al. further illustrate that the control structures set up during colonialism have endured, shaping the current institutional landscape of many African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8).

The legacy of the slave trade, as highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon, has also significantly influenced African economic trajectories. The slave trade not only had immediate economic impacts but also fostered a culture of mistrust that persists today, affecting economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural impact complements the institutional damage wrought by colonial extractive practices, creating a dual barrier to economic growth. The enduring effects of these institutions are evident in the current economic conditions of many African nations, where the lack of public goods and infrastructure, as well as entrenched mistrust, continue to impede development. The historical evidence shows that colonial institutions, whether extractive or exploitative, have left a lasting imprint on Africa's economic landscape, shaping its growth trajectories in profound ways. This is evident in the persistence of extractive institutions and the cultural legacy of the slave trade, both of which continue to influence contemporary economic conditions. The specific historical evidence of the persistence of extractive institutions and the cultural legacy of the slave trade is the clearest indicator of the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories.
