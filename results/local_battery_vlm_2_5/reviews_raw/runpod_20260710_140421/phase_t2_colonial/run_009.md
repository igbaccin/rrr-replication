The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the extent to which colonial legacies, particularly the institutions imposed by colonial powers, have persistently influenced Africa's economic outcomes. One key disagreement centers on the mechanisms through which colonial institutions operate: some scholars argue that these institutions directly hindered economic development by creating extractive structures, while others posit that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced, involving complex interactions with local conditions and historical contingencies. Additionally, there is a debate over whether the persistence of these institutions is a result of their inherent extractive nature or due to the lack of time for colonial rulers to implement more productive institutions. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for comprehending the long-term effects of colonialism on African economies and for informing policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth.

A stream of scholarship focuses on the direct impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These extractive institutions, characterized by the absence of secure property rights and limited access to economic opportunities, were designed to benefit the colonial powers at the expense of the local populations. The persistence of these institutions post-independence has been a critical factor in the stagnation of economic growth in many African countries (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). For example, in Latin America, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence, hindering economic development for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This stream highlights the enduring legacy of colonial institutions and their role in shaping economic trajectories, suggesting that the extractive nature of these institutions has had a profound and lasting impact on African economies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) report, “The economic profitability of alternative policies.”

Acemoglu et al. establish that extractive institutions were more likely to be established in areas where they were profitable, such as regions with high population density or abundant labor. These institutions created profit opportunities for European colonists, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trade networks. For example, in Africa, the relatively high population density created a profit opportunity for slave traders in supplying labor to American plantations. This pattern of extractive institution establishment was observed in other regions with similar economic conditions as well as in Africa. Acemoglu et al. further argue that the persistence of these extractive institutions has had a lasting impact on African economies. In Latin America, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence, hindering economic development for much of the nineteenth century. This persistence of extractive institutions into the independence era highlights the enduring legacy of colonial rule and its role in shaping economic trajectories. The impact of these institutions varies across African countries. Acemoglu et al. suggest that the correlation between institutions and human capital reflects the fact that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education. This lack of human capital development has been a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization in many African countries. The economic profitability of alternative policies introduced by colonial institutions has had a profound and lasting impact on African economies, shaping their growth trajectories in significant ways.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by arguing that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, offering a nuanced view on African economic growth trajectories (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). The thesis, which posits that regions that were rich in pre-colonial times have experienced slower growth due to colonial institutions, overlooks the complex interplay of factors that influenced economic development. Austin's work underscores the importance of examining long-term historical contexts to understand the differentiated causal relationships involved. This approach reveals that economic growth theories must account for recurring situations and path dependence, which are not adequately captured by the 'reversal of fortune' thesis.

Austin (2007, p.16) provides a concrete example of this nuanced approach by examining the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939. This period saw an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The institutional changes in Ghana during this period were driven by the need to adapt to new economic realities, such as the increased demand for cocoa and the resulting land scarcity. This adaptation led to significant economic growth, demonstrating that institutional changes can have a profound impact on economic development. This qualification underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories, one that acknowledges the complex interplay of factors that influence economic development. This nuanced view highlights the importance of considering the specific historical contexts and institutional changes that have shaped African economies over time.

The slave trade is argued to have had a direct adverse effect on African economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28), offering an alternative explanation to colonial institutions as the primary driver of Africa's economic trajectory. Nunn and Wantchekon and Nunn point out that the slave trade depleted the continent's human capital and fostered a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust, in turn, has impeded economic development by undermining social cohesion and hindering collective action. The internal effects of the slave trade, such as the disruption of social structures and the erosion of trust, are at least twice as impactful as the external effects, such as the loss of labor and skills (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) concur that colonial institutions are not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. Hopkins notes the evolution of colonialism from mercantilist to modern forms, highlighting that while early colonialism lacked productive institutions, the late nineteenth century saw the emergence of both productive and extractive options. The medical advances of this period also facilitated European settlement, influencing colonial policies towards Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg challenge the notion of persistent African poverty, arguing that structural impediments are not the primary cause. They note that the impact of colonial institutions has been relatively small compared to the nearly five centuries of precolonial slave trading. This perspective is supported by Nunn, who establishes a negative correlation between slave export intensity and current GDP per capita in African countries. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg qualify this by emphasizing the need for future research to concentrate on charting and explaining differences in long-term growth trajectories, suggesting that the path-dependence nature of African economic development may be more complex than previously thought. This complexity is evident in the Kenyan case, where income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans played a significant role in shaping economic dynamics.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that the development of institutions governing public choice in early modern England was pivotal in fostering secure property rights and financial innovations, which in turn drove economic growth. North (1989, p.3) extends this argument by questioning why the development of complex institutions to handle increased interdependence is not automatic, suggesting that institutional evolution is influenced by more factors than just economic interdependence. This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional change and its impact on economic growth, a theme that resonates with the broader discussion on African economic trajectories. North and Weingast (1989, p.5) note that applying the new economics of organization to political problems, especially those involving enforcement of bargains over time, is still in its early stages. This implies that while the role of institutions in economic growth is well-established in European contexts, the mechanisms by which these institutions operate and their applicability to other regions, such as Africa, remain areas for further exploration.

Pamuk (2007, p.16) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) (Pamuk 2007, p.16) both highlight how historical events and institutional differences shaped long-term economic disparities. Pamuk (2007, p.16) argues that the Black Death triggered structural changes that led to a wage gap between northwestern and southern Europe by 1450. This divergence was initiated by the demographic shock of the Black Death, not merely a result of later developments. The institutional changes that followed, such as shifts in urbanization rates and interest rates, played a crucial role in this economic divergence. This perspective aligns with Sokoloff and Engerman's (2000, p.2) analysis of how institutional differences in New World societies, shaped by initial conditions like inequality, influenced long-term economic trajectories. Both authors underscore the importance of institutional structures in economic outcomes, suggesting that the path of institutional development can significantly affect growth patterns. This implies that while historical shocks can catalyze economic divergence, the underlying institutional framework determines the nature and extent of this divergence.

Colonial institutions play a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by establishing frameworks that persist long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) demonstrate that extractive institutions introduced by colonial powers often endure, shaping economic outcomes long after the colonial era. Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) elaborates on this by noting that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly over time, with later phases of colonialism offering different institutional choices than earlier ones. This temporal variation in colonial institutions highlights the dynamic nature of institutional development and its impact on economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48) further illustrate that the profitability of extractive institutions influenced their establishment, with high population density making such institutions more lucrative for European colonizers. This economic calculus underscores the strategic decisions made by colonial powers, which had lasting effects on African economies. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) build on this by showing that institutions in societies with greater inequality tended to advantage elites, restricting economic opportunities for the broader population. This elite advantage perpetuated inequality, further shaping economic growth patterns. This institutional framework, established during colonial times, continues to influence current economic conditions in Africa. The persistence of these institutions, as well as the economic policies they support, remains a critical factor in understanding Africa's economic development. The shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Austin 2008, p.1; Hopkins 2009, p.12) is that colonial institutions are a key determinant of African economic growth trajectories, and the precise nature of these institutions, whether extractive or inclusive, significantly influences long-term economic outcomes. The remaining disagreement concerns the extent to which these institutions can be reformed or replaced. Austin (2008, p.1; 2008, p.2) argues that the causal relationships involved in the reversal of fortune are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al.'s formulations recognize. This suggests that the path dependence of colonial institutions is not as rigid as some theories propose. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the variation in institutional choices over time, as documented by Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13). This variation shows that while colonial institutions have a lasting impact, they are not immutable and can be influenced by changing historical contexts.
