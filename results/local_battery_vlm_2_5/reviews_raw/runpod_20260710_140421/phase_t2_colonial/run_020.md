The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue with significant implications for understanding contemporary economic disparities. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who argue that colonial institutions directly influenced economic outcomes and those who contend that other factors, such as culture or geographic conditions, played a more significant role. The central question is whether the institutional legacies of colonialism are the primary drivers of Africa's economic trajectory or if other variables, such as cultural attitudes or geographic endowments, are more influential. The scholarly community is divided on this issue. For example, some scholars argue that the extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers have persisted and hindered economic development, while others suggest that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated. Additionally, some researchers propose that cultural factors, such as mistrust, have been more detrimental to economic growth. This review will explore these competing perspectives and the evidence supporting them. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) assert that European colonizers established extractive institutions in colonies where high mortality rates made long-term settlement unfeasible. These extractive institutions were economically profitable for Europeans, particularly in areas with high population density and abundant labor, as in Meso-America. The extractive institutions in Africa, which included slave trading and forced labor, were also profitable due to the high population density and the availability of labor. This pattern was consistent across various regions, with extractive institutions being more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas. One implication of this is that the extractive institutions established during colonialism continue to affect economic growth trajectories in Africa by limiting the development of human capital. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) explain that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization. This mechanism of human capital limitation is one of the primary ways in which extractive institutions have impacted African economic growth.

In Ghana, the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). The increasing scarcity of land in cocoa-growing areas prompted significant institutional changes. For instance, the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs banned the creation of new farms in 1939, reflecting the growing pressure on land resources (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional response to land scarcity illustrates how colonial institutions adapted to local economic conditions, influencing the trajectory of economic growth in the region. This policy was abandoned in 1946, indicating that institutional changes were not always sustainable or effective in the long term.

The colonial period also saw significant infrastructural developments that facilitated economic growth. For example, the length of motorable roads in Ghana tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles between 1930 and 1932, and the number of cars and lorries imported during the decade 1921-30 was just over 13,800 (Austin 2007, p.8). This infrastructural development was crucial for transporting goods, including cocoa exports, and competed down the railway's share of cocoa exports to 58 percent (Austin 2007, p.8). These infrastructural improvements were pivotal in mechanising goods transport, except from farm to local market, where dependence on head loading commonly continued (Austin 2007, p.8).

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) shows that British colonial rule fostered the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more than other colonial powers. This educational development was not uniform across colonial regimes. The British actively supported missionary schools, which were central to their indirect rule strategy, while the French restricted missionary activities. This difference in colonial policies led to divergent educational trajectories: in 1938, primary-school enrolment rates in French West Africa were roughly half those in British West Africa. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) argue that the focus on shrinking is crucial for understanding long-run economic performance in Africa. The British colonial education system, with its emphasis on missionary schools, likely contributed to a more robust educational foundation, which in turn could have influenced economic performance by reducing the rate and frequency of economic shrinkage. However, this relationship is complex, as the link between institutions and economic shrinkage has received less attention than the link between institutions and growth. Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists today, significantly impacting trust and economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This mistrust, which is a key factor in understanding African economic growth trajectories, is rooted in the historical dynamics of the slave trade. The slave trade not only disrupted African societies by forcibly removing individuals, but also by creating an environment of mistrust and social fragmentation. This mistrust has enduring effects on economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for collective action and economic cooperation. The slave trade's impact on trust is particularly significant because trust is a crucial factor in economic development, as it facilitates transactions, reduces transaction costs, and fosters cooperation. The impact of the slave trade on trust is so profound that it accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This mistrust can be seen as a legacy of the slave trade, which has persisted over centuries and continues to shape economic interactions in Africa today. The historical evidence on selection during the slave trades further underscores the adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development. Societies with institutions that were sufficiently developed were able to facilitate trade with the Europeans, suggesting that the slave trade had a selective impact on African societies, with those that had more developed institutions being less affected. This selective impact of the slave trade on African societies further highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic development trajectories.

The historical context and structural factors of African economic development reveal that different periods of imperialism and ecological conditions have profoundly shaped economic dynamics. This is evident from the work of Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4), who together show that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly over time, with early mercantilist forms differing markedly from later modern imperialism. This variation is crucial because it highlights that the impact of colonial institutions on African economies was not uniform. This historical specificity is further supported by Frankema and van Waijenburg, who argue that the persistent nature of African poverty is not solely due to structural impediments but also influenced by varying degrees of income inequality and different types of economic dynamics, such as commercialization (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This suggests that the economic trajectories of African countries were shaped by a complex interplay of historical, ecological, and institutional factors, with colonial institutions playing a critical role in determining the path of economic development. This interplay underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how different forms of imperialism and ecological conditions have influenced African economic growth trajectories.

Colonial institutions significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories through their persistence and impact on human capital development. Extractive institutions, which were established by European colonizers, persisted long after independence (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions were particularly profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas, where there was more to be extracted by European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This extractive approach hindered the development of human capital, as societies under extractive institutions typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of extractive policies, such as monopolies and regulations, which remained intact after independence for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical evidence underscores the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic development.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth is further complicated by the differentiation of causal relationships over time. The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which argues that colonialism led to a decline in economic development in Africa, oversimplifies the complex historical dynamics at play (Austin 2008, p.1). This view is challenged by the idea that colonialism can be divided into different phases, each with distinct institutional outcomes (Hopkins 2009, p.12). For instance, mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism had different impacts on institutional development. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some constraints on European settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This periodization highlights the need to consider the specific historical context and the evolving nature of colonial institutions in understanding their impact on African economic growth.

The economic performance of Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century was more robust than previously characterized. Many of these economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence, indicating a degree of economic development that had been overlooked (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The focus on incentives for investment and innovation, which is central to much of the work on institutions and economic performance, may overlook the link between institutions and economic decline (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This suggests that improving long-term performance in Africa requires addressing the factors that contribute to economic shrinking, rather than solely focusing on growth. The complex interplay of historical, ecological, and institutional factors, including the persistence of extractive institutions and the differentiation of causal relationships, underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped African economic growth trajectories. The historical evidence of the persistence of extractive institutions and the differentiation of causal relationships over time shows that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development is multifaceted and requires a detailed examination of specific historical periods and contexts.
