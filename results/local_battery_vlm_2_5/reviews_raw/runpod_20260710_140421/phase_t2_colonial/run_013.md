The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the extent to which these institutions have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. One critical debate centers on whether colonial institutions have persistently hindered economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or cultural attributes, play a more significant role. Some scholars argue that colonial institutions established extractive systems that continue to impede economic development, while others contend that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated and that a more nuanced understanding of historical and cultural factors is necessary.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high European mortality rates has had a lasting impact on current income per capita. These institutions, designed to extract resources and promote economic development, persisted long after colonial rule ended, perpetuating economic stagnation. Similarly, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48) highlight that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas, where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. This profitability drove the establishment of forced labor systems and other exploitative practices, which in turn delayed industrialization and hindered economic growth. The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations, which remained intact for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). However, the extent to which these institutions have shaped African economic growth trajectories remains a subject of ongoing debate, with some scholars suggesting that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognized.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The profitability of extractive institutions, such as forced labor systems, was often higher in densely populated areas, making them more likely to be implemented by European colonists. In regions with high population density, such as Meso-America and parts of Africa, the presence of abundant labor facilitated the establishment of exploitative systems that were economically beneficial for the colonizers. Acemoglu et al. explain that "the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America was conducive to the establishment of forced labor systems, while the relatively high population density in Africa created a profit opportunity for slave traders in supplying labor to American plantations" (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The extractive nature of these institutions not only delayed industrialization but also limited the development of human capital, as the masses typically did not obtain education under such systems. This mechanism, where extractive institutions delayed industrialization by stifling human capital development, is a critical downstream implication of the colonial institutional legacy (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these institutions into the independence era, where they continued to shape economic policies and governance structures, further underscores their profound impact on African economic growth trajectories.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16; 2008, p.1); (Austin 2007, p.16) argue that the historical context of African economic institutions is crucial for understanding economic development, focusing on specific periods and localised changes. The shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional change was driven by the increasing scarcity of land, which forced local communities to adopt new strategies for resource management. The length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles in 1930-32, facilitating the mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana (Austin 2007, p.8). This mechanisation process completed the shift from traditional to modern transport methods, significantly impacting the economy. Austin (2008, p.1) further elaborates that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, contra Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.1). This view highlights the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such. This historical context provides a nuanced understanding of how localised changes and institutional revolutions shaped economic development in Africa, offering insights into the complex interplay between historical factors and economic growth trajectories.

The development of secure property rights and financial institutions was pivotal in fostering long-term economic success in England, as North and Weingast (1989, p.30) (North 1989, p.9) show. The security of property rights, both in terms of time and economic growth, was a direct result of these institutional changes. North (1989, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic, suggesting that the path to economic growth is contingent on specific institutional evolutions. This indicates that the trajectory of economic growth in Africa, shaped by its unique institutional history, may diverge significantly from that of England.

Sokoloff and Engerman, along with Bryant, discuss how inequality in New World societies shaped institutions that limited economic opportunities for the broader population, thus affecting growth trajectories. The shared finding is that differentials in development have been attributed to institutional differences, with greater inequality leading to institutions that favored elites and restricted competition (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2; Bryant 2006, p.17). Sokoloff and Engerman demonstrate that in societies with extreme inequality, institutions were designed to maintain elite status, often at the expense of broader economic growth. This institutional bias towards elites limited access to economic opportunities for the majority of the population, which in turn affected overall growth trajectories (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.12). Bryant argues that patrimonial systems of domination, which were prevalent in the East, set limits on what was historically possible in terms of institutions and the balance of class forces, further illustrating how institutional structures shaped by inequality can constrain economic development (Bryant 2006, p.12). The implications of these findings suggest that in African contexts, where colonial institutions often reinforced pre-existing inequalities, similar institutional biases may have hindered economic growth by limiting the economic opportunities available to the broader population.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) share a common posture that African economic history has made significant progress but remains fraught with challenges. Hopkins (2009, p.12) argues that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa. He contends that colonialism is often treated as a monolithic period, when in fact it encompassed diverse forms of imperialism, each with different implications for institutions. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.12) observes that early mercantilist colonialism lacked productive institutions even in Europe, making it impossible to export them overseas. However, by the late nineteenth century, medical advancements enabled European settlement, and both productive and extractive institutions became viable options. Hopkins (2019, p.11) builds on this by highlighting the complexities in using instrumental variables and the pitfalls of path dependence, which can oversimplify historical processes and overlook intervening influences. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) underscores the need for nuanced historical analysis. Hopkins (2009, p.12) further elaborates that colonial rulers in Africa did not have sufficient time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, suggesting that the extractive nature of colonial rule was not solely responsible for Africa's economic stagnation. This perspective is crucial because it shifts the focus from colonial institutions as the sole determinant of African economic growth trajectories, emphasizing instead the complex interplay of historical factors.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade created a legacy of mistrust in Africa that persists today, significantly affecting economic development. This mistrust is rooted in the exploitative nature of the slave trade, which fostered a deep-seated suspicion among African communities. The institutional framework of the slave trade, characterized by violence and coercion, eroded social capital and weakened community cohesion, thereby hindering economic growth. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade continues to influence contemporary economic interactions, as evidenced by reduced levels of trust in African societies. This mistrust impedes economic cooperation and hinders the formation of productive institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) further highlight that the internal channel of mistrust, stemming from within African societies, is at least twice as impactful as external factors (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust is a critical barrier to economic development, as it undermines the social capital necessary for effective economic coordination and growth.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories by creating enduring legacies that continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. Extractive institutions, established during the colonial era, persist today and have significantly impacted income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These institutions were often put in place where Europeans found extractive policies more profitable, particularly in areas with high population density (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The persistence of these extractive institutions has been a critical factor in the economic underdevelopment of many African countries. This is because the masses in societies with extractive institutions typically did not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Furthermore, the internal mistrust within African societies, exacerbated by the slave trade, has been a significant barrier to economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is not uniform across the continent. In some regions, the extractive institutions set up by colonial powers have persisted long after independence, continuing to hinder economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). However, the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than some formulations recognize, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical context (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). For instance, the distinction between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism shows that the options available to colonial powers varied over time (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2009, p.13). This temporal variation in colonial policies suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is a complex interplay of historical factors. The long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic growth are evident in the persistence of extractive policies and the enduring mistrust within societies, which continue to impede economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2).
