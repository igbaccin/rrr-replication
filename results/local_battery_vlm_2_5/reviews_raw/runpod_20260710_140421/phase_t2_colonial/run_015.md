The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history. The question at stake is whether the economic outcomes observed in contemporary Africa can be attributed to the legacy of colonial institutions, and if so, how these institutions influenced economic growth. The debate centers on the persistence of colonial-era institutions and their impact on economic development, with some scholars emphasizing the enduring effects of extractive institutions imposed by colonial powers, while others highlight the complexity of historical factors and the need for a more nuanced understanding of institutional change. This debate is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial rule and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic growth in Africa. The authors highlight that European colonists established extractive institutions in areas where high mortality rates made long-term settlement difficult. These extractive institutions, characterized by forced labor and resource exploitation, were more profitable in densely populated regions. These extractive institutions were sustained long after colonial rule ended, persisting through independence and shaping the economic landscape of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further elucidate that these extractive institutions impeded human capital development, as the masses typically did not or could not obtain education under such regimes. This lack of human capital development delayed industrialization and economic growth in these regions. The variability in the persistence of these institutions suggests that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping economic growth trajectories, the specific outcomes were influenced by local conditions and post-colonial policies.

Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) shared a finding that British colonial rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This finding holds implications for understanding African economic growth trajectories. This approach fostered higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa. The British colonies had 7.1% enrolment rates in 1938, while French West Africa had only 3.6%. Meanwhile, the French colonial administration restricted the activities of non-Catholic missions, which hindered educational development. This divergence in educational policies had lasting effects on the region's educational trajectories. This evidence suggests that the British colonial approach to education had a more positive impact on human capital development in Africa. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) also noted that the Kenyan case exhibited varying degrees of income inequality, particularly between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans. This suggests that while education was a key factor, it was not the only determinant of economic growth in colonial Africa.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) both argue that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the relationship between historical factors and economic development in Africa. They suggest that the causal relationships involved are more nuanced and complex than previously recognised. This is because the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths into a single narrative, failing to account for the diverse and evolving institutional landscape of Africa. These authors argue that examining growth theories against long-term history is essential for understanding the intricate relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence. This perspective is crucial for understanding the impact of specific historical events and institutional changes on economic development in Africa. For example, Austin (2007, p.16) highlights that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant institutional changes in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the increasing population density and the need for more efficient resource allocation. Austin (2008, p.1) observes that the causal relationships involved are more nuanced than acknowledged in existing formulations. This suggests that while institutional changes in factor markets were important, they were just one piece of a larger puzzle. The broader implications of this nuanced view are that economic development in Africa is shaped by a complex interplay of historical, institutional, and economic factors, each contributing uniquely to the region's economic trajectory. This perspective underscores the importance of a more detailed and context-specific analysis of African economic history.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that this mistrust, rooted in the historical experience of the slave trade, is a significant factor in understanding Africa's economic growth trajectories. This mistrust manifests in reduced levels of social cohesion and cooperation, which are critical for economic development. The slave trade disrupted traditional African societies, leading to a breakdown in social trust and cohesion. This disruption had profound effects on the region's economic development, as trust is essential for economic transactions and the functioning of markets. The internal channel, which refers to the mistrust and social disruption within African societies, is at least twice as large as the external channel, which involves the direct economic impacts of the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The internal channel's significant impact underscores the depth of the social and economic damage wrought by the slave trade. Nunn (2008, p.19) further elaborates on the selection process during the slave trades, highlighting that only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans. This institutional development was crucial for economic success, as it enabled societies to engage in trade and build economic infrastructure. The slave trade's detrimental effects on economic development extend beyond institutional disruption; they also encompass the loss of human capital and the destruction of social structures crucial for economic growth. This loss of human capital and destruction of social structures had long-lasting effects on Africa's economic development, as it hindered the region's ability to build the institutions and infrastructure necessary for economic growth. The legacy of the slave trade continues to shape Africa's economic trajectory, as the mistrust and social disruption it caused persist to this day.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, a role that has often been marginalized (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This contribution is evident in various economic activities, including agriculture, trade, and craftsmanship, where women played crucial roles. Colonial institutions, however, often undermined these contributions by codifying patriarchal structures that disadvantaged women. This institutional bias had profound implications for economic growth, as it limited women's access to resources and opportunities, thereby hindering their productive potential. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) note that reducing the frequency of economic shrinking is crucial for improving long-run performance in Africa, a process that requires addressing institutional barriers that disproportionately affect women (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). These barriers include limited access to education, credit, and land, which are essential for economic empowerment. Addressing these institutional biases is therefore critical for harnessing the full economic potential of African women and achieving sustainable economic growth.

Hopkins, along with other scholars, has explored the economic history of Africa to understand its institutional reforms and poverty alleviation strategies. This body of work does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins highlights the evolution of colonialism, noting that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly between the mercantilist period and the modern imperialism of the late nineteenth century. During the earlier phase, productive institutions were scarce even in Europe, making their implementation overseas unlikely. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances aided European settlement. Hopkins further notes that colonial rulers often did not have sufficient time to dismantle pre-existing unproductive institutions, which aligns with Olsson's argument that colonialism did not always entrench extractive regimes as Acemoglu et al. claim (Hopkins 2009, p.13). Hopkins also critically examines the use of instrumental variables and path dependence in economic history, arguing that these methods can lead to oversimplified determinism that ignores intervening influences. This critique underscores the complexity of African economic history and the need for nuanced approaches that consider multiple factors over time (Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins recognizes the historical creation of poverty through European capitalism, which integrated other societies into networks of dependence and exploited them through unequal exchange. This perspective indicates that addressing the legacy of colonial institutions requires institutional reforms and a broader understanding of the historical processes that have shaped African economies (Hopkins 2019, p.6).

Colonial institutions played a critical role in shaping African economic trajectories, with extractive institutions being particularly detrimental. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48) argue that Europeans established extractive institutions in colonies where they were more profitable, often in densely populated areas. These institutions persisted after independence, hindering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This persistence is evident in the continuation of extractive policies in Latin America long after independence. The legacy of these institutions is both economic and cultural. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4) show that the slave trade, a key component of colonial extractive practices, fostered a culture of mistrust that continues to affect economic development. This mistrust is a significant barrier to economic growth, as it erodes social capital and hinders cooperative economic activities. The cultural impact of colonial institutions, therefore, is as profound as their economic effects.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economies is not uniform and varies across different regions and historical periods. Austin (2008, p.1; 2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex historical processes at play. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10; 2022, p.17) show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the notion that African economies were uniformly underdeveloped during the colonial period. This variability underscores the need for nuanced approaches that consider the specific historical and contextual factors at play. The historical evidence indicates that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is a complex interplay of economic, social, and cultural factors, not a simple story of exploitation. The persistence of extractive institutions and the cultural legacies of colonialism are key factors that continue to shape African economic trajectories. The historical evidence, such as the persistence of extractive policies in Latin America and the cultural impact of the slave trade, provides a clear picture of the enduring effects of colonial institutions. The precise measurement of economic performance, such as per capita income levels, further illuminates the varied impact of colonial institutions across different regions.
