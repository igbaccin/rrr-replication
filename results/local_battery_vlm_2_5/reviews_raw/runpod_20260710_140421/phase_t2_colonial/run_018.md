The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the explanation for Africa's relative economic stagnation compared to other regions. One position emphasizes the enduring impact of colonial institutions, which were often extractive and designed to benefit European colonizers at the expense of local populations. These institutions, such as forced labor systems and monopolies, were established to maximize profits for European colonizers, leading to persistent underdevelopment in African economies. Another position, however, argues that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is more complex and nuanced. This view suggests that the impact of colonial institutions varies across different historical periods and contexts, and that other factors, such as cultural and social dynamics, also play significant roles. The debate centers on whether colonial institutions were the primary drivers of Africa's economic trajectory or if other factors, such as geographic endowments and cultural legacies, were more influential. This debate is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonialism on economic development and for informing policies aimed at fostering growth in formerly colonized regions. The persistence of extractive institutions, as argued by Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8), highlights the enduring impact of colonial policies on African economies, suggesting that these institutions have continued to hinder economic growth long after colonial rule ended. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) report, “The economic profitability of alternative policies.”

Europeans were more likely to implement extractive institutions when they were more profitable. High population densities, providing a supply of labor that could be exploited in agriculture or mining, made these institutions particularly profitable for European colonizers. For instance, the abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated forced labor systems. Similarly, high population densities in Africa created opportunities for slave traders to supply labor to American plantations. Extractive institutions were also more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas where there was more to extract. The persistence of these extractive institutions long after colonial rule ended highlights their enduring impact on African economies. This persistence is evident in the continuation of extractive policies in Latin America, where monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century. These findings underscore the profound and lasting effects of colonial institutions on African economic development.

Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic growth in Africa, arguing that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than recognized by Acemoglu et al. (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This differentiation is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth trajectories. Austin (2008, p.1; 2007, p.16) underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence. This approach allows for a more granular analysis of how colonial institutions shaped economic outcomes in Africa. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) demonstrates how the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This revolution was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, which fundamentally altered the economic landscape. The effectiveness of these institutional changes varied. The policy to ban new farms in Ashanti, for example, proved excessive and was abandoned in 1946, highlighting the contextual limitations of institutional reforms. Austin (2007, p.8) further illustrates this point by showing how the mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana, driven by the introduction of lorries, completed the process of mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana. This mechanisation significantly impacted the economy by reducing the railway's share of cocoa exports and lowering transport costs. The reliance on head loading for farm-to-market transport continued, showing that institutional changes did not always yield uniform economic benefits. These examples underscore the complex and context-dependent nature of institutional change in Africa, where the legacy of colonial institutions interacts with local conditions to shape economic growth trajectories in diverse ways.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) show that the slave trade created lasting institutional and cultural legacies that continue to affect African economic growth trajectories. The slave trade, through its destructive impact on African societies, led to the development of a culture of mistrust that persists to this day. This mistrust, in turn, has had a profound impact on economic development, accounting for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This finding is corroborated by studies documenting the importance of trust for economic development. Nunn (2008, p.19) further illustrates that the slave trade had an adverse effect on economic development, particularly by disrupting pre-existing institutions that facilitated trade. The slave trade led to the selection of societies with underdeveloped institutions, which in turn hindered their ability to facilitate trade and economic growth. This disruption of pre-existing institutions, combined with the cultural legacy of mistrust, has created a complex interplay of factors that have shaped Africa's economic growth trajectories. This variability underscores the complex and context-dependent nature of the slave trade's impact on African economic growth.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered a more robust development of formal education systems compared to other colonial powers, a pattern that had significant implications for the region's economic growth. Frankema (2012, p.15) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) share that British colonies in Africa had higher primary-school enrolment rates and more extensive educational infrastructure than their French counterparts. This differential impact on education was partly due to the British policy of indirect rule, which welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their administrative efforts. The British approach promoted education and established a foundation for a more educated workforce, which is vital for economic development. The educational benefits of British rule did not always result in immediate economic growth. This suggests that while education was a critical investment, its benefits were not immediately realized in terms of economic growth, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial institutions and long-term economic performance.

The broader context of African economic history and institutional reforms reveals that different factors beyond colonial institutions have significantly influenced the continent's development trajectory. Hopkins and Frankema and van Waijenburg highlight that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development has been complex and multifaceted. Hopkins (2009, p.12) suggests that the nature of colonialism evolved over time, with early mercantilist forms giving way to more modern imperialism by the late nineteenth century. This evolution introduced different institutional frameworks that shaped Africa's economic landscape. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) further elucidate this by noting that the historical narrative of African poverty has been overly simplified, with recent literature often emphasizing structural impediments. This shared finding (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) underscores that the colonial legacy is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. One specific mechanism that emerges from this context is the role of medical advances in facilitating European settlement. Hopkins (2009, p.12) points out that medical improvements reduced disease-related constraints on European settlement, which in turn influenced colonial policy towards Africa. This medical progress allowed for more sustained European presence, which had downstream implications for the types of institutions that were established. The productivity or extractiveness of these institutions varied across regions and time periods. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) qualify this by highlighting the importance of income inequality and varying economic dynamics, such as commercialization, in shaping long-term growth trajectories. The Kenyan case, for instance, illustrates how differences in income inequality among European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans contributed to divergent economic outcomes. This suggests that while colonial institutions played a role, their impact was mediated by local economic dynamics and social structures.

North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that secure property rights and financial innovations were pivotal in driving Europe's long-term economic success. The financial revolution in England, marked by the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework fostered economic growth by creating a stable environment for long-term capital markets. North (1989, p.3) elaborates on the importance of institutions in managing complex interdependencies, suggesting that the development of sophisticated institutions requires specific conditions to evolve. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that prioritize population and savings as primary determinants of economic growth. The implications of these findings suggest that the evolution of institutions governing public choice and secure property rights are critical for sustained economic development. The dynamic interplay between institutions and economic growth is complex. For instance, the financial revolution in England highlights how institutional changes can accelerate economic growth, but the path to such transformations is contingent on historical and political contexts. This complex relationship underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic trajectories, especially in regions with different historical legacies, such as Africa.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions often hindering development. Acemoglu et al. show that high population density in Africa made extractive institutions profitable for Europeans, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trading (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This extractive framework was further solidified by the persistence of colonial-era institutions into the independence era, as seen in the enduring extractive policies in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence is a key mechanism through which colonial legacies continue to influence economic outcomes in Africa.

The impact of colonial institutions on economic development is also evident in the cultural and social dimensions of African societies. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and significantly impacts economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This cultural legacy is intertwined with the institutional frameworks established during colonial rule, creating a complex web of factors that continue to affect African economies. The financial revolution in England highlights how institutional changes can accelerate economic growth, but the path to such transformations is contingent on historical and political contexts (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This complex relationship underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutions shape economic trajectories, especially in regions with different historical legacies, such as Africa.

A key point of agreement among scholars is that colonial institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, contra geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). Austin challenges the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than is recognised in Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Nunn's formulations (Austin 2008, p.1). The disagreement hinges on the extent to which colonial institutions were extractive and the persistence of these institutions into the post-colonial era. The specific historical evidence of the persistence of extractive policies in Latin America and the cultural legacy of mistrust in Africa settles this disagreement (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).
