Colonial institutions have played a significant role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, a topic of intense scholarly debate. The central question is how colonial-era institutions influenced post-independence economic development. This debate is not merely academic; it has profound implications for understanding the historical roots of contemporary economic disparities and for informing policy decisions aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth in Africa. The disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms through which colonial institutions affected economic outcomes. One position argues that extractive institutions, imposed by colonial powers, have persisted and hindered economic growth. Another position suggests that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other factors, such as cultural and geographical elements, have played a more significant role. A third position contends that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that colonialism led to a reversal in the relative incomes of former colonies, oversimplifies the complex historical and economic processes at play. These differing views highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped African economic trajectories, and how this knowledge can inform institutional reforms and economic policies.

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that European settlers established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted after independence and significantly affected current income per capita. Extractive institutions, such as forced labor systems and monopolies, were more profitable in densely settled areas where there was more to extract (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This perspective is supported by Austin (2008, p.1), who emphasizes the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history to reveal relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such (Austin 2008, p.1). This historical evidence suggests that the control structures set up during the colonial era have had a lasting impact on African economies, shaping their development trajectories in profound ways.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the profitability of extractive institutions, such as forced labor systems and slave trade, was a key factor in their establishment. The persistence of these institutions into the independence era is evident in the continued presence of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers, which remained intact for much of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical evidence suggests that the control structures set up during the colonial era have had a lasting impact on African economies, shaping their development trajectories in profound ways. The impact of extractive institutions on human capital development is particularly noteworthy. Extractive institutions often limited access to education for the masses, which in turn delayed industrialization and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories.

This view underscores the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because of similar situations and path dependence. Austin (2007, p.16) shows that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). This institutional change was driven by the need to adapt to changing economic conditions, demonstrating how historical context shapes institutional development. However, Austin (2007, p.16) also notes that the policy responses to land scarcity were not always effective, indicating that the relationship between institutional change and economic growth is complex and not always straightforward (Austin 2007, p.8). This complexity suggests that while institutional changes are a key factor in shaping economic growth trajectories, their impact can be nuanced and dependent on various factors. Both authors highlight that the economic history of Africa is a complex and nuanced field of study, shaped by various factors including colonial institutions. Hopkins (2009, p.12) points out that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) and Frankema (2012, p.15) suggest that colonial powers varied significantly in their impact on educational development in sub-Saharan Africa, with British rule fostering formal education more effectively than other colonial regimes. The British colonial administration welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, which led to higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa. The British promoted missionary schools, while the French restricted the activities of Christian missions, especially non-Catholic missions. This educational disparity is evident in the primary-school enrolment rates, which were 7.1 percent in British West Africa compared to 3.6 percent in French West Africa in 1938. The British approach to education underpinned their indirect rule system, which relied on local institutions for administration, taxation, and investment. Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Broadberry and Gardner, highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This contribution was multifaceted. Women played crucial roles in both production and reproduction, contributing to economic growth through their labor in agriculture, trade, and domestic spheres. The historical developments in West Africa, including kinship, commerce, and nascent capitalism, paralleled similar dynamics elsewhere in Africa, where gender and economic growth were deeply intertwined. Patriarchal alliances struck between colonial administrations and African chiefs and elders resulted in the systematization and codification of patriarchy across African societies. This systematic marginalization of women's roles in economic development was a significant factor in shaping African economic trajectories. Broadberry and Gardner underscore the importance of reducing the frequency of economic shrinking, a phenomenon that has received less attention in the literature on institutions and economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). While economic growth has often been the focus of institutional analyses, the role of institutions in preventing economic decline is equally important. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped not just growth but also economic stability in Africa.

The slave trade had a direct adverse effect on African economic development, offering an alternative explanation to colonial institutions as the primary driver of Africa's economic trajectory (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). Nunn and Wantchekon argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists today and impedes economic growth. This mistrust is a persistent factor that continues to influence economic outcomes. Nunn elaborates on this by highlighting that the slave trade disrupted precolonial institutions, which were crucial for facilitating trade and economic development. This disruption extended beyond the direct loss of human capital to the institutional frameworks that supported economic activity. The historical evidence shows that societies with developed institutions were better positioned to engage in trade with Europeans, suggesting that the institutional damage wrought by the slave trade had lasting economic consequences. The adverse effects of the slave trade on economic development are further compounded by the erosion of trust, which is critical for economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). The slave trade's impact on African economic development thus extends beyond immediate economic losses, encompassing long-term institutional and cultural changes that continue to affect economic trajectories today.

Colonial institutions' role in shaping African economic growth trajectories is underscored by their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. argue that extractive institutions, established during colonial rule, persisted long after independence, hindering economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This institutional persistence is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner, who show that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that colonial institutions had already significantly shaped economic trajectories by that point (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The extractive nature of these institutions, which prioritized resource exploitation over development, created a lasting legacy of economic underperformance. These institutions not only extracted resources but also stifled the development of human capital, as Acemoglu et al. note that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The impact of colonial institutions extends beyond immediate economic losses to encompass long-term cultural and institutional changes. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade, a key aspect of colonial exploitation, caused a culture of mistrust within Africa that persists to this day (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This erosion of trust, critical for economic development, further compounds the adverse effects of the slave trade on African economic development. Hopkins notes that colonial rulers did not have enough time to dismantle the unproductive institutions that were already in place, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions is deeply entrenched in African societies (Hopkins 2009, p.13). This entrenched legacy of colonial institutions continues to affect economic trajectories today, as evidenced by the persistence of extractive policies and the erosion of trust within African societies. The historical evidence of persistent extractive institutions and the erosion of trust within African societies shows that colonial institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on African economic growth trajectories.
