The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the extent to which these institutions have influenced contemporary economic outcomes. At the heart of this debate lies a fundamental question: to what extent do colonial institutions, characterized by their extractive nature, continue to exert a significant impact on Africa's economic development? One position posits that colonial institutions established extractive frameworks that persist to this day, stifling economic growth. This view is countered by another, which argues that the impact of colonial institutions has been overstated, and that other factors, such as long-term historical processes and cultural dynamics, play a more significant role. This debate is crucial for understanding the enduring effects of colonialism and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic development in Africa. A shared finding across multiple sources is that the institutional legacies of colonialism have had profound and lasting effects on African economies (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. The profitability of extractive institutions, particularly in regions with high population density, drove Europeans to implement these systems. High population density provided a readily exploitable labor force, making extractive institutions more lucrative for European colonizers. For instance, in Meso-America, the abundance of Amerindian labor facilitated forced labor systems, while Africa's high population density created opportunities for slave traders to supply labor to American plantations. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions were tailored to maximize economic gains, often at the expense of local populations. The persistence of these extractive institutions well into the post-colonial era further underscores their enduring impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) note that these institutions remained largely intact after independence, as seen in Latin America where Spanish-imposed monopolies and regulations persisted throughout the nineteenth century. This institutional continuity suggests that the legacies of colonial rule are deeply embedded in the economic structures of former colonies, shaping their growth trajectories long after political independence.

Institutions play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth trajectories, as evidenced by historical examples where institutional frameworks have either facilitated or hindered development (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework allowed for the development of new financial instruments and the creation of the Bank of England, which dramatically reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. In turn, this institutional environment supported the growth of a long-term capital market and overall economic development. The English financial revolution, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694, exemplifies how institutional changes can lead to significant economic transformations. However, the development of complex institutions to handle increasing interdependence is not automatic and requires specific historical and political conditions. This highlights the importance of institutional design in facilitating economic growth, as seen in the historical context of England. This institutional framework set the stage for sustained economic growth, illustrating how the right institutional environment can drive long-term economic success.

Austin critiques the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, arguing it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This critique is rooted in the notion that the causal relationships involved in economic growth are more differentiated and complex than previously recognized. Austin emphasizes the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence. This perspective highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of economic growth trajectories, particularly in Africa, where historical contexts vary significantly. Austin's work underscores that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This period saw the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, driven by the increasing demand for cocoa and the need for more efficient land use (Austin 2007, p.8). Austin's analysis of Ghana's economic history illustrates how institutional changes can be triggered by specific historical and economic conditions, leading to significant transformations in factor markets and economic structures. However, the effectiveness of these institutional changes is not always guaranteed, as seen in the excessive and largely ineffective land ban policy implemented in 1939 (Austin 2007, p.16). This qualification underscores the importance of contextual factors and the potential limitations of institutional reforms in achieving sustained economic growth.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade has had a direct and enduring adverse effect on African economic development. The slave trade's impact is partly mediated by the development of a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day and has substantial economic implications. This mistrust, rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has eroded social capital and hindered collective action, both of which are crucial for economic development. The mechanism through which the slave trade influences contemporary economic outcomes involves both internal and external channels. The internal channel, which operates through the erosion of trust within African societies, is particularly significant. Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that the slave trade's historical selection process favored societies with institutions capable of facilitating trade with Europeans, highlighting the role of precolonial institutions in shaping Africa's economic trajectory. However, the legacy of the slave trade's institutional impact is complex, as it also involves the development of mistrust, which can undermine the effectiveness of these institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) complement this by linking the importance of trust to various economic and social outcomes, underscoring the broader implications of the slave trade's legacy on African development.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) assert that the institutional frameworks in colonial societies, shaped by economic inequality, played a pivotal role in determining long-term development trajectories. They argue that in societies with pronounced inequality, institutions often favored elites, thereby restricting broader economic opportunities. This dynamic is evident in the New World, where elite-dominated institutions limited competition and entrenched inequality. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) extend this analysis to Anglophone African economies, highlighting that many of these economies achieved per capita income levels above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This economic performance suggests that, despite the challenges posed by colonial institutions, there were periods of relative prosperity. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.17) also note that improving long-term economic performance in Africa will require addressing institutional factors that contribute to economic shrinking, rather than focusing solely on growth. This implies that the legacy of colonial institutions, which often favored elites and limited broader economic participation, continues to influence contemporary economic dynamics in Africa.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) argue that the economic history of Africa has been instrumental in shaping institutional reforms, but this scholarship does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories. This argument underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of African economic development. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) emphasize the importance of distinguishing between different phases of colonialism, noting that the nature of colonial institutions varied significantly over time. For instance, Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some of the constraints on European settlement (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This distinction is crucial because it shows that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth was not uniform across different periods. However, Hopkins (2019, p.11) warns against over-reliance on instrumental variables and the notion of path dependence, which can lead to deterministic views that overlook intervening influences (Hopkins 2019, p.11). This caution is particularly relevant when considering the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth, as it highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and contextual factors at play.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with their impact varying significantly across different periods and regions. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that extractive institutions, established during colonial rule, persisted long after independence and continue to affect current economic outcomes. These institutions, characterized by monopolies and regulations that benefited colonial powers, hindered economic development by restricting competition and limiting access to economic opportunities. Similarly, Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4) show that the slave trade, a key aspect of colonial exploitation, caused a culture of mistrust that persists to this day, further impeding economic growth. This mistrust has eroded social capital, a critical factor in economic development, by undermining cooperation and collective action.

The persistence of extractive institutions can be attributed to several factors, including the lack of incentives for colonial powers to implement productive institutions and the entrenched interests of local elites who benefited from the status quo. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2002, p.48) demonstrate that in regions with high population density, extractive institutions were more profitable for Europeans, leading to their establishment and persistence. This profitability was driven by the availability of forced labor, which made agriculture and mining ventures more lucrative. Furthermore, Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) highlights that the timing of colonial rule and the specific historical context in which institutions were established are crucial for understanding their impact on economic growth. The extractive nature of colonial institutions in Africa was a product of the specific economic and political conditions of the time.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is further complicated by the interplay between historical and contemporary factors. Austin (2008, p.1; 2008, p.2) argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that Africa's current economic underdevelopment is a result of colonial exploitation, oversimplifies the complex relationships between historical events and economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the historical and contextual factors that have shaped Africa's economic growth trajectories. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2; 2000, p.12) support this view by showing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. In addition, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that the control structures set up in the nonsettler colonies during the colonial era persisted, while there is little doubt that the institutions of law and order and private property established during the early phases of colonialism in Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the United States, Hong Kong, and Singapore have formed the basis of the current-day institutions of these countries. This shows that the impact of colonial institutions on economic growth is not uniform across different regions and periods, and that a nuanced understanding of the historical and contextual factors at play is necessary to fully grasp their effects.
