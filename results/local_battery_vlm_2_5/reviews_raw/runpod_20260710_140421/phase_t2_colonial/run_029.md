The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic historiography and development studies. At stake is the extent to which colonial legacies have entrenched extractive institutions that continue to hinder economic development, or whether other factors, such as cultural attitudes or geographic endowments, play a more significant role. The disagreement centers around whether colonial institutions are the primary cause of Africa's economic stagnation, or if other factors, such as the slave trade and its cultural consequences, are more pivotal. Colonial institutions are often seen as extractive, designed to benefit the colonizers at the expense of the colonized. Some scholars argue that the impact of colonial institutions is nuanced, with different historical periods and paths influencing economic outcomes in complex ways. This debate is crucial for understanding the persistence of economic disparities and for informing policies aimed at fostering growth and development. The persistence of these extractive institutions into the independence era is evident in the continuation of monopolies and regulations created by colonial powers, which remained intact after independence for most of the nineteenth century (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This highlights the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic growth in Africa, where the legacy of extractive institutions continues to influence contemporary economic trajectories.

The adverse effects of the slave trade on African economic development, as highlighted by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28), offer a compelling alternative explanation for the continent's underdevelopment, challenging views that attribute it solely to colonial institutions or more recent events. Nunn and Wantchekon highlight that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which persists and significantly impedes contemporary economic development. This mistrust is a critical factor in understanding the enduring impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic trajectory. The internal channel, where trust within communities is eroded, is at least twice as impactful as the external channel, where trust in outsiders is affected (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal erosion of trust is particularly damaging because it undermines the social capital necessary for economic cooperation and growth. Nunn supports this perspective by noting that precolonial institutions that facilitated trade were crucial for economic development, and the disruption caused by the slave trade further weakened these institutions (Nunn 2008, p.19). The legacy of mistrust persists as a barrier to economic progress, as evidenced by the ongoing challenges in public goods provision and state development (Nunn 2008, p.28). This underscores the deep and lasting impact of the slave trade on Africa's economic landscape, where the erosion of trust continues to hinder development efforts.

British colonial policies, particularly in the realm of education, were more conducive to developing formal education systems than those of other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). Frankema (2012, p.15) highlights that British rule welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, which led to higher primary-school enrolment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa. This educational development was further bolstered by the British promotion of missionary schools, which contrasts with the French policy of shutting down such institutions (Frankema 2012, p.4). Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that the varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans in Kenya underscore the complex dynamics of economic development in Africa. This inequality suggests that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is determined by colonial institutions and the socio-economic structures that emerged during and after the colonial period (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The educational policies of British colonial rule, which fostered higher enrolment rates, did not necessarily translate into persistent economic growth. This discrepancy indicates that while education is a crucial factor, it is not the sole determinant of economic development in Africa.

Austin shows that the transition from land abundance to scarcity in Ghana during the colonial era triggered significant institutional changes in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2007, p.16). Broadberry and Gardner corroborate this institutional shift by highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This indicates that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth trajectories in Africa by fostering market-oriented reforms. The emergence of these markets was driven by the need to manage scarce resources efficiently, which in turn stimulated economic activity. This institutional adaptation suggests that colonial rule, despite its exploitative nature, inadvertently facilitated economic growth by creating conditions that necessitated market-based solutions. Broadberry and Gardner also note that improving long-run performance in Africa will require a reduction in the rate and frequency of economic shrinking (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This qualification underscores the complexity of economic development in Africa, where the impact of colonial institutions is just one piece of the puzzle.

Bryceson (2002, p.11) and Peters (2004, p.19) argue that economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has led to deep-rooted social changes and depeasantization, highlighting the importance of noneconomic spheres such as democratization and good governance. This restructuring has been driven by the commodification of land and labor relations, which has fragmented family and kin-based groups and produced conflicts between generations and genders (Peters 2004, p.14). These social changes are deeply intertwined with political and social transformations that have reshaped African societies. Economic restructuring has also led to the emergence of new social classes and the exclusion of certain groups from economic opportunities (Bryceson 2002, p.5). For instance, the expansion of rural populations and the contraction of land availability have made nonagricultural activities increasingly important for rural poverty alleviation (Bryceson 2002, p.11). Austin (2008, p.2) qualifies this by noting that the causal relationships involved in these processes are more differentiated than is often recognized, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical and contextual factors at play. This nuanced view underscores the complexity of economic development in Africa, where social changes and economic restructuring are intertwined in ways that require careful consideration of both economic and noneconomic factors.

The literature on progress and challenges in African economic history research highlights the evolving understanding of Africa's economic past. Hopkins and Olsson (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) concur that the colonial period was marked by significant institutional disparities, with early mercantilist phases lacking productive institutions and later phases introducing both productive and extractive ones. This institutional evolution was influenced by medical advances that facilitated European settlement and altered colonial policies. These institutional shifts were not uniform across Africa; rather, they varied based on the timing and nature of colonial rule. The historical creation of poverty, as described by Hopkins (2019, p.11), was a complex process involving the integration of African societies into global networks of dependence and exploitation. The extent to which these institutional changes directly impacted economic development remains a subject of debate. Hopkins (2019, p.6) notes that the historical creation of poverty was a result of unequal exchange mechanisms. This perspective underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped economic trajectories, highlighting the importance of considering both the temporal and spatial dimensions of colonial rule. This nuanced view is crucial for understanding the long-term effects of colonial institutions on African economic development, as it acknowledges the complex interplay between historical factors and contemporary economic outcomes.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is evident in the persistence of extractive institutions that stifled development. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that the extractive institutions established during colonial rule persisted long after independence, forming the basis of current-day institutions in many African countries. These institutions, which were designed to extract resources rather than foster economic development, have had a lasting impact on economic outcomes. This perspective is reinforced by Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4), who demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, a legacy that continues to affect economic development today. This highlights the deep-seated and enduring effects of colonial institutions on African economies.
