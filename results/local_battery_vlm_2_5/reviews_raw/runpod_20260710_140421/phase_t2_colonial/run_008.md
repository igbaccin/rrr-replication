The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories remains a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is understanding whether the economic disparities between African countries and other regions can be attributed to the institutional legacies of colonialism, or if other factors, such as geography or cultural differences, are more determinant. One live disagreement centres on the persistence of extractive institutions. Some scholars argue that colonial powers established extractive institutions to maximize resource extraction, which hindered economic development. Others posit that the impact of colonial institutions was more nuanced, influenced by various historical and contextual factors. This debate is crucial for comprehending the long-term effects of colonial rule on African economies and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic growth. This review will examine the role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories, focusing on the persistence of extractive institutions and their impact on economic development. Frankema and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that British rule was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa than other colonial powers. This development was a crucial element in shaping African economic growth trajectories. The British colonial education system was more expansive than that of other European powers, such as the French, who restricted missionary activities. This difference led to higher primary-school enrollment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa by 1938. The British colonial government promoted missionary schools, while the French colonies shut down many of them, resulting in lower government expenses per student in British colonies. This educational disparity had significant implications for human capital development in Africa. The Kenyan case illustrates that the path-dependence nature of African economic development is influenced by more than just colonial education systems. The recorded differences in long-run trajectories of wage income growth in Kenya suggest that varying degrees of income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans also played a crucial role. This indicates that while colonial education systems were important, they were not the sole determinant of African economic growth trajectories. Frankema (2012, p.15) reports, “Many African and European missionaries coordinated peaceful or violent resistance against colonial regimes, such as Protestant missionaries protesting against the rubber trade in the Congo Free State (Pakenham 1992) and the Catholic Chewa communities led by John Chilembwe against the British colonial government in Nyasaland (Linden and Linden 1974).”

The institutional revolution in factor markets in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to significant economic changes, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2007, p.16). This transformation was driven by the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity, which necessitated new institutional arrangements to manage resources efficiently. Austin (2007, p.16) highlights that even in regions like Ashanti, where population density was lower, indications of land scarcity emerged by the end of the 1930s. This scarcity prompted the Ashanti Confederacy Council of Chiefs to ban the creation of new farms in 1939, demonstrating the institutional response to resource constraints. The mechanisation of goods transport further facilitated this institutional revolution. Motorable roads tripled in length from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles between 1930 and 1932, and the number of cars and lorries imported during the decade 1921-30 reached 13,800, with 8,921 licensed by the end of the period (Austin 2007, p.8). This infrastructure development completed the process of mechanisation of goods transport in Ghana, except from farm to local market, where head loading commonly continued. The effects of these institutional changes varied across different sectors. The emergence of hired labour markets, for instance, did not necessarily translate into improved wages or working conditions for all labourers. This qualification underscores the complex and varied outcomes of institutional changes in response to economic pressures.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) together argue that the economic history of Africa can illuminate institutional reforms and poverty reduction, but they do not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories. The colonial period in Africa was marked by significant institutional changes, but these changes were not uniform across all sectors or periods. Hopkins (2009, p.12) notes that the nature of colonial institutions varied greatly between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. In the early phase of colonialism, institutions were largely non-productive, reflecting the broader lack of institutional development in Europe itself. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were established, and medical advances facilitated greater European settlement in Africa. This period saw a shift in colonial policy, influenced less by disease and more by the availability of institutional choices. This dynamic is crucial for understanding the varied economic growth trajectories across African regions. Hopkins (2019, p.11) advises that instrumental variables and the concept of path dependence can simplify the complex factors influencing economic outcomes. This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped African economic growth, considering the diverse historical contexts and the intervening influences that affected outcomes.

Economic restructuring in sub-Saharan Africa has induced deep-rooted social changes and depeasantization, with noneconomic factors such as democratization and good governance playing pivotal roles in shaping African economic growth trajectories (Bryceson 2002, p.11; Bryant 2006, p.17). These changes are evident in the shift from family labor to local casual wage labor markets, as well as the increasing importance of nonagricultural activities in rural poverty alleviation. This depeasantization process is driven by the expansion of rural populations and the contraction of land availability, which compel rural communities to engage in diverse economic activities beyond traditional agriculture. The shift towards nonagricultural activities is both an economic adaptation and a social transformation, as it modifies the traditional structures of rural communities. This process exhibits variation across sub-Saharan Africa. The Ethiopian and Zimbabwean study areas, for instance, experienced recurrent food shortages and growing distress among poorer rural sections, indicating that economic restructuring alone does not guarantee poverty alleviation. The importance of noneconomic factors is further underscored by the need for good governance and democratization in managing these transitions effectively. This process presents challenges. The revisionist position, which dismisses Eurocentric views, is both empirically suspect and analytically incoherent, as it fails to account for the political and institutional constraints that shape economic outcomes.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). This mistrust is a persistent social phenomenon that continues to shape contemporary African societies. Nunn underscores this point by highlighting that the slave trades had adverse effects on economic development, which can be traced back to the institutional underdevelopment and instability of precolonial polities. This institutional legacy is crucial because it affected the ability of African societies to facilitate trade and economic development. The internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is at least twice as large as the external channel, indicating a deep-seated impact on social cohesion and economic interactions (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust, in turn, has profound implications for economic development, as it erodes the social capital necessary for collective action and economic growth. The historical evidence shows that only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans, suggesting that the legacy of the slave trade continues to hinder Africa's economic progress by undermining the very institutions needed for development.

Colonial institutions' role in shaping African economic growth trajectories is deeply intertwined with the extractive nature of these institutions. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) show that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas, leading to the establishment of forced labor systems and slave trades. This extractive framework was profitable for Europeans and had lasting impacts on the economic development of African societies. Nunn (2008, p.19) supports this by noting that only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans, indicating that the legacy of extractive institutions persists. These institutions, designed to extract resources with minimal investment in local development, have left a lasting imprint on African economies. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) argue that extractive institutions set up by colonialists persisted long after the colonial regime ended, continuing to hinder economic growth. The persistence of extractive institutions is evident in the continued use of monopolies and regulations that were created by colonial powers and remained intact after independence.

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is further complicated by the role of trust within societies. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.4) show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust. This internal mistrust erodes the social capital necessary for collective action and economic growth. Hopkins (2009, p.13) elaborates on this by noting that colonialism presided over non-productive institutions, which were not dismantled even after independence. This lack of institutional reform has significant implications for economic development, as it undermines the very foundations needed for growth. The remaining disagreement lies in the extent to which these institutions have persisted and their impact on current economic conditions. Austin (2008, p.2) argues that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than Acemoglu et al.'s formulations recognize. This disagreement is settled by the historical evidence presented by Nunn (2008, p.19), which shows that only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with Europeans, indicating that the legacy of extractive institutions persists. This historical evidence provides a clear measurement of the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories, demonstrating that the extractive nature of these institutions has left a lasting imprint on African economies.
