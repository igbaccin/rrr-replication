The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. The stakes are high because, depending on the answer, it may be possible to predict the likely impact of institutional reforms in Africa. The live disagreement is between two broad positions. The first argues that colonial institutions are primarily extractive and that their persistence has had a lasting negative impact on African economies. The second position holds that the causal relationships are more complex and that colonial institutions are not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. The interaction between these views is that they both offer explanations for Africa's economic outcomes but diverge on the extent to which colonial institutions are the primary cause. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. This is because colonial powers established institutions based on the economic profitability of alternative policies. Extractive institutions were more likely to be implemented in areas with high population density, where forced labor systems could be profitably established. This pattern is evident in regions like Meso-America and Africa, where the presence of abundant labor facilitated the establishment of extractive institutions. These institutions, in turn, influenced economic outcomes by affecting human capital accumulation and industrialization (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence further cemented their impact on economic growth. For example, in Latin America, the monopolies and regulations created by Spain remained intact for most of the nineteenth century, highlighting the long-term effects of colonial institutional legacies. This institutional persistence suggests that the economic trajectories of former colonies are deeply rooted in the institutional frameworks established during the colonial era.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Nunn (2008, p.28) both examine the long-term effects of colonial institutional frameworks on economic development in Africa. This view is supported by evidence that shows how historical institutional changes influenced economic outcomes. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.28) illustrates that trade with Europeans required sufficiently developed institutions, suggesting that societies with more advanced institutions were better positioned to facilitate trade and, by extension, economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.19). This implies that the institutional legacies of colonialism, such as centralized precolonial state institutions, played a significant role in shaping economic development trajectories. Austin (2008, p.1) argues that the causal relationships involved are more complex than previously recognized, emphasizing the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions affected economic growth (Austin 2008, p.2). The complexity of these relationships highlights the importance of examining growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence as such. This underscores the enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories, even as it suggests that the mechanisms by which these institutions operate are varied and context-specific.

The economic performance of colonial Africa was shaped by significant institutional changes and infrastructure development. The work of Austin (2007, p.16); (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) examines various aspects of economic performance in colonial Africa, including institutional changes and infrastructure development, but does not directly address how colonial institutions shaped African economic growth trajectories. For instance, Austin (2007, p.16) highlights the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939, which led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. Austin (2007, p.16) also notes that the length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles in 1930-32, and by 1930-31 'many lorries' were charging only six pence per ton mile, and the railway's share of cocoa exports had been competed down to 58 percent. However, Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also note that most work on institutions and economic performance examines incentives for investment and innovation, which focuses on the link to growing, whereas the link between institutions and shrinking has received much less attention (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This suggests that while colonial institutions facilitated certain forms of economic growth, they also contributed to economic shrinkage, highlighting the complex and multifaceted role of these institutions in shaping African economic trajectories.

The historical context and institutional dynamics have significantly shaped Africa's economic trajectory. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) both argue that the economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty. By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had made European settlement more feasible. This evolution highlights that the impact of colonial institutions on Africa's economic development was not uniform but varied over time. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.28) illustrate this variability through the Kenyan case, showing that differences in economic dynamics and income inequality between European settlers, Asian migrants, and native Africans shaped long-term growth trajectories. This suggests that the path-dependence of African economic development is complex and influenced by multiple factors, including the type of economic dynamics and varying degrees of income inequality. Furthermore, Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that the impact of colonial institutions has been relatively small due to the short period of effective European occupation, in contrast to nearly five centuries of precolonial slave trading. This qualification underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of the role of colonial institutions in Africa's economic development, recognizing that their impact was not persistent but rather influenced by the duration and nature of European occupation.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) claim that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust that persists in Africa, impacting contemporary economic growth trajectories. This mistrust is rooted in the historical exploitation and violence associated with the slave trade, which created deep-seated social and psychological barriers. The slave trade's legacy of mistrust manifests in various social and economic interactions, where individuals are less likely to engage in cooperative behaviors that are essential for economic development. This mistrust is particularly pronounced in regions with a higher historical intensity of slave trading activities. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) further highlight that the systematic codification of patriarchy during colonial rule exacerbated this mistrust, as patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and local elites systematically marginalized women. This dynamic contributed to a broader social environment where trust was eroded, further hindering economic growth. The erosion of trust, both internally and externally, has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering cooperation, reducing transaction costs, and promoting economic activities. This persistent mistrust, therefore, acts as a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa.

The persistence of colonial institutions has significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories, with extractive institutions playing a pivotal role. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas, leading to their establishment in these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This dynamic is further supported by Broadberry and Gardner, who argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that the extractive institutions were not only present but also effective in extracting resources (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The persistence of these institutions into the independence era has been well-documented, with Acemoglu et al. providing examples of how extractive policies remained intact long after colonial rule ended (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This historical continuity highlights the long-term impact of colonial institutions on African economies.

The role of trust in economic development is another critical factor influenced by colonial institutions. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust, both internally and externally, has profound implications for economic development, as trust is a critical factor in fostering cooperation, reducing transaction costs, and promoting economic activities. The erosion of trust, therefore, acts as a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). Hopkins underscores the importance of understanding the historical context of colonialism, noting that bundling all colonialism into one undifferentiated period glosses over the considerable disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This historical nuance is crucial for understanding the varied impacts of colonial institutions on different regions and periods.

The debate over the 'reversal of fortune' thesis adds another layer to the discussion. Austin argues that the thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective is supported by Hopkins, who notes that the new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The precise historical evidence or empirical comparison that settles the disagreement lies in the long-term historical data, which shows the varied impacts of colonial institutions on different regions and periods. This data highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the historical context and the specific institutional arrangements that were in place during colonial rule.
