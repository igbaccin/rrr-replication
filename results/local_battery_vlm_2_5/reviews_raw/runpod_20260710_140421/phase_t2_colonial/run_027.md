The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a pivotal question in understanding the continent's economic development. At stake is the extent to which historical institutional legacies influence contemporary economic outcomes, and whether current policies can mitigate or leverage these legacies. One live disagreement revolves around the persistence of extractive institutions established during colonial rule and their impact on economic development. Some scholars argue that these institutions have a long-lasting negative effect on economic growth, while others contend that the relationship is more complex and nuanced, involving multiple historical and contextual factors. A related debate focuses on whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed primarily to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. The debate is not merely academic; it has significant implications for policy-making and economic development strategies in Africa. The question of whether colonial institutions are the primary determinant of economic outcomes or whether other factors, such as geographic conditions or cultural practices, play a more significant role remains unresolved. This question is central to understanding the complex interplay between historical legacies and contemporary economic development in Africa. It is clear that the persistence of extractive institutions, as well as the broader impact of colonial rule, are critical factors in shaping the economic trajectories of African nations. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that colonial institutions significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. They posit that the type of institutions introduced during colonial rule was influenced by economic factors, particularly the profitability of extractive institutions in areas with high population density. In regions where extractive institutions were more profitable, Europeans were more likely to implement them. This dynamic is evident in the establishment of forced labor systems in Meso-America and the slave trade in Africa, both of which were driven by the availability of labor and the economic opportunities they presented. The persistence of these extractive institutions post-independence further solidified their impact on economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further elucidate that the correlation between extractive institutions and low human capital levels reflects a primary mechanism through which these institutions delayed industrialization. The masses in societies with extractive institutions typically did not or could not obtain education, which hindered economic progress. Thus, the institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with enduring effects on human capital and industrial development.

Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16; 2008, p.1); (Austin 2007, p.16) argue that colonial institutional changes in factor markets, such as land and labour, played a pivotal role in shaping economic growth trajectories in Africa. The shift from abundant land to localised scarcity in Ghana, for instance, led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. This institutional change was driven by the increasing scarcity of land, which led to the creation of new markets and the mechanisation of goods transport. Austin (2007, p.16) notes that the length of motorable roads tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles in 1930-32, facilitating the movement of goods and people. This infrastructural development, coupled with the emergence of new markets, significantly altered the economic landscape of Ghana. However, Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this by arguing that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than recognised in previous formulations, suggesting that the impact of these institutional changes was not uniform across different regions and periods. This differentiation highlights the complexity of colonial institutional changes and their varied effects on economic growth trajectories in Africa.

Frankema's analysis of colonial education policies shows that British rule in Africa promoted formal education more extensively than other colonial powers (Frankema 2012, p.15; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This educational investment was driven by the British policy of indirect rule, which relied on local institutions to administer colonial territories. British authorities welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their indirect rule system, leading to higher primary-school enrollment rates in British West Africa compared to French West Africa. This educational infrastructure laid the groundwork for subsequent economic development by equipping the local population with skills and knowledge necessary for modern economic activities. Broadberry and Gardner's examination of economic growth in Anglophone Sub-Saharan Africa further underscores this point. The authors highlight that by the early twentieth century, many Anglophone African economies had achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This economic performance was partly driven by the educational investments made during the colonial period. However, Broadberry and Gardner also note that improving long-run performance in Africa requires reducing the rate and frequency of economic shrinking, not just increasing growth rates (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This qualification suggests that while colonial education policies had positive effects on economic development, the overall economic trajectory in Africa is influenced by a complex interplay of factors beyond education alone. The legacy of colonial education policies continues to shape African economies today, as the skills and knowledge acquired during the colonial period remain integral to economic activities in the region.

The broader context of African economic history and institutional reforms offers insights into how various factors have influenced Africa's development. Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) agree that African poverty has not been a persistent historical phenomenon due to structural impediments. The economic history of Africa, particularly the colonial period, has been marked by significant disparities in institutional development. Hopkins notes that the period of colonialism can be divided into two distinct phases: the mercantilist phase, where productive institutions were scarce, and the modern phase, where both productive and extractive institutions coexisted. This division highlights the evolving nature of colonial institutions and their impact on African economies. However, Frankema and van Waijenburg argue that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic development has been relatively small compared to the long-term effects of precolonial slave trading. This perspective suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions, while significant, is not the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. The Kenyan case study, for instance, illustrates how varying degrees of income inequality between different ethnic and migrant groups have shaped long-term growth trajectories. This finding underscores the complexity of African economic development, where multiple factors, including colonial institutions, interact to influence economic outcomes.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice were crucial in fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. This perspective, shared by North (North and Weingast 1989, p.30; North 1989, p.9), underscores the role of institutional development in economic growth. The financial revolution, with the creation of the Bank of England and new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights (North 1989, p.9). This institutional framework not only facilitated long-term capital markets but also laid the groundwork for sustained economic growth. North further elaborates that the development of complex institutions to manage increasing interdependence is not automatic. This insight suggests that the path to economic growth is fraught with challenges, requiring specific institutional arrangements to overcome opportunism and ensure long-term economic stability (North 1989, p.3). This perspective underscores the complexity of institutional development and its critical role in economic growth, providing a framework that can be applied to understand the impact of colonial institutions on African economic trajectories.

The slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today and significantly impacts trust levels (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). The mechanisms through which the slave trade undermined trust are complex, involving both internal and external channels. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) identify the internal channel as particularly significant, suggesting that the disruption of social cohesion and the erosion of interpersonal trust within African societies were profound. This internal mistrust, deeply rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, has far-reaching implications for contemporary economic development. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.28) highlights how the adverse effects of the slave trade are not limited to economic underdevelopment but also include the underdevelopment of precolonial state institutions. This institutional weakness, exacerbated by the legacy of mistrust, further hinders Africa's economic growth. The findings of Nunn are particularly relevant as they show that the adverse effects of the slave trade are not merely economic but also institutional, impacting the ability of African states to provide essential public goods such as education, health, and infrastructure. This institutional underdevelopment, coupled with the persistent mistrust, creates a vicious cycle that continues to impede Africa's economic trajectory. The enduring impact of the slave trade on African economic development underscores the need for comprehensive strategies that address both the economic and institutional legacies of this historical trauma.

The colonial period left a profound and enduring mark on African economic growth trajectories, with institutions playing a pivotal role in shaping these outcomes. Acemoglu et al. show that extractive institutions, which prioritized the exploitation of resources and labor for colonial benefit, were more likely to be established in areas with high population density and abundant labor (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). This institutional framework persisted long after colonial rule ended, as evidenced by the continuation of extractive policies and structures in post-independence states (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The persistence of these institutions is a critical factor in understanding the current economic conditions in many African countries. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the economic fortunes of formerly rich areas declined due to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the causal relationships involved (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped economic development over time. Hopkins concurs that colonialism introduced non-productive institutions, but also suggests that the short duration of colonial rule in some areas meant that these institutions were not fully entrenched (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The historical evidence shows that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a detailed examination of the specific institutional frameworks established during the colonial period and their long-term effects.

The legacy of colonial institutions is not limited to economic factors but also encompasses social and cultural dimensions. Nunn and Wantchekon demonstrate that the slave trade, a key aspect of colonial exploitation, fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3). This mistrust persists to this day and is a significant barrier to economic development, as it hampers cooperation and collective action. The institutional underdevelopment and persistent mistrust create a vicious cycle that continues to impede Africa's economic trajectory.
