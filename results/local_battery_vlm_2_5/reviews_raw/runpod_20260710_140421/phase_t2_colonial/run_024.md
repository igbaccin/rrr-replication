Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, and the extent of their influence remains a contentious issue among scholars. The debate centres on whether colonial institutions are primarily extractive, hindering economic development, or if they can also be productive, fostering growth. One position argues that colonial institutions, particularly those established in high-mortality regions, were inherently extractive and persisted post-independence, significantly impacting current economic conditions. A rival position posits that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is more nuanced, involving complex interactions over time and varying across regions. The stakes are high, as understanding the role of colonial institutions can inform policy decisions aimed at fostering economic development and reducing poverty. What is clear is that the legacy of colonial institutions continues to influence African economies today, and unpacking this legacy is crucial for devising effective strategies for future growth. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are a key factor in shaping African economic growth trajectories. They assert that extractive institutions, which were more profitable in densely populated areas, were often established by European colonizers. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) explain that high population density made extractive institutions more profitable. For example, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America was conducive to the establishment of forced labor systems, while the relatively high population density in Africa created a profit opportunity for slave traders in supplying labor to American plantations. This pattern of extractive institution establishment was not limited to labor systems; other types of extractive institutions were also more profitable in densely settled and prosperous areas where there was more to be extracted by European colonists. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) further argue that the persistence of extractive institutions significantly delayed industrialization. They suggest that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which was a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions delayed industrialization. This finding is consistent with the broader argument that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have had a lasting impact on economic development in African countries.

Austin (2008, p.1; 2007, p.16) argue that African economic growth trajectories cannot be reduced to a singular causal mechanism. This is because the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that African poverty is a result of colonial institutions, oversimplifies the complex interplay of historical factors. The thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, ignoring the nuanced relationships that recur due to similar situations and path dependence. Austin (2008, p.1) shows that examining growth theories against long-term history reveals these differentiated causal relationships. Austin (2007, p.16) supports this by demonstrating that the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939 led to an institutional revolution in factor markets. This shift was marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. The institutional changes in Ghana varied across regions. In Ashanti, for instance, indications of land scarcity emerged by the end of the 1930s, leading to policies that aimed to ban the creation of new farms. This regional variation highlights the complexity of institutional changes and their impact on economic growth. The institutional revolution in Ghana also saw significant improvements in transport infrastructure, with the length of motorable roads tripling from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles between 1930 and 1932. This infrastructure development facilitated the mechanisation of goods transport, except from farm to local market, where dependence on head loading commonly continued. The varied responses to land scarcity and the differential impact of transport infrastructure on economic growth underscore the need for a more differentiated understanding of how historical institutional changes have shaped African economic growth trajectories.

Hopkins and others (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11) argue that the economic history of Africa offers valuable lessons for understanding institutional reforms. Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights that colonialism in Africa evolved significantly over time, with distinct phases marked by different institutional outcomes. In the early mercantilist phase, productive institutions were scarce, and colonial powers lacked the capacity to implement them. However, by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances facilitated European settlement. This evolution suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth varied across different historical periods. The historical creation of poverty, as Hopkins (2019, p.11) explains, was driven by European capitalism, which integrated other societies into networks of dependence and exploited them through unequal exchange. British colonial policies, as Frankema (2012, p.15) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) share a finding, were more favorable to educational development in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other colonial powers, highlighting the nuanced impact of colonial institutions. This is evident in the differing educational trajectories of British and French West Africa, where British colonies had higher primary-school enrolment rates and lower government expenses per student. The British approach to colonial education was marked by a separation of state and church, which allowed for a more inclusive educational system. This approach welcomed missionaries of all denominations to support their system of indirect rule, facilitating the spread of education. The French, on the other hand, restricted missionary activities, leading to lower educational development. This pattern suggests that the type of colonial rule significantly influenced educational outcomes. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions shaped African economic trajectories.

Colonial Africa's economic performance was more nuanced than previously thought, as Broadberry and Gardner along with Akyeampong and Fofack (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.4) highlight. While Broadberry and Gardner focus on the economic growth in Anglophone Africa, Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize the significant role of African women in this growth. The presence of African women in economic activities was a central factor in economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack identify a specific mechanism by which African women contributed to economic growth: their involvement in production and reproduction processes. Broadberry and Gardner note that improving long-run performance in Africa requires reducing the rate and frequency of economic shrinkage (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.17). This suggests that while African women's contributions were significant, the broader economic context, including institutional factors, also played a crucial role in shaping economic trajectories. The institutional factors that Akyeampong and Fofack cite as disadvantageous to women, such as patriarchal alliances between colonial administrations and African chiefs, also influenced broader economic dynamics. This indicates that while African women's economic contributions were vital, the overall economic performance was shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including institutional dynamics that both supported and hindered growth.

The slave trade has left enduring institutional and cultural legacies that continue to shape African economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon, along with Nunn, argue that the slave trade created a culture of mistrust and weakened institutional development, which persist today and account for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). The slave trade disrupted societal structures and undermined the development of strong, centralized institutions, which are crucial for economic growth. This disruption is evident in the historical record, where only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to engage in trade with Europeans (Nunn 2008, p.19). The internal channel of mistrust, stemming from the slave trade, is at least twice as large as the external channel, highlighting the profound impact of internal dynamics on economic development (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This internal mistrust has cascading effects on various aspects of economic development, including international trade and political institutions. Moreover, the legacy of the slave trade has implications for contemporary economic policies and institutional reforms, as trust remains a critical factor in economic development. The enduring effects of the slave trade on African economic development underscore the need for policies that address the historical legacies of mistrust and institutional weakness.

These institutions persisted long after independence, hindering economic development by impeding investment and innovation. The persistence of these extractive institutions is further supported by Hopkins (2009, p.12), who notes that colonial rulers often did not have the time or inclination to dismantle existing unproductive institutions. This historical legacy of extractive institutions has had a profound impact on contemporary African economies, as they continue to struggle with institutional weaknesses that impede growth.
