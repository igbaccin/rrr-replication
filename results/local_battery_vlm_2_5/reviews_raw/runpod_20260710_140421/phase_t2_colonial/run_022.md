A central question in the study of African economic growth is the role played by colonial institutions. At stake is the extent to which current economic trajectories are shaped by historical institutional legacies and how these might inform contemporary policy and development strategies. One prominent position holds that colonial institutions, characterized by extractive practices, have had a lasting impact on African economies. A different perspective underscores the role of cultural factors, such as trust, in molding economic outcomes. These positions conflict on the primacy of institutions versus cultural factors in determining economic growth. The literature argues that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35; Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) detail that extractive institutions were particularly profitable in densely populated areas, leading Europeans to implement forced labor systems and other exploitative practices. This dynamic was driven by the economic profitability of such institutions, which capitalized on the availability of labor and resources. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) further illustrate that the institutions established during colonialism persisted well into the post-independence era, forming the basis for current governance structures. These institutions often remained extractive, continuing to hinder economic development. The persistence of extractive institutions into the independence era is evidenced by the continuation of monopolies and regulations, which remained intact for much of the nineteenth century in Latin America (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). This pattern suggests that the legacy of colonial institutions can be seen in the enduring governance structures of former colonies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) argue that extractive institutions contributed to low levels of human capital, which in turn delayed industrialization. This mechanism illustrates how colonial institutions affected economic growth directly and indirectly by influencing human capital development. The enduring impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth trajectories is evident in the persistence of extractive practices and their lasting effects on governance and development.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Hopkins (2019, p.11) both agree that the economic history of Africa provides a nuanced understanding of the continent's development challenges, yet it does not directly elucidate how colonial institutions shaped African economic growth trajectories. They jointly establish the importance of distinguishing between different phases of colonialism, noting that the nature of institutions varied significantly over time. Hopkins (2009, p.12) highlights that early colonialism lacked productive institutions, whereas later phases saw the emergence of both productive and extractive institutions. This temporal distinction is crucial for understanding the varying impacts of colonial rule on African economies. Hopkins (2019, p.11) advises caution against overly simplistic views of path dependence, which can obscure the complex interplay of factors influencing economic outcomes. This perspective underscores the need for a more dynamic analysis of colonial institutions, one that accounts for the evolving nature of colonial rule and its multifaceted effects on African economies.

Economic performance under colonial rule in Africa was significantly influenced by the development of infrastructure, which facilitated trade and economic activity. Austin (2007, p.16) and Broadberry and Gardner (Austin 2007, p.16; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10) highlight that the construction of roads and railways, as well as the mechanisation of goods transport, played a crucial role in integrating local economies and enhancing productivity. The expansion of motorable roads in Ghana, for instance, tripled from 2,241 miles to 6,738 miles between 1930 and 1932, enabling more efficient transportation of goods and people (Austin 2007, p.8). This infrastructure development aimed to foster economic growth by reducing transportation costs and improving market access. The benefits of these infrastructure developments varied. The economic performance in Anglophone African economies by the early twentieth century reveals that while some regions saw significant growth, others remained stagnant (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This disparity underscores the complex interplay of factors, including local conditions and colonial policies, that shaped economic outcomes during this period. For example, the development of formal education systems, which varied widely across different colonial powers, also played a role in shaping economic trajectories. British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education in sub-Saharan Africa, which had downstream implications for economic growth (Frankema 2012, p.4).

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.4) and Bryceson (2002, p.11) argue that gender dynamics and social transformations have been pivotal in shaping African economic development. African women's contributions to economic growth and development have been significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Colonial administrations and African chiefs and elders formed patriarchal alliances that systematically codified and reinforced patriarchy across African societies. This institutionalization of patriarchy had profound implications for gender dynamics and economic growth, as it often marginalized women's economic roles. For example, the dependence on family labor in rural areas has been replaced by local casual wage labor markets, which has led to increasing importance of nonagricultural activities in rural poverty alleviation. These social changes, driven by economic restructuring, have had deep-rooted impacts on African societies, including the depeasantization process. This process involves the shift from traditional agricultural practices to more diversified economic activities, which has been crucial for economic growth and development in sub-Saharan Africa. The increasing importance of nonagricultural activities in rural poverty alleviation illustrates how social changes can drive economic growth.

North (1989, p.9) and North and Weingast (1989, p.30) show that institutions played a crucial role in fostering economic growth in Europe during the seventeenth century. They argue that the evolution of financial institutions, such as the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, significantly reduced transaction costs and enhanced the security of property rights. This institutional framework was pivotal in enabling long-term capital markets and sustaining economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.5) and North (1989, p.3) builds on this argument by emphasizing that the development of complex institutions responds to the increasing complexity of interdependence in society. This perspective underscores the importance of institutional design in facilitating economic growth, as institutions must evolve to address the challenges posed by growing interdependence. Furthermore, the financial revolution in England, marked by the establishment of the Bank of England, exemplified how institutional changes could dramatically lower transaction costs and bolster economic growth. This financial revolution supported England's long-term success and highlighted the necessity of institutions in achieving both economic growth and political freedom. This nuanced view of institutional development highlights the dynamic nature of economic growth and the critical role that institutions play in shaping its trajectory.

The slave trade's legacy in Africa persists through cultural and institutional factors that continue to influence economic growth trajectories. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3) and Nunn (2008, p.28) argue that the slave trade fostered a culture of mistrust, which hinders economic development. This mistrust manifests in various ways, including reduced social capital and weakened community cohesion. For instance, Nunn (2008, p.19) shows that the slave trade disrupted pre-existing social structures and trade networks, making it difficult for African societies to rebuild and develop economically. The institutional and cultural legacies of the slave trade, such as mistrust and weakened social structures, persist and continue to affect economic growth trajectories. These legacies create barriers to economic development by undermining social capital and hindering the establishment of effective institutions. The effects of the slave trade continue to shape Africa's economic landscape today.

Colonial institutions have played a pivotal role in shaping African economic growth trajectories, with their legacies persisting long after independence. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8) show that extractive institutions, established during colonial rule, have endured and significantly impacted current income per capita. These institutions, characterized by exploitative policies and weak property rights, have hindered economic development by stifling investment and innovation. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the continued presence of monopolies and regulations that were initially implemented by colonial powers (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). The extractive nature of these institutions is further underscored by their profitability, which was enhanced by high population densities that provided a labor supply for forced labor systems (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35).

The impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth is also evident in the cultural and social legacies they have left behind. Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.3; 2011, p.4) demonstrate that the slave trade, a key aspect of colonial exploitation, has fostered a culture of mistrust within African societies. This mistrust, a direct result of the exploitative practices of the slave trade, continues to undermine social capital and hinder economic development. The persistence of these cultural legacies is a significant barrier to economic growth, as they create an environment of distrust that impedes cooperation and collective action. The mistrust engendered by the slave trade is a persistent legacy that accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4).

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is further complicated by the interaction between these institutions and the broader economic and social context. Hopkins (2009, p.12; 2009, p.13) highlights the disparity between mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism, noting that by the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present. This period saw medical advances that removed some constraints on European settlement, altering the dynamics of colonial policy towards Africa. The persistence of extractive institutions is due to both colonial rule and pre-existing unproductive institutions (Hopkins 2009, p.13). The interaction between these pre-existing institutions and the extractive policies of colonial powers has created a complex legacy that continues to shape Africa's economic landscape.

The precise role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a subject of ongoing debate. While there is agreement that colonial institutions have had a lasting impact, the extent to which these institutions have directly caused economic stagnation remains a point of contention. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) argue that extractive institutions were more profitable in densely settled areas, leading to their establishment in these regions. Hopkins (2009, p.13) suggests that colonial rulers lacked the time to dismantle the unproductive institutions that were already in place. The historical evidence of the profitability of extractive institutions in densely settled areas, as documented by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35), provides a clearer picture of the direct causal role of colonial institutions in shaping economic growth trajectories. This evidence shows that the profitability of extractive institutions was a key factor in their establishment and persistence, highlighting the direct impact of colonial institutions on economic development.
