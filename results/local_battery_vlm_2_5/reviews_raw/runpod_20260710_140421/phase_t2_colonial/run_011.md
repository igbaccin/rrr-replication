The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories remains a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how historical legacies influence contemporary economic outcomes, and whether institutions established during colonial rule have a persistent impact on economic development. The debate centres on whether colonial institutions were primarily extractive, designed to benefit the colonizers at the expense of the colonized, or whether they laid the groundwork for modern economic institutions. One position argues that colonial institutions were inherently extractive and have had a lasting negative impact on African economies. This view emphasizes the persistence of these institutions and their role in stifling economic growth. Another position suggests that the impact of colonial institutions is more nuanced, with some positive effects that have contributed to economic development. This perspective acknowledges the complexity of colonial rule and the varied institutional legacies it left behind. Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.8; 2002, p.35) argue that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism have significantly shaped African economic growth trajectories. These changes varied based on the economic profitability of alternative policies. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) explain that extractive institutions were more likely to be established when they were profitable for European colonizers. This profitability was influenced by factors such as high population density, which provided a supply of labor that could be forced to work in agriculture or mining. For instance, the presence of abundant Amerindian labor in Meso-America facilitated forced labor systems, while high population density in Africa created opportunities for slave traders. This persistence had significant implications for economic development. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35) further note that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which delayed industrialization. This low level of human capital was a primary mechanism through which extractive institutions hindered economic growth. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) qualify that the correlation between institutions and human capital reflects the broader impact of extractive institutions on societal development, suggesting that the legacy of colonial institutions is complex and multifaceted.

The adverse effects of Africa's slave trades are proposed as a rival explanation for African underdevelopment, challenging those who attribute it solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.28). The slave trade's impact on economic development is rooted in a culture of mistrust, which persists to this day. These institutions were crucial for economic success, as societies with centralized precolonial state institutions today provide more public goods, such as education, health, and infrastructure. Nunn (2008, p.28) further notes that a large proportion of the early trade between Africans and Europeans was in commodities other than slaves, indicating that only societies with sufficiently developed institutions were able to facilitate trade with the Europeans. This historical evidence underscores the significance of precolonial institutions in shaping economic development trajectories. However, the persistence of mistrust and underdevelopment suggests that the legacy of the slave trade is complex and multifaceted, extending beyond the immediate economic impacts.

The literature on the economic history of Africa and institutional reforms highlights the importance of understanding historical context and development challenges, even though it does not directly address how colonial institutions explain African economic growth trajectories (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Hopkins 2019, p.11). Hopkins (2009, p.12) argues that colonialism brought about a significant shift in the nature of institutions, from a period where "good" institutions scarcely existed to a later phase where both productive and extractive institutions were present. This shift was influenced by medical advances that reduced the constraints on European settlement, altering colonial policy towards Africa. The timing and nature of colonial institutions thus played a crucial role in shaping African economic development. Hopkins (2019, p.11) underscores the challenges in using instrumental variables and the pitfalls of path dependence, suggesting that these methods can lead to oversimplifications and a deterministic view of history. This critique implies that the relationship between colonial institutions and economic growth is more complex and nuanced than often portrayed. The complexity of these historical influences means that the legacy of colonial institutions on African economic growth is multifaceted and requires a detailed understanding of the specific contexts and mechanisms at play.

British rule fostered formal education in sub-Saharan Africa more effectively than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This effectiveness was rooted in the British colonial strategy of indirect rule, which involved collaborating with local institutions and Christian missions to provide educational services. The British promoted missionary schools, which led to higher primary-school enrollment rates compared to French colonies. This was evident in the 1938 statistics where British West Africa had a 7.1% enrollment rate, nearly double that of French West Africa at 3.6% (Frankema 2012, p.4). This educational development, however, did not uniformly translate into sustained economic growth. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012, p.4) argue that the varying degrees of income inequality and differing economic dynamics within and across African regions complicate this trajectory (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). The colonial educational legacy, while foundational, does not singularly determine economic outcomes, highlighting the multifaceted nature of African economic development.

The 'reversal of fortune' thesis, as elaborated by Austin (2008, p.1; 2007, p.16), underscores the need for differentiated causal relationships in African economic development. This perspective draws on long-term historical analysis to reveal recurring relationships and path dependence, moving beyond the simplifications of the reversal of fortune thesis. Austin (2008, p.1; 2007, p.16) emphasizes the importance of examining growth theories against the backdrop of extended historical periods, thereby illuminating intricate causal pathways that are often overlooked. This approach highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development in Africa, where historical trajectories and institutional changes play pivotal roles. For instance, Austin's (2007, p.8) examination of Ghana during 1874-1939 illustrates how shifts from land abundance to localised scarcity led to institutional revolutions in factor markets. These changes were marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour, demonstrating how specific historical contexts can drive institutional transformations. The extent to which these institutional changes translate into sustained economic growth is a nuanced issue. For example, the adoption of policies to ban new farms in response to land scarcity in Ghana, as noted by Austin (2007, p.16), shows the complexities involved in implementing and sustaining such policies. This underscores the importance of considering the broader historical and institutional contexts when evaluating the impact of colonial institutions on African economic growth.

The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) and North (1989, p.9) argue that financial innovations, such as the creation of the Bank of England and the development of new financial instruments, played a critical role in England's long-term economic success. These institutions reduced the cost of transacting, which reflects increased security of the time dimension of property rights. This security is critical for both a long-term capital market and economic growth itself. The financial revolution in England is a clear example of how institutional changes can drive economic development. This perspective contrasts with neoclassical theories that emphasize population and savings as the principal determinants of economic growth. The English financial revolution demonstrates that institutions matter in economic growth by providing the necessary framework for secure property rights and efficient financial transactions. The development of these institutions is not automatic. North (1989, p.3) highlights that even with third-party enforcement, institutions face challenges such as opportunism and cheating, which can hinder their effectiveness. This underscores the complexity of institutional development and the need for continuous adaptation and enforcement mechanisms. The implications of this for African economic growth trajectories are significant, as they suggest that the adoption of secure property rights and financial innovations can foster long-term economic success, provided that the institutional framework is robust and adaptable.

The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is profound, with evidence showing that these institutions have had lasting impacts on economic development. Acemoglu et al. show that European colonists established extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates, which persisted and significantly affected current income per capita (Acemoglu et al. 2001, p.8). These extractive institutions, characterized by forced labor and monopolies, were more profitable in densely settled areas, creating a profit opportunity for European colonists (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The persistence of these institutions long after colonial rule ended underscores their enduring influence on economic trajectories. Austin argues that the reversal of fortune thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1; Austin 2008, p.2). This view underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions have shaped economic development in Africa.

Hopkins points out that colonialism in Africa can be divided into two phases: mercantilist and modern forms of imperialism. In the first phase, productive institutions scarcely existed, even in Europe, so could scarcely have been an option overseas (Hopkins 2009, p.12). By the late nineteenth century, both productive and extractive institutions were present, and medical advances had removed some of the constraints on European settlement. This differentiation in phases of colonialism highlights the varying impacts of colonial institutions on economic development. Nunn and Wantchekon show that the slave trade caused a culture of mistrust to develop within Africa, which persists to this day and accounts for at least half of the reduced-form effect of the slave trade on trust (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.3; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This culture of mistrust has significant implications for economic development, as trust is crucial for economic growth and political institutions.
