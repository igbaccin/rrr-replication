The role of colonial institutions in shaping African economic growth trajectories is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the intellectual challenge of understanding how historical legacies, particularly those stemming from colonial rule, continue to influence contemporary economic outcomes. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which colonial institutions, specifically extractive ones, have persisted and shaped economic development. Some scholars argue that these institutions have had a profound and lasting impact on economic growth, while others contend that the effects are more nuanced and complex, involving multiple historical periods and paths. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex causal relationships at play in African economic growth trajectories. Austin (2008, p.1) and Austin (2007, p.16) share a critical stance against the idea that colonial institutions were the sole determinant of Africa's economic trajectory. Austin and Austin argue that the causal relationships involved are more differentiated than previously recognised. This critique is anchored in the need to examine growth theories against long-term history, revealing relationships that recur because the situations are similar, as well as because of path dependence. Austin illustrates this by examining the shift from land abundance to localised scarcity in Ghana during 1874-1939. This shift led to an institutional revolution in factor markets, marked by the emergence of widespread markets in land use rights and hired labour. Austin highlights that even in regions with lower population density, such as Ashanti, there were indications of land scarcity by the end of the 1930s, which led to policies aimed at managing land use. This institutional change was driven by localised scarcity and not solely by colonial institutions. Austin suggests that the relationships involved are more differentiated than the 'reversal of fortune' thesis recognises, implying that local factors and path dependence play a significant role in shaping economic trajectories. The implication is that African economic growth trajectories are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including localised scarcity and institutional changes driven by internal dynamics, rather than being solely determined by colonial institutions. This nuanced view underscores the importance of considering multiple factors and historical contexts when analyzing African economic growth.

British rule in sub-Saharan Africa fostered formal education more effectively than other colonial powers (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10; Frankema 2012, p.15). This pattern is evident in the differing educational development trajectories under British and French colonial regimes. The British approach to education was marked by a more inclusive policy that welcomed missionaries of all denominations, which supported their system of indirect rule. This policy contrast is exemplified by the diverging educational trajectories in French and British West Africa. In 1938, primary-school enrolment rates in French West Africa were roughly half of that in British West Africa. This discrepancy was further highlighted by the average government expense per student, which was considerably higher in the French colonies. This educational disparity had significant implications for post-colonial economic development. The link between institutions and economic performance is complex. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) also point out that much of the work on institutions and economic performance focuses on incentives for investment and innovation, which is tied to economic growth. This focus may overlook the role of institutions in mitigating economic shrinkage, a critical factor in long-term economic performance. The educational policies under British rule, therefore, not only fostered economic growth but also potentially provided a buffer against economic decline.

Hopkins (2009, p.12) and Frankema and van Waijenburg (Hopkins 2009, p.12; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4) jointly argue that the historical context of African economic development is crucial for understanding the continent's economic trajectories. Both authors highlight that the diversity in colonial experiences, particularly the shift from mercantilist to modern forms of imperialism, played a significant role in shaping African institutions and economic outcomes. Hopkins notes that by the late nineteenth century, medical advances had facilitated European settlement, altering colonial policies towards Africa and potentially influencing the type of institutions established (Hopkins 2009, p.12). This shift is pivotal because it suggests that the timing and nature of colonial intervention mattered significantly in determining the institutional landscape. Frankema and van Waijenburg support this view by demonstrating that the impact of colonialism varied across regions, with some areas experiencing more profound institutional changes than others (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.4). This variability in colonial experiences implies that the economic trajectories of African countries were not uniformly determined by colonial rule but rather by the specific institutional legacies left behind. Frankema and van Waijenburg also point out that the differences in long-term growth trajectories within Africa call for a more nuanced understanding of the path-dependence nature of African economic development (Frankema and van Waijenburg 2012, p.28). This nuanced view suggests that while colonial institutions were influential, they were not the sole determinants of Africa's economic path.

This perspective is supported by Ogilvie (2007, p.27) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.2), who argue that the institutional frameworks of pre-industrial Europe shaped economic outcomes through complex interdependencies. This perspective contrasts with the view that institutions in pre-industrial Europe were shaped by efficiency considerations, and instead focuses on the role of distributional conflicts and the broader institutional framework. Ogilvie (2007, p.27) points out that institutions such as serfdom, the community, and craft guilds were part of an interconnected system, with each institution influencing and being influenced by others. This interdependency implies that the economic performance of these societies was not merely a result of individual institutions but of the entire framework. This pattern of institutional evolution, where elites were able to maintain their status through restrictive policies, directly impacted economic growth. However, North and Weingast (1989, p.30) suggest that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, particularly in seventeenth-century England, was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This implies that while distributional conflicts and institutional interdependencies were significant, the specific institutional changes that ensured secure property rights were also vital for long-term economic development.

The colonial institutions imposed on African societies by European powers have had a profound and lasting impact on economic growth trajectories. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.35; 2001, p.8) show that the establishment of extractive institutions in colonies with high mortality rates was a direct result of European colonial policies. These institutions, designed to maximize resource extraction with minimal investment in local development, have persisted long after independence. The persistence of these extractive institutions is evident in the continued reliance on resource extraction and the lack of significant industrial development in many African economies today. This institutional legacy has created a cycle of underdevelopment, where the lack of investment in human capital and infrastructure perpetuates economic stagnation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35). The extractive nature of these institutions has also led to a mistrust of government and institutions, further hindering economic development by impeding cooperation and collective action (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011, p.4). This mistrust is deeply rooted in the historical trauma of the slave trade, which disrupted precolonial institutions and led to a loss of human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.35).
