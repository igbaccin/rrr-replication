The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue, with scholars debating whether gender dynamics are a primary driver or a secondary factor in economic growth. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether gender equality is a catalyst for economic development or a consequence of it. One position argues that gender equality, particularly in education and employment, directly fosters economic growth by increasing human capital and productivity. One perspective suggests that economic development fosters greater gender equality, as rising incomes and improved living standards diminish the need for traditional gender roles. This debate is crucial because it informs policies aimed at sustainable economic development and social justice. Understanding the causal relationship between gender and development can guide effective interventions to promote both economic growth and gender equality. The interplay between gender dynamics and long-term economic performance is complex, involving factors such as human capital investment, institutional efficiency, and the skill premium. Goldin (2006, p.3); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes. This transformation was not merely evolutionary; it involved a shift from women working out of necessity to those seeking careers, marking a significant change in both horizon and human capital investment. Women's roles in the labor force evolved from piece workers in manufacturing and service sectors to more professional occupations, particularly in teaching and clerical work. This historical perspective highlights that women's economic participation was a persistent factor in long-term economic development, not just a modern phenomenon. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) also observe that historical plough use correlates with unequal gender roles today, indicating that while women's contributions have been substantial, gender norms and labor force participation are influenced by deep-seated cultural factors. This indicates that the quiet revolution in women's employment, while transformative, has been shaped by enduring cultural and historical contexts.

The institutional impact on economic growth in New World societies highlights how unequal power distributions shaped by institutions led to policies that restricted competition and maintained inequality (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). In New World societies, institutional frameworks often reinforced the power of elites, leading to economic structures that favored large-scale plantations and slave labor, which in turn created extremely unequal distributions of wealth and human capital (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic of institutional support for elite interests, which restricted competition and maintained inequality, is further illustrated by the fact that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This lack of education, coupled with the institutional barriers, further entrenched the inequality, as the majority of the population was unable to accumulate the human capital necessary for economic advancement.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) share the view that labor allocation between agriculture and industry is a critical driver of economic growth. This disequilibrium is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence. Low income in the standard story is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates. The developmental deficit highlighted here is produced by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner further illustrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, contrary to previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This is contrary to the view expressed in early economic histories of Africa, which assumed that any economic growth above subsistence resulted from European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). The implications of these findings highlight the complex interplay between labor allocation and economic growth, suggesting that misallocation of resources can lead to significant developmental deficits. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) highlight the role of institutions in shaping economic growth. Secure property rights and human capital formation are critical for fostering economic development. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England exemplifies how secure property rights can drive economic growth. This process is underpinned by credible commitments from the regime, which are essential for secure contracting across time and space. Such commitments foster the development of markets and impersonal exchange, which in turn support specialization and division of labor. This institutional framework is crucial for economic growth, as it reduces transaction costs and increases productivity. North (1989, p.1) underscores the significance of transaction costs in economic models, arguing that institutions matter when transaction costs are positive. This perspective aligns with the view that secure property rights and human capital formation are essential for economic growth, as they reduce transaction costs and enhance productivity. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) caution that secure property rights are a necessary condition for economic growth, but they are not sufficient on their own. Other factors, such as the robustness of the local economy and the strength of property rights, also play a role. This qualification suggests that the impact of gender and development on long-run economic outcomes may be influenced by the interplay between secure property rights and other economic factors.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) argue that the accumulation of useful knowledge and technological advancements are critical drivers of economic growth. This accumulation allows societies to enhance productivity and efficiency, leading to sustained economic growth. These adjustments facilitate rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drive broader societal transformations. For instance, the transition to a more capital-intensive production system, as described by de Vries (1994, p.17), exemplifies how increased labor input and new consumption patterns can precede and facilitate industrial growth. This highlights that while technological advancements are pivotal, their effects are mediated through complex social and institutional frameworks, which may influence long-run economic outcomes.

This literature provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the multifaceted determinants of economic development. McCloskey (2015, p.9) and van Zanden (McCloskey 2015, p.9; van Zanden 1999, p.1) share a common posture that economic growth does not necessarily equate to broad-based improvements in well-being. McCloskey (2015, p.9) argues that ideas and ideologies, not mere economic interests, drove economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. He illustrates this by noting that the French elite's resistance to bourgeois economic and political successes significantly influenced the country's economic trajectory, showing that economic growth can be hindered by cultural and ideological factors. Similarly, van Zanden (1999, p.1) challenges optimistic views of early modern economic growth by demonstrating that real wages declined during the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, indicating that economic development did not uniformly improve living standards. This perspective is further supported by Komlos (1998, p.7), who finds that industrialization did not uniformly benefit all segments of society. For instance, the range of heights across occupations increased in the United States during the first half of the nineteenth century, suggesting that the benefits of modern economic growth were unevenly distributed. Komlos (1998, p.7) also notes that the coefficient of variation of physical stature increased over time in Bavaria and was greater in urban than in rural areas, indicating that income and nutritional resources were becoming more unevenly distributed. This implies that while economic growth can occur, its benefits may not be evenly distributed, and certain groups may even experience a decline in well-being, highlighting the complex relationship between economic growth and societal outcomes, including those related to gender and development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Ogilvie (2007, p.25) both examine economic institutions and structural changes, focusing on how these factors shaped long-run economic outcomes without directly addressing gender and development. Broadberry and Gupta discuss the role of economies of localization and the benefits derived from the proximity of producers in the same industry, which can be linked to the work of Marshall. They also emphasize the importance of merchant distribution and finance in achieving high productivity in the traded goods sector. This focus on economic institutions and their impact on productivity and economic growth is mirrored in Ogilvie's analysis of pre-industrial Europe, where she argues that distributional conflicts provide a better explanation for core economic institutions than efficiency. Ogilvie highlights how poor contract enforcement, property rights, and high risks limited investments in physical and human capital. These institutional factors, while not directly addressing gender and development, underscore the complex interplay between economic institutions and long-run economic outcomes. Simson (2019, p.27) shows that budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. Simson's analysis of post-independence budget policies in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda reveals the challenges of implementing a proactive policy agenda in economically undiversified countries. This finding qualifies the downstream implication of institutional factors on economic growth by showing that even in the presence of seemingly unfavorable institutional conditions, economic outcomes can still be influenced by other factors.

The Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.15; Nunn 2008, p.27). The Black Death's impact on Europe's economic development was marked by a significant shift in labor dynamics. The labor shortages caused by the plague led to a decline in the skill premium, as the scarcity of labor increased the bargaining power of workers. This decline was further encouraged by the drop in interest rates across Europe, which facilitated human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The economic impact of the Black Death varied across regions, with northwestern Europe experiencing higher urbanization rates compared to southern Europe. Urbanization, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, highlighted the divergent trajectories of these regions (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The economic repercussions of the Black Death extended beyond labor markets and urbanization. The plague epidemics of the sixth, fourteenth, and seventeenth centuries had complex effects on nutritional status. In the aftermath of these mortality crises, the availability of agricultural land increased, which could have improved nutritional quality. For instance, farm capital such as cows was not reduced to the same extent as the human population, potentially benefiting those who remained (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). The benefits of increased land availability were not uniformly distributed, as urban dwellers might have been separated from essential goods like milk. This pattern suggests that while the Black Death had profound economic effects, its impact on nutritional status was nuanced and dependent on regional factors.

Gender and development significantly influence long-run economic outcomes by shaping human capital investments and economic structures. Women's employment patterns and educational attainment are pivotal in this process. The transformation of women's roles in the U.S. economy, from piece workers and domestic laborers to professionals, highlights a shift from mere economic necessity to occupational identity (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift is paralleled by changes in African economies, where women played crucial roles in both pre-colonial and colonial periods, contributing substantially to economic growth and development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The integration of women into the workforce and their increased educational attainment are key drivers of long-term economic performance, as these factors enhance human capital formation and economic productivity (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

Economic institutions and policies also play a critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Institutions that favor elites can restrict competition and perpetuate inequality, as seen in New World societies where factor endowments and institutional frameworks led to extreme wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These institutional structures often result in unequal distributions of wealth and human capital, which in turn affect economic growth and development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The skill premium, a measure of the return on human capital investment, is a strong predictor of long-term economic performance. Countries with lower skill premiums tend to have higher levels of human capital formation, which is essential for sustained economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25). This dynamic is evident in historical data, where the skill premium and its determinants help explain economic divergences within Europe and globally (van Zanden 2009, p.25).

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is further complicated by the interplay between institutions and human capital. Institutions that secure property rights for elites can hinder the entrepreneurial activities of the broader population, limiting investment and economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional framework often leads to a situation where the majority of the population lacks the necessary human capital to participate fully in economic activities, further exacerbating inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Societies with inclusive institutions that promote human capital development tend to have more equitable economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The historical evidence from European colonies shows that institutional changes introduced by colonialism, not geographic factors, are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This institutional perspective highlights the importance of policy and governance in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as seen in the differential impacts of colonial policies on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is settled by the historical evidence of human capital formation and institutional frameworks. Shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1; van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The precise disagreement is whether economic institutions or human capital formation is the primary driver of long-run economic outcomes. This is settled by the empirical comparison of skill premiums and their determinants, which show that countries with lower skill premiums tend to have higher levels of human capital formation, as well as the historical evidence from European colonies, which demonstrates that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former colonies.
