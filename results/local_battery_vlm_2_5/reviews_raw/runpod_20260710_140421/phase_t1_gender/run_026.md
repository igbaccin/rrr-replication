The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development trajectories. At stake is the understanding of how historical gender roles and economic policies have interacted to produce contemporary economic disparities. Two rival positions have emerged in the literature: one emphasizes the impact of gender dynamics on economic growth, while the other focuses on the role of institutions and factor endowments in shaping economic outcomes. The debate centers on whether gender dynamics operate independently or are mediated by broader institutional and economic factors. This review synthesizes the literature on the causal role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes, examining how gender dynamics and economic institutions interact to influence economic growth and development. Goldin (2006, p.3) asserts that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes, a perspective shared by Akyeampong and Fofack (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This transformation was marked by shifts in women's roles within the economy, moving from piece workers in manufacturing and service sectors to more professional occupations. These women experienced little on-the-job learning and saw only slight gains from formal education beyond elementary school. This shift in women's employment patterns was a transformative change that significantly impacted economic outcomes. The quiet revolution in women's employment, as described by Goldin (2006, p.3), involved a change from agents who worked out of necessity to those who sought employment for identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation was about economic participation and the societal recognition of women's roles. Akyeampong and Fofack note that gender discrimination tends to lower the average productivity of workers and the equilibrium wage for males and females in the short run and may adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This suggests that the quiet revolution in women's employment was a shift in roles and a critical factor in economic development. The implications of this transformation are profound, as it highlights the need for policies that recognize and support women's contributions to the economy. This recognition is crucial for sustained economic growth and development, as it ensures that women's roles are not marginalized but valued and integrated into the broader economic framework.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.23) share a view that colonialism's impact on economic development is rooted in the institutional changes it introduced, which often led to long-run economic outcomes that reversed relative incomes among former European colonies. The mechanism through which this reversal occurred is the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite, which led to the majority of the population being held up by this powerful elite after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is particularly relevant for understanding how gender roles and development might have been shaped during this period. The extractive institutions that Acemoglu et al. describe would have likely affected women disproportionately, as they were often marginalized from economic and political power structures. This marginalization could have limited women's access to education and economic opportunities, further reinforcing their subordinate roles in society. Austin qualifies the implications of this dynamic by showing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis can oversimplify causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This suggests that while extractive institutions may have had a significant impact on economic development, other factors such as racial discrimination and market rigidity also played crucial roles.

North and Weingast, alongside North and Ogilvie (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5; Ogilvie 2007, p.25), argue that secure property rights are a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies that depend on specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space is crucial for fostering economic growth and market development. This is supported by North (1989, p.5), who argues that economic growth can occur as a consequence of changes in institutions that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights, thereby increasing productivity. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) qualifies this by noting that the efficiency approach to institutions is complicated by vague claims about societal benefits and the lack of straightforward efficiency results for bargaining outcomes. This suggests that while secure property rights are crucial, the actual implementation and perceived benefits of these institutions can vary widely. Moreover, the distributional conflicts that arise from these institutions can shape economic outcomes in unpredictable ways. Thus, the role of institutional frameworks in fostering economic growth is complex and multifaceted, requiring a nuanced understanding of both the benefits and the challenges posed by different institutional arrangements.

Technological and ideological drivers are crucial for economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr show (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets highlights that economic growth demands a rapid transformation of society, including changes in occupational structures and urbanization, driven by advancing technology and the institutional and ideological adjustments it necessitates. This transformation is not merely economic but also social, involving shifts in family formation and societal views on individual roles and achievements. Mokyr's emphasis on the growth of useful knowledge as the mainspring of economic growth complements Kuznets's perspective, suggesting that the expansion of collective knowledge underpins economic development. The interplay between technological advancements and societal changes creates a dynamic environment where economic growth is both a cause and an effect of broader societal transformations. McCloskey argues that ideas and ideologies, along with technological changes, were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey's argument underscores the importance of cultural and ideological factors in shaping economic outcomes, highlighting that the success of economic growth initiatives depends on the prevailing ideologies and societal attitudes toward innovation and progress. Thus, the role of technological and ideological drivers in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, involving both the adoption of new technologies and the alignment of societal values with economic goals.

The relationship between economic development and living standards, as highlighted by van Zanden (2009, p.27) and de Vries (1994, p.17), is intricately linked to factors such as skill premiums, labor input, and biological well-being during industrialization. De Vries (1994, p.17) further elaborates on this by discussing the Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution and was characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns (de Vries 1994, p.17). This period saw a shift in the production system becoming more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers, with a focus on quality over quantity in terms of human capital endowment for children. Consequently, the major achievements of industrial society, such as lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, and higher educational levels, were primarily achieved through the household productive system rather than the larger formal economy (de Vries 1994, p.17). This pattern suggests that the economic benefits of industrialization were not evenly distributed, with some groups, particularly those with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital, potentially experiencing declines in biological well-being. Komlos (1998, p.7) supports this notion, showing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment, especially among groups with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital (Komlos 1998, p.7). This uneven distribution of benefits underscores the complex interplay between economic development and living standards, highlighting that while some groups benefited from industrialization, others were left behind, experiencing worsening living conditions.

Research on economic development and living standards in Europe during the early modern period reveals that economic growth did not uniformly translate into improved living standards (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Van Zanden argues that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living was not straightforward, with declining real wages observed during the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries despite periods of economic growth. This pattern contrasts with optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, which suggest that economic development should have led to a higher standard of living. Koepke and Baten further illustrate this disconnect by demonstrating that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia. They note that even in the aftermath of mortality crises, when agricultural land became more available, nutritional quality did not significantly improve. This finding suggests that the benefits of economic development were not evenly distributed and that factors beyond economic growth, such as social and gender inequalities, played a crucial role in determining living standards. The evidence highlights that economic growth alone does not guarantee improvements in living standards, as other social and economic factors can mitigate or even negate these benefits. This pattern is especially pronounced during periods of significant mortality crises, where economic growth was decoupled from nutritional improvements. The nuanced relationship between economic development and living standards underscores the complexity of long-run economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) show that regional economic performance varied significantly over time, with some regions experiencing higher levels of economic performance than others. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9; 2006, p.25) demonstrate that the great divergence between Europe and Asia was already well underway before 1800, highlighting that this divergence was driven by a complex interplay of factors that predated the Industrial Revolution. The implications of these findings are significant, as they suggest that regional economic divergence is not solely a product of modern economic forces but rather a result of long-term structural changes that can be traced back to pre-modern eras. Pamuk (2007, p.15; 2007, p.16) argues that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, underscoring the role of historical events in shaping regional economic trajectories. This highlights that the economic impact of historical events, such as the Black Death, can have lasting effects on regional economic performance. The findings from these studies collectively underscore the importance of long-term structural changes in shaping regional economic outcomes, and how these changes can have enduring effects on economic performance over time.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Stern (2004, p.8) argue that economic growth can be driven by factors beyond standard policy interventions, such as labor allocation disequilibrium and trade specialization. Temin (2002, p.4) posits that the post-WWII Golden Age saw rapid economic growth due to a misallocation of resources between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium, separate from low income levels, highlights how structural changes in resource allocation can drive economic growth. Stern (2004, p.8) extends this by noting that trade theory suggests developing countries specialize in labor-intensive goods, which can reduce environmental degradation. Simson (2019, p.27) qualifies this by showing that budget policies in post-independence East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. This qualification suggests that while structural changes can drive growth, the impact of budget policies can vary significantly across regions.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6) explore how economic policy and inequality shape long-term growth, focusing on how changes in productivity and skills influence economic outcomes. They argue that shifts in the structure of occupations and skills are pivotal in shaping economic inequality. This dynamic is further illustrated by the role of human capital in economic development, as Lindert and Williamson posit that the incentive to invest in human capital is driven by the pay gaps and earnings inequality set in motion by unbalanced productivity advances (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This mechanism aligns with the broader understanding that economic policies and institutional structures significantly influence economic outcomes, but it does not specifically address how gender and development shape these outcomes over the long run. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) extend this by showing how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The unequal distribution of wealth and income in these societies was heavily influenced by factor endowments and the abundance of labor with low human capital. This institutional framework reinforced inequality and shaped the long-term economic trajectories of these societies. The specific impact of gender dynamics within these institutional structures is not explicitly addressed. This omission highlights a gap in the literature, suggesting that while economic policies and institutional frameworks are crucial, the role of gender in shaping long-term economic outcomes remains underexplored.

Gender dynamics play a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The skill premium, a key indicator of economic performance, is closely linked to factor endowments and the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) shows that the skill premium reflects the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of capital markets, which in turn influence long-term economic performance. This relationship is further supported by the work of Goldin (2006, p.1), who argues that the transformation of women's employment and education significantly impacted economic dynamics. Goldin (2006, p.1) and van Zanden (2009, p.27) both show that the skill premium and human capital formation are central to understanding economic trajectories. The role of gender in shaping these dynamics is evident in the historical contributions of African women to economic growth and development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the centrality of women to production and reproduction. This contribution is further underscored by the findings of Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.28), who show that changes in the structure of occupations and the rate of skills deepening are critical forces driving economic growth. The interplay between gender dynamics and economic institutions is also evident in the work of Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48), who argue that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, which in turn affected human capital formation. This suggests that gender dynamics are integral to understanding the long-term economic outcomes shaped by institutional frameworks. The historical evidence of women's contributions to economic growth and the impact of gender dynamics on human capital formation provide a clear picture of the role of gender in shaping long-term economic outcomes.
