The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a pivotal question in economic history and development studies. A central debate revolves around the extent to which gender dynamics and development trajectories mutually influence each other over the long term. One position argues that gender roles and economic development are intertwined, with gender dynamics significantly influencing economic outcomes. One perspective posits that institutional factors are the primary drivers of long-run economic performance, with gender dynamics also playing a role. Understanding this relationship is crucial for grasping the mechanisms behind economic growth and inequality, as well as for informing policies aimed at sustainable development. The interplay between gender and development involves both immediate economic gains and long-term structural changes that can reinforce or mitigate existing inequalities. This interplay is evident in both historical and contemporary contexts, where the roles of women in the economy have evolved significantly over time. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” van Zanden (2009, p.27) reports, “There is an obvious link to factor endowments: interest rates reﬂecting relative abundance of capital (and the efﬁciency of capital markets) appear to be an important factor determining the skill premium.”

This quiet revolution in women's employment, as detailed by Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3), was a pivotal factor in reshaping long-run economic outcomes. This transformation is evident in the shift from women being predominantly piece workers in manufacturing or laboring in the service sector to a more diverse range of occupations. Goldin (2006, p.1) describes this shift as a change from "jobs" to "careers," where women increasingly sought employment that defined their identity and societal worth. This evolution was not merely evolutionary or stalled; it involved significant changes in human capital investment and occupational horizons. The implications of this transformation are profound. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) highlight that gender discrimination, if left unaddressed, can lower average productivity and adversely affect long-run growth. They note that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating that cultural and historical factors play a crucial role in shaping gender dynamics in the economy. This historical perspective underscores the importance of understanding the long-term impacts of gender roles on economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) also observe that while the link between gender norms and female labor force participation is well-established, the origins of these cultural differences are less understood. This gap in knowledge suggests that further research is needed to fully grasp the mechanisms by which gender roles influence economic outcomes. The historical transformation of women's employment roles has had a significant impact on long-run economic outcomes, but the underlying cultural and historical factors that shape these roles require deeper investigation.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) share a central claim that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have shaped long-run economic outcomes. The institutional framework established by colonial powers, particularly in the New World, significantly influenced the distribution of wealth and income. For example, Acemoglu et al. note that societies where the property rights of the elite were secure often saw the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite, which could hold up the majority of the population after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate that the unequal distribution of wealth in these economies was exacerbated by the abundance of labor with low human capital and the generous awards of property and tribute to the earliest settlers (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework restricted competition and created a persistent cycle of inequality. Austin complicates this narrative by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). He suggests that the stagnation of certain economies, such as South Africa, can be attributed to systematic racial discrimination and its effects on human capital formation and market flexibility (Austin 2008, p.23). This qualification highlights that while institutional changes are crucial, the broader socio-economic context, including racial dynamics, also plays a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The literature on institutions and economic growth, while crucial for understanding long-term economic performance, does not explicitly address how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). Lindert and Williamson's work on the Kuznets Curve and North and Weingast's analysis of institutional frameworks underscore the importance of economic and political structures in fostering growth. North and Weingast argue that secure contracting across time and space is a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The specific mechanisms through which gender and development influence these institutional frameworks are underexplored.

Economic growth during the Industrial Revolution did not uniformly improve the biological well-being or nutritional status of all segments of society. Komlos (1998, p.7) and van Zanden (Komlos 1998, p.7; van Zanden 1999, p.1) show that while aggregate purchasing power increased, this did not necessarily translate into better health outcomes for all individuals. Economic growth can exacerbate inequality in nutritional resources, leading to a divergence between average physical stature and income. This pattern is evident in Bavaria and the United States, where the benefits of modern economic growth accrued unevenly, and some groups were made worse off (Komlos 1998, p.7). For example, handloom weavers, who possessed inelastic human or physical capital, experienced declining well-being. Similarly, van Zanden (1999, p.1) finds that there is no clear connection between economic growth and improvements in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This lack of a straightforward relationship suggests that economic development does not automatically lead to better nutritional status or biological well-being. The persistence of social inequality further complicates this picture. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) demonstrate that social inequality has a significant negative impact on nutritional status, with a height difference of 0.63 centimeters between high and low social strata (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This finding underscores the importance of considering social and gender inequalities when examining the relationship between economic development and living standards.

Historical events, such as slave trades and post-independence budget policies, have significantly shaped economic development. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that historical events have played a crucial role in shaping economic development. These events have influenced economic outcomes through various mechanisms, including the disruption of social structures and the redistribution of resources. For instance, the slave trades in Africa led to a high level of ethnic fractionalization, which in turn hindered economic development. Nunn (2008, p.4) explains that the insecurity caused by the slave trades confined people within ethnic boundaries, constructing spheres of interaction that limited economic growth. This ethnic fractionalization is a significant determinant of various factors necessary for economic development. The impact of historical events on economic development varies. Simson (2019, p.6) notes that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, despite the prevailing narrative. This qualification suggests that the relationship between historical events and economic development is complex and context-dependent. While historical events have undeniably shaped economic development, their impact varies across different regions and time periods. Therefore, it is essential to consider the specific historical context when examining the relationship between historical events and economic development.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and McCloskey (Ogilvie 2007, p.25; McCloskey 2015, p.9) argue that economic change in Europe was influenced by a multitude of factors, including distributional conflicts and the role of ideas and ideologies. Ogilvie demonstrates that the core economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by distributional conflicts rather than efficiency considerations. These institutions were designed to mitigate risks and protect against theft and violence, but they ultimately limited investments in physical and human capital. McCloskey (2015, p.9; 2015, p.8) extends this argument by showing that ideas and ideologies were crucial drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey argues that cultural struggles and ideological shifts were pivotal in shaping economic development. For example, McCloskey notes that in France, the dominance of anti-bourgeois and anti-libertarian ideologies among the elite significantly hindered economic growth. The success of bourgeois economic and political developments in Holland and England influenced the trajectory of economic growth in France, highlighting the broader impact of ideas and ideologies. Pamuk (2007, p.15; 2007, p.16) further qualifies this by noting that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk argues that these structural changes, including severe labour shortages and declining skill premiums, were critical in shaping the economic landscape of Europe. These findings suggest that the impact of historical events, such as the Black Death, and the role of ideas and ideologies were interconnected in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) (Temin 2002, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17) examine various aspects of economic growth and labor allocation, but do not directly address how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. Temin (2002, p.4) attributes the rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age to a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, driven by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9). This misallocation, distinct from low income levels, highlights a developmental deficit that influenced economic trajectories. De Vries (1994, p.17) builds on this, arguing that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This revolution had profound economic consequences, including a shift towards capital-intensive production systems and a focus on quality over quantity in reproduction (de Vries 1994, p.17). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further complicate this picture by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging views that attribute economic growth solely to European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This suggests that indigenous economic developments, driven by labor allocation and increased productivity, played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes prior to and during colonial periods.

Economic growth is profoundly influenced by a nation's skill premium and technological advancements, as well as the efficiency of its institutions. Van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) share the finding that economic growth is driven by factors such as the skill premium and technological progress, which require institutional adjustments for efficient use. This premium is also linked to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the presence or absence of barriers to entry in skilled professions. Economic growth is fundamentally about advancing technology, as Kuznets (1973, p.1) shows, which demands institutional and ideological adjustments. These adjustments facilitate the efficient use of innovations, driving economic growth. For instance, the dynamic drives of modern economic growth mean reaching out geographically and expanding to less developed areas, (Kuznets 1973, p.1). This process necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drive changes in other aspects of society, such as family formation and urbanization. Mokyr (2005, p.4) qualifies this by arguing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. This useful knowledge is the collective understanding of a society, which has expanded significantly over time. Greater specialization and professionalization have led to a vast increase in the total amount of knowledge that societies possess, (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This expansion of social knowledge has been a key driver of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.3).

Gender and development play a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Goldin shows that changes in women's employment and education have transformed societal dynamics, influencing economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack, however, highlight that African women's contributions to economic growth have been significant, especially during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The skill premium, as van Zanden argues, is a key predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27), and this is closely linked to the role of gender in economic development. In societies where women have greater access to education and employment, the skill premium tends to be lower, indicating higher levels of human capital formation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This suggests that the economic contributions of women are foundational to long-term economic growth. The institutional frameworks that facilitate or hinder these contributions are crucial. Sokoloff and Engerman illustrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). Institutions that support equitable access to education and employment for women, therefore, are essential for sustainable economic development. The historical evidence from various societies shows that when women are integrated into the economy, it leads to higher levels of human capital and economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; van Zanden 2009, p.27).
