The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue among economic historians. Central to this debate is the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development. One position emphasizes the significance of gender roles in determining economic outcomes, arguing that historical gender norms and the economic participation of women have profound effects on long-term economic performance. Some scholars argue that institutional and economic factors drive economic growth more than gender dynamics. The live disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms at play: whether gender roles and economic participation significantly shape economic outcomes or if other factors, such as institutions and factor endowments, are more decisive. The interplay between gender and development is not merely an academic query; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic strategies aimed at fostering sustainable growth. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform strategies for reducing inequality and promoting economic development. The role of gender in economic growth is not straightforward. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” van Zanden (2009, p.27) reports, “There is an obvious link to factor endowments: interest rates reﬂecting relative abundance of capital (and the efﬁciency of capital markets) appear to be an important factor determining the skill premium.”

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3); (Goldin 2006, p.3) highlight that African women played a pivotal role in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. These scholars emphasize the centrality of women to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. In the pre-colonial era, women were integral to agricultural production, trade, and craftsmanship, contributing significantly to the economic fabric of African societies. Their roles extended beyond mere labor; women were often entrepreneurs, managing markets and small businesses. This economic agency was a fundamental aspect of their societal roles, not just a byproduct of necessity. During the colonial period, women continued to be vital economic actors, often adapting to new economic structures imposed by colonial powers. They were instrumental in maintaining household economies and often took on new roles in wage labor and domestic service. The colonial era also brought new forms of gender discrimination, which affected women's economic participation and opportunities. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) show that historical plough use correlates with unequal gender roles today, indicating that certain agricultural practices exacerbated gender disparities. This historical context underscores the complexity of gender roles in economic development, highlighting that while women have been significant economic actors, their contributions have often been overlooked or undervalued.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6); (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33) examine how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. These policies, designed to maintain elite control, often resulted in the exclusion of certain groups from economic opportunities. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite implies that the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic highlights the institutional barriers that can hinder economic development by limiting the participation of a broader segment of society. Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.5; McCloskey 2015, p.9) examine how institutions and ideologies shaped economic growth, particularly focusing on the political factors underpinning market development and the role of ideas in driving economic change. They argue that secure property rights and reliable political institutions are critical for fostering economic growth. North and Weingast emphasize that the success of seventeenth-century England's economic development can be attributed to the evolution of institutions governing public choice, which ensured secure contracting across time and space. This political stability facilitated impersonal exchange, which is essential for specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). He contends that the cultural and ideological environment in France, for example, was not conducive to economic growth due to the dominance of anti-bourgeois sentiments among the elite. This ideological resistance hindered the adoption of economic principles that had proven successful in other regions. This indicates that while political stability and supportive ideologies are important, they are not the only drivers of economic growth. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is multifaceted, encompassing both political and ideological dimensions.

Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Mokyr (2005, p.4) share the view that technological advancements and institutional adjustments are key drivers of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) notes that the dynamic forces of modern economic growth necessitate rapid changes in economic structure and societal aspects, including family formation and urbanization (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr (2005, p.4) further elucidates that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that the collective knowledge of societies has expanded significantly over time (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This expansion of knowledge facilitates economic growth by enabling more efficient use of innovations. However, de Vries (1994, p.17) qualifies this by highlighting the Industrious Revolution, a period marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns that preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.17). This indicates that while technological advancements are crucial, the role of increased labor input and changing consumption patterns cannot be overlooked in driving economic growth. The interaction between technological progress and institutional changes, therefore, is complex and multifaceted, with each component playing a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) argue that post-WWII economic growth in Europe was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium was a significant misallocation of resources, not just a result of policy changes or other ordinary factors. Temin contends that this misallocation was separate from the low income that typically generates conditional convergence. He attributes the growth in Europe to a developmental deficit produced by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9). Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) elaborate on this by noting that the rapid expansion in the derived demand for skills served to offer great and increasing incentive to investment in human capital. This dynamic is critical because it highlights the role of skill development in driving economic growth during this period. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28) qualifies this mechanism. The inability of the labor market to quickly adapt to the increasing demand for skills suggests that while skill development was a key driver, the overall economic growth was constrained by the labor market's capacity to meet this demand. The interplay between labor allocation and skill development thus played a pivotal role in shaping Europe's economic growth during the post-WWII era.

van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) both document that European economic development and the standard of living were shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including skill premiums, urbanization rates, and wage gaps. This mechanism is supported by Pamuk (2007, p.15), who notes that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The decline in the skill premium under severe labor shortages and the subsequent drop in interest rates encouraged human capital formation, which in turn drove economic growth. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) argues that there is no clear connection between the development of grain wages, the standard of living, and economic growth in Europe during the early modern period (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This suggests that while skill premiums and wage gaps were influential, they were not the sole determinants of economic development. The complexity of these interactions underscores the multifaceted nature of European economic growth, where various factors contributed to the overall trajectory of development.

The literature on biological well-being and nutritional status does not directly address how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). It does provide insights into how economic growth and development can have uneven effects on different population groups. Komlos (1998, p.7) shows that during the Industrial Revolution, the benefits of economic growth were not evenly distributed, leading to increased inequality in nutritional resources and physical stature. This uneven distribution suggests that while some groups might have experienced improved living standards, others, particularly those with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital, such as handloom weavers, could have been made worse off. This implies that the long-run economic outcomes shaped by gender and development are about the distribution of benefits, not just aggregate economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Nunn (2008, p.27) together argue that African economic performance has been historically underappreciated, with significant economic growth occurring before colonial interventions. This growth was driven by various factors, including technological advancements and institutional developments, which laid the groundwork for future economic progress. Nunn's work complements this view by emphasizing the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development, suggesting that the region's underdevelopment cannot be attributed solely to colonialism or more recent events (Nunn 2008, p.27). The legacy of the slave trades, including heightened ethnic fractionalization, has had lasting impacts on economic growth and development. Nunn further elaborates that the slave trades created an environment of uncertainty and insecurity, which in turn required individuals to invest in weapons for self-defense (Nunn 2008, p.4). This diversion of resources away from productive activities likely impeded economic development. The implications of these findings are significant, as they underscore the need to consider a broader range of historical factors when analyzing long-run economic outcomes in Africa.

The relationship between gender and development is pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, emphasizing their centrality to production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Van Zanden's analysis of the skill premium and its impact on long-term economic performance aligns with this perspective, highlighting that a low skill premium often reflects a high level of human capital formation, which is crucial for economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Both sources underscore the importance of human capital and the role of women in economic growth, suggesting that policies aimed at enhancing human capital and reducing gender disparities can significantly boost long-run economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is another critical factor. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which often concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework can impede economic development by limiting the opportunities for the majority of the population to invest in human capital and entrepreneurship (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Sokoloff and Engerman further illustrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The interplay between gender and institutional factors is evident in Lindert and Williamson's work, which shows that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening are critical forces driving economic growth and distribution (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). These findings collectively highlight that institutions that promote equality and human capital investment are essential for long-run economic development.

The historical context of gender roles and economic development is deeply intertwined with institutional frameworks. Akyeampong and Fofack note that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating that cultural and institutional legacies play a significant role in shaping current economic outcomes (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical perspective is complemented by Goldin's analysis of the transformation in women's employment and education in the U.S., which shows that women's participation in the workforce and their investment in human capital have been crucial drivers of economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.1). The shared finding is that both historical and contemporary gender roles are shaped by institutional factors that determine access to education, employment, and economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). These institutional frameworks are pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as they determine the distribution of resources and opportunities across genders.

The precise historical evidence that settles this disagreement lies in the comparative analysis of skill premiums and human capital formation across different regions and periods. Van Zanden's analysis of the skill premium in Europe during the 'little divergence' and 'Great Divergence' periods shows that differences in the price of human capital can explain significant economic disparities (van Zanden 2009, p.25). This historical evidence underscores the importance of human capital and institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes, providing a clear measurement type that highlights the role of gender and development in economic growth.
