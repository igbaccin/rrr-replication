The relationship between gender and development is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes. The debate centers on whether gender dynamics act as a primary driver or a secondary factor influenced by other variables. One position holds that gender roles and economic participation significantly shape economic trajectories. An opposing view suggests that economic development is primarily driven by other factors, such as institutional quality and labor market dynamics, with gender playing a secondary role. Another perspective posits that historical antecedents, including colonial legacies, have shaped current gender roles and economic outcomes. The interplay between gender and development is complex, with historical and institutional factors often intertwining to influence long-run economic performance. The central question is how gender and development interact to shape economic outcomes over the long term. This question is significant because it addresses the fundamental mechanisms by which societies evolve economically, with implications for policy and economic theory. Institutions and labor market dynamics are key factors in this evolution, as they directly influence economic participation and productivity. The role of gender in shaping these dynamics is a critical area of inquiry, as it can reveal how societal structures and economic outcomes are interconnected. This interplay is evident in the historical transformation of women's roles in the labor market, where changes in employment and education have had profound effects on economic development. The transformation of women's roles in the labor market has been a significant factor in economic development, as it has led to increased human capital investment and productivity. This transformation is evident in the shift from "jobs" to "careers," where women's employment is no longer just a means of survival but a source of identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1; Goldin 2006, p.3). van Zanden (2009, p.27) reports, “There is an obvious link to factor endowments: interest rates reﬂecting relative abundance of capital (and the efﬁciency of capital markets) appear to be an important factor determining the skill premium.”

Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4) agree that economic growth is driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drive societal shifts in family formation, urbanization, and individual perceptions of societal roles and achievements. The implication of these arguments is that technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge are critical drivers of long-run economic outcomes. McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies this implication by asserting that ideas and ideologies, along with technological advancements and knowledge accumulation, played a significant role in economic change. McCloskey (2015, p.8) demonstrates that the success of modern economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was influenced by the cultural and ideological struggles that shaped societal views on economic growth and development. This suggests that the role of ideas and ideologies in shaping long-run economic outcomes cannot be overlooked, as they significantly influence how technological advancements and useful knowledge are utilized.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) focus on economic growth and labor allocation, arguing that the Industrious Revolution and post-WWII Golden Age of European growth were driven by shifts in labor allocation and increased labor input, respectively (Temin 2002, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17). The Industrious Revolution, for example, saw households increase their labor input and alter their consumption patterns, which facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This phenomenon underscores the significance of labor allocation in driving economic growth. This disequilibrium was separate from low income levels, indicating that resource misallocation can significantly impact economic outcomes. The implications of these findings extend beyond the immediate economic context, suggesting that labor allocation and household production patterns play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Furthermore, de Vries (1994, p.17) notes that the Industrious Revolution led to a more capital-intensive production system oriented towards full-time male workers, which had profound consequences for the entire economy. This shift in labor allocation facilitated economic growth and influenced broader societal changes, including improvements in morbidity and mortality rates, nutrition, and educational levels. The role of labor allocation in driving economic growth highlights the importance of considering household production patterns and resource misallocation when examining long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that institutions governing public choice and property rights are crucial for economic growth. Colonial institutions introduced new governance structures and property rights, shaping long-run economic outcomes. These institutions fostered secure contracting across time and space, enabling specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) show that the success of these institutions was remarkable, as evidenced by robust capital markets. North (1989, p.1) qualifies this by noting that neoclassical models, which emphasize population and savings, hold only under the restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. With positive transaction costs, institutions matter, and secure property rights are essential for economic growth. This suggests that the long-run economic outcomes in regions with colonial institutions can be attributed to the institutional frameworks that facilitated secure contracting and productivity growth.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions introduced by colonialism significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes. They suggest that these institutions created a powerful elite who secured property rights for themselves, which led to the persistence of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.5) observe that this dynamic was particularly pronounced in colonies with abundant natural resources and low human capital, where elites used their political power to maintain control over the economy. This concentration of power and wealth in the hands of a few restricted competition and hindered the development of inclusive institutions. Austin (2008, p.1) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that institutional changes during colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, oversimplifies the causal mechanisms at play. He argues that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, which can obscure the complex interplay between institutions, human capital, and economic growth. Austin (2008, p.23) further qualifies that the stagnation of certain economies, such as South Africa's during the apartheid regime, was influenced by systematic racial discrimination, which hindered human capital formation and market flexibility. This suggests that the long-run economic outcomes in former European colonies were determined by institutional changes during colonialism and by the specific historical and social contexts in which these changes occurred.

Goldin contends that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes, offering an alternative explanation to the role of gender and development (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This transformation was marked by a shift from women working out of economic necessity to those seeking employment as a means of personal and societal identity. Goldin's work highlights that this shift involved a change in employment patterns and a significant increase in human capital investment, as women began to pursue careers. This transformation was not limited to the United States; Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was multifaceted, encompassing both production and reproduction, and was crucial for the economic development of African societies. The historical role of women in economic development is further underscored by the finding that cultures with a history of plough use exhibit more unequal gender roles today, suggesting a long-term impact of historical gender dynamics on economic outcomes. The specific mechanisms by which these historical gender roles influence contemporary economic outcomes remain complex and multifaceted. The transformation in women's employment and the historical contributions of African women to economic development illustrate the profound and enduring impact of gender dynamics on long-run economic outcomes.

The skill premium and economic divergence are closely linked to long-term economic performance, with van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) both showing that the skill premium serves as a predictor of economic outcomes. This suggests that economies with higher skill premiums may face barriers to entry in skilled professions, which can hinder economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further illustrate this point by highlighting the benefits of economies of localization, which are derived from the proximity of producers in the same industry. These benefits include specialized information flows, skilled labor supply, and thick markets for inputs and outputs. The formation of industrial districts to reap these benefits does not necessarily imply large-scale urbanization and may be consistent with proto-industrialization. This implies that while the skill premium is a significant factor in economic divergence, the specific mechanisms by which it influences long-run economic outcomes can vary depending on the institutional and economic context. This underscores the importance of understanding the historical context in which the skill premium operates, as it can have profound and enduring impacts on long-run economic outcomes.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is deeply intertwined with broader development processes. Women's integration into the labor force and educational systems has been a quiet revolution that transformed employment, education, and family dynamics (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift represents a fundamental change in how women engage with the economy, moving from 'jobs' to 'careers' (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation is particularly evident in the context of human capital formation, where women's education and skill development have become crucial factors in economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The skill premium, which reflects the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of capital markets, is a significant predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This skill premium is also linked to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation, underscoring the importance of gender-inclusive policies in driving economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the exclusion of women from productive activities and human capital investment can adversely affect long-run growth. This exclusion has historical roots that persist through institutional barriers. The historical role of African women in economic growth and development, both pre-colonial and colonial, highlights the significant contributions of women to economic productivity (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This contribution is often overlooked in traditional economic narratives, which tend to focus on male-dominated sectors. The persistence of gender discrimination in economic growth is a critical factor that affects long-run economic outcomes, as it limits the full utilization of human capital (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The historical plough use and unequal gender roles today show a strong and robust positive correlation, indicating that cultural and historical factors play a significant role in shaping contemporary gender norms and economic participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical evidence underscores the enduring impact of gender roles on economic development.

The institutional framework of societies also plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) show that institutions that advantage elites through various government policies restrict competition and contribute to the persistence of inequality. These institutions often result in the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite, which can hold up the majority of the population after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is particularly evident in societies with extractive institutions, where the masses typically do not or cannot obtain education, further limiting human capital formation (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The historical context of New World societies with greater inequality shows that economic structures were greatly influenced by factor endowments, leading to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This inequality is deeply rooted in the institutional frameworks that govern societies.
