The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question that intersects with broader debates about the determinants of economic growth and inequality. At stake is the understanding of how social structures and historical processes influence economic trajectories over extended periods. A central disagreement revolves around the mechanisms through which gender dynamics and development processes interact to produce long-term economic effects. One position emphasizes the direct causal role of gender inequality in economic outcomes, arguing that historical gender roles and discrimination have significant and enduring impacts on economic performance. Another position, however, suggests that the relationship between gender and economic development is mediated by other factors, such as institutions and human capital formation. These competing views underscore the need to disentangle the complex interplay between gender, development, and economic outcomes over the long run.

Gender dynamics have historically influenced economic outcomes by shaping labor market participation and human capital investment. In the U.S., for instance, women's employment patterns underwent a significant transformation from the late 19th to the mid-20th century, evolving from piecework and domestic labor to more professional roles. This shift was marked by a transition from "jobs" to "careers," where women began to invest more in human capital and seek occupations that defined their identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation represented a quiet revolution in women's economic roles, not just an evolutionary process. Similarly, in Africa, women played crucial roles in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their contributions were central to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their economic impact (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). These findings highlight how gender dynamics have shaped economic outcomes by influencing labor market participation and human capital investment.

The role of institutions in mediating the relationship between gender and economic development is another key area of inquiry. Institutions can either reinforce or mitigate gender inequalities, thereby influencing long-term economic performance. In New World societies, for example, institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These policies often led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income, with significant implications for economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Similarly, the skill premium, which is a predictor of long-term economic performance, is closely linked to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation. A low skill premium generally reflects a high level of human capital formation, which is essential for economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; van Zanden 2009, p.25). These findings underscore the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping the relationship between gender, development, and economic outcomes.

The reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies highlights the complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development. The reversal of fortune thesis posits that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are responsible for the reversal in relative incomes, not geographic factors. These changes often involved the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite, leading to extractive institutions that hindered economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The 'reversal of fortune' thesis has been critiqued for oversimplifying causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This critique suggests that the relationship between gender, development, and economic outcomes is more nuanced and context-dependent than previously thought (Austin 2008, p.23; Austin 2008, p.1). The complex interplay between institutions, gender dynamics, and economic development requires a more nuanced understanding of the historical processes that shape long-term economic outcomes.

Goldin argues that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Women began to invest more in human capital, seeking education and training that enhanced their economic contributions. This transformation was not merely evolutionary; it was a significant shift in societal norms and economic structures. Women's roles in production and reproduction were central to these economies, challenging views that marginalize their historical economic contributions. The persistence of gender discrimination, even in contexts of economic growth, qualifies this transformative potential. Historical plough use, for instance, correlates with unequal gender roles today, indicating that cultural norms can hinder women's full participation in the workforce (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This suggests that while the quiet revolution in women's employment has been pivotal, its effects are contingent on broader cultural and institutional factors that shape long-term economic outcomes. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for appreciating the complex interplay between gender, development, and economic growth.

This reversal is a direct result of the institutional legacies left by colonial powers, which continue to influence economic trajectories today. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Nunn (2008, p.27) argue that the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite under colonial rule led to extractive institutions that hindered economic growth. These institutions, which secured property rights for the elite while leaving the majority without effective protection, created a barrier to investment and entrepreneurial activity. This dynamic is exemplified by the role of human capital in societies with extractive institutions, where the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin (2008, p.1) notes that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. The impact of colonialism on economic development is further complicated by the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades, which Nunn (2008, p.4) argues had long-term detrimental effects on economic development. This suggests that the long-term economic outcomes of colonialism are influenced by the historical legacies of slavery and ethnic fractionalization, in addition to institutional changes.

De Vries (1994, p.17) and van Zanden (2009, p.27)(de Vries 1994, p.17) highlight how increased labor input and new consumption patterns influenced economic performance during the Industrious Revolution. This revolution led to a more capital-intensive production system oriented towards full-time male workers, shifting market demand towards family-consumed goods and focusing on the quality of human capital in children (de Vries 1994, p.17). This suggests that while increased labor input and new consumption patterns were crucial for economic performance, the efficiency of institutions and the level of human capital formation played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Pamuk highlights the role of urbanization rates as a key indicator of productivity and economic development in the pre-modern period. Simson qualifies this by noting that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive. The varying trajectories of economic development in different regions underscore the importance of considering multiple factors, including historical contexts and institutional efficiencies, in understanding long-run economic outcomes.

The literature on nutritional status and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). The literature shows that the Industrial Revolution's effects on nutrition varied widely across different groups and regions. This suggests that nutritional status, a key indicator of biological well-being, was unevenly affected by economic growth. Komlos (1998, p.7) argues that the Industrial Revolution was characterized by widespread inequality in nutritional resources, with urban areas and certain occupations experiencing greater disparities. These disparities in nutritional status likely had significant long-term effects on health and productivity. This indicates that economic growth did not uniformly improve nutritional outcomes, and social inequalities exacerbated these disparities. The literature suggests that the uneven distribution of nutritional resources during economic growth phases could have long-lasting effects on population health and productivity. This underscores the importance of considering gender dynamics in assessing the long-term effects of economic growth on nutritional status.

These insights are crucial for understanding how technological advancements have historically driven economic growth and development (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4).  These technological advancements necessitate significant institutional and ideological adjustments to be efficiently harnessed. Kuznets highlights that economic growth is intricately linked to societal changes, including shifts in family formation, urbanization, and occupational structures. He observes that the dynamic forces of economic growth extend geographically and temporally, continuously expanding to less developed areas. This expansion is facilitated by major changes in transport and communication (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr elaborates on this by suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. He defines useful knowledge as beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena, which are shaped by consensus and confidence within societies (Mokyr 2005, p.4). Collective knowledge drives economic growth, not individual knowledge. The implication is that societies with higher levels of useful knowledge are more likely to experience sustained economic growth. Mokyr also notes that the relationship between knowledge and economic growth is not linear; it requires continuous investment in human capital and the professionalization of knowledge. This investment ensures that the total amount of knowledge in society expands, leading to greater economic productivity (Mokyr 2005, p.3). The expansion of knowledge and its institutional embedding are key to understanding how technological progress shapes long-run economic outcomes.

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is well-documented, with North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (Ogilvie 2007, p.25) highlighting the importance of secure property rights and effective institutions governing public choice in fostering economic growth. This institutional framework is crucial for economic development, as it ensures that property rights are enforced and contracts are secure. The specific mechanisms through which these institutions operate can vary significantly. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) illustrate how institutions in New World societies, characterized by high levels of inequality, often favored elites and restricted competition. This institutional bias towards elites can perpetuate economic inequality, as seen in societies with abundant mineral resources and a low supply of human capital. The resulting economic structures, dominated by large plantations and slave labor, further entrenched wealth disparities. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) suggests that distributional conflicts often drive the development of core economic institutions, not efficiency. These conflicts can lead to institutions that benefit certain groups at the expense of others, thereby shaping long-run economic outcomes. The implication is that while secure property rights and effective institutions are necessary for economic growth, the specific design and enforcement of these institutions can either mitigate or exacerbate existing inequalities, with significant implications for long-run economic outcomes.

Gender and development play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes by influencing institutional design and human capital formation. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that the institutions established by colonial powers significantly impacted long-term economic growth, with extractive institutions leading to poor economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Similarly, van Zanden highlights that the skill premium, a key indicator of human capital investment, is closely linked to institutional efficiency and can predict long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This shows that institutions that support human capital formation and secure property rights are crucial for economic growth. The specific design and enforcement of these institutions can either mitigate or exacerbate existing inequalities, with significant implications for long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is evident in the historical contexts discussed by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6).

The role of gender in economic development is also critical, as it directly affects human capital formation and economic productivity. Goldin argues that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., highlighting the importance of gender dynamics in economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.1). Akyeampong and Fofack similarly show that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, emphasizing the centrality of women to production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This indicates that gender dynamics are integral to understanding long-run economic outcomes, as they influence both human capital investment and economic productivity.

The interplay between gender and institutions further shapes economic trajectories. Acemoglu et al. argue that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to systematic cross-country differences in human capital correlated with institutional differences (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Akyeampong and Fofack extend this by showing that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, affecting female labor force participation and economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This shared finding (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10) underscores the importance of institutional design in shaping gender dynamics and long-run economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions and gender dynamics are pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Both Acemoglu et al. and van Zanden show that institutional efficiency and human capital formation are crucial for economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; van Zanden 2009, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which colonial institutions alone can explain the reversal of fortune, as Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the measurement of skill premiums and their determinants, as van Zanden shows that these can help explain the 'Great Divergence' and the 'little divergence' occurring within Europe (van Zanden 2009, p.25).
