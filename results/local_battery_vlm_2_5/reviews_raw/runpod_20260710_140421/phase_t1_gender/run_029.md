The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical question in economic history and development economics, as it illuminates the intricate relationship between social structures and economic performance. At stake is the understanding of how gender dynamics influence economic growth and development over extended periods. One of the live disagreements centers around the significance of gender roles in economic development. Some scholars emphasize the pivotal role of gender equality in fostering economic growth, while others posit that economic growth is primarily driven by institutional factors and human capital accumulation. This debate is crucial for policy-making, as it determines whether interventions should focus on gender equality or other factors. The interplay between gender and development is complex and multifaceted, with historical contexts shaping the dynamics between economic growth and gender roles. For instance, the contribution of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods underscores the centrality of gender dynamics in economic history. Similarly, the transformation of women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. highlights how changes in gender roles can drive economic development. The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism further illustrates the interconnectedness of gender, development, and economic outcomes. The question of whether economic growth is driven by gender equality or other factors remains a contentious issue. Goldin (2006, p.3); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes, challenging views that see this change as merely evolutionary or stalled. Women's entry into the labor market was a significant shift that altered economic dynamics, marking a change from a gradual process. This transformation involved more than just numbers; it changed the nature of work itself, shifting from 'jobs' to 'careers' that involved long-term horizons and substantial human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift had profound implications for economic growth and development, as women's increased participation in the labor force brought about changes in family dynamics and societal structures. This contribution was foundational, shaping the economic landscape in ways that continue to influence long-run outcomes. The historical context of gender roles and economic development is complex. For instance, plough use in historical agricultural practices has been linked to unequal gender roles today, suggesting that cultural and historical factors can perpetuate gender disparities despite economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This indicates that while women's employment revolution is a critical factor, the long-run economic outcomes are also shaped by deep-seated cultural norms and historical practices that may either facilitate or hinder gender equality.

North and Weingast, along with Temin, argue that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (Temin 2002, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). The institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England were pivotal in securing property rights and fostering economic growth. The development of these institutions ensured that the rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. This predictability was essential for secure contracting across time and space, which is a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The secure property rights that emerged from these institutions allowed for the efficient allocation of resources, which in turn facilitated economic growth. This process is distinct from the low income that generates conditional convergence, which is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates. Instead, the developmental deficit highlighted by Temin is produced by a misallocation of resources due to institutional factors (Temin 2002, p.9). This misallocation can hinder economic growth by preventing the efficient use of resources. North also notes that the neoclassical model, which has served as the basis of economic reasoning for most scholars, holds only under the severely restrictive assumption of zero transaction costs. With positive transaction costs, institutions matter, and secure property rights are essential for economic growth (North 1989, p.1). This underscores the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes, independent of gender and development factors.

Technological advancements and institutional changes drive economic growth (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This process necessitates rapid changes in the labor force's structure and location, as well as in occupational status groups and societal views on achievement. Van Zanden supports this perspective, noting that skill premiums are related to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation, and that the absence or presence of formal or informal barriers to entry in these professions can significantly impact economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The skill premium, which reflects the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of capital markets, is a crucial factor in determining long-term economic performance. A low skill premium generally indicates a high level of human capital formation, which is typically observed in rich countries. This dynamic highlights the interdependence of technological progress and institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Mokyr qualifies this by suggesting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. Mokyr argues that the total amount of knowledge that societies possess has vastly expanded due to greater specialization and professionalization, which has led to increased investment in human capital (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This implies that while technological advancements and institutional changes are crucial, the accumulation and dissemination of useful knowledge play a pivotal role in sustaining long-run economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner, alongside de Vries, both examine the economic performance of economies, but neither directly addresses how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. Broadberry and Gardner argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges previous characterizations of the region's economic performance, which often assumed that any economic growth above subsistence resulted from European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). McPhee's claim that economic growth in West Africa was due to railways, malaria prophylaxis, and British rule exemplifies this perspective (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This perspective is a product of the colonial period and reflects a broader view that economic development in Africa was largely driven by external factors. De Vries's work on the Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, suggests a different mechanism. De Vries shows that increased labor input and new consumption patterns played a critical role in economic growth, which was more through the household productive system than the larger formal economy (de Vries 1994, p.17). This implies that internal factors, such as changes in household production patterns, could have significant economic impacts independent of external interventions. This perspective qualifies the view that economic growth in Africa was primarily driven by colonial interventions, suggesting that internal economic dynamics, such as changes in household production, could have played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This highlights the need to consider both internal and external factors when examining the economic performance of African economies.

The literature on biological well-being and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). Instead, it highlights the complex interplay between economic growth and biological well-being, revealing that economic progress does not always translate into improved health outcomes. This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that the economic gains were not uniformly shared, and certain groups, such as handloom weavers, were disproportionately affected. This divergence between economic growth and biological well-being underscores the importance of considering the distribution of economic benefits when examining long-run economic outcomes. Koepke and Baten support this perspective by demonstrating that nutritional status in Europe did not significantly improve over the last two millennia, despite increases in GDP per capita (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This finding indicates that economic development alone is insufficient to guarantee improvements in biological well-being. Moreover, Koepke and Baten's analysis reveals that both gender and social inequality played significant roles in determining nutritional outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This suggests that addressing these inequalities is crucial for ensuring that economic growth translates into improved health and well-being for all segments of the population.

Sokoloff and Engerman, together with Acemoglu et al., argue that colonial institutions in New World societies with greater inequality favored elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in these societies was reinforced by the economic structures that evolved early in their histories. For example, in societies where institutions secured property rights for the elite, the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after undertaking investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This concentration of power and wealth created a barrier to economic mobility and innovation, as the masses were often unable to obtain education or participate fully in the economy. In such environments, the lack of human capital formation and market flexibility further hindered economic growth (Austin 2008, p.23). This highlights how institutional frameworks that perpetuate inequality can have long-lasting effects on economic development, shaping long-run outcomes by limiting the opportunities for broader segments of the population to participate in and benefit from economic growth.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is deeply intertwined with the broader development frameworks that either facilitate or hinder economic growth. Goldin (2006, p.3) shows that the transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. involved a shift from "jobs" to "careers", where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift was crucial for economic growth, as it expanded the labor force and increased productivity. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting their central role in production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Women's labor and entrepreneurial activities drove economic activities that supported broader development.

The institutional frameworks that govern economic activities play a pivotal role in determining long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that institutions that secure property rights for elites can lead to a concentration of power, limiting the broader population's ability to invest and participate in economic growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional design can perpetuate inequality, as seen in the persistence of unequal wealth distributions in societies with extractive institutions. Similarly, van Zanden (2009, p.27) shows that the skill premium, a measure of the return on human capital, is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Societies with efficient institutions for human capital formation tend to have lower skill premiums, indicating a more equitable distribution of economic opportunities.

The historical evidence from various regions underscores the importance of inclusive institutions in driving economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) illustrate how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional design limited the economic opportunities for broader segments of the population, hindering long-run economic growth. Societies with more inclusive institutions, where property rights and economic opportunities were more widely distributed, experienced more robust economic growth. The case of South Africa during the apartheid regime, where systematic racial discrimination hindered human capital formation and economic growth, further highlights how institutional frameworks that perpetuate inequality can have long-lasting effects on economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The historical evidence from the U.S. and Africa shows that inclusive institutions, which provide equal opportunities for human capital investment and economic participation, are crucial for long-run economic growth.
