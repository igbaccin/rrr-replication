The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of study, as it addresses the fundamental question of how societal structures and economic policies interact to influence economic growth and development over extended periods. At stake is the understanding of how historical patterns of gender inequality and development have shaped contemporary economic disparities. The live disagreement revolves around the extent to which gender dynamics and development policies are causal in shaping economic outcomes. On one hand, some scholars argue that gender inequality and discriminatory policies have had profound and lasting effects on economic development. On the other hand, other scholars posit that institutional factors and historical contexts play a more significant role in determining economic outcomes, with gender dynamics being a secondary or even incidental factor. This tension underscores the need to critically examine the interplay between gender, development, and economic growth. A central theme in this debate is the extent to which historical gender roles and economic policies have shaped contemporary economic disparities, and whether addressing gender inequality can lead to more equitable and sustainable economic development. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Goldin (2006, p.3) (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that women have significantly contributed to economic growth and the transformation of employment and education patterns across different historical contexts. The transformation of women's roles in the labor market, particularly in manufacturing and service sectors, played a crucial role in economic development. Women often took on piecework in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses, roles that offered little opportunity for on-the-job learning or significant gains from formal education beyond elementary school. The expansion of professional roles for women, especially in teaching and clerical positions beginning in the 1910s, altered labor market dynamics. This shift involved changing societal perceptions of women's roles, moving from 'jobs' to 'careers' that required long-term human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation highlights the evolving nature of women's contributions to economic growth, where their roles became more integrated into the broader economic fabric.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin both focus on how colonial institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes, highlighting that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is tied to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.23). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite under colonial rule often led to extractive institutions. This institutional framework created a situation where the majority of the population, lacking effective property rights protection, faced significant barriers to investing in human capital and economic development. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize was complex, with colonial institutions often hindering the growth of human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic is further complicated by the fact that colonial institutions could systematically suppress human capital formation, thereby limiting the economic potential of the colonized populations. Austin adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis must be qualified by the broader context of historical periods and paths. Economic stagnation in regions like South Africa during the apartheid regime, for example, was deeply influenced by systematic racial discrimination (Austin 2008, p.23). This indicates that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, involving institutional changes and broader socio-economic factors.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) examine economic performance in African and New World societies, focusing on per capita income levels and the role of institutions in shaping inequality. They establish that economic performance varied significantly across these regions, with inequality being a key determinant of long-run economic outcomes. Sokoloff and Engerman show how institutional structures in New World societies advantaged elites, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional bias was exacerbated by the abundance of low-skilled labor and the absence of significant European migration. The resulting economic structures, dominated by large plantations and slave labor, further entrenched inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). Broadberry and Gardner, however, qualify this perspective by arguing that some Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges the notion that African economies were uniformly stagnant, highlighting the nuanced impact of colonial institutions on economic development. Their finding implies that the relationship between inequality and economic performance is not straightforward, as regional variations in per capita income suggest that other factors, such as colonial policies and local institutions, also played crucial roles.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and Ogilvie (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Ogilvie 2007, p.25) together show that economic divergence between Europe and Asia was driven by institutional factors that shaped the economic landscape. One key mechanism was economies of localization, which facilitated the concentration of producers in specific industries. This concentration allowed for the exchange of specialized information and the development of a skilled labor force, which in turn fostered productivity gains. Such economies of localization were instrumental in the rise of specialized industrial production in highly urbanized economies, contrasting with the proto-industrial settings where rural peasants supplemented their agricultural incomes with some industrial production. Ogilvie (2007, p.25) notes that these benefits were often stifled by poor contract enforcement and property rights, leading to high risks, limited investments, and short time horizons (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). This institutional inefficiency underscores the complexity of economic development, where the benefits of localization were not uniformly realized. Pamuk (2007, p.15) further elaborates on this complexity by showing that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The skill premium began to decline under severe labor shortages, encouraging human capital formation. This structural change, coupled with the recovery of the population, influenced the trajectory of economic development in different regions of Europe. The wage gap and urbanization rates became key indicators of productivity and economic development, highlighting the regional variations in economic outcomes. These findings suggest that the interplay of institutional factors, such as economies of localization and the impact of the Black Death, played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with regional variations influenced by local institutions and historical events.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are key drivers of economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Mokyr (2005, p.4) argue that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and institutional adjustments. Kuznets (1973, p.4) identifies that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn influences societal aspects such as family formation, urbanization, and societal views on individual roles and achievements (Kuznets 1973, p.4). This useful knowledge expands society’s total knowledge base, facilitating economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.17) notes that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a distinct phenomenon preceding and facilitating the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.17). This suggests that while technological and institutional changes are crucial, they must be accompanied by shifts in labor patterns and consumption to fully realize economic growth. The interplay between technological advancements, institutional adjustments, and shifts in labor and consumption patterns collectively drives economic growth, shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ideas and ideologies played a pivotal role in driving economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848, a period that saw significant shifts in economic growth and development (McCloskey 2015, p.9; Stern 2004, p.8). These ideas often emanated from cultural struggles, such as the "Franco-French War" in France, where economic growth was secondary to imposing rigid forms of Catholicism and maintaining traditional social structures. This ideological framework influenced economic policies and outcomes, as seen in the anti-bourgeois and antilibertarian sentiments among France's elite, which hindered economic progress despite the presence of clever advocates of trade-tested betterment. Stern (2004, p.8) further illustrates the environmental implications of economic specialization, noting that the Hecksher-Ohlin trade theory suggests that developing countries specialize in labor and natural resource-intensive activities, while developed countries focus on human capital and manufactured capital-intensive activities. This specialization can lead to increased environmental degradation in middle-income countries, highlighting how economic development patterns influenced by ideologies can have downstream environmental impacts. This interplay between ideas, ideologies, and environmental factors underscores the multifaceted nature of economic change, where economic growth is a function of technological and institutional drivers, as well as the broader cultural and ideological context in which it occurs.

Institutional changes are pivotal for fostering economic growth, as North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.5) argue. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) identify secure property rights as a critical factor, enabled by institutional frameworks that facilitate secure contracting across time and space. Temin (2002, p.4) builds on this by highlighting that economic growth can be driven by resource misallocation, separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence (Temin 2002, p.9). This suggests that institutional frameworks can influence growth through both direct support for economic transactions and indirect effects on resource allocation. Temin (2002, p.4) notes that while institutional changes can drive growth, they are not the sole determinant, as other factors such as human capital and policy also play significant roles (Temin 2002, p.4). This implies that while institutional changes are necessary for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own, and their impact is contingent on the broader economic and social context.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Komlos (1998, p.7) share a skeptical view of the relationship between economic development and living standards in Europe, arguing that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved biological well-being or overall standard of living. For instance, Komlos (1998, p.7) notes that the coefficient of variation of physical stature increased over time in Bavaria and was greater in urban than in rural areas, indicating that income and nutritional resources were becoming more unevenly distributed. This pattern suggests that economic development can exacerbate inequalities, with certain groups, such as handloom weavers, experiencing declines in biological well-being despite overall economic growth.

The uneven distribution of economic benefits is further complicated by social and gender inequalities. For example, Koepke and Baten (2005, p.28) found that both social inequality and gender inequality had negative effects on nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values, whereas the effect of gender inequality was about half as strong. This finding underscores the importance of considering the broader social context when examining the relationship between economic development and living standards. The implications of these studies suggest that while economic development can drive growth, it does not automatically lead to improved living standards for all segments of the population. This underscores the need for policies that address social and gender inequalities to ensure that the benefits of economic growth are more evenly distributed.

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted and deeply intertwined with broader social and economic structures. In the U.S., the transformation of women's employment and education significantly altered family dynamics and societal perceptions of work (Goldin 2006, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift from "jobs" to "careers" reflects a broader change in how women's roles are perceived and valued in the economy. Meanwhile, in Africa, women's contributions to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods highlight their critical role in production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical perspective underscores the importance of recognizing women's economic agency across different contexts and time periods.

The interplay between gender and economic development is further complicated by the institutional frameworks that govern societies. In societies with extractive institutions, the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite can hinder economic growth by limiting the opportunities for the majority of the population to invest in human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This dynamic is evident in New World societies, where institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The historical evidence from these societies shows that the distribution of wealth and human capital was extremely unequal, with significant implications for long-run economic outcomes.

Moreover, the role of human capital in economic growth is a critical factor in understanding the relationship between gender and development. The transformation of women's roles in the economy, coupled with investments in human capital, has been a driving force behind economic growth in various contexts (Temin 2002, p.4; Temin 2002, p.9). This is evident in the post-WWII Golden Age in Europe, where unusually rapid economic growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. The historical evidence from these periods shows that the misallocation of resources, particularly human capital, can have significant implications for economic growth and development.

The underlying point of agreement is that gender and development are intertwined, and that the role of women in the economy is a critical factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This is evident in the historical evidence from the U.S. and Africa, where women's contributions to economic growth and development have been significant (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The remaining disagreement centers on the institutional frameworks that govern societies and their impact on economic growth. In societies with extractive institutions, the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite can hinder economic growth by limiting the opportunities for the majority of the population to invest in human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the transformation of women's roles in the economy and the investments in human capital that have driven economic growth in various contexts (Temin 2002, p.4).
