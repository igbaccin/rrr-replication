The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a pivotal question in understanding the determinants of economic growth and inequality. At stake is the intellectual debate over the relative importance of gender dynamics versus other factors in shaping economic trajectories. One position emphasizes the crucial role of gender equality in fostering economic development, arguing that empowering women leads to increased productivity and human capital investment. Another position, however, posits that institutional factors, such as property rights and political structures, are the primary drivers of economic outcomes, with gender dynamics playing a secondary role. This disagreement highlights the need to disentangle the complex interplay between gender, development, and long-run economic performance. The debate centers on whether gender equality is a necessary precondition for sustainable economic growth or if it is merely a byproduct of broader institutional changes. The key question is whether the role of gender is direct and causal, or if it is mediated through other factors.

Gender dynamics in economic development are often intertwined with broader institutional and structural factors. Goldin argues that a significant shift in women's employment and family dynamics occurred in the U.S., transforming "jobs" into "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation involved a change in identity and societal worth, indicating a deeper integration of women into the economic fabric. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). These findings suggest that gender dynamics can drive economic development themselves, in addition to being a product of broader economic changes.

Economic institutions and policies play a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, often intersecting with gender dynamics. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight how institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The interplay between institutional factors and gender dynamics is further complicated by the role of human capital. Lindert and Williamson argue that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening are critical forces driving economic growth (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This suggests that human capital investment, which can be influenced by gender dynamics, is a key factor in economic development. The skill premium, as van Zanden argues, is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This indicates that the economic integration of women, which can enhance human capital, is crucial for sustainable economic growth. The interaction between gender dynamics and institutional factors is complex, with each influencing the other in a feedback loop that shapes long-run economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women played a pivotal role in economic development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Goldin 2006, p.3). This perspective challenges the notion that women's contributions were marginal, instead emphasizing their centrality to production and reproduction. Pre-colonial African societies often integrated women into agricultural and trade activities, enabling them to contribute significantly to economic output. This integration reflected cultural norms that valued women's economic roles, in addition to being a function of necessity. During the colonial period, women continued to be crucial economic actors, adapting to new economic structures and opportunities. For example, in many African societies, women were involved in trade, often as market sellers, which provided them with economic independence and contributed to household income. This economic activity extended to commercial ventures, demonstrating women's agency and entrepreneurial skills, in addition to being confined to subsistence. Goldin argues that women's economic roles were often undervalued and underreported, leading to a distorted view of their contributions (Goldin 2006, p.3). This undervaluation is evident in historical records that often overlook women's economic activities, focusing instead on male-dominated sectors. Goldin's analysis of U.S. women workers in the early 20th century reveals that the vast majority were in low-skilled, low-paid jobs, with limited opportunities for advancement. This pattern suggests that even in developed economies, women's economic roles were constrained by structural barriers. These barriers, such as limited access to education and training, restricted women's ability to participate fully in the economy, thereby limiting their potential contributions to economic growth.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin share a finding that colonial institutions played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.23). While Acemoglu et al. focus on the securing of property rights for elites, Austin highlights that the systematic racial discrimination under colonial rule hindered human capital formation and market flexibility. These institutional changes, whether securing property rights for elites or imposing racial discrimination, fundamentally altered the economic trajectories of colonized regions. Nunn adds that the economic impact of colonialism is one factor to consider. Nunn's work on the slave trades in Africa underscores that pre-colonial factors, such as the slave trades, also had lasting effects on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and insecurity, which in turn hindered long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4). The reversal of fortunes among former European colonies is attributable to pre-colonial factors that shaped the economic landscape, in addition to colonial institutions.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) argue that the institutions governing public choice shaped long-run economic outcomes by fostering secure property rights and economic growth in seventeenth-century England. The institutions governing public choice in England were crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. The relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is complex. This suggests that the economic impact of institutions depends on the broader social and economic context in which they operate. The institutions governing public choice did not merely facilitate economic growth; they also shaped the distribution of wealth and income, with significant implications for long-term economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) examine the economic performance in Africa and Asia, focusing on historical economic growth and divergence. This divergence was driven by the development of urbanized economies in Europe. In Europe, economies of localization led to the formation of industrial districts, which concentrated diverse industries and fostered technological progress (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This process, driven by merchant distribution and finance, facilitated high productivity in the traded goods sector in north-western Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that economic growth in Africa was a result of African economies having already achieved a certain level of development prior to colonial rule, in addition to European colonial interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) note that the benefits of urbanization and industrialization were unevenly distributed across Asia. While some regions, such as the Yangzi delta in China, experienced significant economic growth, others, such as southern India, did not. This variation in economic performance highlights the complex interplay between local economic conditions and broader global trends. The historical divergence between Africa and Asia, as well as the variation within Asia, underscores the importance of understanding the specific economic and social contexts that shaped long-run economic outcomes in these regions.

Van Zanden (2009, p.27) and de Vries (1994, p.17) share a posture that economic growth and living standards are complexly tied to the interplay between human capital and economic structures. He posits that a high skill premium often indicates a low level of human capital formation, while a low skill premium suggests a more efficient institutional framework for human capital development. This institutional efficiency is crucial for understanding the economic divergence within Europe between 1400 and 1800. de Vries (1994, p.17) further elaborates on this theme by highlighting how the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, transformed the production system into a more capital-intensive one, oriented towards the requirements of full-time male workers. This shift in the production system had profound consequences for the entire economy, including the structure of market demand and the focus on quality in human capital formation. de Vries (1994, p.18)  argues that the Industrious Revolution laid the groundwork for the major achievements of industrial society, such as lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, and higher educational levels. Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this by showing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. Komlos (1998, p.2) further elaborates on the uneven distribution of nutritional resources, indicating that not all groups benefited equally from modern economic growth. This uneven distribution suggests that while economic growth and living standards improved for some, others were left behind, highlighting the complex interplay between economic growth, human capital, and living standards.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) jointly establish that economic growth is driven by factors including labor allocation and technological advancements. Temin (2002, p.4) focuses on the disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium is distinct from low income, which is typically attributed to insufficient physical and human capital relative to saving rates. Instead, the developmental deficit Temin highlights arises from a misallocation of resources. This suggests that while technological progress is crucial, it must be accompanied by structural changes in the economy and society to fully realize its growth potential. Mokyr (2005, p.4) further elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He argues that the accumulation of useful knowledge, defined as the union of all individual pieces of knowledge, is a key driver of economic growth. This accumulation is facilitated by greater specialization and professionalization, which increase the total amount of knowledge available to society. Instead, it takes time for new knowledge to be incorporated into the economy and affect GDP. This implies that the benefits of technological advancements and useful knowledge unfold over a longer period, in addition to not being fully realized in the short term. This extended timeline for the realization of economic benefits from technological advancements and useful knowledge underscores the complex interplay between economic growth, human capital, and living standards.

The relationship between economic growth and inequality is complex and multifaceted. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Ogilvie (2007, p.25) argue that the interplay between growth and inequality is influenced by the structure of pay by skill and the incentives for investment in human capital. This dynamic is driven by unbalanced productivity advances and the deepening of skills, which in turn shape the distribution of earnings and the incentives for human capital investment. Ogilvie (2007, p.25) (McCloskey 2015, p.9) adds that the efficiency of institutions in facilitating economic growth and aggregate economic welfare is contingent upon effective contract enforcement and secure property rights. These factors are crucial for mitigating risks and fostering long-term investment in both physical and human capital. McCloskey (2015, p.8) qualifies this by noting that ideas and ideologies play a significant role in shaping economic outcomes, even when efficient institutions are present. This suggests that while institutional efficiency is important, the broader cultural and ideological context can either amplify or constrain the benefits of economic growth and human capital investment. The interplay between these factors underscores the complex nature of economic development and the need for a holistic approach that considers both institutional and ideological dimensions.

Pamuk (2007, p.15) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) identify that historical economic divergences in Europe were shaped by events such as the Black Death and nutritional status over millennia. These factors collectively suggest that population dynamics and resource availability played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes in Europe. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) also highlight that both gender inequality and social inequality had significant negative effects on economic outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This indicates that while population dynamics and resource availability were important, social and gender inequalities also played a role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Gender and development have been pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with institutions and human capital playing critical roles. Van Zanden shows that the skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance, and is related to the efficiency of institutions for the formation of human capital (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Institutions that promote human capital formation and reduce barriers to entry in skilled professions foster economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack highlight the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This underscores the importance of gender dynamics in economic development, as women's contributions to both production and reproduction are central to economic growth. Furthermore, the historical role of women in economic development suggests that gender equality is a key factor in fostering long-run economic outcomes. The centrality of women to production and reproduction in African societies during these periods demonstrates that gender inequality can hinder economic development. The persistence of gender discrimination over historical time also shows that addressing gender inequality is crucial for sustainable economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10).

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further illustrated by Sokoloff and Engerman, who argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework highlights how unequal distributions of wealth and income can be maintained through policies that favor elites, thereby limiting economic opportunities for the majority. Acemoglu et al. also emphasize the impact of institutions on economic development, arguing that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutional changes, which often favored elites and restricted competition, had lasting effects on economic development. This shows that the institutional framework of a society plays a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize further underscores the importance of institutions in economic development, as societies with extractive institutions typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The interplay between gender, development, and long-run economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted. Institutions that promote human capital formation and reduce barriers to entry in skilled professions, as well as those that address gender inequality, are essential for fostering economic growth. The historical contributions of women to economic development in Africa, and the institutional frameworks that maintained inequality in New World societies, demonstrate the importance of these factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The persistence of gender discrimination over historical time, and the impact of institutional changes introduced by colonialism, further highlight the need for policies that promote gender equality and reduce inequality. The underlying point of agreement across these studies is that institutions and human capital are critical factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The remaining disagreement centers on the specific mechanisms through which institutions and human capital influence economic development. Akyeampong and Fofack show that the centrality of women to production and reproduction in African societies during pre-colonial and colonial periods demonstrates that gender inequality can hinder economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical evidence of African women's contributions to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods settles the disagreement by providing a clear measurement of the impact of gender dynamics on long-run economic outcomes.
