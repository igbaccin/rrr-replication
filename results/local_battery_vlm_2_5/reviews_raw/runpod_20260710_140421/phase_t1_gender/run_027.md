The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the significance of gender dynamics in economic growth. At stake is the extent to which historical gender roles and economic development are intertwined, and whether addressing gender inequality can catalyze sustained economic growth. The disagreement centers on the causal mechanisms at play: some scholars argue that gender inequality is a direct impediment to economic development, while others posit that economic development itself drives changes in gender roles. Additionally, there is a debate over whether the impact of gender on economic outcomes is mediated through institutional changes or through more direct economic factors. This debate is crucial for understanding the complex interplay between gender and economic development over the long run, and for informing policies aimed at fostering economic growth. The following sections delve into these debates, beginning with the role of women in economic development. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) report, “Fewer works have sought to analyze the origins, dynamics and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn 2013), or what happens to gender discrimination in the context of economic growth (Stotsky 2006).”

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. This pattern of involvement in economic activities underscores the pivotal role of women in sustaining economic activities. However, the nature of their work often limited their access to formal human capital investment, which in turn constrained their economic mobility. This limitation is significant because it suggests that women's economic contributions were not fully realized due to structural barriers that hindered their access to education and training. Furthermore, the historical context of plough use in certain cultures has been linked to unequal gender roles today, indicating that cultural practices have long-term implications for gender dynamics in the economy (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical perspective underscores the enduring impact of gender roles on economic development, suggesting that addressing gender discrimination requires a nuanced understanding of historical and cultural factors. Colonialism shaped economic outcomes by establishing institutions that either protected private property or extracted rents, thereby transforming the economic trajectories of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite under colonial rule often meant that the majority of the population faced insecure property rights, hindering investments and entrepreneurial activities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These extractive institutions, which were often a legacy of colonial rule, had profound implications for human capital formation and economic growth. For instance, in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to a systemic correlation between poor institutions and low human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48).

The impact of colonialism on economic development varies across regions. For example, the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which posits that the kind of institutions established by European colonialists led to the transformation of some of the more prosperous parts of the non-European world into the poorest economies today, oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This perspective is supported by evidence from South African economic history, where systematic racial discrimination under apartheid hindered both human capital formation and the growth of demand (Austin 2008, p.23). The legacy of colonial institutions thus interacts with local historical conditions to shape long-run economic outcomes, highlighting the complex interplay between colonial legacies and endogenous factors in economic development.

This complexity is mirrored in the relationship between economic growth and living standards in other regions, such as Africa and Asia. Komlos (1998, p.7) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) found that despite economic growth, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This uneven distribution of benefits meant that while some groups, such as landowners, prospered, others, like handloom weavers, experienced a decline in living standards (Komlos 1998, p.7). The divergence between average physical stature and income trends highlights how economic growth can exacerbate inequalities, with those possessing inelastic human or physical capital being particularly vulnerable. The findings of van Zanden (1999, p.1) show that there is no clear connection between economic growth and improvements in the standard of living, challenging optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth (van Zanden 1999, p.3). This perspective is supported by Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19), who argue that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia, despite recent GDP per capita estimates indicating increasing development between 1000 and 1800 AD. The authors suggest that both gender inequality and social inequality have played significant roles in shaping nutritional outcomes, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This indicates that while economic growth may have occurred, it did not necessarily lead to improvements in living standards for all segments of society. The complex interplay between economic growth, inequality, and living standards in Europe underscores the need to consider the distributional effects of economic development.

This disequilibrium was particularly pronounced in sectors where women were heavily represented, such as agriculture and domestic service. (Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). The rapid economic growth during this period was not solely attributable to policy or other ordinary factors, but rather to a misallocation of resources between sectors. This misallocation created a developmental deficit, where resources were not optimally distributed according to their productivity. Lindert and Williamson highlight that the structure of pay by skill and the derived demand for skills played a significant role in driving economic growth. This suggests that the rapid expansion in the demand for skilled labor incentivized investment in human capital, which in turn fueled economic growth. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker indicates that the benefits of this growth were not evenly distributed. This points to a broader pattern in which economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. The implications of this are significant for understanding the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as the allocation of labor and the distribution of skills can have profound effects on economic growth and inequality.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) share the finding that economic growth is driven by technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). He defines useful knowledge as beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena that can be harnessed to improve economic outcomes. This knowledge is collectively held and socially constructed, expanding as societies become more specialized and professionalized. Kuznets (1973, p.4) similarly emphasizes that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. He notes that modern economic growth involves not only technological advancements but also rapid changes in economic structure, urbanization, and societal views on individual roles and achievements. This dynamic process necessitates a continuous expansion of knowledge and adaptation to new technologies. Thus, the benefits of technological progress are not uniformly distributed across society, and the allocation of labor and skills can have profound effects on economic growth and inequality.

The long-run economic performance of Africa and Asia has been a subject of extensive historical inquiry, with Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) showing that these regions exhibited significant economic growth long before European colonial interventions. This perspective highlights the role of economies of localization, where the proximity of producers in the same industry facilitated the flow of specialized information and the availability of skilled labor. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further note that high productivity in the traded goods sector in northwestern Europe arose through merchant distribution and finance as much as through production. This suggests that while local economic factors played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, the distribution of these benefits was influenced by broader societal structures and institutions.
