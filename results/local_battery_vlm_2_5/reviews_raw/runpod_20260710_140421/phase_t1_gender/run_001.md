The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history, where scholars disagree on the mechanisms through which gender dynamics influence economic growth. One side of the debate argues that gender roles and institutions significantly shape economic outcomes by determining access to education, employment, and productive assets. This perspective highlights how gender-based barriers can lead to disinvestment in human capital, affecting long-run growth. The opposing view, however, suggests that economic growth is primarily driven by factors such as institutional efficiency and technological advancement, with gender dynamics playing a secondary role. This debate is crucial for understanding the historical determinants of economic development and for informing policies aimed at fostering inclusive growth. The central question is whether gender and development operate through distinct causal pathways or whether they are intertwined in a complex web of interdependencies. The stakes are high, as the answer to this question could reshape our understanding of economic history and the design of development policies. The following sections explore these competing views through the lens of different scholarly streams.

One stream of scholarship emphasizes the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, arguing that the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the presence of barriers to entry in professions are crucial factors. These institutions can either facilitate or hinder the accumulation of human capital, which in turn affects economic growth. Van Zanden argues that skill premiums are related to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation, and that low skill premiums generally reflect a high level of human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This perspective is supported by Acemoglu et al., who highlight that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to a correlation between institutions and human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The implications of these findings are significant, as they suggest that institutional changes can alter the trajectory of economic development by influencing the distribution of human capital. However, this stream of scholarship does not explicitly address the role of gender in shaping these institutional dynamics, leaving a gap in our understanding of how gender and development interact.

However, the influence of gender on these dynamics remains underexplored, despite Kuznets (1973, p.4) and van Zanden (2009, p.27) highlighting their significance. Kuznets posits that economic growth is fundamentally driven by advancing technology, which necessitates institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Mokyr further elaborates on this by asserting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that the accumulation of collective knowledge drives economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.4). However, Mokyr also notes that the rate and nature of economic growth are influenced by the consensus-shaping mechanisms within societies, which determine which beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena will predominate (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This implies that while technological advancements and institutional adjustments are crucial, the societal consensus on useful knowledge can either accelerate or impede economic growth.

Goldin and Akyeampong and Fofack argue that women's contributions to economic development have been historically significant and often overlooked (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Goldin describes the transformation of women's employment in the U.S., noting that women were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector, with little on-the-job learning and minimal gains from formal education beyond elementary school (Goldin 2006, p.3). This shift from "jobs" to "careers" involved a change in identity and societal worth, driven by human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). They note that gender discrimination can adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Akyeampong and Fofack also point out that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, suggesting that cultural norms shaped by historical agricultural practices persist and influence contemporary gender dynamics (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical context underscores the enduring impact of gender roles on economic development, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how societal norms and historical practices shape long-run economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. and Austin argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is a result of institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These institutions, as Sokoloff and Engerman show, were influenced by factor endowments and shaped the distribution of wealth and human capital in these societies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Austin 2008, p.1). The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite meant that the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, which favored the elite, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. The implications of these institutional changes are profound, as they not only affected economic outcomes but also influenced the distribution of human capital. This dynamic highlights how colonial institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes by concentrating wealth and power, which in turn affected human capital formation and economic growth. However, Austin qualifies this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin shows that the stagnation of the South African economy during the last 15 years of the apartheid regime owed much to the effects of systematic racial discrimination in hindering both human capital formation and the growth of demand (Austin 2008, p.23). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is more complex and multifaceted than initially proposed, involving not just institutional changes but also the interplay of racial discrimination and human capital formation.

North and Weingast's work on the political factors underpinning economic growth in seventeenth-century England, together with North's focus on institutions, underscores the importance of secure property rights and credible commitment in fostering economic development (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). Their shared claim is that the evolution of institutions governing public choice, particularly those that enforce property rights and enable secure contracting, was pivotal in driving economic growth. The mechanism at play here is the reduction of transaction costs, which allows for more efficient and productive economic exchanges. This institutional framework facilitated the growth of markets and specialization, which in turn drove economic development. McCloskey's analysis of the role of ideas and ideologies in economic change further qualifies this institutional focus, suggesting that while institutions are important, they are not the sole drivers of economic growth. McCloskey argues that the ideas and ideologies prevalent in a society can significantly influence its economic trajectory (McCloskey 2015, p.9; McCloskey 2015, p.8). This qualification implies that the impact of institutions on economic outcomes may be mediated by broader cultural and ideological factors. For instance, the success of economic reforms in seventeenth-century England was not just due to institutional changes but also to the ideological shifts that supported these changes, such as the embrace of bourgeois values and libertarian principles. This interplay between institutions and ideologies suggests a more complex relationship between institutional frameworks and economic growth than initially proposed, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the factors driving long-run economic outcomes.

Temin shows that the post-WWII Golden Age of European economic growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17). This disequilibrium, characterized by a misallocation of resources, is distinct from the low income that generates conditional convergence. The misallocation of resources in this context refers to a situation where labor is not efficiently distributed across sectors, leading to suboptimal economic outcomes. This phenomenon can be seen as a form of structural unemployment, where workers are employed but not in the most productive sectors. This misallocation is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence, which is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) further elaborate on this by highlighting that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that economic growth was not solely dependent on European colonial interventions. This suggests that the misallocation of labor resources, as highlighted by Temin, is a broader phenomenon that can affect economic growth in various regions, not just Europe. De Vries (1994, p.17) argues that the Industrious Revolution, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, was characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns. This implies that the misallocation of labor resources may not always lead to suboptimal economic outcomes, as it can also drive economic growth through increased productivity and consumption.

van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) argue that economic development in Europe did not uniformly improve the standard of living between 1500 and 1800, despite the economic growth during the Industrial Revolution. This suggests that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards for all segments of the population. Furthermore, Komlos (1998, p.7) highlights that the benefits of modern economic growth accrued unevenly, with some groups, such as handloom weavers, experiencing a decline in physical stature and nutritional resources. This uneven distribution of benefits indicates that economic development can exacerbate social and gender inequalities. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) provide additional evidence that social inequality and gender inequality have negative effects on nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values. Gender inequality, on the other hand, had about half the strength of social inequality. This demonstrates that while economic development can lead to increased productivity and consumption, it can also drive social and gender inequalities, which in turn affect long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) argue that distributional conflicts played a pivotal role in shaping economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. They show that these conflicts led to the development of core institutions such as serfdom, guilds, and property rights, which in turn influenced long-term economic development. Ogilvie (2007, p.25) notes that poor contract enforcement and limited property rights stifled economic dynamism. Meanwhile, Pamuk (2007, p.15) documents that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence was driven by severe labor shortages and a decline in the skill premium. Economic institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, thus played a crucial role in determining the trajectory of economic development in Europe. These institutions, however, did not always promote efficiency. Ogilvie (2007, p.9) observes that the efficiency of institutions was often vague and complex, with scholars debating whether these institutions were efficient for society as a whole or merely adequate. This debate underscores the intricate relationship between economic institutions and long-run economic outcomes. These distributional conflicts and the resulting institutions not only shaped economic development but also influenced social and gender dynamics, which in turn affected long-run economic outcomes.

Gender and development have played pivotal roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with institutions and human capital formation being key factors. Van Zanden shows that the skill premium, influenced by factor endowments and institutional efficiency, is a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This skill premium reflects the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the presence or absence of barriers to entry in these professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27). Goldin argues that a quiet revolution transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., highlighting the significant changes in women's roles and the shift from "jobs" to "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation involved a change from agents who work because they need the money to those who are employed because occupation and employment define one's fundamental identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). The role of gender in economic development is further underscored by Akyeampong and Fofack, who argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This contribution is evident in the centrality of women to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). These findings collectively show that gender dynamics have been integral to economic development, with institutions and human capital formation playing crucial roles.

Institutional frameworks have also been instrumental in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, shaped by distributional conflicts, not only influenced economic development but also affected social and gender dynamics (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Sokoloff and Engerman show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework highlights how economic structures and factor endowments influence the distribution of wealth and human capital (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The persistence of inequality is further evidenced by the extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income that prevailed in these economies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These findings underscore the intricate relationship between economic institutions, distributional conflicts, and long-run economic outcomes. The role of these institutions in shaping economic development is evident in the efficiency of human capital formation and the barriers to entry in professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This skill premium reflects the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the presence or absence of barriers to entry in these professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27).

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is evident in the transformation of women's roles and the institutional frameworks that have influenced economic development. The underlying point of agreement is that institutions and human capital formation are crucial factors in long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The remaining disagreement concerns the specific mechanisms by which these factors influence economic development. Van Zanden and Goldin show that the skill premium and the transformation of women's roles are key factors in economic development (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Goldin 2006, p.1). Acemoglu et al. argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism are the primary drivers of the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the role of institutions in shaping economic development, as evidenced by the efficiency of human capital formation and the barriers to entry in professions (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This evidence highlights the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with gender dynamics playing a crucial role in economic development.
