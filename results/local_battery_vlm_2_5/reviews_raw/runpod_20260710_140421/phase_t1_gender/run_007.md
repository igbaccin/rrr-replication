The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious area of study. The stakes are high, as understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth and reducing inequality. At the heart of the debate lies a disagreement over the causal mechanisms at play. One position argues that gender dynamics, particularly the empowerment of women, directly influence economic development by increasing human capital investment and labor force participation. This perspective posits that societies with greater gender equality tend to experience more robust economic growth due to the fuller utilization of their human resources. The opposing view contends that economic development is primarily driven by institutional factors, such as property rights and political structures, which can either facilitate or hinder growth regardless of gender dynamics. This rivalry underscores the need to disentangle the complex interplay between gender, development, and long-run economic outcomes. This discussion begins by examining the historical transformation of women's roles in the economy and its implications for long-term economic performance. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” van Zanden (2009, p.27) reports, “There is an obvious link to factor endowments: interest rates reﬂecting relative abundance of capital (and the efﬁciency of capital markets) appear to be an important factor determining the skill premium.”

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Akyeampong and Fofack (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that the economic empowerment of women has been transformative in shaping long-run economic outcomes, challenging views that see this change as merely evolutionary or stalled. The industrial revolution in the early 20th century saw women transitioning from piecework in manufacturing to more stable employment in the service sector. This shift was not merely a change in job roles; it represented a profound transformation in women's economic identity. Women began to see their work as a defining aspect of their societal worth, moving from "jobs" to "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transition involved significant human capital investment, which in turn drove long-term economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack further substantiate this by highlighting the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. The role of women in the economy is also influenced by historical factors, such as the use of the plough in agricultural societies. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) note that societies with a history of plough use tend to have less equal gender norms and lower female labor force participation today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical context suggests that the economic empowerment of women is a process that is deeply intertwined with cultural and historical factors that shape long-run economic outcomes.

North and Weingast, along with McCloskey, argue that institutional changes and ideological shifts were pivotal for fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; McCloskey 2015, p.9). The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England created a framework where property rights were secured, and economic growth was facilitated through impersonal exchange. This institutional framework was underpinned by credible commitment, which ensured that rules governing economic exchange were enforced and could be changed in a predictable manner. This institutional stability was crucial for economic growth, as it allowed for secure contracting across time and space, which is a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The role of these institutions and ideologies in shaping long-run economic outcomes presents challenges. Ogilvie notes that poor contract enforcement and property rights can ultimately choke off economic dynamism, highlighting the importance of effective institutions in sustaining long-run economic growth (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). The implications of these findings suggest that while institutional changes and ideological shifts are crucial for economic growth, the effectiveness of these institutions in enforcing property rights and contracts is equally important.

Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to the institutional changes introduced by colonialism, and this shared finding is echoed by Sokoloff and Engerman (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These changes often concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite, securing their property rights but leaving the majority of the population vulnerable to exploitation. This dynamic is evident in the colonial sugar economies, where the abundance of labor with low human capital and valuable mineral resources contributed to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional setup, where elites benefited from secure property rights while the majority faced risks of exploitation, hindered economic dynamism and sustained inequality. Austin qualifies this mechanism by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the historical process by compressing different periods and paths, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of how colonial institutions interacted with local contexts over time (Austin 2008, p.1). The implications of these findings suggest that while colonial institutions played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, the specific historical and contextual factors must be considered to fully understand the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies.

Nunn (2008, p.27) and Simson (2019, p.27) argue that Africa's slave trades had lasting impacts on economic development by influencing ethnic fractionalization. This process occurred as the slave trades exacerbated ethnic divisions and insecurity. This insecure environment required individuals to rely on their own ethnic groups for protection, which in turn led to the construction of ethnic boundaries and the formation of ethnic identities. This process led to a high level of ethnic fractionalization, which in turn has been linked to underdevelopment. The slave trades had a profound impact on Africa's economic development by contributing to the high level of ethnic fractionalization that persists today. Pamuk (2007, p.16) shows that urbanization rates, another key indicator for levels of productivity and economic development, were also affected by the Black Death. This event had a significant impact on the economic development of Europe, particularly in the northwest, where urbanization rates were higher. The Black Death led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, which in turn had long-term effects on economic development. This wage gap was driven by a skill premium, which began to decline under the conditions of severe labor shortages. The decline continued with the drop in interest rates across Europe in the aftermath of the Black Death, which should be expected to encourage human capital formation. The skill premium in Italy rose again with the recovery of the population in the sixteenth century. This pattern of urbanization and wage growth in the northwest of Europe highlights how historical events can have long-term effects on economic development, shaping the trajectories of different regions.

Technological and institutional changes are key drivers of economic growth, shaping labor patterns and useful knowledge to promote modern economic development (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). This includes shifts in family formation, urbanization, and societal views on roles and achievements. He posits that the expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all pieces of individual knowledge, has vastly increased. This expansion, driven by greater specialization and professionalization, has led to a total amount of knowledge that is significantly larger than ever before. This expansion of useful knowledge, as outlined by Mokyr, supports Kuznets’s (1973, p.4) assertion that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments. The implication is that as societies become more knowledgeable, they also become more efficient in utilizing innovations, thereby promoting economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.17) qualifies this by noting that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This suggests that while technological advancements are crucial, the underlying labor patterns and consumption behaviors also play a significant role in shaping economic development.

Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) and North (1989, p.5) jointly assert that the divergence in economic growth between Europe and Asia was already significant before 1800, driven by a combination of economic and institutional factors. One specific mechanism underlying this divergence is the role of economies of localization, which facilitated the development of specialized industries and urban centers in Europe. These localized economies benefited from the proximity of producers, enabling the exchange of specialized information and the development of skilled labor markets (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). The formation of these industrial districts did not necessarily imply large-scale urbanization and could coexist with proto-industrialization. This pattern suggests that while economic localization was a key driver, it did not always lead to immediate urbanization. The benefits of a diverse range of industries, as emphasized by Jacobs, could not be realized in a proto-industrial setting, indicating a more nuanced relationship between economic development and urbanization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This institutional framework is crucial for understanding how economic growth can be sustained over the long term. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) qualify this by noting that while economic growth may be driven by institutional changes, the nutritional status of populations did not significantly improve over the last two millennia in Europe. This suggests that while institutional changes can drive economic growth, they do not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards.

van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) share a focus on the economic performance and living standards in historical contexts, examining how economic growth and living standards are influenced by various factors (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This premium is linked to factor endowments and to the efficiency of institutions that facilitate human capital formation. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) complement this by showing that many Anglophone African economies achieved levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous notions that economic growth in Africa was solely due to European interventions (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This suggests that indigenous factors, including institutional efficiency and human capital formation, played a significant role in economic development. Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this perspective by noting that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). This indicates that while economic growth and institutional efficiency are important, they do not guarantee improvements in living standards if the benefits of growth are unevenly distributed.

Economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry (Temin 2002, p.4; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). This disequilibrium was not simply a matter of overall resource scarcity but a specific misallocation of labor. This reallocation was a critical factor in the economic growth observed during this period. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) emphasize that the pay gaps and earnings inequality generated by this unbalanced productivity advance served as a strong incentive for investment in human capital. This investment, in turn, fueled further economic growth by enhancing the skills and productivity of the labor force. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker highlights a qualification: while the reallocation of labor was a significant driver of growth, the benefits were not evenly distributed. The mismatch between the demand for skilled labor and the supply of skilled workers suggests that while economic growth was rapid, the distribution of its benefits was uneven, potentially limiting the overall improvement in living standards. This perspective underscores the importance of considering both the aggregate effects of labor reallocation and the distributional outcomes of economic growth.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, with significant contributions from both women's economic activities and the institutional frameworks that govern their participation. Historical evidence shows that women have been integral to economic growth and development, particularly in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3; 2014, p.10) demonstrate that African women significantly contributed to economic growth during these periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. This perspective is supported by Goldin (2006, p.1), who argues that the transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. was a quiet revolution that changed the fundamental identity and societal worth of women's work. The shift from "jobs" to "careers" involved substantial human capital investment, highlighting the economic significance of women's roles. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing women's contributions to economic development and the need to consider gender dynamics in historical analyses of economic growth.

The institutional context in which gender dynamics play out is crucial for understanding long-run economic outcomes. Institutions that advantage elites can restrict competition and contribute to the persistence of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6; 2000, p.5) argue that New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework is also relevant to the role of gender, as it can influence the distribution of economic opportunities and resources. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) show that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, highlighting the impact of institutional frameworks on economic outcomes. These findings suggest that the role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is closely tied to the institutional context, which can either facilitate or hinder women's economic participation and contributions.

The skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance, and gender dynamics play a significant role in shaping this premium. Van Zanden (2009, p.27; 2009, p.25) argues that the skill premium is linked to factor endowments and institutional efficiency, which in turn influence the demand for and supply of skilled labor. This perspective is supported by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8; 1985, p.28), who argue that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening are critical forces driving the British Kuznets Curve across the 19th century. The pay gaps and earnings inequality set in motion by unbalanced productivity advance served to offer great and increasing incentive to investment in human capital, much like John Stuart Mill alleged in 1848. This suggests that the skill premium is influenced by both the demand for and supply of skilled labor, and that gender dynamics play a significant role in shaping these factors. The underlying point of agreement is that the skill premium is a key predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). The precise remaining disagreement is whether the skill premium is primarily driven by demand for skilled labor or by the supply of skilled workers (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). The historical evidence on the skill premium in construction workers between 1750 and 1820 settles this disagreement by showing that the skill premium is influenced by both the demand for and supply of skilled labor, and that gender dynamics play a significant role in shaping these factors.
