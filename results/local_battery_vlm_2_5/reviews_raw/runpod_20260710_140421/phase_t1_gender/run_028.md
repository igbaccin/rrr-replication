The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating whether gender dynamics are a primary driver or a secondary effect of economic growth. One position argues that gender inequality is a persistent barrier to economic development, while another position suggests that economic growth can mitigate gender inequality over time. The live disagreement centers on whether gender dynamics are endogenous to economic growth or whether they operate as exogenous constraints that shape long-run economic outcomes independently. Understanding the causal relationship between gender and development is crucial for comprehending the mechanisms behind economic growth and the distribution of wealth and power. This question is particularly salient given the historical changes in women's employment and education, which have been transformative in shaping modern economies. The skill premium, a key indicator of economic performance, has been linked to human capital formation and institutional efficiency, suggesting that gender dynamics, which influence both, play a significant role in long-run economic outcomes.

The changing landscape of women's employment and education has been a critical factor in economic development. Goldin highlights the transformation in women's employment from piece workers in manufacturing to more professional roles, marking a shift from "jobs" to "careers" (Goldin 2006, p.1). This transition represents a quiet revolution that has altered women's roles in the economy. Similarly, Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize the significant contributions of African women to economic growth and development, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). These contributions continued to shape economic outcomes over time. This historical perspective underscores the enduring impact of gender dynamics on economic development, as women's roles in the economy have evolved in response to broader economic changes. The skill premium, a predictor of long-term economic performance, is closely linked to human capital formation and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This relationship suggests that gender dynamics, which influence both human capital and institutional frameworks, are integral to understanding long-run economic outcomes.

Akyeampong and Fofack, along with Nunn, examine the role of women in economic development, particularly through historical contributions and the impact of gender roles on contemporary economies (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.27). The centrality of women to production and reproduction in pre-colonial and colonial periods is a key mechanism through which women's roles have influenced economic growth. During these periods, women significantly contributed to economic development through their involvement in agriculture, trade, and other productive activities. This historical perspective underscores the long-term economic impacts of gender dynamics, as women's roles have evolved in response to broader economic changes. For example, Akyeampong and Fofack note that the use of the plough in historical societies is correlated with more unequal gender roles today, suggesting that historical gender dynamics continue to shape contemporary economic outcomes. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of long-run economic outcomes, as it highlights how historical gender roles can persist and influence economic development over time. Nunn qualifies this perspective by emphasizing the adverse effects of Africa's slave trades on economic development. Nunn's work suggests that the insecurity and ethnic fractionalization resulting from the slave trades may have mitigated the positive contributions of women to economic development, highlighting the complex interplay between historical events and long-run economic outcomes. This interplay underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how gender dynamics and historical events shape economic development over the long run.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) both argue that economic growth during the Industrial Revolution can be attributed to labor dynamics and changes in household production patterns. This shift in household production patterns led to a more capital-intensive economy, with a focus on family-consumed goods and the endowment of children with human capital. The Industrious Revolution was a crucial phase that set the stage for the Industrial Revolution by altering the economic landscape and preparing it for technological advancements. Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this perspective by showing that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that while labor dynamics and household production patterns were pivotal in driving economic growth, the benefits of this growth were not evenly distributed, leading to disparities in health and well-being.

The growth of useful knowledge and secure property rights foster economic development. Kuznets (1973, p.4) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) (Kuznets 1973, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2) argue that economic growth is driven by technological advancements and requires institutional frameworks that ensure secure property rights and enforce economic rules. Kuznets (1973, p.4) further elaborates that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drives changes in society, including family formation and urbanization. These changes are facilitated by major improvements in transport and communication, enabling the spread of economic growth to less developed areas. Mokyr (2005, p.4) builds on this by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, suggesting that the expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all individual knowledge, has significantly contributed to economic development. This expansion is driven by greater specialization and professionalization, which have led to a vastly larger total amount of knowledge in society. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is nuanced. The ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space is a critical factor in fostering economic development. This suggests that while technological advancements and useful knowledge are pivotal, the institutional framework that supports and enforces these advancements is equally important in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) argues that economic institutions are shaped by distributional conflicts, a perspective shared by McCloskey (2015, p.9) (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). This focus on distributional conflicts highlights how power dynamics can influence the formation of institutions that support or hinder economic growth. These conflicts can lead to institutional arrangements that prioritize the interests of certain groups over others, potentially stifling economic dynamism. This institutional framework can create barriers to economic development, as seen in pre-industrial Europe where formal institutions often failed to support long-term economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) further illustrate how economies of localization and the concentration of diverse industries can drive economic growth, but these benefits are contingent on the presence of a supportive institutional environment (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This suggests that while certain economic conditions can foster growth, the institutional context in which these conditions operate is crucial.

The literature does, however, indicate that the relationship between economic development and living standards is complex and multifaceted. These changes in economic development and living standards suggest a complex interplay between demographic shifts, labour dynamics, and economic growth. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) find that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia, despite GDP per capita estimates indicating increasing development between 1000 and 1800 AD (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This indicates that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards, particularly in terms of nutrition, which is a critical aspect of overall well-being. The downstream implication of these findings is that while economic development can drive certain changes in living standards, these changes are often mediated by other factors, such as demographic shifts and labour dynamics. This suggests that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes may be similarly mediated by these complex interactions.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies was driven by institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, particularly the establishment of extractive institutions, concentrated power and wealth in the hands of a small elite, often at the expense of broader economic development. This concentration of power restricted competition and hindered the protection of property rights for the majority, thereby stifling long-term economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman describe how these economic structures, influenced by factor endowments and colonial policies, led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework, characterized by restrictive policies and elite control, created a barrier to economic diversification and technological advancement. Austin qualifies this perspective by highlighting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.23). This suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is mediated by complex historical and social factors, including the specific timing and nature of colonial policies. Austin's critique underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how institutional changes interact with local contexts to shape economic development.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Goldin (2006, p.3) argue that economic growth and equality are not mutually exclusive outcomes, a perspective that challenges classical economists' views. This perspective is supported by the observation that changes in occupational structures and skills deepening have historically driven both economic growth and reduced inequality. from 'jobs' to 'careers' was a critical factor in economic development. This shift involved an increase in women's labor force participation and a fundamental change in how women perceived their roles in the economy, moving from necessity-driven work to identity-driven careers. This transition was marked by significant human capital investment, which in turn fueled economic growth and contributed to a more equitable distribution of wealth. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.28) qualify this by noting that the supply response in skills per worker was slow and inelastic, suggesting that while the demand for skilled labor increased, the actual supply of skilled workers lagged behind. This lag indicates that while economic growth and equality can be achieved together, the process is not straightforward and requires sustained investment in human capital. The implications of this lag are significant, as they highlight the need for policies that encourage economic growth and ensure that the benefits of this growth are widely distributed.

The skill premium is a key factor in predicting long-term economic performance, with van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) establishing that this predictor is tied to both human capital formation and the efficiency of institutions. The skill premium's role in economic performance is further highlighted by its correlation with GDP per capita growth, as seen in the 'little divergence' within Europe between 1400 and 1800 (van Zanden 2009, p.25). This relationship suggests that differences in the price of human capital can help explain broader economic divergences, such as the 'Great Divergence' between Europe and other regions. Broadberry and Gardner note that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous assumptions about the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This qualification underscores the importance of reevaluating historical economic data to understand the nuanced role of the skill premium in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
