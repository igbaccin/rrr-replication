The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history. The debate centers on the causal mechanisms through which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development over the long term. The live disagreement hinges on whether gender roles and economic development are mutually reinforcing or whether other factors, such as institutions or labor allocation, are more pivotal. Some scholars argue that gender roles significantly contribute to economic growth and development, both historically and contemporaneously. Others posit that institutional frameworks and labor allocation are the primary drivers of economic outcomes, with gender playing a secondary role. This debate is crucial because it informs policy decisions and theoretical frameworks in economic development. The question of whether gender and development are intertwined or distinct factors in economic outcomes remains unresolved.

The role of gender dynamics in economic development is evident in historical contexts. Goldin (2006, p.3) highlights that women's employment and education significantly transformed U.S. society, shifting from "jobs" to "careers" and altering societal perceptions of women's roles. This transformation involved a shift from economic necessity to identity and worth, indicating a broader change in societal values and economic structures. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) similarly argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They note that gender discrimination can lower productivity and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers. This historical perspective underscores the importance of gender dynamics in economic development, suggesting that gender roles and economic outcomes are intertwined.

The causal mechanisms linking gender and economic development are complex and multifaceted. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) note that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating a long-term impact of gender dynamics on economic outcomes. This finding suggests that historical gender roles can persist and influence contemporary economic development. Temin (2002, p.4) argues that unusually rapid economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry, highlighting the role of labor dynamics in economic development. This perspective suggests that gender dynamics, as part of broader labor dynamics, can influence economic outcomes. However, Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, suggesting that institutions play a more significant role in economic outcomes than gender dynamics. This debate underscores the complexity of the causal mechanisms linking gender and economic development, indicating that both gender dynamics and institutional frameworks are important factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between gender roles, labor dynamics, and institutional frameworks is a key area of inquiry in understanding economic development.

Goldin observes that this transformation was a significant shift in the labor dynamics of women. This change was marked by a transition from "jobs" to "careers," where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment. This shift is also evidenced by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that women's roles in production and reproduction were central to these economic dynamics, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. The implications of these historical shifts in women's employment are significant, as they suggest that the economic outcomes of societies are deeply intertwined with the roles and opportunities available to women. However, the historical use of the plough in certain cultures has been linked to unequal gender roles today, showing that cultural and historical factors can perpetuate gender discrimination and limit women's participation in the workforce (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10).

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and North (1989, p.5) emphasize the role of institutions in fostering economic growth, focusing on the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. They argue that secure property rights, underpinned by credible commitments from the regime, are crucial for economic growth. This perspective is echoed by North (1989, p.1), who highlights that institutions matter in economic growth, particularly when transaction costs are positive. The credibility of institutions in enforcing property rights and contracts is essential for secure contracting across time and space, which is a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). McCloskey (2015, p.8) notes that ideas and ideologies also play a significant role in economic change. She argues that the success of economic growth in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was driven by the embrace of bourgeois values and libertarianism, which fostered an environment conducive to economic development. This suggests that while institutions are important, the ideologies that shape these institutions are equally critical. The historical evidence from France, where anti-bourgeois sentiments persisted, shows that economic growth can be throttled by ideologies that resist change and innovation (McCloskey 2015, p.9).

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn focus on how institutional changes introduced by colonialism affected long-run economic outcomes. Specifically, they highlight that societies with extractive institutions typically did not allow the masses to obtain education, which is crucial for economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Nunn supports this perspective by showing that Africa's slave trades, which were a product of colonial institutions, had an adverse effect on economic development. The slave trades disrupted social structures and hindered the accumulation of human capital, which is essential for long-term economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27).

Austin qualifies the downstream implications of this institutional perspective. He argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. Austin posits that the stagnation of the South African economy during the last 15 years of the apartheid regime owed much to the effects of systematic racial discrimination in hindering both human capital formation and the growth of demand, as well as generally reducing the flexibility of markets (Austin 2008, p.23). This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions set the stage for long-run economic outcomes, the specific historical context and local dynamics also play a crucial role in shaping these outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) establish that specific historical events have significantly shaped long-term economic trajectories by influencing the distribution of wealth and human capital. They show that the impact of the Black Death and colonial interventions led to structural changes in regional economies. For example, the Black Death initiated a long-term wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450, with the skill premium declining under severe labour shortages (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This gap persisted despite the recovery of the population in the sixteenth century, indicating that the Black Death's effects were not transient. These structural changes, coupled with the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in colonial economies, highlight the enduring impact of historical events on economic development. Broadberry and Gardner further illustrate that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging earlier notions of African economic stagnation (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This finding suggests that pre-colonial institutions and local dynamics played a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, even in the face of colonial interventions.

The role of labor allocation in driving economic growth is evident in both Europe's post-WWII Golden Age and the Industrious Revolution. Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) jointly establish that shifts in labor dynamics and increased productivity were pivotal in these periods. Temin posits that the rapid growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium is distinct from the low income that generates conditional convergence, as it is produced by a misallocation of resources rather than low levels of physical and human capital (Temin 2002, p.9). De Vries (1994, p.17) similarly highlights how the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, facilitated the Industrial Revolution. The increased productivity and labor input during these periods suggest that labor dynamics played a significant role in driving economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.17) cautions that the focus on market goods that minimize home labor inputs and the redistribution within the household could have limited human capital formation in children (de Vries 1994, p.18). This qualification underscores the complex interplay between labor dynamics, productivity, and economic growth, indicating that while labor allocation is crucial, its impact is moderated by broader social and economic factors.

The literature on biological well-being and economic growth does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). The distribution of economic gains is a pivotal mechanism through which growth affects biological well-being. The distribution of economic benefits is not uniform across society, and the resulting inequality can negatively impact those at the lower end of the income spectrum. This pattern is exacerbated during periods of urbanization, which can separate urban dwellers from essential goods like milk, further complicating nutritional outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). However, Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) qualify this pattern by showing that gender inequality also plays a significant role in determining nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values, whereas the effect of gender inequality was about half as strong (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This indicates that while economic growth may not uniformly improve biological well-being, addressing gender inequality could mitigate some of its negative effects.

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, with both direct and indirect impacts. Akyeampong and Fofack, in their analysis of African women’s contributions to economic growth, highlight that women’s roles in pre-colonial and colonial periods were pivotal, often overlooked in traditional economic narratives. This perspective is supported by Goldin, who argues that women’s employment and education significantly transformed family dynamics and societal structures (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Gender inequality, as shown by Akyeampong and Fofack, not only affects immediate economic outcomes but also has long-term implications for human capital investment, which in turn influences productivity and growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). Sokoloff and Engerman's work on the New World societies underscores how institutional frameworks that reinforced inequality often restricted competition and hindered broader economic development (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional bias, as Acemoglu et al. argue, can lead to the securing of property rights for elites at the expense of the broader population, thereby limiting entrepreneurial opportunities and investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Temin's analysis of Europe’s post-WWII growth highlights that economic growth can be driven by labor allocation disequilibria between agriculture and industry, a dynamic that could be influenced by gender roles and development (Temin 2002, p.4). Austin's critique of the 'reversal of fortune' thesis shows that the compression of historical periods and paths can oversimplify the complex interplay between gender, development, and economic outcomes (Austin 2008, p.1). The shared finding of these studies is that gender inequality, particularly in the context of historical institutions, plays a critical role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis, which attributes economic disparities to colonial institutions, oversimplifies the complexity of historical and institutional factors. This critique is grounded in the specific historical evidence of South African manufacturing, which shows that racial discrimination systematically hindered human capital formation and market flexibility (Austin 2008, p.23). Akyeampong and Fofack's analysis of African women's contributions to economic growth provides a counterpoint, demonstrating that addressing gender inequality could mitigate some of the negative effects of institutional biases (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The specific historical evidence of women's roles in pre-colonial and colonial Africa, as well as the measurement of human capital investment, settles the disagreement by showing that gender inequality has both immediate and long-term economic impacts.
