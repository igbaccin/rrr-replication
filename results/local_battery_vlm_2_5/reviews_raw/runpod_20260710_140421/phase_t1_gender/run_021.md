The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history. The debate centers around whether gender dynamics can significantly influence economic trajectories, and if so, through what mechanisms. Some researchers argue that gender roles and economic outcomes are deeply intertwined, with historical gender norms and labor market participation shaping long-term economic performance. Others contend that while gender may play a role, its effects are secondary to broader economic and institutional factors. One live disagreement revolves around the extent to which gender inequality, as shaped by historical and institutional factors, can either hinder or accelerate economic development. The stakes are high, as understanding these dynamics can inform policies aimed at fostering sustainable economic growth. Gender inequality can manifest in various forms, including unequal access to education, employment opportunities, and political representation, all of which can have profound implications for economic development. This review will explore how historical gender dynamics and institutional frameworks have shaped long-run economic outcomes, focusing on the interplay between gender roles, human capital investment, and economic growth. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Goldin (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3) identify that women's roles in the labor market and the structure of occupations are critical factors in the evolution of economic outcomes. The transition from 'jobs' to 'careers' for women, as Goldin argues, altered the landscape of human capital investment. This shift is marked by a change in women's employment from piecework in manufacturing and service sectors, where learning on the job was minimal, to more stable and identity-defining careers (Goldin 2006, p.3). This transition was accompanied by an increase in formal education and human capital investment, which in turn influenced the structure of occupations and earnings inequality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). The transition varied across sectors and regions. The supply of skilled labor was often inelastic, meaning that even as the demand for skills increased, the supply of educated workers did not keep pace. This imbalance created a significant incentive for investment in human capital, but the slow response in skills supply suggests that broader societal and institutional factors may have constrained women's ability to fully capitalize on these opportunities. The implications of these dynamics are profound: while the shift towards careers offered women greater economic stability and identity, the slow supply response in skills indicates that the full potential of this transition was not realized. This highlights the need to examine both the demand for skilled labor and the supply-side factors affecting women's ability to acquire and deploy human capital.

Temin (2002, p.4) and North and Weingast (1989, p.2) share a perspective that various factors shape economic growth and institutional frameworks, though they do not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (Temin 2002, p.4; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Temin (2002, p.4) identifies a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry as a driver of rapid economic growth in post-WWII Europe. This disequilibrium is distinct from the low-income conditions that generate conditional convergence, as it is produced by a misallocation of resources rather than low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates (Temin 2002, p.9). This focus on institutional frameworks and property rights underscores the importance of credible commitments in economic development. Broadberry and Gardner observe that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). This finding suggests that economic growth above subsistence can occur independently of European colonial interventions, indicating that indigenous factors may have played a more significant role in economic development than previously acknowledged (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that secure contracting across time and space is a necessary condition for modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). The nuance here is that while secure contracting is crucial, the specific mechanisms through which it interacts with gender and development dynamics remain under-explored in this literature.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) demonstrate that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes by creating inequality through government policies that restricted competition. These policies advantaged elites, who maintained their power by securing property rights for themselves, while limiting the majority's ability to invest and innovate. This dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where the abundance of mineral resources and low human capital labor contributed to extreme wealth and income inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The persistence of these unequal distributions was further entrenched by the large slave plantations that dominated these economies, creating a system where the majority of the population was held back by the powerful elite. Austin qualifies this implication, noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of colonial institutions on economic outcomes may be more nuanced than initially proposed (Austin 2008, p.1).

Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African economic development has been shaped by the significant contributions of women, both pre-colonially and during the colonial period (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Nunn 2008, p.27). African women have played a central role in both production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their economic impact. The role of women in Africa is critical, as they have historically been involved in agriculture, trade, and other economic activities. This involvement has had a profound effect on economic growth and development. The historical context of African economic development is further complicated by the impact of slave trades, which have had lasting effects on the continent's economic trajectory. Nunn shows that the slave trades contributed to ethnic fractionalization, which in turn has hindered economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4). The insecurity and uncertainty caused by the slave trades led to a need for defensive measures, which diverted resources away from productive activities. This diversion of resources had a detrimental effect on economic development, as it reduced the availability of labor and capital for productive uses. The legacy of the slave trades continues to shape African economic development today, as the high level of ethnic fractionalization persists. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) further elucidate this dynamic by noting that plough use historically correlates with unequal gender roles, which in turn affect female labor force participation. This historical context is crucial for understanding the long-run economic outcomes in Africa, as it highlights the interconnectedness of gender roles, ethnic fractionalization, and economic development.

Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) find that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved biological well-being. This is because economic growth can be unevenly distributed, leading to disparities in nutritional resources and physical stature. This dynamic is evident in the uneven distribution of height across different occupations and regions during the Industrial Revolution. For instance, handloom weavers, who possessed inelastic human or physical capital, were particularly affected by this uneven distribution. The supply of land, on the other hand, benefited landowners at the opposite end of the income distribution. This pattern suggests that the benefits of economic growth may not always trickle down to all segments of society, especially those with inelastic capital supplies. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.28) further elucidate this dynamic by noting that both gender inequality and social inequality have negative effects on biological well-being. They find that social inequality corresponded to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values, whereas the effect of gender inequality was about half as strong. This indicates that while economic growth can lead to improvements in biological well-being, it is often accompanied by increased inequality, which can offset these benefits. The legacy of the slave trades continues to shape African economic development today, as the high level of ethnic fractionalization persists. This is not to say that economic growth is irrelevant to biological well-being. Rather, it highlights the importance of considering the distribution of economic growth and the role of inequality in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Economic growth and technological advancements shape long-run economic outcomes by influencing the skill premium and driving institutional adjustments. van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) argue that economic growth is driven by advancing technology and that the skill premium is a predictor of long-term economic performance. This implies that countries with higher levels of human capital formation tend to have lower skill premiums, which in turn can drive economic growth. These structural changes are facilitated by major advancements in transport and communication, which enable the sequential spread of economic growth to less developed areas. Mokyr (2005, p.4) extends this idea by arguing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth. The expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all pieces of individual knowledge, has vastly increased the total amount of knowledge that societies possess. This increased knowledge base drives technological advancements and economic growth. This consensus-shaping mechanism determines which beliefs will predominate and influence the nature of economic growth. Therefore, the interplay between technological advancements, institutional adjustments, and the growth of useful knowledge collectively shapes long-run economic outcomes by driving economic growth and influencing the skill premium.

Gender and development play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The transformation of women's employment and education in the United States, for instance, significantly altered economic dynamics. Women's roles shifted from "jobs" to "careers," marking a change in societal perceptions of women's work and its economic value (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift was driven by a change in women's occupational identities, where work became a defining aspect of one's worth and societal standing (Goldin 2006, p.1). The skill premium, defined as the wage differential between skilled and unskilled labor, predicts long-term economic performance. A low skill premium generally reflects a high level of human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27). In societies where women have greater access to education and career opportunities, the skill premium tends to be lower, indicating a more equitable distribution of human capital. This dynamic is crucial for understanding how gender and development intersect to influence economic growth. The historical evidence shows that societies with higher levels of human capital formation among women tend to experience more sustained economic growth. This is because investment in human capital, particularly among women, leads to a more skilled workforce capable of driving technological advancements and economic growth (Goldin 2006, p.3)(van Zanden 2009, p.27).
