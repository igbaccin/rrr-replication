The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a pivotal question in economic history. At stake is the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development, and whether these dynamics are more consequential than other factors. The debate centers on the extent to which gender roles and economic development are interdependent. One perspective argues that gender roles significantly impact economic development, particularly through human capital investment and labor force participation. Another perspective posits that economic development and gender roles are shaped by institutions, which can either reinforce or mitigate gender inequalities. These positions conflict over whether gender roles and economic development are mutually reinforcing or whether institutional frameworks are the primary drivers. The interplay between these factors is complex, and understanding their roles is crucial for comprehending the long-term economic trajectories of societies. The historical evolution of gender roles and economic development provides insights into how these dynamics can shape long-run economic outcomes. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3); (Goldin 2006, p.3) challenge the marginalization of African women's contributions to economic development, highlighting their significant role during pre-colonial and colonial periods. They argue that African women were central to both production and reproduction, contrary to views that overlook their economic agency. This perspective is supported by Goldin, who describes how women in the U.S. transitioned from being mere workers to professionals, indicating a shift in societal perceptions of women's economic roles. This transition involved a change from "jobs" to "careers, " where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). Such a shift suggests that when women are integrated into economic development, they contribute significantly to growth. Akyeampong and Fofack note that gender discrimination can lower average productivity and affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This qualification underscores the importance of addressing gender discrimination to fully harness the economic potential of women. The historical evidence from both pre-colonial Africa and the U.S. indicates that women's economic contributions are pivotal to long-run economic outcomes, but this potential is contingent on reducing gender-based barriers.

Technological advancements and institutional adjustments are pivotal for long-term economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr argue (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4). These changes are both economic and social, involving shifts in occupational structures and urbanization (Kuznets 1973, p.4). Similarly, Mokyr highlights the role of useful knowledge in economic growth, suggesting that the expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all individual pieces of knowledge, is crucial for sustained economic development (Mokyr 2005, p.4). This perspective underscores the importance of technological progress and institutional efficiency in driving long-term economic outcomes. This qualification suggests that while technological advancements are necessary, the efficiency of institutions in forming human capital is also critical for translating technological progress into economic growth.

Institutional changes introduced by colonialism played a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, particularly in reversing relative incomes among former European colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Acemoglu et al. and Sokoloff and Engerman both argue that colonial institutions significantly influenced economic structures and wealth distributions. Acemoglu et al. argue that in societies where the elite's property rights were secure, the majority of the population faced risks of being held up by the powerful elite after undertaking investments, thereby stifling economic growth. Sokoloff and Engerman highlight that the economic structures in these colonies were heavily influenced by factor endowments, leading to highly unequal distributions of wealth and income. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite further exacerbated inequality, as the masses typically did not or could not obtain education in such societies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin qualifies the 'reversal of fortune' thesis by noting that it oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. He argues that the stagnation of economies like South Africa during the apartheid regime was due to systematic racial discrimination, which hindered human capital formation and reduced market flexibility (Austin 2008, p.1). This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions were crucial, the specific historical and social contexts in which they operated also played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, involving the initial institutional frameworks and the evolving social and economic conditions that either reinforced or mitigated their effects.

Broadberry and Gardner, together with Broadberry and Gupta, examine economic performance in Africa and Asia, focusing on historical economic growth and divergence (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). They challenge previous narratives that attribute economic growth in these regions solely to external interventions, such as colonial rule. Broadberry and Gupta further note that the benefits of industrialization were not limited to urban areas but could also be realized in rural settings, challenging the notion that industrialization requires large-scale urbanization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). This insight suggests that the economic growth in Africa and Asia was driven by both European colonial interventions and local economic activities and industrialization processes.

North and Weingast, along with North, examine how institutions and ideologies shaped economic growth by fostering secure property rights and market development, particularly focusing on the political factors underpinning economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). These scholars highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that a critical political factor is the degree to which the regime or sovereign is committed to or bound by these rules. This commitment underpins economic growth by enabling secure contracting across time and space, which is necessary for the creation of modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). McCloskey qualifies this view by arguing that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey contends that the cultural and ideological landscape played a significant role in shaping economic outcomes (McCloskey 2015, p.9). This perspective suggests that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes may be influenced by the interplay between institutional frameworks and ideological shifts, which together create the conditions for economic growth.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) share a posture that economic growth and labor dynamics are pivotal for understanding the long-run economic outcomes of different historical periods, but they do not address how gender and development shaped these outcomes. This disequilibrium is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence (Temin 2002, p.4; Temin 2002, p.9). De Vries (1994, p.17) argues that the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, was a distinct phenomenon preceding and facilitating the Industrial Revolution. This revolution led to a more capital-intensive production system and a market demand focused on family-consumed goods, indicating a shift in household production patterns (de Vries 1994, p.17; de Vries 1994, p.18). This shift had profound consequences for the entire economy, including lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, and higher educational levels. However, Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this by noting that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). This implies that the benefits of economic growth accrued unevenly, with some groups, particularly those with inelastic human or physical capital, being made worse off. Therefore, the trends of average physical stature and income could have diverged from one another, highlighting the complex interplay between economic growth and biological well-being. This suggests that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes may be influenced by the uneven distribution of resources and the varying impacts of economic growth on different groups.

Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) and Ogilvie (2007, p.25) share a focus on economic policies and distributional conflicts, but they do not directly engage with how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8)(Ogilvie 2007, p.25). The shared posture is that economic growth and distributional equity are not mutually exclusive. The mechanisms they identify are varied, but both highlight the importance of institutional frameworks in shaping economic outcomes. Ogilvie (2007, p.25) complements this view by emphasizing the role of poor contract enforcement and property rights in choking off economic dynamism (Ogilvie 2007, p.25). This suggests that the effectiveness of economic policies in promoting growth and equity depends on the institutional context, including the enforcement of contracts and the protection of property rights. The downstream implication is that economic policies aimed at reducing inequality must address institutional failures that limit investment in human capital. Simson (2019, p.27) qualifies this by showing that post-independence budget policies in East Africa were not exceptionally clientelist or unproductive, indicating that the relationship between economic policies and long-run outcomes is complex and context-dependent (Simson 2019, p.27). This underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how economic policies and distributional conflicts interact with institutional frameworks to shape long-run economic outcomes.

The literature on economic development and standard of living does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Koepke and Baten (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19) share a focus on the complex relationship between economic growth and living standards in Europe. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) shows that the relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 was not straightforward (van Zanden 1999, p.3). Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) argue that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This suggests that economic development did not necessarily translate into immediate improvements in living standards. One specific mechanism that emerges from this literature is the role of demographic shocks, such as the Black Death, in altering economic structures. Pamuk (2007, p.15) demonstrates that the Black Death initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This demographic shock had profound effects on economic and social structures, influencing wage levels and urbanisation rates (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The impact of such shocks on gender dynamics and their subsequent influence on long-run economic outcomes remains under-explored. This highlights the need for further research into how demographic changes, including those driven by gender dynamics, interact with economic development to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Nunn (2008, p.27) and Stern (2004, p.8) jointly demonstrate that environmental and historical factors have significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes, with Africa's slave trades being a critical determinant of its economic development. Nunn argues that slave trades were a significant factor explaining Africa's high level of ethnic fractionalization today. The environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) concept suggests that economic growth can lead to environmental improvement, which could potentially mitigate some of the adverse effects of historical factors. Stern (2004, p.8) notes that the EKC has a very flimsy statistical foundation and its demise may be imminent due to new generation of decomposition and efficient frontier models. This implies that the relationship between economic growth and environmental improvement is not as straightforward as the EKC hypothesis suggests. Therefore, while historical factors like slave trades have had profound effects on economic development, the interaction between economic growth and environmental factors adds another layer of complexity to the long-run economic outcomes.

The role of gender in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted, with historical and institutional factors playing crucial roles. Goldin (2006, p.1) and Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) both highlight the transformative impact of women's employment and education on economic development. Goldin (2006, p.1) argues that the shift from "jobs" to "careers" for women involved significant human capital investment, changing societal perceptions of women's roles. Meanwhile, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) demonstrate that African women's contributions to economic growth were substantial during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role. Their findings show that gender discrimination can lower average productivity and adversely affect long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers. This underscores the importance of recognizing women's historical contributions to economic development.

The impact of institutions on economic outcomes is further illustrated by Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6). Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) contend that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and geographic factors. They argue that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to systematic cross-country differences in human capital. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) similarly show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework shaped economic structures that favored large plantations and slave labor, leading to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and human capital. The historical evidence shows that economic institutions shaped by colonial policies were instrumental in determining long-run economic outcomes.
