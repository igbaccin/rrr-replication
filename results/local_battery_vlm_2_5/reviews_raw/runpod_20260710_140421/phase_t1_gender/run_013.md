The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship can inform policies aimed at fostering inclusive economic growth. One live disagreement centers on the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic development. Some scholars argue that gender roles and policies affecting women's economic participation are critical determinants of long-run economic outcomes. Others contend that institutional factors, such as property rights and political structures, are more decisive in shaping economic development. This debate is not merely academic; it has real-world implications for policy-making and economic planning. The central question is whether gender dynamics operate through institutional channels to influence long-run economic outcomes, or if other factors, such as human capital investment and technological advancement, are more pivotal. The answer to this question can shape how we understand and address economic disparities globally. This review begins with the role of gender in economic development, focusing on the contributions of women to economic growth and the underlying mechanisms that facilitate or hinder these contributions. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” van Zanden (2009, p.27) reports, “There is an obvious link to factor endowments: interest rates reﬂecting relative abundance of capital (and the efﬁciency of capital markets) appear to be an important factor determining the skill premium.”

Goldin (2006, p.3); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argues that a quiet revolution in women's employment transformed long-run economic outcomes. This revolution involved a shift in women's roles from piece workers in manufacturing to more skilled professions, marking a significant change in the economic landscape. Women's employment evolved from mere necessity to a defining aspect of personal identity and societal worth. This transformation was a profound shift that reshaped economic dynamics, going beyond mere evolution. Akyeampong and Fofack expand on this by highlighting that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This contribution was multifaceted, involving women in various productive activities that were central to economic sustainability. The role of women in economic development is further underscored by the historical context, where women's labor was crucial for economic growth. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) note that the persistence of gender discrimination over historical time can adversely affect long-run growth. This qualification suggests that while women's contributions were significant, the broader economic outcomes were also influenced by persistent gender inequalities. This points to the complex interplay between women's economic roles and the broader socio-economic structures that either facilitate or hinder their contributions.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) share a view that institutions in societies with greater inequality often advantage elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite can create a scenario where the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional setup can hinder economic growth by preventing the broader population from participating fully in economic activities, thereby limiting overall productivity and innovation. In such societies, the lack of effective property rights protection for the majority can deter investment and entrepreneurship, as individuals may fear expropriation or exploitation by the elite (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional framework, which favours the elite, can create a vicious cycle where inequality persists and economic growth is stunted. Austin (2008, p.23) qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the relationship between institutions and economic outcomes is more complex and nuanced than it might initially appear (Austin 2008, p.1).

North and Weingast argue that institutions play a crucial role in economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). North (1989, p.5) elaborates that productivity can increase through changes in institutions that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights. This institutional framework is essential for economic growth, as it reduces transaction costs and encourages investment and entrepreneurship. Temin (2002, p.4) expands on this by noting that differences in education between countries can eliminate the prediction of unconditional convergence, although they still leave room for conditional convergence for groups of similar countries. This suggests that institutional factors, such as property rights and education, are crucial for economic growth and can lead to convergence clubs where countries with similar institutions experience similar growth rates. Temin (2002, p.9) notes that the developmental deficit highlighted by a misallocation of resources is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence. This suggests that while institutions are important for economic growth, they are not the only factor.

de Vries (1994, p.17) and Komlos (1998, p.7) focus on economic and social changes during industrialization, examining aspects like labor input patterns and nutritional status (de Vries 1994, p.17; Komlos 1998, p.7). The Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, significantly influenced the economic structure by making it more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers. This shift had profound consequences, including a focus on quality over quantity in child endowment with human capital. It was through household productive systems that major achievements of industrial society were realized. These achievements included lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort. The benefits of industrialization were not uniformly distributed, leading to divergent trends in average physical stature and income. Komlos (1998, p.7) notes that while economic growth occurred, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that the benefits of industrialization did not uniformly translate into improved living standards for all segments of society. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) further qualify this by showing that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia, despite recent GDP per capita estimates indicating increasing development between 1000 and 1800 AD. This implies that the economic growth during industrialization did not necessarily lead to improved nutritional outcomes, highlighting the complexities of translating economic development into tangible improvements in living standards.

Lindert and Williamson, along with Broadberry and Gardner, examine the dynamics of economic growth and inequality without directly addressing how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8)(Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1). The two authors show that growth and inequality are intricately linked, with shifts in occupational structures and skills playing a critical role in shaping both. This dynamic is evident in the British case, where unbalanced productivity advances and skills deepening drove significant changes in earnings inequality during the 19th century (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This mechanism underscores the importance of human capital investment in mitigating inequality, as rapid expansion in the derived demand for skills incentivized such investment. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker suggests that the benefits of economic growth may not be evenly distributed across society. This qualification highlights the complexities of translating economic growth into tangible improvements in living standards, a theme echoed in the context of African economic performance. Broadberry and Gardner challenge the prevailing narrative that African economies were stagnant prior to colonial intervention, showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This finding underscores the importance of reevaluating historical economic performance, as pre-colonial economic growth may have been more robust than previously thought.

Broadberry and Gupta, along with Pamuk, examine the early modern economic divergence, focusing on the great divergence between Europe and Asia before 1800 and the structural changes initiated by the Black Death in Europe (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Pamuk 2007, p.15). These studies highlight that the Industrial Revolution was a long-term process with deep historical roots. The economies of localization in Europe, which benefited from specialized information flows and skilled labor supplies, played a crucial role in this divergence. This localization was consistent with proto-industrialization (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Pamuk's analysis of urbanization rates in Europe underscores the structural changes that occurred post-Black Death, with northwestern Europe experiencing higher productivity and economic development compared to southern Europe (Pamuk 2007, p.16). The implications of these structural changes are profound, as they set the stage for the long-term economic divergence between Europe and Asia. The economic and social transformations initiated by the Black Death and the subsequent structural changes in Europe highlight the complex interplay between historical events and long-term economic outcomes, suggesting that similar events in other regions could have led to different economic trajectories.

Technological advancements are critical for sustained economic development, as Kuznets and van Zanden (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Kuznets 1973, p.4) jointly emphasise. Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that the dynamic drives of modern economic growth necessitate rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn demand institutional and ideological adjustments. These adjustments facilitate the efficient use of innovations, enabling a country's economic growth to be defined as a long-term rise in capacity to supply increasingly diverse economic goods to its population. The skill premium, therefore, serves as a predictor of long-term economic performance, suggesting that countries with higher skill premiums are likely to experience slower economic growth. This relationship underscores the importance of technological advancements and institutional efficiency in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Mokyr (2005, p.4) qualifies this pattern by noting that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, as societies with greater consensus on useful knowledge tend to experience higher rates of economic growth. This qualification suggests that the relationship between technological advancements and economic growth is mediated by the societal consensus on useful knowledge.

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is significantly influenced by the evolution of women's roles in the economy. Goldin (2006, p.3) argues that women's employment shifted from piecework in manufacturing and service sectors to more professional roles, marking a transition from "jobs" to "careers". This shift involved a change in the fundamental identity and societal worth attributed to women's work, indicating a broader transformation in economic structures. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3; 2014, p.10) build on this by showing that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their analysis highlights that women's roles in production and reproduction were central to economic development, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. This historical perspective underscores the importance of recognizing women's economic activities and their impact on long-run economic outcomes.

Moreover, the institutional frameworks that govern economic activities play a crucial role in shaping these outcomes. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33; 2002, p.48) argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism. These changes, which often favored elites, restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6; 2000, p.5) further illustrate this point by showing that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies. This institutional bias limited the economic opportunities for the broader population, thereby affecting long-run economic outcomes. The skill premium, as a predictor of long-term economic performance, is also influenced by these institutional factors. van Zanden (2009, p.27; 2009, p.25) shows that skill premiums are related to the efficiency of institutions for the formation of human capital and the absence or presence of barriers to entry in these professions. This relationship highlights the importance of institutional efficiency in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The underlying point of agreement is that institutional frameworks and the evolution of women's roles are critical factors in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8; 1985, p.28) show that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, highlighting the potential for inclusive economic policies that benefit both genders. The precise mechanisms by which these factors interact remain a subject of debate. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) identify historical plough use as a factor that correlates with unequal gender roles today, suggesting that cultural and historical factors play a significant role in shaping gender dynamics. The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the empirical comparison of gender norms and female labor force participation across different cultures, as shown by the strong and robust positive correlation between historical plough use and unequal gender roles today.
