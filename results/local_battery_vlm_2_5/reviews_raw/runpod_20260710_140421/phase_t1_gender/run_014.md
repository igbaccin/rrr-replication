The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a pivotal question in economic history, as it illuminates how societal structures and historical processes influence contemporary economic disparities. At the heart of this debate lies a disagreement between scholars who attribute economic outcomes to institutional changes introduced by colonialism and those who emphasize the significance of gender dynamics in economic development. Some scholars argue that the reversal of relative incomes among former European colonies is primarily due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which either protected private property or facilitated rent extraction. Others contend that gender dynamics, particularly the contributions of women to economic growth and development, have been historically significant and often overlooked. This disagreement underscores the need to understand how gender and development intersect to shape long-run economic outcomes. The stakes are high, as resolving this debate can provide insights into the mechanisms underlying economic growth and development, informing policies aimed at reducing economic disparities. The question of how gender dynamics shape economic outcomes remains a central issue in understanding the economic history of different regions. Goldin (2006, p.3) documents how women in the U.S. were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses, with little or no learning on the job and slight gains from formal human capital beyond common or elementary school. This pattern of underinvestment in women's human capital has been a critical factor in the historical marginalization of women's economic contributions. Goldin (2006, p.3) observes a notable shift beginning in the 1910s, when the number of professional women, especially teachers and clerical employees, grew substantially. This expansion suggests that changes in societal norms and economic structures can lead to increased opportunities for women, thereby enhancing their contributions to economic development. The implication is that policies aimed at reducing gender discrimination and increasing investment in women's human capital can have profound effects on long-run economic outcomes.

These technological advancements and institutional changes are pivotal in driving economic growth, a fact that Kuznets and Mokyr both underscore (Kuznets 1973, p.4)(Mokyr 2005, p.4). Kuznets posits that economic growth demands significant institutional and ideological adaptations to harness technological progress efficiently. In tandem, Mokyr asserts that the accumulation of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth in the West. This process is facilitated by the expansion of useful knowledge, which includes both individual and social knowledge. Greater specialization and professionalization within societies have led to an exponential increase in the total amount of knowledge available, thereby propelling economic growth. Mokyr points out that the advantages of this knowledge expansion are not evenly spread; they tend to concentrate in societies with mechanisms that shape consensus and dictate prevalent beliefs (Mokyr 2005, p.3). This implies that while technological advancements can spur economic growth, the socio-cultural context in which these advancements occur plays a crucial role in determining their impact. The downstream implication is that societies with robust consensus-shaping mechanisms are more likely to harness technological progress effectively, leading to sustained economic growth. This continuous expansion and adaptation are facilitated by major changes in transport and communication, which enable the sequential spread of economic growth to less developed areas.

These institutional changes, in turn, have had profound effects on long-term economic outcomes, as seen in the context of colonialism. Acemoglu et al. and Austin both focus on how colonial institutions affected economic development, but Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Austin 2008, p.23). A key mechanism for this institutional change is the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite. This elite, often the colonial rulers, could secure property rights for themselves while leaving the majority of the population vulnerable to exploitation. The lack of property rights protection for the broader population stifled investment and entrepreneurial activity, as individuals risked being held up by the powerful elite after making investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Austin adds that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies complex historical processes by condensing different periods and paths. Austin contends that South Africa's stagnation resulted from both colonial institutions and the specific policies of racial discrimination that followed (Austin 2008, p.23). This qualification suggests that while colonial institutions play a significant role, the specific policies and historical contexts of each colony also shape long-term economic outcomes. The impact of colonialism on economic development is thus multifaceted, involving both institutional changes and the specific historical contexts of each colony.

Institutional changes introduced by European powers during colonialism have been shown to significantly shape long-run economic outcomes by perpetuating inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; North and Weingast 1989, p.2). Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) demonstrate that in New World societies with greater inequality, institutions favored elites through various government policies, which restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality. These policies were influenced by factor endowments, such as valuable mineral resources and an abundant low-skilled labor force, which led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that secure property rights and economic growth are underpinned by institutions governing public choice, which enforce rules and ensure they are followed. One downstream implication of these policies is the creation of large plantations and the dominance of slave labor in certain economies. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) note that while robust economies can exist despite uncertainties, modern economies require the ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space. The evolution of institutions governing public choice is thus crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, and the specific policies and historical contexts of each colony also shape long-term economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17)  establish that labor allocation and economic growth are pivotal phenomena that, while critical to understanding economic development, do not directly address gender and development as causal factors in long-run economic outcomes. These works highlight distinct periods of economic growth driven by different mechanisms. This perspective underscores that economic growth can be influenced by the efficient reallocation of labor resources, which is a separate phenomenon from the low income that generates conditional convergence (Temin 2002, p.9). De Vries (1994, p.17)  examines the Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, which preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution (de Vries 1994, p.17). This period saw a shift in household production patterns, with a focus on quality over quantity in human capital formation (de Vries 1994, p.17). The downstream implication of these findings is that while labor allocation and economic growth are crucial, they do not inherently account for the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This is because the mechanisms identified by Temin and de Vries do not explicitly consider the gender dynamics that might influence labor allocation and economic growth.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) show that historical economic growth in Africa and Asia has been more robust and nuanced than previously thought. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging the notion that African economic performance was uniformly poor. This indicates that the economic performance of Africa and Asia was influenced by complex, endogenous drivers in addition to European colonial interventions. The implication is that the economic trajectories of Africa and Asia were shaped by a variety of factors, including local economic conditions and regional dynamics. This nuanced view of historical economic growth in Africa and Asia underscores the importance of considering multiple factors in understanding long-run economic outcomes. The role of gender in shaping these outcomes remains largely unexplored in this literature.

This complexity is further illustrated by Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Komlos (1998, p.7), who argue that the relationship between economic development and the standard of living in Europe is multifaceted, highlighting that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved biological well-being. Komlos (1998, p.7) shows that during industrialization, some groups, such as handloom weavers, experienced worsened living standards due to uneven income distribution and poor childhood nourishment. This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that economic growth can exacerbate inequalities, potentially affecting long-run economic outcomes. For instance, Koepke and Baten (2005, p.28) note that gender inequality significantly impacts nutritional status, indicating that social and gender inequalities can influence the distribution of economic benefits. This nuanced view of historical economic growth in Europe underscores the importance of considering multiple factors, including gender and social inequalities, in understanding long-run economic outcomes.
