The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history and development studies. At stake is the understanding of how gender dynamics influence economic growth and inequality over extended periods. The debate centers around the extent to which gender roles and development trajectories are intertwined, with some scholars emphasizing the direct causal impact of gender equality on economic outcomes, while others point to the mediating effects of institutional and structural factors. The live disagreement revolves around whether gender dynamics directly drive economic growth or if they operate through broader institutional and developmental frameworks. This disagreement is significant because it shapes policies aimed at fostering economic development and reducing inequality. Institutions, such as property rights and labor market regulations, are often cited as crucial mediators in the relationship between gender and economic outcomes. The interplay between gender roles and these institutions is complex, with some arguing that gender equality can enhance economic growth by increasing labor force participation and human capital investment. Some scholars argue that gender's effect on economic outcomes is complex, shaped by factors such as historical context, cultural norms, and broader developmental trajectories. Institutional changes introduced by colonialism significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes, particularly the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These changes often led to the creation of societies where the property rights of a small elite were secure, while the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after undertaking investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Such policies fostered extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income, with large plantations and slave labor dominating the economies of these colonies (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional framework maintained inequality and impeded broader economic development by curtailing competition and limiting opportunities for most of the population. The effects of these institutional changes vary across colonies. Austin argues that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This qualification suggests that while institutional changes were crucial, their effects varied depending on the specific historical and economic contexts of each colony.

Economic transformations and labor dynamics, such as those during the Industrious Revolution, significantly influenced long-run economic outcomes (de Vries 1994, p.17; Temin 2002, p.4). This transition also modified market demand structures, increasingly focusing on family-consumed goods and prioritizing child quality over quantity (de Vries 1994, p.17). This transformation was crucial in achieving many of the major advancements of industrial society, including lower morbidity and mortality rates, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort (de Vries 1994, p.17). Komlos (1998, p.7) observes that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to inadequate income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that the benefits of economic growth were unevenly distributed, particularly affecting groups with inelastic supplies of human or physical capital, such as handloom weavers (Komlos 1998, p.7). This uneven distribution of benefits implies that while economic transformations drove long-run economic outcomes, they also exacerbated inequalities, potentially hindering broader economic development. These findings underscore the complex interplay between economic transformations, labor dynamics, and long-run economic outcomes, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of how these factors shape economic development. The economic benefits of growth did not uniformly translate into improved well-being for all segments of the population.

Historical events, such as the Black Death and Africa's slave trades, significantly influenced long-term economic outcomes by reshaping economic structures and labor dynamics (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9; Nunn 2008, p.27). For instance, the Black Death in Europe led to a decline in the skill premium due to severe labor shortages, which in turn encouraged human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This pattern reflects a broader mechanism where historical shocks disrupt existing economic structures, creating new opportunities for growth. Pamuk (2007, p.16) notes that while urbanization rates rose in northwestern Europe post-Black Death, this trend was not uniform across the continent. This suggests that the impacts of such events were not homogeneous, and regional factors played a crucial role in shaping long-term economic outcomes. The differential effects of the Black Death across Europe underscore the importance of regional economic conditions in mediating the impacts of historical events on economic development.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with van Zanden, show that research on pre-industrial economies has often focused on understanding economic performance without explicitly linking it to gender dynamics or long-run economic outcomes (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; van Zanden 1999, p.1). These studies highlight a significant gap in the literature, as they tend to overlook the role of gender in shaping economic development. Broadberry and Gardner's work on African economies and van Zanden's analysis of European economic growth both illustrate a common pattern: economic performance is often examined in isolation from broader social and gender dynamics. This oversight is particularly evident in van Zanden's analysis of early modern Europe, where the focus is on real wages and the standard of living without considering the impact of gender roles on these economic indicators. Broadberry and Gardner's study of African economic performance similarly neglects the influence of gender, instead attributing economic growth to factors such as colonial interventions and infrastructure development. This view acknowledges that early economic histories of Africa frequently overlooked indigenous economic activities and the role of local populations in driving economic growth. This qualification suggests that a more comprehensive understanding of pre-industrial economies requires integrating gender dynamics into the analysis of economic performance.

Mokyr and Kuznets argue that economic growth is driven by the accumulation of useful knowledge and advancing technology (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This knowledge is not merely individual; it is social, expanding through greater specialization and professionalization, leading to a vast accumulation of collective knowledge that drives economic growth. Kuznets elaborates on this by noting that economic growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use technological innovations (Kuznets 1973, p.4). These adjustments are necessary for the labor force to participate effectively in the modern economic system, necessitating rapid changes in occupational structures and societal views on achievement. McCloskey's work on ideas and ideologies complements this perspective by showing that economic change in Northwestern Europe was driven by shifts in ideas rather than interests or institutions (McCloskey 2015, p.9). McCloskey argues that the success of modern economic growth in France, for example, was influenced by the examples set by the Anglo-Saxons and their successful economic and political models (McCloskey 2015, p.8). This highlights that the diffusion of ideas across societies can significantly impact economic growth trajectories. The role of gender and development in shaping these knowledge systems and technological advancements remains under-explored, indicating that a more comprehensive understanding of long-run economic outcomes requires integrating gender dynamics into the analysis of economic performance.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2), together with Lindert and Williamson (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8), argue that institutions and ideology play a crucial role in fostering economic growth. They highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was pivotal in creating secure property rights, which in turn facilitated economic growth. The development of these institutions, which governed how rules were enforced and changed, was a critical factor in England's economic success. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) emphasize that the commitment to these rules by the regime or sovereign was essential for the creation of modern economies reliant on specialization and division of labor. North (1989, p.5) further elaborates on the importance of institutions, arguing that economic growth can occur through increasing productivity driven by technological change or changes in institutions that affect property rights. This perspective underscores that economic growth is not solely determined by population and savings, as neoclassical theories suggest, but is significantly influenced by the institutional framework. North (1989, p.5) and Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) both argue for the importance of institutions in economic growth (North 1989, p.1; Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). For example, changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and changes in the rate of skills deepening are critical forces driving economic growth. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker can mitigate the benefits of these changes. This suggests that while institutional adjustments are crucial for economic growth, the effectiveness of these adjustments can be contingent on various factors, including the responsiveness of the labor market to changes in skill demand.

The role of gender in economic development is critical for long-term economic outcomes. The transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. illustrates how gender dynamics can shift economic participation. This shift involves a transition from "jobs" to "careers," where employment defines one's identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). This change involves significant human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). Similarly, African women have historically contributed to economic growth and development, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Their contributions span pre-colonial and colonial periods, underscoring the centrality of women to production and reproduction (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This historical evidence shows that gender dynamics are pivotal in shaping economic outcomes over the long run.

The role of human capital is pivotal in this context. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker can mitigate the benefits of institutional adjustments (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This is consistent with the view that industrial technologies were appropriate only for societies with sufficient human capital (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The correlation between institutions and human capital reflects the fact that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights the importance of human capital investment in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
