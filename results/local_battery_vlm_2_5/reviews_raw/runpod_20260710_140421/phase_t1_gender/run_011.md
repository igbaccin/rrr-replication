The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development. At the heart of this disagreement lies the question of whether gender roles and economic development are mutually reinforcing or whether economic development can proceed independently of gender dynamics. Some scholars argue that gender roles and economic development are mutually reinforcing, with changes in gender roles driving economic development and vice versa. Others contend that economic development can proceed independently of gender dynamics, with economic growth and development driven by factors such as institutions, technology, and human capital. The stakes are high, as understanding the relationship between gender and development can inform policies aimed at promoting economic growth and reducing inequality.

The role of gender dynamics in shaping long-run economic outcomes is evident in the historical transformation of women's employment and education. This, in turn, had implications for the distribution of income and wealth, as women's increased human capital investment contributed to a more equal distribution of income and wealth. The literature on gender and economic development, as argued by Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8); (Goldin 2006, p.3), explores how changes in women's employment, education, and family dynamics have influenced long-run economic outcomes. Goldin (2006, p.3) elaborates on this by documenting the shift in women's employment patterns, noting that women moved from low-skilled, low-paying jobs to more skilled and professional roles. This transition involved a significant investment in human capital, as women began to pursue careers, leading to substantial gains in formal education and on-the-job training. The implication of this shift is that the increased human capital investment by women contributed to a higher skill premium, which in turn drove economic growth. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28) suggests that while the demand for skilled labor increased, the supply of skilled workers did not keep pace, potentially limiting the full realization of economic growth.

Akyeampong and Fofack highlight that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). They note that women's roles in production and reproduction were central to economic activities, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. This centrality is evident in the historical plough use, which correlates with contemporary gender roles, indicating that cultural differences in gender norms are deeply rooted in historical practices (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). Koepke and Baten's work on European nutritional status complements this by showing that nutritional quality improved after mortality crises due to enhanced land availability, suggesting that economic shocks can alter resource distribution and thereby affect nutritional outcomes (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This nuance is important because it suggests that while economic shocks can lead to improvements in nutritional status, the distribution of these benefits depends on various factors, including urbanization and access to resources. The implications of these findings extend to understanding the long-run economic outcomes, as they highlight the significance of women's roles in economic development and the complex interplay between economic shocks, resource distribution, and nutritional status.

Sokoloff and Engerman and Acemoglu et al. show that the institutional frameworks established in New World societies, particularly those with high levels of inequality, were designed to advantage elites (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). These institutions, by restricting competition and securing property rights for the elite, fostered economic structures that perpetuated inequality. This dynamic is evident in the colonial sugar economies, where the abundance of mineral resources and low human capital among the labor force contributed to extreme wealth disparities (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The persistence of these inequalities was further reinforced by policies that limited European migration and granted generous property and tribute awards to early settlers. This institutional bias towards elites created a scenario where the majority of the population, lacking effective property rights protection, faced significant barriers to economic advancement. Acemoglu et al. argue that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses typically did not or could not obtain education, leading to a correlation between institutions and human capital that reinforced existing inequalities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Austin qualifies this by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causal pathways by compressing different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of institutional changes on long-run economic outcomes is more complex than initially proposed (Austin 2008, p.1). This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes and economic inequality interact over time, highlighting the enduring influence of historical institutional frameworks on contemporary economic outcomes.

Institutional and ideological adjustments are essential for economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4) jointly assert. Kuznets highlights how economic growth demands institutional and ideological shifts to accommodate technological advancements and structural changes in society. He observes that economic growth alters occupational structures and reshapes family formations, urbanization patterns, and societal views on individual roles and achievements. This dynamic process is facilitated by major changes in transport and communication, enabling the continuous expansion of economic growth to less developed areas. Mokyr complements this view by emphasizing that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth; he states that the expansion of social knowledge, defined as the union of all pieces of individual knowledge, has vastly increased. This collective knowledge has facilitated economic growth, as it has allowed societies to harness technological advancements more effectively. North and Weingast (1989, p.2) extend this argument by emphasizing the role of institutions governing public choice in fostering secure property rights and economic growth. They argue that the evolution of institutions in seventeenth-century England was crucial for creating a regime committed to rules that underpin economic growth. This institutional framework enabled secure contracting across time and space, which is necessary for modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) also acknowledge that the relationship between institutional changes and economic growth is complex, as other factors such as property rights and robust local economies also play significant roles. This complexity underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how institutional changes and economic growth interact over time.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and North (1989, p.5) argue that economic institutions, which are shaped by the interplay of efficiency and distributional conflicts, play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth. These institutions create formal and informal rules that structure economic interactions, influencing outcomes such as contract enforcement, property rights, and overall economic dynamism. The efficiency approach, which posits that institutions maximize economic growth or aggregate economic welfare, is often invoked to explain institutional outcomes. This approach can be vague and complicated, as it often overlooks the distributional conflicts and power dynamics that shape institutions. Ogilvie (2007, p.25; 2007, p.9) notes that poor contract enforcement and property rights can choke off economic dynamism, limiting investments in physical and human capital. North (1989, p.5; 1989, p.1) further elaborates that economic growth can occur through increasing productivity, driven by technological change or institutional adjustments that affect property rights. McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies this view by emphasizing the role of ideologies and ideas in driving economic change. For instance, McCloskey (2015, p.9; 2015, p.8) highlights that in France, the lack of bourgeois economic and political successes hindered modern economic growth. This suggests that while institutions matter, the broader ideological context in which they operate can significantly influence their impact on economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) both emphasize that economic growth is driven by changes in labor dynamics and resource allocation, but they do not address how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. This labor reallocation was a result of economic policies that encouraged the shift of labor from low-productivity agriculture to higher-productivity industries, which in turn stimulated economic growth. Komlos (1998, p.7) qualifies this view by noting that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). This suggests that while economic growth can be stimulated by labor reallocation and increased labor input, the benefits of this growth may not be evenly distributed, and certain groups, such as those with inelastic supply of human or physical capital, may be made worse off. This uneven distribution of benefits can lead to a divergence between trends of average physical stature and income, which highlights the importance of considering the broader social and economic context in which economic growth occurs. This context can significantly influence the distribution of economic benefits and the long-run economic outcomes.

Pamuk (2007, p.15) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) establish that the economic divergence between regions can be attributed to various historical events and structural changes. One such event was the Black Death, which initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. Pamuk notes that the skill premium began to decline under severe labor shortages, which were exacerbated by the Black Death, and this decline continued with the drop in interest rates across Europe. This period saw a significant shift in economic structures, with urbanization rates serving as a key indicator of productivity and economic development. Urbanization, in turn, influenced the distribution of economic benefits and long-run economic outcomes. These structural changes, including urbanization and shifts in labor dynamics, played a crucial role in shaping the economic landscape of Europe. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) point out that the formation of 'industrial districts' to reap economies of localization does not necessarily imply large-scale urbanization, and may be quite consistent with proto-industrialization. This qualification suggests that while urbanization is a significant factor, it is not the sole determinant of economic divergence and long-run outcomes.

Stern (2004, p.8) and Simson (2019, p.27) share the claim that environmental and budget policies play a crucial role in shaping economic development. The Hecksher-Ohlin trade theory suggests that free trade leads developing countries to specialize in labor- and natural resource-intensive goods, while developed countries focus on human capital and manufactured capital (Stern 2004, p.8). This specialization can influence environmental degradation levels, with developed countries experiencing reductions and middle-income countries increases. The environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) concept emerged in the early 1990s, positing that economic growth could lead to eventual environmental improvement (Stern 2004, p.1). This qualification suggests that the impact of budget policies on economic development is context-dependent and influenced by historical and policy-specific factors. Nunn further complicates this by arguing that Africa's slave trades had an adverse effect on economic development, highlighting the long-term effects of historical events on contemporary economic outcomes (Nunn 2008, p.27; Nunn 2008, p.4).

Skill premiums, which measure the relative returns to skilled labor, are a key predictor of long-term economic performance, with van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) showing that a low skill premium generally reflects a high level of human capital formation. This pattern is evident in both Europe and Africa, where the skill premium is linked to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation. This mechanism suggests that the development of human capital and the efficiency of institutions are crucial for sustaining economic growth. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) qualify this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, indicating that economic growth in these regions was not solely dependent on European colonial interventions. This finding underscores the importance of endogenous factors in shaping economic outcomes, even in the presence of exogenous shocks.

Gender and development significantly shaped long-run economic outcomes through their influence on human capital and institutional efficiency. The skill premium, as van Zanden (2009, p.27) argues, is a key predictor of long-term economic performance. This premium reflects both the relative abundance of capital and the efficiency of capital markets, as well as the level of human capital formation. High skill premiums in low-income countries indicate a lower demand for skilled labor, contrasting with rich countries where the skill premium is lower. This dynamic underscores the importance of human capital investment in driving economic growth. Their role in production and reproduction was central to economic activities, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. This historical evidence shows that endogenous factors, such as human capital development and institutional efficiency, play a crucial role in shaping economic outcomes, even in the presence of exogenous shocks like colonial interventions. The historical plough use, for example, has a strong positive correlation with unequal gender roles today (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This historical evidence shows that gender roles and economic outcomes are intertwined, with gender discrimination affecting long-run growth through disinvestment in human capital.
