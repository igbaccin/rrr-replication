The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue, with scholars disagreeing on the mechanisms through which gender dynamics influence economic growth. At the heart of the debate lies the question of whether gender equality in economic participation and development is a cause or consequence of economic growth. The stakes are high: understanding this relationship is crucial for designing effective policies that can foster sustainable economic development. One position argues that gender equality drives economic growth by increasing the labor force and enhancing productivity. A competing perspective suggests that economic growth fosters greater gender equality as societies become wealthier and education levels increase. Another perspective suggests that the relationship is bidirectional, with gender equality and economic growth reinforcing each other. The interplay between these factors is complex, involving institutional frameworks, cultural norms, and historical contexts. This is particularly evident in the historical transformations of women's roles in the economy, where shifts from piecework to professional careers have been pivotal. For instance, Goldin argues that women's employment in the U.S. underwent a significant transformation, moving from "jobs" to "careers" and involving a change from agents who worked out of necessity to those who sought employment for identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). This change is part of a larger historical context where gender roles and economic participation are closely linked. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) report, “Fewer works have sought to analyze the origins, dynamics and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn 2013), or what happens to gender discrimination in the context of economic growth (Stotsky 2006).”

Goldin argues that a quiet revolution in women's employment significantly altered long-run economic outcomes, a transformation that challenges views that see this change as merely evolutionary or stalled (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This transformation was not uniform across sectors; women often found themselves in manufacturing or service roles with limited opportunities for skill development or advancement. This pattern of employment did not align with the typical career progression seen in male-dominated sectors, where long-term investment in human capital was more common (Goldin 2006, p.3). This dynamic is further illuminated by Akyeampong and Fofack, who highlight that women's contributions to economic growth and development were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods in Africa. The historical context of gender discrimination shows that this change was not straightforward. For instance, Akyeampong and Fofack note that plough use in historical cultures correlates with unequal gender roles today, indicating that cultural factors play a significant role in shaping women's economic participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This suggests that while the quiet revolution in women's employment was transformative, it was also subject to deep-seated cultural and historical influences that continue to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn argue that colonialism shaped long-run economic outcomes by introducing institutional changes and through the impact of historical slave trades (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). One specific mechanism through which colonialism affected economic outcomes was the establishment of extractive institutions that concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite. This concentration of power led to a situation where the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The adverse effects of colonialism were not limited to institutional changes alone, as the legacy of slave trades also played a significant role. Nunn shows that Africa's slave trades had long-term effects on economic development, contributing to high levels of ethnic fractionalization and insecurity, which in turn hindered economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). Austin qualifies the downstream implications of this mechanism by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis simplifies causation. He argues that the thesis compresses different historical periods and paths, suggesting that the impact of colonialism on economic outcomes was more complex and varied than often portrayed (Austin 2008, p.1). This complexity highlights that while colonialism and slave trades had profound effects on long-run economic outcomes, these effects were mediated by a range of historical and contextual factors.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2), together with McCloskey (2015, p.9), highlight that the evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework facilitated secure contracting across time and space, enabling specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). This mechanism allowed for the development of markets and the enforcement of rules governing economic exchange. McCloskey (2015, p.8) adds that the ideas and ideologies of the time also played a significant role in driving economic change. This implies that the institutional changes were not solely responsible for economic growth but were also influenced by broader cultural and ideological factors. North (1989, p.5) further elaborates that economic growth can occur as a consequence of increasing productivity, driven by technological change or changes in institutions that affect the specification and enforcement of property rights. This underscores the importance of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes, but it also suggests that the impact of these institutions was mediated by a range of historical and contextual factors.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) show that the economic structures and factor endowments in different societies shaped institutional development and inequality, influencing long-run economic outcomes. The economic structures in New World societies with greater inequality, such as those with valuable mineral resources and abundant low-skilled labor, favored elites through government policies that restricted competition. This dynamic resulted in extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income, as the large plantations and slave populations dominated these economies. The persistent technological advantages and international markets in slaves further entrenched these inequalities. This pattern of inequality was not limited to the New World; Pamuk (2007, p.16) demonstrates that in Europe, urbanization rates and structural changes post-Black Death also diverged between the northwest and the rest of Europe. These structural changes, such as the decline in the skill premium and the drop in interest rates, encouraged human capital formation and contributed to the wage gap. The skill premium in Italy later rebounded with population growth, suggesting that while structural changes affected inequality, other factors were also at play. The implications of these findings suggest that the interaction between economic structures and factor endowments can create persistent inequalities, which in turn shape long-run economic outcomes.

This gap in the literature highlights the need to integrate gender dynamics into the analysis of economic development and living standards. Van Zanden argues that there is no clear relationship between economic development and changes in the standard of living in Europe between 1500 and 1800 (van Zanden 1999, p.1). This is shared by Komlos, who finds that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment (Komlos 1998, p.7). The unequal distribution of nutritional resources and the widening range of heights across occupations indicates that the benefits of modern economic growth were not evenly spread (Komlos 1998, p.2). The implications of these findings suggest that economic development does not necessarily translate into improved living standards, and that the distribution of resources plays a crucial role in determining outcomes. Koepke and Baten further qualify this by showing that there was no large-scale progress in European nutritional status over the last two millennia (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This suggests that while economic development may have occurred, it did not necessarily lead to improvements in nutritional status, which is a key indicator of living standards.

This robust economic performance, however, did not necessarily translate into improved long-run economic outcomes for all segments of the population, particularly women. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) and Simson (2019, p.27) argue that these regions had higher per capita incomes than previously thought, and that their budget policies were not exceptionally clientelist. This challenges the prevailing narrative of economic stagnation and rent-seeking behaviors. These findings are supported by the observation that budgetary policies in East Africa were not inherently inefficient or misguided. This economic performance was rooted in the region's ability to maintain balanced budgets, which allowed for sustained economic growth despite external challenges. Simson (2019, p.6) notes that these countries faced significant economic challenges, including military instability and economic undiversification. These challenges highlight that while the economic performance was robust, it was not without its constraints. These constraints are important to consider when examining the broader implications of economic development in Africa, as they underscore the complexities and nuances involved in achieving sustained growth.

Gender and development play pivotal roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with significant contributions from women in both pre-colonial and colonial periods. In Africa, women were integral to economic growth and development, contributing through their labor in various sectors, including agriculture, manufacturing, and service industries (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This involvement was crucial to the economic activities of these societies. The role of women in economic development is further underscored by their contributions to human capital formation, which is crucial for sustained economic growth (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Similarly, in the U.S., women's employment and education transformed family dynamics and societal structures, shifting from "jobs" to "careers" and altering the fundamental identity and societal worth of women (Goldin 2006, p.3)(Goldin 2006, p.1). This transformation highlights the broader implications of gender dynamics on economic development, as women's participation in the labor force and their educational attainment significantly influence economic outcomes.

The institutional frameworks established during colonial periods also shaped long-run economic outcomes. In New World societies, institutions that advantaged elites through various government policies restricted competition and contributed to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6)(Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). These policies often involved generous awards of property and tribute to early settlers, which reinforced unequal distributions of wealth and human capital. The economic structures that evolved in these societies were greatly influenced by factor endowments, such as valuable mineral resources and abundant labor with low human capital, which further entrenched inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional legacy is crucial for understanding the long-run economic outcomes in these regions, as it highlights how historical policies and structures continue to impact contemporary economic conditions. In the case of South Africa, systematic racial discrimination hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, affecting economic growth and development (Austin 2008, p.23). This shows that the role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is not limited to colonial periods but extends into the post-colonial era, where policies continue to influence economic performance.

The reversal of fortune thesis, which argues that institutional changes introduced by colonialism led to a reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies, provides a framework for understanding the long-run economic outcomes of these regions (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)(Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This thesis emphasizes the importance of institutions in determining economic performance, as societies with extractive institutions often face challenges in achieving sustained growth. The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further supported by the finding that societies with extractive institutions typically have lower levels of human capital, as the masses did not or could not obtain education (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This highlights the interconnectedness of institutions, human capital, and economic development, as institutions that restrict access to education and opportunities for human capital formation limit economic growth and development. The thesis simplifies causation by condensing different historical periods and trajectories (Austin 2008, p.1). This simplification can obscure the complexities of economic development and the role of gender and institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a point of agreement among scholars, as both Acemoglu et al. and Akyeampong and Fofack demonstrate that institutions and gender dynamics significantly impact economic performance (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The disagreement lies in the specific mechanisms through which these factors operate. Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal of fortune is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, while Akyeampong and Fofack emphasize the role of women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33)(Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The historical evidence of women's contributions to economic activities and the institutional frameworks that advantaged elites settle this disagreement. The measurement of women's labor force participation and the analysis of institutional policies provide a clear picture of how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes.
