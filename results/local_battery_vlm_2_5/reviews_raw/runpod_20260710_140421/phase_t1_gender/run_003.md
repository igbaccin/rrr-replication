The substantive question of how gender and development shape long-run economic outcomes is pivotal in understanding the persistent disparities in wealth and well-being across societies. At stake is the ability to identify the causal mechanisms that drive economic growth and development, which in turn informs policy and theoretical frameworks. The live disagreement centers on whether gender dynamics and development trajectories are primary drivers of long-run economic outcomes or if they are secondary to other factors. Some scholars argue that gender roles and economic development are interconnected, with changes in one influencing the other. Some scholars argue that institutional frameworks and resource allocation are key drivers of long-run economic outcomes, with gender and development dynamics being secondary effects. The interplay between gender and development is thus a critical area of inquiry, as it offers insights into the multifaceted nature of economic growth and the factors that sustain or hinder it. In pre-colonial and colonial Africa, women were central to economic development, particularly in agriculture and trade. This role was foundational to the economic fabric of these societies. Women's contributions extended beyond economic productivity to encompass social reproduction, which is crucial for sustaining economic activities. For instance, women's roles in food production and processing ensured that labor forces were adequately nourished, directly impacting productivity. Additionally, women's involvement in trade networks facilitated the distribution of goods, fostering economic exchange and growth. Goldin's work on U.S. women's employment underscores the transformative impact of women entering the labor force, noting that women often worked as piece workers in manufacturing or in service sectors as domestics and laundresses (Goldin 2006, p.3). This historical perspective suggests that women's economic participation, whether in manufacturing or service sectors, has been a consistent driver of economic development across different contexts. The specific mechanisms through which women's contributions translate into long-run economic outcomes differ. This implies that while women's economic roles are crucial, the persistence of gender discrimination can mitigate their full potential impact on economic development. Therefore, understanding the multifaceted roles of women in economic development requires a nuanced approach that considers both their contributions and the structural barriers they face.

Temin demonstrates that the disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry was a key driver of economic growth during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age (Temin 2002, p.4; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This disequilibrium is separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence. Low income in the standard story is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates. The developmental deficit highlighted here is produced by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9). The misallocation of labor resources between agriculture and industry could lead to a significant loss of productivity, as workers in agriculture are less productive than those in industry. This is because agriculture often relies on traditional methods and has lower capital intensity, whereas industry benefits from technological advancements and higher capital investment. Therefore, a shift of labor from agriculture to industry can significantly boost productivity and economic growth. This shift needs a supportive institutional framework that facilitates labor mobility and provides adequate infrastructure and education. This suggests that the benefits of labor reallocation may be limited in environments where institutional barriers prevent efficient resource allocation.

Colonialism's institutional legacy has shaped long-run economic outcomes by influencing property rights and human capital formation. Acemoglu et al. and Nunn share the view that colonial institutions have had a profound impact on economic development, particularly in former European colonies. Acemoglu et al. demonstrate that in societies where a small elite holds political and social power, the majority of the population risks being held up by the powerful elite after they undertake investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This concentration of power can hinder economic growth by restricting competition and preventing efficient resource allocation. Nunn extends this argument by showing that Africa's slave trades, which were facilitated by colonial institutions, had adverse effects on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and reducing human capital formation (Nunn 2008, p.27). This indicates that the institutional changes introduced by colonialism affected property rights and had broader implications for social cohesion and human capital. Austin qualifies this view by arguing that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies the complex interplay between colonial institutions and economic development by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced perspective underscores the importance of considering the specific historical context in which colonial institutions operated.

The Industrious Revolution, marked by intensified labor input and altered consumption habits, played a pivotal role in reshaping economic outcomes before the Industrial Revolution (Komlos 1998, p.7; de Vries 1994, p.17). Komlos and de Vries highlight how this period saw a shift in household production patterns, becoming more capital-intensive and focused on full-time male workers (Komlos 1998, p.7; de Vries 1994, p.17). This transformation had profound implications for the broader economy, as the structure of market demand increasingly centered on family-consumed goods, and the goals of reproduction shifted towards quality, endowing children with human capital, rather than quantity (Komlos 1998, p.7; de Vries 1994, p.17). The Industrious Revolution thus laid the groundwork for significant achievements in industrial society, including lower morbidity and mortality rates, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort. These improvements were largely achieved through the household productive system (de Vries 1994, p.17). This uneven distribution suggests that the benefits of economic growth did not accrue uniformly across society, highlighting the complexities of how economic development impacts different segments of the population.

Broadberry and Gardner, along with Pamuk, examine regional economic performance in Europe and Africa, focusing on historical factors such as per capita income levels and structural changes due to events like the Black Death (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Pamuk 2007, p.15). These scholars argue that many African economies, particularly those in Anglophone regions, had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This finding challenges the conventional narrative that African economies were stagnant until European colonial interventions, which is a view expressed in early economic histories of Africa (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). This narrative is further complicated by Pamuk's analysis of the Black Death, which initiated long-term structural changes that led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This divergence is attributed to factors such as severe labor shortages and declining interest rates, which encouraged human capital formation (Pamuk 2007, p.15). The implications of these structural changes are profound, as they highlight how historical events can shape long-term economic trajectories. Urbanization rates, a key indicator of productivity and economic development, varied significantly across Europe during this period (Pamuk 2007, p.16). This variation suggests that the impact of the Black Death on economic development was not uniform, and that regional differences in economic performance were already evident before the Industrial Revolution.

The literature on economic development and standard of living does not directly address the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes (van Zanden 1999, p.1; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This raises the question of whether the same mechanisms that influence economic development and standard of living also apply to gender dynamics. One specific mechanism that emerges from Koepke and Baten’s work is the impact of urbanization on the availability of agricultural land and, consequently, nutritional status. This mechanism suggests that urbanization, which is a key indicator of economic development, can have differential impacts on various segments of the population, including those defined by gender. Gender inequality and social inequality have significant negative effects on height, a proxy for nutritional status (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This finding qualifies the impact of urbanization, indicating that while economic development may improve overall nutritional status, it does not uniformly benefit all groups. The implications of this are significant, as they suggest that the benefits of economic development may be unevenly distributed, with gender playing a crucial role in determining who benefits and to what extent.
