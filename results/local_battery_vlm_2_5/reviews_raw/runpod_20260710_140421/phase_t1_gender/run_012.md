The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of study that intersects with broader debates about economic growth and institutional change. At stake is the understanding of how historical and contemporary gender dynamics influence economic trajectories, and whether these dynamics are merely correlative or causally significant. One side of the debate suggests that gender roles and economic development are interdependent, with gender equality driving economic growth and vice versa. This perspective posits that policies aimed at gender equality can lead to sustained economic development. The other side argues that economic development can occur independently of gender dynamics, with institutional and technological factors being more decisive. This view suggests that while gender equality is desirable, it is not a necessary condition for economic growth. The core of the disagreement lies in whether gender dynamics are a primary driver of economic development or a secondary effect of broader economic and institutional changes. This review will explore these themes through the lens of historical and contemporary scholarship, examining how gender and development intersect to shape long-run economic outcomes. Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Mokyr (2005, p.4) identify the drivers of economic growth as technological advancements and the accumulation of useful knowledge. Kuznets argues that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in economic structure, labor force dynamics, and societal views on individual roles and achievements. These changes are driven by advancing technology and require institutional and ideological adjustments for efficient use of innovations. Mokyr emphasizes that the growth of useful knowledge is the mainspring of economic growth, with societies expanding their collective knowledge base through specialization, professionalization, and increased investment in human capital. This expansion of social knowledge is a key driver of economic growth. McCloskey (2015, p.8) qualifies this by noting that ideas and ideologies also play a crucial role in economic change. McCloskey argues that the success of bourgeois economic and political ideas in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 was influenced by the nearby and spectacular examples of economic success in Holland and England, highlighting the importance of cultural and ideological factors in economic growth.
