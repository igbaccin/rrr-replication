The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue, with scholars debating the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development trajectories. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether and how gender-specific factors, such as labor force participation, education, and property rights, interact with broader economic and institutional frameworks to drive long-term economic performance. One position argues that gender dynamics, including women's economic participation and access to resources, are critical determinants of economic growth and development. Another position contends that while gender plays a role, it is secondary to other factors such as institutional quality, technological advancements, and human capital investment. A third position emphasizes the historical and cultural contexts in which gender dynamics operate, suggesting that the impact of gender on economic outcomes varies significantly across different societies and time periods. Understanding the interplay between gender and development is crucial for designing effective policies that promote inclusive economic growth and address persistent inequalities. The stakes are high: accurate insights into this relationship could inform strategies to enhance productivity, reduce poverty, and foster more equitable societies. North and Weingast (1989, p.2); (North 1989, p.5) argue that the development of markets and the enforcement of property rights are crucial factors, which are often influenced by the same historical and social contexts that shape gender discrimination. The evolution of institutions governing public choice in seventeenth-century England, for instance, fostered secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework was underpinned by a credible commitment to rules, which enabled secure contracting across time and space. A similar dynamic is evident in the New World societies, where the institutional structures were significantly influenced by factor endowments, leading to highly unequal distributions of wealth and income. Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) illustrate that the abundance of mineral resources and labor with low human capital in these economies contributed to the persistence of inequality. This institutional framework, characterized by restricted competition and elite advantages, had profound implications for economic development. North (1989, p.5) further elaborates that economic growth can occur through increasing productivity, driven by technological change or institutional shifts that affect property rights. These institutional changes, however, are not universally beneficial. The institutional framework in New World societies, for example, restricted competition and favored elites, ultimately hindering broader economic development.

Technological and ideological drivers are key to long-term economic growth. Kuznets (1973, p.4) and Mokyr (2005, p.4) (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4) argue that economic growth is driven by technological advancements and requires institutional and ideological adjustments. Kuznets (1973, p.4) highlights that economic growth necessitates rapid changes in the structure of the labor force, including shifts in location, occupation, and societal roles. These changes extend beyond the economy, permeating other aspects of society, such as family formation and urbanization. Mokyr (2005, p.4) elaborates on this by emphasizing the role of useful knowledge in driving economic growth. He posits that the accumulation of social knowledge, which encompasses the collective understanding of a society, is crucial for economic development. This social knowledge is not merely the sum of individual knowledge but includes specialized and professionalized information that expands the total amount of knowledge available to society. This expansion is facilitated by consensus-shaping mechanisms that determine which beliefs about the physical environment and natural phenomena will predominate. McCloskey (2015, p.9) qualifies the role of ideas and ideologies in economic growth, arguing that they are the primary drivers of economic change. She contends that ideas, not just interests or institutions, were pivotal in the economic development of Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848. McCloskey (2015, p.9) suggests that economic growth can be throttled in the absence of successful examples of bourgeois economic and political successes, as seen in the case of France. This implies that the ideological environment, shaped by successful examples and the dissemination of useful knowledge, plays a critical role in fostering economic growth. The interplay between technological advancements and ideological shifts thus creates a dynamic that drives long-term economic outcomes.

The Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, facilitated the Industrial Revolution by transforming household production systems and market demands, as de Vries (1994, p.17) and Komlos (1998, p.7) show. This transformation led to a more capital-intensive production system oriented towards full-time male workers and a shift in market demand towards family-consumed goods. The goal of reproduction shifted to focus more on quality, or the endowment of children with human capital, and less on quantity. This shift had profound consequences for the entire economy, including lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort. These improvements were not evenly distributed. Komlos (1998, p.7) argues that despite overall economic growth, biological well-being declined for some groups due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. This suggests that the benefits of economic growth were unevenly distributed, with some groups, such as handloom weavers, being made worse off. Moreover, the uneven distribution of nutritional resources indicates that not all segments of society experienced the same improvements in well-being. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) further qualify this by noting that gender inequality and social inequality both had negative effects on nutritional status, with social inequality corresponding to a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low, and 0.74 cm between extreme values. This implies that while the Industrious Revolution brought about significant economic changes, its impact on long-run economic outcomes was mediated by underlying social and gender inequalities. The implications of these findings underscore the need to consider the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as these inequalities can significantly influence the distribution of benefits from economic growth.

Temin and others investigate the role of historical events in shaping economic growth and labor allocation, focusing on how labor disequilibria and other structural changes have driven economic growth, but do not directly address gender and development in long-run economic outcomes (Temin 2002, p.4; Pamuk 2007, p.15). Their work shows that disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry was a key driver of Europe's post-WWII Golden Age, with rapid economic growth fueled by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.9; Temin 2002, p.4). This mechanism underscores how structural shifts in labor markets can significantly impact economic growth, even in the absence of policy changes or other typical factors. The implications of these findings for understanding long-run economic outcomes are qualified by the broader historical context of economic development. This suggests that while labor allocation and structural changes are crucial, the long-run impact of these factors on economic outcomes may be mediated by broader historical and social contexts, such as the role of gender and development.

Ogilvie and Stern jointly argue that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency considerations, are central to understanding the development of core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.25; Stern 2004, p.8), and these conflicts often intersect with gender dynamics. Ogilvie demonstrates that the rise of institutions such as serfdom and guilds can be attributed to the power dynamics and bargaining processes among different social groups, and not their efficiency in promoting economic growth. This perspective is supported by Stern's analysis of the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC), which illustrates how economic institutions and policies can be shaped by the interests of powerful groups, even if they are not the most efficient or environmentally sustainable (Stern 2004, p.1). The EKC hypothesis suggests that economic growth initially increases environmental degradation but eventually leads to improvement as countries develop. This pattern may be influenced by the specialization of developing countries in labor- and resource-intensive activities, which can exacerbate environmental issues (Stern 2004, p.8). The downstream implication of this distributional conflict framework is that economic institutions and policies are often the result of negotiations and power struggles among different interest groups, and not driven solely by economic efficiency. This highlights the importance of understanding the political and social dynamics that shape economic institutions and their long-run impacts on development.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that the role of African women in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods has been significantly underappreciated. They highlight that African women played a crucial role in production and reproduction, directly contributing to economic growth. This contribution was central to the economic dynamics of the time. Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) explain that historical plough use is correlated with unequal gender roles today, indicating that the economic activities of women were deeply embedded in the societal structures of the past. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) build on this by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, underscoring the economic agency of African societies prior to significant European interventions. This challenges the notion that economic growth in Africa was solely a result of colonial influences, instead suggesting that indigenous economic activities, including those driven by women, were pivotal.

The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies can be attributed to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). Acemoglu et al. and Nunn both show that colonial institutions shaped long-run economic outcomes by influencing property rights and human capital formation. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite under colonial rule often left the majority of the population vulnerable to exploitation, limiting their ability to invest and innovate (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). Moreover, the lack of effective property rights protection in societies with extractive institutions prevented the masses from obtaining education, further hindering economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The adverse effects of colonial institutions are evident in Africa, where the slave trades exacerbated ethnic fractionalization, creating an environment of uncertainty and insecurity that impeded economic growth (Nunn 2008, p.4). Austin qualifies this institutional perspective by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). He argues that systematic racial discrimination under apartheid in South Africa hindered human capital formation and market flexibility, contributing to economic stagnation (Austin 2008, p.23). This suggests that while colonial institutions played a significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, other factors such as racial discrimination and market inflexibility also contributed to economic underdevelopment in specific contexts.

Lindert and Williamson's argument that growth and equality are not mutually exclusive outcomes is supported by Goldin's work on the transformation of women's employment and education in the U.S. (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). This transformation was marked by a shift from women working primarily out of economic necessity to a focus on careers that offered long-term investment in human capital. This shift involved a significant change in societal perceptions of women's roles, aligning with the idea that economic growth can be inclusive. This transformation was driven by an increase in the demand for skilled labor, which in turn incentivized investment in human capital. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker meant that the pay gaps and earnings inequality set in motion by unbalanced productivity advance favored relatively rapid expansion in the derived demand for skills (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). Goldin notes that this transformation was not uniform across all sectors; the vast majority of women workers were engaged in low-skill jobs with little opportunity for on-the-job learning or formal education beyond elementary school (Goldin 2006, p.3). This qualification suggests that while economic growth can drive equality, the benefits are not evenly distributed without targeted policies.
