The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of study, as it addresses fundamental questions about the distribution of economic opportunities and the determinants of growth. At the heart of this debate lies a live disagreement over the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic development and the mechanisms through which these effects manifest. Some scholars emphasize the historical contributions of women to economic growth, arguing that their roles have been systematically underestimated. Others focus on the institutional frameworks that either facilitate or hinder women's economic participation, highlighting how these structures can perpetuate inequality. Still others consider the broader economic context, including labor allocation and human capital investment, as key factors in understanding the long-run impacts of gender and development. The interplay between these factors is complex, and understanding their dynamics is essential for comprehending the broader economic outcomes that have shaped societies over time. The role of women in economic growth is a particularly compelling question in this context, as it sheds light on the historical contributions of women to development and the institutional barriers that have limited their full participation. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8); (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that African women have been pivotal in shaping economic growth in pre-colonial and colonial Africa. They highlight that women's roles in production and reproduction were central to economic development, challenging views that marginalize their contributions. This perspective is bolstered by Akyeampong and Fofack, who note that women's roles in manufacturing and service sectors were crucial in the economic landscape of the time. Akyeampong and Fofack's work shows that the economic contributions of African women were foundational to the economic growth. Their analysis also reveals a significant gap in the literature regarding the long-term effects of gender discrimination on economic growth. While Akyeampong and Fofack acknowledge that gender discrimination can lower productivity and adversely affect long-run growth, they do not explicitly link these findings to the broader economic contexts in which gender dynamics operate. This gap underscores the need for further research into how gender dynamics influence long-term economic outcomes.

Goldin (2006, p.3) and Komlos (1998, p.7) jointly reveal that economic growth does not always translate into improved social well-being, particularly for marginalized groups. These authors show that women's employment and education in the U.S. underwent a profound transformation during the Industrial Revolution, and that biological well-being declined for some groups despite economic growth. The shift in women's roles was marked by a significant increase in human capital investment, as women began to pursue careers that required more education and training. This transformation was not uniform across all sectors; the vast majority of women workers were still engaged in low-skilled jobs with little opportunity for advancement. The expansion of professional occupations for women, particularly in teaching and clerical work, began to alter the economic landscape. This shift had implications for family dynamics, as women's increased participation in the labor force changed traditional gender roles and expectations. This suggests that while women's increased participation in the labor force and education had positive effects on their economic status, these gains were not uniformly distributed across all sectors and regions.

Acemoglu et al. and Nunn argue that institutional changes introduced by colonialism have played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes in former European colonies, specifically highlighting the reversal in relative incomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Nunn 2008, p.27). These institutional changes, particularly those that concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite, often led to the majority of the population being held up after undertaking investments, thereby stifling economic growth. Nunn's work on Africa's slave trades further illustrates this dynamic, showing how the adverse effects of the slave trades have had lasting impacts on economic development by hindering human capital formation and reducing market flexibility (Nunn 2008, p.4). Austin, however, qualifies this perspective by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths (Austin 2008, p.1). This nuanced view suggests that while colonial institutions have had profound effects, the complexity of historical trajectories means that other factors, such as racial discrimination and market inflexibility, have also played significant roles in shaping economic outcomes. The interaction between these institutional changes and other historical factors underscores the multifaceted nature of economic development in former colonies.

The political institutions that emerged in seventeenth-century England played a crucial role in fostering secure property rights and economic growth (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5). This institutional change was pivotal because it reduced the uncertainty associated with property rights, enabling long-term investments and economic transactions to flourish. This mechanism is evident in the development of England's capital markets, which thrived under the secure contracting environment established by the new political institutions. The relationship between property rights and economic growth is complex. North and Weingast (1989, p.30) note that while secure property rights are a necessary condition for economic growth, they are not sufficient on their own. Other factors, such as the robustness of the economy at the local level and the ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space, are also crucial. This nuanced understanding highlights the multifaceted nature of economic development, where institutional changes interact with various historical and economic factors to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) jointly argue that economic growth is driven by labor allocation between sectors, particularly the shift from agriculture to industry. Temin (2002, p.4) highlights that this disequilibrium in labor allocation is a distinct factor from low income levels, which are typically associated with conditional convergence. This misallocation of resources plays a crucial role in economic growth. De Vries (1994, p.17) furthers this by noting that the Industrious Revolution, marked by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, significantly preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This revolution transformed the production system, making it more capital-intensive and focused on the requirements of full-time male workers. The goal of reproduction shifted towards quality, emphasizing the endowment of children with human capital. de Vries (1994, p.17) also points out that the household productive system achieved many of the major benefits of industrial society, such as lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, higher educational levels, and greater domestic comfort. These findings underscore the importance of labor allocation and the Industrious Revolution in shaping long-run economic outcomes, but also highlight that the benefits of industrial society were not universally distributed.

The role of ideas and technology in driving long-term economic growth is a well-established theme in economic history, with Mokyr and Kuznets (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4) both highlighting the centrality of knowledge and technological advancement in shaping economic outcomes. Mokyr posits that the growth of useful knowledge is the primary driver of economic growth, suggesting that the accumulation of societal knowledge has vastly expanded over time, leading to increased economic productivity. This perspective aligns with Kuznets' argument that economic growth is intrinsically linked to technological progress and the institutional adaptations that facilitate its efficient use. Kuznets further elaborates that the dynamic forces of modern economic growth necessitate rapid structural changes in society, including shifts in occupational status groups and urbanization patterns. These changes encompass broader societal transformations. McCloskey (2015, p.9) extends this idea by emphasizing that ideologies and ideas played a crucial role in driving economic change. McCloskey also notes that the success of these ideas is contingent on broader cultural and political contexts, which can either facilitate or hinder their adoption. This qualification underscores that while ideas and technology are pivotal, their impact is mediated by the social and political environment in which they operate.

The relationship between economic development and living standards in early modern Europe is not straightforward. Van Zanden (1999, p.1) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) challenge the optimistic views of early modern economic growth and nutritional status improvements. They argue that there is no clear connection between economic growth and changes in the standard of living during this period. This perspective is supported by the finding that real wages declined over the long run, particularly during the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. The decline in real wages is a significant indicator of the lack of a clear relationship between economic development and living standards, as it suggests that economic growth did not translate into improved living conditions for the majority of the population. This finding is further supported by the observation that the development of grain wages did not correspond with improvements in the standard of living, casting doubt on the optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth. The absence of a clear connection between economic growth and living standards during this period suggests that other factors, such as social and political institutions, may have played a more significant role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. One implication of this is that the nutritional status of the population did not improve significantly over the last two millennia, despite recent estimates indicating increasing development between 1000 and 1800 AD. The lack of large-scale progress in nutritional status further underscores the complex relationship between economic development and living standards.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) show that institutions favoring elites through government policies contributed to the persistence of inequality in New World societies, a pattern that was reinforced by the economic structures and political power distribution. The economic structures in these societies were deeply influenced by factor endowments, particularly the abundance of mineral resources and low human capital labor. This led to extremely unequal distributions of wealth and income. The persistence of inequality was further exacerbated by policies that restricted European migration and granted generous awards of property and tribute to early settlers. These policies created a system where large plantations dominated the economy, and the population was overwhelmingly composed of enslaved individuals. The efficiency of these large plantations and the demographic composition of the population reinforced the unequal distribution of wealth and human capital, even among the free population. This pattern of inequality was not limited to the New World; similar dynamics were observed in other regions, such as Anglophone African economies, which had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. Broadberry and Gardner challenge the notion that economic growth in these regions was solely due to European colonial interventions, highlighting the complex interplay between economic structures, political power, and inequality. The persistence of inequality in these societies underscores the broader implications of institutional frameworks that favor elites, shaping long-run economic outcomes in profound ways.

Gender and development play pivotal roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with historical evidence highlighting the significance of women's contributions to economic growth and development. Sokoloff and Engerman show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These dynamics underscore the broader implications of institutional frameworks that favor elites, shaping long-run economic outcomes in profound ways. Both papers show that the economic structures that evolved in these societies were greatly influenced by the factor endowments and the policies that maintained inequality, highlighting the role of gender in economic development.

The role of human capital and education in economic development is also crucial. Lindert and Williamson demonstrate that changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and changes in the rate of skills deepening are critical forces driving the British Kuznets Curve across the 19th century (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). Acemoglu et al. argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This underscores the importance of human capital investment in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Both papers show that the structure of pay by skill and the incentive to invest in human capital are critical factors in economic development.
