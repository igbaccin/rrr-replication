The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history, with scholars debating the extent to which gender dynamics influence economic growth and development trajectories. At stake is the fundamental question of whether and how gendered labor practices, educational opportunities, and social norms affect economic performance over the long term. Some scholars argue that gender roles and opportunities are critical determinants of economic outcomes, shaping human capital investment and labor market participation. Some scholars suggest that institutions and factor endowments are the primary drivers of economic growth, with gender dynamics playing a secondary role. The disagreement centers on whether gender dynamics operate through institutional mechanisms or are simply a byproduct of broader economic structures. The debate is crucial for understanding the historical roots of contemporary economic disparities and for informing policies aimed at fostering inclusive economic growth. A thorough examination of the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes must consider the interplay between gendered labor practices, human capital investment, and institutional frameworks. The role of women in employment has been a significant factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes, challenging traditional views that marginalize their contributions (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). These women were predominantly piece workers or domestics, a pattern that persisted despite the fact that a minority held professional positions, often as teachers and clerical employees. The implications of such discrimination are profound, as it leads to a disinvestment in human capital, particularly for female workers. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.10) also argue that historical practices, such as the use of ploughs, have influenced modern gender norms and labor force participation, suggesting that cultural factors deeply embedded in societal structures play a crucial role in determining economic outcomes for women. This historical context underscores the need for a nuanced understanding of how gender dynamics have evolved over time, shaping the economic landscape in ways that are both enduring and transformative.

Komlos (1998, p.7) and Koepke and Baten (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19) highlight that economic growth during industrialization did not uniformly translate into improved health outcomes. Komlos (1998, p.7) identifies a pattern of increasing inequality in nutritional resources, noting that the benefits of economic growth accrued unevenly, with some groups, such as handloom weavers, experiencing worsening conditions (Komlos 1998, p.7). This uneven distribution of benefits suggests that while aggregate economic indicators may show growth, the underlying biological well-being of certain populations could decline. This pattern is supported by Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19), who find that social inequality had a significant negative impact on nutritional status, with a height difference of 0.63 cm between high and low social strata (Koepke and Baten 2005, p.28). This implies that the benefits of economic growth during industrialization were not evenly distributed across different social groups, and that gender inequality also played a role in determining nutritional outcomes. While the exact mechanisms behind these inequalities remain complex, it is clear that the relationship between economic growth and health outcomes is not straightforward.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6); (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8) examine how institutions that favor elites through government policies contribute to the persistence of inequality in New World societies. They focus on economic structures and political power distribution. These institutions, shaped by factor endowments and colonial policies, created highly unequal distributions of wealth and income. For instance, the abundance of valuable mineral resources and low human capital labor in some colonies led to policies that restricted European migration and awarded property and tribute to early settlers. This dynamic, which favored large plantations and slave labor, exacerbated wealth inequality. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) argue that policies can influence both growth and equality, challenging the belief that these goals are mutually exclusive. They highlight that unbalanced productivity advances and skill deepening can drive economic growth while also influencing income distribution. The slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker can limit the effectiveness of these policies in reducing inequality. Therefore, while economic policies can address inequality, their impact is contingent on the responsiveness of the labor market to skill deepening initiatives. This suggests that the relationship between economic growth and inequality is complex and influenced by various factors, including the responsiveness of the labor market to skill deepening initiatives.

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) and Austin (2008, p.1) share a posture that institutional changes introduced by colonialism affected long-run economic outcomes. They argue that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies resulted from these institutional changes. This reversal can be attributed to the concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite, which led to the majority of the population being held up by the powerful elite after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This mechanism is particularly pertinent to the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as it highlights how institutional structures can disproportionately affect different segments of the population, including women. The impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes is further nuanced by Austin's (2008, p.23) observation that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths. This qualification suggests that the effects of colonial institutions on economic development are not uniform across time and space, and that other factors, such as human capital formation and market flexibility, also play crucial roles. Therefore, understanding the long-run economic outcomes requires a more granular analysis of how colonial institutions interacted with local conditions and social structures, including gender dynamics.

The role of gender in development and shaping long-run economic outcomes is a complex issue that involves both historical and contemporary dynamics. Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This perspective is supported by Goldin, who highlights the transformation in women's employment and education in the U.S., indicating a shift from economic necessity to identity and societal worth (Goldin 2006, p.1). This shift suggests that women's roles in the economy have evolved beyond mere labor, becoming integral to broader social and economic structures. The contributions of women to economic development are further underscored by the skill premium, which van Zanden identifies as a predictor of long-term economic performance (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This premium reflects the efficiency of human capital formation and institutional frameworks, both of which are influenced by gender dynamics. The skill premium's role in economic divergence highlights the importance of understanding how gender influences the formation and utilization of human capital over time.
