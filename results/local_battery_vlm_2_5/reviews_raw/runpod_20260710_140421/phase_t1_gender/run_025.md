The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history and development economics. At stake is the understanding of how gender dynamics influence economic growth, distribution, and structural change. The debate hinges on whether gender roles and policies are endogenous or exogenous to economic processes, and whether they are critical drivers of economic development or merely peripheral factors. Some scholars argue that gender roles and policies are deeply intertwined with economic institutions, shaping long-run economic outcomes through their impact on human capital investment, labor force participation, and economic growth. Others contend that gender dynamics are secondary to broader economic forces, such as technological change, institutional frameworks, and market structures. This disagreement reflects a broader tension between theories that emphasize the primacy of economic structures and those that highlight the importance of social and cultural factors. The role of gender in long-run economic outcomes can be understood through the lens of how it interacts with development, particularly in shaping economic institutions and growth trajectories. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) report, “Fewer works have sought to analyze the origins, dynamics and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn 2013), or what happens to gender discrimination in the context of economic growth (Stotsky 2006).”

The literature on gender and economic development explores how changes in women's employment, education, and family dynamics have influenced long-run economic outcomes (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Goldin 2006, p.3). Lindert and Williamson and Goldin jointly argue that the transformation of women's roles in the economy, from piece workers and domestics to professionals and career-oriented employees, has been a critical factor in shaping economic growth and distribution. This transformation involved a shift from "jobs" to "careers," where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). Goldin (2006, p.3)  describes how women experienced little or no on-the-job learning and saw only slight gains from formal human capital beyond common or elementary school, highlighting the initial barriers to women's economic advancement. The expansion of professional roles for women, particularly in teaching and clerical work, beginning in the 1910s, marked a significant turning point in women's economic participation. Lindert and Williamson (1985, p.8) elaborate on this by noting that the pay gaps and earnings inequality set in motion by unbalanced productivity advance served to offer great and increasing incentive to investment in human capital (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This incentive drove women to invest more in their education and skills, which in turn contributed to economic growth. However, the slow and inelastic supply response in skills per worker indicates that the benefits of this human capital investment were not immediate, suggesting that the long-run economic outcomes were shaped by a complex interplay of factors including policy, education, and societal attitudes towards women's roles in the economy.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) argue that African women played a pivotal role in economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, a contribution that has been underappreciated. The role of women in pre-colonial Africa was central to production and reproduction, which were intertwined with economic development. This contribution extended to productive sectors that drove economic growth. The historical evidence shows that women were actively engaged in agriculture, trade, and craft production, which were critical to the economic fabric of African societies. The dynamics of gender roles and economic contributions were influenced by cultural and technological factors, such as the use of the plough in agricultural practices. This technological factor had a significant impact on gender roles and economic outcomes. The positive correlation between historical plough use and unequal gender roles today underscores the enduring effects of these historical practices on contemporary economic and social structures. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.10) qualify this perspective by noting that the economic performance of African economies was often underestimated, with many Anglophone African economies achieving levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century. This challenges the notion that African economic growth was solely a product of European colonial interventions, highlighting the agency and contributions of African women in shaping economic outcomes. The economic contributions of African women during these periods were integral to the overall economic development of the region.

Austin (2008, p.23) and Temin (2002, p.4) examine economic growth during specific historical periods, such as the post-WWII Golden Age in Europe and the origins of South African manufacturing, but do not directly address how gender and development shaped long-run economic outcomes. One mechanism they jointly establish is the role of resource misallocation. Temin traces the downstream implication of this misallocation, arguing that the disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry drove Europe's unusually rapid economic growth. This perspective highlights the importance of understanding the specific historical contexts and resource allocations that shaped economic growth in different periods. This does not account for the role of gender in shaping these allocations or the long-run economic outcomes.

Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) and de Vries (1994, p.17) jointly argue that technological advancements and institutional frameworks are pivotal drivers of economic growth. Mokyr highlights that the accumulation of "useful knowledge" is the mainspring of economic growth, enabling societies to collectively know more than their predecessors. This expansion of knowledge fosters greater specialization and professionalization, leading to a vast increase in social knowledge. Kuznets complements this by emphasizing that economic growth requires not only technological advancements but also institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently use innovations. He points out that high aggregate growth rates are associated with rapid changes in economic structure, which in turn drive changes in family formation, urbanization, and societal views on achievement. These adjustments are crucial for the labor force to participate effectively in the modern economic system. De Vries adds a nuanced perspective by introducing the Industrious Revolution, a phenomenon characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns that preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This period saw a shift in the household production system, becoming more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers. The structure of market demand also evolved, focusing increasingly on family-consumed goods. de Vries qualifies the downstream implications of these changes. The major achievements of industrial society, such as lower morbidity and mortality, were realized more through the household productive system than the larger formal economy. This underscores the importance of considering the broader social and economic context in which technological advancements and institutional frameworks operate, and how these factors interact to shape long-run economic outcomes. This interaction highlights the complexity of economic growth, where technological progress and institutional changes are intertwined with broader social transformations.

Economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved biological well-being (Komlos 1998, p.7; Koepke and Baten 2005, p.19). This disparity was driven by income inequality and uneven distribution of resources, which Komlos (1998, p.7) notes, led to diverging trends between average physical stature and income. This mechanism is evident in the case of handloom weavers, who possessed inelastic human or physical capital and were made worse off by the onset of industrialisation. This effect varied regionally and by socio-economic group. Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) further highlight that social inequality and gender inequality both negatively impacted nutritional status, with social inequality having a stronger effect. This suggests that while economic growth may increase aggregate purchasing power, it does not guarantee improved biological well-being for all segments of society.

Sokoloff and Engerman (2000, p.6) and Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33) show that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality. The unequal distribution of wealth and human capital in these societies was reinforced by institutional frameworks that favoured the elite, such as policies restricting European migration and generous awards of property and tribute to early settlers. This institutional bias was particularly pronounced in economies dominated by large plantations and slave labour, which further entrenched wealth disparities. Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.48) note that in societies with extractive institutions, the masses often lacked access to education, exacerbating human capital disparities. This educational divide further limited the economic mobility of the non-elite population, as they were unable to acquire the skills necessary to compete with the elite. This institutional entrenchment of inequality had profound implications for long-run economic outcomes, as it created a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite maintained their dominance through institutional control, perpetuating the economic marginalisation of the majority.

Gender and development play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with historical evidence showing that gender-based disparities in access to education and economic opportunities create self-reinforcing cycles of inequality. The persistent marginalization of women from formal education and high-skilled labor markets is a key factor in this dynamic. For example, in societies with extractive institutions, the masses, including women, often lacked access to education, exacerbating human capital disparities (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48; Goldin 2006, p.3). This educational divide further limited the economic mobility of women, as they were unable to acquire the skills necessary to compete with men in the labor market. In addition, the role of women in economic activities has been historically undervalued, with many women confined to low-skilled, low-paying jobs that offered little opportunity for advancement (Goldin 2006, p.3). This institutional entrenchment of inequality has profound implications for long-run economic outcomes, as it creates a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite, predominantly men, maintain their dominance through institutional control, perpetuating the economic marginalization of women. This pattern of inequality is evident in both colonial and post-colonial societies, where gender discrimination has been a persistent feature of economic and social structures (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

The impact of gender discrimination on economic growth is multifaceted. Historical evidence shows that gender discrimination tends to lower the average productivity of workers and the equilibrium wage for both men and females in the short run and may adversely affect long-run growth through the disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This disinvestment in human capital is particularly detrimental to economic growth, as it limits the pool of skilled labor available for innovation and technological advancement. Furthermore, the persistence of gender discrimination over historical time has significant implications for long-run economic outcomes, as it creates barriers to women's participation in the labor market and limits their access to economic opportunities (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The historical evidence shows that women's contributions to economic growth and development have been significant, both in pre-colonial and colonial periods. African women, for example, played a crucial role in economic activities, contributing to production and reproduction in ways that were central to the economy (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Their contributions have often been overlooked or undervalued, leading to a distorted understanding of the role of gender in economic development.

The role of institutions in perpetuating gender-based inequality is another critical factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes. Institutions that favor elites and restrict competition can entrench inequality, as seen in New World societies with greater inequality. These institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). This institutional entrenchment of inequality has profound implications for long-run economic outcomes, as it creates a self-reinforcing cycle where the elite maintain their dominance through institutional control, perpetuating the economic marginalization of women. The historical evidence shows that the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which had significant implications for gender-based inequality (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33; Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). These institutional changes created barriers to women's participation in the labor market and limited their access to economic opportunities, further entrenching gender-based inequality.
