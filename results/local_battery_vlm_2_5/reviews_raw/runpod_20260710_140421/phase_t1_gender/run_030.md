The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a critical area of study, as it addresses fundamental questions about the determinants of economic growth and the distribution of wealth. At stake is the understanding of how historical and contemporary gender dynamics influence economic performance, and whether policy interventions can alter these outcomes. A live disagreement persists between those who view gender dynamics as a primary driver of economic growth and those who see them as a secondary factor, influenced by other, more foundational economic forces. This debate is crucial for informing policy and for understanding the deep-seated factors that shape economic trajectories over time. The question of how gender and development interact to influence long-run economic outcomes is particularly salient in the context of historical economic growth and the persistence of inequality. Goldin and Akyeampong and Fofack (Goldin 2006, p.3; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) argue that the transformation of women's employment, education, and family dynamics was a pivotal factor in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This shift was a significant structural change that altered the economic landscape. They emphasise that gender discrimination tends to lower the average productivity of workers and the equilibrium wage for males and females in the short run and may adversely affect long-run growth through the disinvestment in human capital, especially for female workers. This perspective challenges views that marginalize women's roles in economic development. The downstream implication of this transformation is evident in the changing dynamics of women's employment. Goldin (2006, p.3) notes that a scant minority of women were professional workers, often teachers and clerical employees, a group that expanded enormously beginning in the 1910s (Goldin 2006, p.3). This expansion suggests that as women gained more opportunities in professional sectors, their economic contributions became more visible and impactful. However, Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) qualify this by pointing out that historical plough use and unequal gender roles today show a strong and robust positive correlation, indicating that cultural factors also play a significant role in shaping women's economic participation (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). This highlights that while the transformation in women's employment was crucial, cultural and historical factors also influenced long-run economic outcomes.

Lindert and Williamson, along with Sokoloff and Engerman, argue that institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). These institutions, often shaped by colonial legacies, created economic structures that favored large-scale enterprises and concentrated wealth, hindering widespread economic development. This institutional framework perpetuated inequality and influenced long-run economic outcomes by limiting the broader population's access to education and economic opportunities. The concentration of political and social power in the hands of a small elite further exacerbated this issue, as the majority of the population risked being held up by the powerful elite after undertaking investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). However, Acemoglu et al. qualify this by noting that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize may capture the possible interaction between industrialization and some country characteristics correlated with institutions, suggesting that human capital and institutional differences are intertwined (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). This implies that while institutions play a crucial role, the broader socio-economic context, including human capital development, is also vital in shaping long-run economic outcomes.

Technological advancements are a significant driver of economic growth, as Kuznets and Mokyr (Kuznets 1973, p.4; Mokyr 2005, p.4) show. Kuznets (1973, p.4) argues that technological progress necessitates rapid societal changes, including shifts in occupational structures, urbanization, and evolving views on social roles. These changes are economic and social, requiring institutional and ideological adjustments to efficiently utilize innovations. Mokyr (2005, p.4) underscores the role of "useful knowledge" in driving economic growth, suggesting that the accumulation of collective knowledge is crucial for long-term economic development. This implies that societies with mechanisms for consensus-shaping and the dissemination of useful knowledge are better positioned to achieve sustained economic growth. However, Mokyr (2005, p.3) qualifies this by noting that the impact of technological advancements on economic growth is not immediate but occurs over a long period, suggesting that the benefits of technological progress may take generations to fully materialize.

de Vries (1994, p.17) and Komlos (1998, p.7) argues that the Industrious Revolution, marked by heightened labor input and new consumption habits, reshaped family dynamics and well-being. This phenomenon, which preceded the Industrial Revolution, led to a more capital-intensive production system focused on full-time male workers and family-consumed goods. The shift prioritized the quality of children's human capital over quantity, achieving significant improvements in morbidity, mortality, nutrition, and education. This suggests that while the Industrious Revolution brought about structural changes that improved certain aspects of well-being, the benefits were not universally distributed. The uneven distribution of resources meant that some groups, particularly those with inelastic human or physical capital, were made worse off. This implies that the long-run economic outcomes of the Industrious Revolution were contingent on the distribution of resources and the specific conditions of different social groups.

North and Weingast (1989, p.2) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) share a focus on the role of institutional evolution in shaping economic development in Europe, highlighting secure property rights and market development as key factors. In seventeenth-century England, the evolution of institutions governing public choice was crucial for fostering secure property rights and economic growth. This institutional framework not only governed economic exchange but also enforced rules and ensured a sovereign's commitment to them. The ability to engage in secure contracting across time and space was a necessary condition for the creation of modern economies dependent on specialization and division of labor (North and Weingast 1989, p.30). This perspective is supported by Broadberry and Gupta's analysis, which underscores the importance of economies of localization and the benefits derived from the proximity of producers in the same industry. These benefits include the availability of a skilled labor supply, specialized machine builders, and thick markets for inputs and outputs (Broadberry and Gupta 2006, p.9). Broadberry and Gupta also note that the formation of industrial districts may be consistent with proto-industrialization. This qualification suggests that while institutional evolution and secure property rights are crucial, the specific conditions and patterns of economic development can vary significantly across different regions and historical contexts. Therefore, the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted, with secure property rights and market development playing a pivotal role.

McCloskey's assertion that ideas and ideologies were the primary drivers of economic change in Northwestern Europe from 1600 to 1848 (McCloskey 2015, p.9; North 1989, p.5) aligns with the broader view that the role of institutions and ideologies in shaping long-run economic outcomes is multifaceted. McCloskey argues that the cultural and ideological landscape significantly influenced economic growth. This perspective is supported by North's emphasis on the importance of institutions in economic growth, highlighting that changes in institutions, particularly those affecting property rights, can increase productivity (North 1989, p.1). Yet, McCloskey's focus on ideas suggests that the cultural and intellectual environment can either facilitate or hinder economic development, depending on the prevailing ideologies. This dynamic is illustrated by McCloskey's example of France, where reactionary parties and anti-bourgeois sentiments impeded economic growth despite the presence of clever advocates of trade-tested betterment (McCloskey 2015, p.8). Thus, the interplay between ideas and institutions is crucial in determining long-run economic outcomes. The implications of this interplay are further complicated by Pamuk's findings on the long-term structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450 (Pamuk 2007, p.15). This historical event underscores how exogenous shocks can alter economic trajectories, highlighting the importance of both institutional frameworks and cultural ideologies in shaping economic development.

van Zanden (2009, p.27) and Stern (2004, p.8) argue that economic institutions and environmental factors play a pivotal role in shaping economic growth. The skill premium is also linked to the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation and the presence or absence of barriers to entry in skilled professions. This implies that countries with robust institutions for human capital development and efficient capital markets tend to have lower skill premiums, which in turn fosters economic growth. Stern (2004, p.8) complements this view by highlighting how the Hecksher-Ohlin trade theory suggests that developing countries, endowed with abundant labor and natural resources, specialize in producing goods intensive in these factors. This specialization can lead to a reduction in environmental degradation levels in developed countries and increases in middle-income countries, as the latter focus more on human capital and manufactured capital-intensive activities. This dynamic underscores the interconnectedness of economic institutions, environmental factors, and economic growth. Ogilvie (2007, p.25) further qualifies this by noting that poor contract enforcement and property rights can choke off economic dynamism, highlighting the importance of strong institutional frameworks in sustaining growth. The interplay between these factors suggests that while economic institutions and environmental conditions are crucial for economic growth, their impact is moderated by the efficiency of human capital formation and the robustness of property rights. This interplay is essential for understanding how different factors contribute to long-run economic outcomes, even if they do not directly address the role of gender and development.

Simson (2019, p.27) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) share a finding that the economic development of a region can be significantly influenced by factors such as budget policies and nutritional status, which in turn are shaped by historical events and socio-economic conditions. Simson demonstrates that post-independence budget policies in East Africa, though often criticized for being clientelist and unproductive, were not necessarily detrimental to economic development. The region's economic headwinds, including military instability and economic undiversification, posed significant challenges to implementing a proactive policy agenda, whether developmental or patrimonial. This suggests that the economic outcomes in these countries were more constrained by external factors than by internal policy inefficiencies. Koepke and Baten similarly highlight the importance of nutritional status in economic development, showing that periods of mortality crises, such as plague epidemics, could paradoxically improve nutritional quality due to enhanced land availability. This underscores the complex interplay between health, agriculture, and economic growth. These findings imply that economic development is deeply influenced by historical and environmental factors. However, this perspective is qualified by Nunn, who argues that Africa's slave trades had adverse long-term effects on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and insecurity. This historical trauma further complicated the region's economic trajectory, suggesting that the legacy of such events continues to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Temin (2002, p.4) and Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) both highlight the role of labor allocation in shaping long-term economic outcomes. Temin (2002, p.4) argues that post-WWII Europe's rapid growth was driven by a disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry. This disequilibrium was separate from the low income that generates conditional convergence. Low income in the standard story is produced by low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates. The developmental deficit highlighted here is produced by a misallocation of resources (Temin 2002, p.4; Temin 2002, p.9). Broadberry and Gardner support this perspective by showing that many Anglophone African economies had levels of per capita income above subsistence by the early twentieth century, challenging previous characterizations of the region's economic performance. This finding suggests that economic growth in Africa was not solely dependent on European colonial interventions, but rather was influenced by internal dynamics and resource allocation (Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.1; Broadberry and Gardner 2022, p.10). However, this perspective is qualified by Nunn, who argues that Africa's slave trades had adverse long-term effects on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and insecurity. This historical trauma further complicated the region's economic trajectory, suggesting that the legacy of such events continues to shape long-run economic outcomes.

Gender and development play a pivotal role in shaping long-run economic outcomes. This is evident in the quiet revolution that transformed women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S., as shown by Goldin (2006, p.3). African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, highlighting the importance of gender dynamics in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The skill premium, a predictor of long-term economic performance, is intrinsically linked to human capital formation and institutional efficiency (van Zanden 2009, p.27). This shows that gender roles and economic development are interconnected, as the skill premium reflects the efficiency of institutions for human capital formation (van Zanden 2009, p.27; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

The role of institutions in shaping economic outcomes is further underscored by the impact of colonial interventions. Institutions in New World societies with greater inequality advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework, which secured the property rights of the elite, often left the majority of the population at risk of being held up by the powerful elite after they undertook investments (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional perspective is complemented by the argument that policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, as shown by the dynamics of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). The skill premium is a crucial factor in understanding the 'little divergence' occurring within Europe between 1400 and 1800, and it helps explain the 'Great Divergence' as well (van Zanden 2009, p.25).

The legacy of historical trauma, such as Africa's slave trades, had adverse long-term effects on economic development by increasing ethnic fractionalization and insecurity (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This historical trauma further complicated the region's economic trajectory, suggesting that the legacy of such events continues to shape long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). The origins, dynamics, and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time are crucial for understanding the role of gender in economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). The link between gender norms and female labor force participation is well-established, but little is known about the origin of these cultural differences (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The historical role of African women in economic growth and development challenges views that marginalize their contributions, showing that gender dynamics are integral to economic development (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3).

The underlying point of agreement is that institutions and historical dynamics significantly shape long-run economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). The remaining disagreement is whether the reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism or geographic factors (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The specific historical evidence that settles this disagreement is the impact of colonial interventions on the property rights of the elite and the majority of the population (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This shows that the institutional framework introduced by colonialism played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic outcomes.
