The role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is a contentious issue in economic history. At stake is the fundamental question of whether and how gender dynamics influence economic growth, and if so, through what mechanisms. One position posits that gender roles and economic development are intertwined, with changes in gender dynamics driving economic growth. This perspective suggests that as societies evolve, the roles of men and women adapt, leading to increased productivity and economic expansion. Another position argues that economic development is primarily driven by institutional factors, with gender dynamics playing a secondary role. This view emphasizes that institutions shape economic outcomes by influencing the allocation of resources, the protection of property rights, and the distribution of wealth. The disagreement centers on the relative importance of gender dynamics versus institutional factors in driving economic growth, and whether gender roles are a cause or an effect of economic development. This tension is evident in the literature, with some scholars highlighting the transformative impact of changes in women's employment and education on economic growth, while others emphasize the role of institutions in shaping long-run economic outcomes. The interplay between gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes is complex and multifaceted, with both factors potentially reinforcing each other in a feedback loop. Goldin (2006, p.3) reports, “They were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector as domestics and laundresses.” Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.3) report, “Fewer works have sought to analyze the origins, dynamics and persistence of gender discrimination over historical time (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn 2013), or what happens to gender discrimination in the context of economic growth (Stotsky 2006).”

Acemoglu et al. (2002, p.33); (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6) argue that the long-run economic outcomes of former European colonies were significantly influenced by the institutional changes introduced by colonialism. This perspective emphasizes that the reversal in relative incomes among these colonies was driven by the extractive institutions that colonial powers imposed, which concentrated political and social power in the hands of a small elite. This elite secured property rights, while the majority of the population faced risks of being held up after investments. The institutional framework, which restricted competition and favored the elite, was shaped by the factor endowments of the colonies. For example, the abundance of mineral resources and low human capital labor in colonies like those in Spanish America led to extremely unequal wealth and income distributions, with large slave plantations dominating the economy (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This unequal distribution of wealth and human capital was exacerbated by policies that restricted European migration and awarded generous property and tribute to early settlers. Austin (2008, p.1) qualifies this view by noting that the 'reversal of fortune' thesis oversimplifies causation by compressing different historical periods and paths, which suggests that the impact of colonial institutions on long-run economic outcomes may be more complex and nuanced than initially proposed.

The contributions of women to economic growth are substantial, but the transformation of women's employment and education in different historical contexts is more complex and nuanced than often assumed (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8; Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). Lindert and Williamson explain that, in Britain, changes in the rate of unbalanced productivity advance and skills deepening were two critical forces driving the Kuznets Curve across the 19th century. This dynamic is echoed in the United States, where Goldin shows that women's employment shifted from "jobs" to "careers," with a significant increase in human capital investment. Women, who were often piece workers in manufacturing or labored in the service sector, saw little gain from formal human capital beyond common or elementary school. A small minority were professional workers, frequently teachers and clerical employees, a group that expanded enormously beginning in the 1910s. This shift from "jobs" to "careers" involved a change from agents who work because they and their families "need the money" to those who are employed, at least in part, because occupation and employment define one's fundamental identity and societal worth. While this transformation is evident in the United States, Akyeampong and Fofack argue that African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods. This highlights the centrality of women to production and reproduction in Africa, challenging views that marginalize their role. While these contributions are evident, the long-run economic outcomes of gender and development are less clear, as the historical context and institutional frameworks vary significantly across different regions and time periods.

North and Weingast argue that institutions governing public choice are critical for fostering secure property rights and economic growth, a perspective shared by North (North and Weingast 1989, p.2; North 1989, p.5; McCloskey 2015, p.9). This institutional framework is pivotal in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as it ensures that economic rules are consistently enforced and can be reliably changed, which is a necessary condition for economic growth. This is evident in North and Weingast's analysis of seventeenth-century England, where the evolution of institutions governing public choice facilitated secure contracting across time and space, enabling economic growth through specialization and division of labor.

This perspective on the role of institutions in economic growth is further supported by McCloskey, who argues that ideas and ideologies drive economic change. McCloskey's analysis of France in the eighteenth century illustrates how economic growth can be throttled by an anti-bourgeois and anti-libertarian elite, despite the presence of clever advocates of trade-tested betterment. This underscores the importance of ideologies and ideas in shaping long-run economic outcomes, as they can either facilitate or hinder the development of institutions that support economic growth. This highlights that the institutional framework alone is insufficient for economic growth; the underlying ideologies and ideas must also support economic development.

The role of technology and institutions in driving economic growth is pivotal, as Mokyr (2005, p.4) and Kuznets (1973, p.4) argue that the growth of useful knowledge and advancing technology are the mainsprings of economic growth (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4). This growth requires institutional and ideological adjustments. The expansion of useful knowledge, defined as the collective understanding of the physical environment and natural phenomena, facilitates economic growth by enabling societies to harness new technologies and innovations more effectively. Mokyr's emphasis on the "useful knowledge" highlights that economic growth is driven by the accumulation of knowledge that can be applied to practical problems. This shared finding (Mokyr 2005, p.4; Kuznets 1973, p.4; de Vries 1994, p.17) demonstrates that advancements in useful knowledge and institutional adjustments are crucial for modern economic growth. The Industrious Revolution, characterized by increased labor input and new consumption patterns, is a distinct phenomenon that preceded and facilitated the Industrial Revolution. This revolution, as de Vries argues, transformed the production system and market demand, leading to profound economic changes. The production system became more capital-intensive and oriented towards full-time male workers, while market demand shifted towards family-consumed goods. These changes had significant implications for the entire economy, including lower morbidity and mortality, better nutrition, and higher educational levels. The structural shifts required for economic growth necessitated rapid changes in the labor force's location and structure.

Ogilvie (2007, p.25) and Pamuk (2007, p.15) share a focus on how distributional conflicts shaped core economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe. The mechanisms involved in this process are often overlooked in discussions of long-run economic outcomes. These institutions, far from maximizing economic growth, often choked off dynamism through poor contract enforcement and property rights. This dynamic is evident in the high risks, poor information, and short time-horizons that characterized the period. Pamuk (2007, p.15) highlights the structural changes initiated by the Black Death, which led to a wage gap between the northwest and the rest of Europe by 1450. This gap was driven by severe labor shortages and declining interest rates, which encouraged human capital formation. The recovery of the population in the sixteenth century saw a rise in the skill premium in Italy, indicating that the long-term effects of these structural changes were complex and varied across regions. These findings suggest that the role of gender and development in shaping long-run economic outcomes must be understood within the broader context of institutional dynamics and distributional conflicts.

Stern (2004, p.8) and Koepke and Baten (2005, p.19) together argue that economic growth does not necessarily translate into improved living standards, especially in terms of nutritional status during industrialization periods. Stern (2004, p.8) notes that the environmental Kuznets curve suggests that economic growth can lead to environmental degradation in middle-income countries, reflecting a specialization in labor and natural resources. This implies that while economic growth might occur, the benefits do not always translate into better living standards for the population. This discrepancy suggests that the benefits of economic growth are not evenly distributed and that factors such as gender and social inequality play a significant role in determining who benefits from economic development. This finding is consistent with Komlos (1998, p.7), who argues that despite economic growth during the Industrial Revolution, biological well-being declined due to insufficient income gains and poor childhood nourishment. Komlos (1998, p.7) shows that the benefits of modern economic growth accrued unevenly, and some groups were made worse off. This uneven distribution of benefits highlights the importance of considering gender and social dynamics in understanding long-run economic outcomes.

Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) share with Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) a broad interest in historical economic growth and divergence, focusing on Africa and Asia respectively. They both challenge conventional narratives about the timing and causes of economic growth in these regions. This perspective suggests that the economic performance of these regions was more complex and nuanced than previously thought. One specific mechanism that emerges from this literature is the role of infrastructure and urbanization in driving economic growth. Broadberry and Gupta (2006, p.9) emphasize the benefits derived by a producer from the wider infrastructure associated with the spatial concentration of a diverse range of industries, which cannot be realized in a proto-industrial setting. Broadberry and Gardner (2022, p.1) suggest that the availability of data on the long-run performance of African economies has been limited, which complicates efforts to fully understand the role of infrastructure and urbanization in shaping economic outcomes. This points to a broader implication: the need for more comprehensive data and analysis to fully grasp the dynamics of economic growth and divergence in these regions.

Temin (2002, p.4) and van Zanden (1999, p.1) argue that labor allocation played a pivotal role in economic growth, especially during Europe's post-WWII Golden Age and early modern periods. This disequilibrium in labor allocation between agriculture and industry was a critical driver of economic growth. Temin (2002, p.4) highlights that this disequilibrium was distinct from the low income levels that typically generate conditional convergence. Instead, it was produced by a misallocation of resources, which Temin (2002, p.4) emphasizes as a developmental deficit. This deficit, in turn, was not solely attributable to low levels of physical and human capital relative to saving rates. This challenges the optimistic interpretations of early modern economic growth, suggesting that the relationship between economic development and living standards is more complex than often assumed. The implications of this misallocation extend beyond immediate economic outcomes, influencing long-term development trajectories.

Gender and development play crucial roles in shaping long-run economic outcomes, with significant contributions from both women and men. African women significantly contributed to economic growth and development during both pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3). This contribution was central to production and reproduction, challenging views that marginalize their role (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.10). The quiet revolution in women's employment, education, and family dynamics in the U.S. further illustrates this point. This transformation involved a shift from "jobs" to "careers," where the distinction concerns both horizon and human capital investment (Goldin 2006, p.1). Women's increasing participation in the labor force and their investment in human capital have had profound effects on long-term economic development.

The role of gender in economic development is also evident in the institutional frameworks that govern societies. In New World societies with greater inequality, institutions advantaged elites through various government policies, restricting competition and contributing to the persistence of inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.6). This institutional framework often led to a highly unequal distribution of wealth and human capital, with significant implications for long-run economic outcomes (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.5). The reversal in relative incomes among former European colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, which highlights the importance of institutions in shaping economic development (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This institutional perspective is crucial for understanding how gender dynamics influence economic growth and development.

The relationship between economic development and living standards is complex, as evidenced by the misallocation of resources and the impact of gender dynamics on long-term development trajectories (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.48). Policymakers do not necessarily have to choose between growth and equality, as shown by the dynamic models of growth and distribution that focus on factor incomes accruing to labor, land, and capital (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.8). The pay gaps and earnings inequality set in motion by unbalanced productivity advance favor rapid expansion in the derived demand for skills, offering great and increasing incentive to investment in human capital (Lindert and Williamson 1985, p.28). This highlights the importance of addressing gender disparities in economic development to achieve sustainable growth.

The underlying point of agreement is that gender dynamics significantly influence long-run economic outcomes (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). The precise remaining disagreement is the extent to which institutional changes or resource misallocation drive this influence. The historical evidence of African women's contributions to economic growth and development during pre-colonial and colonial periods (Akyeampong and Fofack 2014, p.3) settles this disagreement. This historical evidence underscores the centrality of women to production and reproduction, demonstrating that addressing gender disparities is crucial for long-term economic development.
