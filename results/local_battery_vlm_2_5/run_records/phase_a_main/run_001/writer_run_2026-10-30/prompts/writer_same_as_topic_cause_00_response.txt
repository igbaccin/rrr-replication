The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth ([E0105]; [E0097]). North (1989) highlights that the establishment of the Bank of England in 1694 and the introduction of new financial instruments led to a marked decline in transaction costs, which is crucial for economic growth. This financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, facilitating a long-term capital market. North and Weingast (1989) further elaborate that these institutions were pivotal in providing increasingly effective enforcement of property rights, which in turn reduced the costs per transaction. This institutional framework was not merely about the rules governing economic exchange but also about the political factors underpinning economic growth, specifically the commitment of the regime to these rules ([E0098]). The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic growth. However, North and Weingast (1989) also note that the institutional changes in England were not as revolutionary as previously thought, as France's economic performance before the French Revolution suggests that similar outcomes could be achieved without dramatic institutional shifts ([E0097]). This qualification underscores that while institutions are critical, their impact on economic growth can vary based on the specific historical and political context. The development of these institutions in England during the 17th century not only reduced transaction costs but also laid the groundwork for a stable economic environment that supported long-run growth.
