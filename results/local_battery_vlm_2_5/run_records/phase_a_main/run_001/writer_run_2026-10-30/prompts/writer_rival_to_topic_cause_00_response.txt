Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe ([E0129]; [E0161]).  The centrality of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is particularly evident in the case of New World societies, where initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power significantly influenced the development of institutions.  Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the extreme inequality in these societies led to the creation of institutions that favored elites.  For example, policies that restricted competition and limited access to resources were implemented to maintain the elite's dominant position.  These policies, in turn, hindered economic growth by stifling innovation and reducing overall productivity.  The persistence of these institutions over time further entrenched inequality, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that impeded long-term economic development.  However, the impact of these institutions on economic growth was not uniform across all societies.  For instance, Ogilvie notes that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were not solely driven by efficiency considerations but were also shaped by the power dynamics between different social groups ([E0130]). This perspective underscores that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a variety of factors including distributional conflicts and power dynamics.
