Hopkins and Simson ([E0073]; [E0153]) both argue that institutional reforms are pivotal for addressing poverty in Africa. The African experience has shown that the quality of public goods does not depend on ethnic homogeneity, and that the development of progressive institutions is possible even in ethnically heterogeneous societies. This perspective is particularly relevant when considering the broader view that institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth. Hopkins' work highlights that the new economic history of Africa has informed institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. This approach underscores the importance of "good" institutions, which provide incentives for investment essential for development. Simson's analysis of East Africa's post-independence expenditure trends further supports this view, showing that the region's economic trajectory was influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, and debt service payments. This suggests that targeted institutional reforms can significantly impact economic outcomes, even in the face of complex historical and political contexts. Hopkins ([E0074]) also notes the widening scope of development policy to incorporate governance, indicating a shift towards recognizing the role of institutions in economic development. This recognition has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. However, Simson ([E0154]) qualifies this by pointing out that the growth in military spending and debt service payments in post-colonial Africa posed significant challenges to economic stability. This highlights the need for institutional reforms that can mitigate such expenditures and redirect resources towards productive investments.
