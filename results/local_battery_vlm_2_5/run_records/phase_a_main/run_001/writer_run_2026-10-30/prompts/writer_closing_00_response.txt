The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate economic transactions and enforce property rights. North shows that institutions which specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth [E0106]. Austin argues that colonial administrators adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions [E0026]. This adaptation highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to local conditions. However, the legacy of mistrust, fostered by historical events such as the slave trade, continues to undermine the effectiveness of institutions and reduces the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative economic activities [E0025]. This mistrust remains a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa, as it hinders the development of institutions that could otherwise facilitate economic transactions and enforce property rights. The historical evidence of institutional adaptation and the ongoing challenges posed by mistrust point to the need for institutions that can effectively address these issues. This is where the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001) comes in, showing that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists did not maximize long-run growth [E0009]. This dynamic is further complicated by the fact that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, who may not prioritize long-run growth over short-term gains [E0009]. This perspective is shared by North, who emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth [E0105]. The historical evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies [E0010]. The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism, rather than geographic factors [E0010]. The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa [E0073] [E0074]. This synthesis shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and their role in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical and empirical evidence presented.
