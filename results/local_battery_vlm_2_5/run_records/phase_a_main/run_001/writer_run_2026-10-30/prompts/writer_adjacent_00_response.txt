Akyeampong and Fofack [E0017] [E0065] argue that the role of women and colonial education in African development reveals how specific aspects of colonial rule affected economic outcomes. Women's economic contributions were significant during both pre-colonial and colonial periods, but their access to land and resources was often limited by colonial policies that reinforced patriarchal alliances and customary law [E0017]. This codification of women's subordinate status under colonial rule had profound economic implications, as it restricted their ability to participate fully in the cash crop economy. For instance, the migration of farmers in search of land for cocoa cultivation in the Gold Coast was a key driver of economic growth, yet women were often relegated to marginal lands for food farming [E0017]. Frankema [E0065] highlights that the demand for missionary education in sub-Saharan Africa was influenced by new social and political institutions introduced during colonial rule. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn affected economic development. However, the impact of colonial education systems was not uniform across Africa. British rule, in particular, was more conducive to the development of formal education compared to other colonial powers [E0065]. This differential impact on education systems suggests that the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. Frankema [E0066] does not directly address the causal role of institutions in economic growth, but it does indicate that the development of formal education systems was a critical factor in economic outcomes.
