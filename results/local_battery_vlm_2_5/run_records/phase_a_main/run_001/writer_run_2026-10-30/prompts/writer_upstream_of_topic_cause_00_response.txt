Acemoglu et al. ([E0001]; [E0009]) argue that differences in European mortality rates during colonial periods significantly influenced the types of institutions established in colonies. High mortality rates in certain regions led Europeans to adopt extractive institutions, which were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole rather than promote long-term economic development. This extractive institutional framework was not merely a function of geography or climate, but a direct result of the strategic decisions made by European colonizers based on the expected mortality rates. For example, Acemoglu et al. ([E0001]) note that in regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states aimed at maximizing short-term resource extraction. Conversely, in regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies. Acemoglu et al. ([E0009]) highlight that these extractive institutions were designed to maximize rents for European colonists rather than to foster long-term growth, leading to persistent economic underdevelopment in regions with high mortality rates. However, Acemoglu et al. ([E0001]) further illustrate that the persistence of these extractive institutions is not inevitable, as they can be altered through political and economic reforms, suggesting that institutional change remains a viable path for economic development in regions historically burdened by extractive colonial legacies.
