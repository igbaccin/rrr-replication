The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is a central question in economic development, with significant implications for understanding historical trajectories and contemporary policy. The debate hinges on whether institutions are the primary drivers of economic growth or if other factors, such as geography or culture, play a more critical role. One live disagreement centers around the extent to which institutions determine economic outcomes. Some scholars argue that institutions are the key to long-term economic growth, while others posit that institutions are merely a reflection of deeper cultural or geographic factors. Another disagreement focuses on the nature of institutions themselves: are they primarily shaped by efficiency concerns, or are they the result of distributional conflicts and political struggles? These debates are crucial for policating whether institutional reforms can lead to sustained economic development. The debate highlights the need to understand the causal mechanisms through which institutions influence economic growth and the conditions under which institutional change occurs. Acemoglu et al. The development of new financial instruments and political institutions in England during the 17th century significantly reduced transaction costs, fostering long-run economic growth (North 1989, p.9; North and Weingast 1989, p.29). This financial revolution reflected increased security in the time dimension of property rights, facilitating a long-term capital market. This institutional framework encompassed both the rules governing economic exchange and the political factors underpinning economic growth, particularly the regime's commitment to these rules (North and Weingast 1989, p.2). The success of these institutions is evident in the capital markets, where secure property rights fostered economic growth. This qualification underscores that while institutions are critical, their impact on economic growth can vary based on the specific historical and political context. The development of these institutions in 17th-century England reduced transaction costs and laid the groundwork for a stable economic environment that supported long-run growth.

Ogilvie argues that distributional conflicts, rather than efficiency, better explain key economic institutions in pre-industrial Europe (Ogilvie 2007, p.12; Sokoloff and Engerman 2000, p.2). The centrality of distributional conflicts in shaping economic institutions is particularly evident in the case of New World societies, where initial inequalities in wealth, human capital, and political power significantly influenced the development of institutions. Sokoloff and Engerman argue that the extreme inequality in these societies led to the creation of institutions that favored elites. For example, policies that restricted competition and limited access to resources were implemented to maintain the elite's dominant position. These policies, in turn, hindered economic growth by stifling innovation and reducing overall productivity. The persistence of these institutions over time further entrenched inequality, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that impeded long-term economic development. The impact of these institutions on economic growth varied across societies. For instance, Ogilvie notes that the economic institutions of pre-industrial Europe, such as serfdom and guilds, were shaped by both efficiency considerations and the power dynamics between different social groups (Ogilvie 2007, p.3). This perspective underscores that the relationship between institutions and economic growth is complex and multifaceted, influenced by a variety of factors including distributional conflicts and power dynamics.

Hopkins (2009, p.18) and Simson (2019, p.2) both argue that institutional reforms are pivotal for addressing poverty in Africa. The African experience has shown that the quality of public goods does not depend on ethnic homogeneity, and that the development of progressive institutions is possible even in ethnically heterogeneous societies. This perspective is particularly relevant when considering the broader view that institutions fundamentally drive long-run economic growth. Hopkins' work highlights that the new economic history of Africa has informed institutional reforms aimed at eliminating poverty among the bottom billion people. This approach underscores the importance of "good" institutions, which provide incentives for investment essential for development. Simson's analysis of East Africa's post-independence expenditure trends further supports this view, showing that the region's economic trajectory was influenced by industrial policy, capital investments, and debt service payments. This suggests that targeted institutional reforms can significantly impact economic outcomes, even in the face of complex historical and political contexts. Hopkins (2009, p.8) also notes the widening scope of development policy to incorporate governance, indicating a shift towards recognizing the role of institutions in economic development. This recognition has enabled the new economic history of Africa to gain attention from development specialists. Simson (2019, p.11) qualifies this by pointing out that the growth in military spending and debt service payments in post-colonial Africa posed significant challenges to economic stability. This highlights the need for institutional reforms that can mitigate such expenditures and redirect resources towards productive investments.

High mortality rates in certain regions led Europeans to adopt extractive institutions, which were designed to rapidly transfer resources to the metropole rather than promote long-term economic development. This extractive institutional framework resulted from the strategic decisions made by European colonizers based on the expected mortality rates. For example, Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) note that in regions with high mortality, such as the Congo or the Gold Coast, Europeans established extractive states aimed at maximizing short-term resource extraction. In regions with lower mortality rates, such as the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Europeans settled and established institutions that enforced the rule of law and encouraged investment. This institutional divergence had profound implications for the long-term economic trajectories of these colonies.

Akyeampong and Fofack (2014, p.15) and Frankema (2012, p.15) argue that the role of women and colonial education in African development reveals how specific aspects of colonial rule affected economic outcomes. This codification of women's subordinate status under colonial rule had profound economic implications, as it restricted their ability to participate fully in the cash crop economy. These institutions altered the perceived benefits of formal education, which in turn affected economic development. The impact of colonial education systems varied across Africa. This differential impact on education systems suggests that the institutional frameworks imposed by colonial powers played a crucial role in shaping long-run economic growth in Africa. Frankema (2012, p.1) indicates that the development of formal education systems was a critical factor in economic outcomes.

Nunn and Wantchekon (2011, p.27) and Austin (2008, p.2) argue that the slave trade in Africa led to a culture of mistrust that persists today, impacting contemporary levels of trust within African societies. This mistrust is a significant factor in the region's economic underdevelopment, as it impedes the formation of social capital and the development of institutions that support economic growth. The slave trade disrupted traditional social structures and created an environment where individuals were more likely to mistrust others, leading to a persistent culture of mistrust that has hindered economic development. Austin (2008, p.15) notes that colonial administrations, in response to indigenous economic and political action, modified their institutional prescriptions. This adaptation, however, did not fully mitigate the mistrust fostered by the slave trade. The legacy of mistrust continues to affect economic outcomes, as it undermines the effectiveness of institutions and reduces the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative economic activities. This mistrust, therefore, remains a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa.

The role of institutions in shaping long-run economic growth is underscored by their ability to facilitate economic transactions and enforce property rights. North shows that institutions which specify efficient property rights and provide effective enforcement are crucial for economic growth (North 1989, p.6). Austin argues that colonial administrators adapted their institutional prescriptions in response to indigenous economic and political actions (Austin 2008, p.15). This adaptation highlights the dynamic nature of institutions and their responsiveness to local conditions. The legacy of mistrust, fostered by historical events such as the slave trade, continues to undermine the effectiveness of institutions and reduces the willingness of individuals to engage in cooperative economic activities (Austin 2008, p.2). This mistrust remains a significant barrier to economic growth in Africa, as it hinders the development of institutions that could otherwise facilitate economic transactions and enforce property rights. The historical evidence of institutional adaptation and the ongoing challenges posed by mistrust point to the need for institutions that can effectively address these issues. This is where the work of Acemoglu et al. (2001, p.27) comes in, showing that institutions designed to maximize rents to European colonists did not maximize long-run growth (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This dynamic is further complicated by the fact that institutions are shaped by politically powerful groups, who may not prioritize long-run growth over short-term gains (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.33). This perspective is shared by North, who emphasizes that the creation of institutions like the Bank of England led to a dramatic decline in transaction costs, reflecting increased security of property rights critical to economic growth (North 1989, p.9). The historical evidence shows that the interaction between institutions and the opportunity to industrialize played a central role in the long-run development of former colonies (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The reversal in relative incomes among former colonies is due to institutional changes introduced by colonialism (Acemoglu et al. 2002, p.6). The new economic history of Africa can inform institutional reforms and help eliminate poverty among the bottom billion people in Africa (Hopkins 2009, p.18; Hopkins 2009, p.8). This synthesis shows that institutions matter in economic growth and development, and their role in shaping long-run economic growth is evident in the historical and empirical evidence presented.
